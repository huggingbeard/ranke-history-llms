THE COMMUNITIES OF SCHUYLER COUNTY, NEW YORK, 1927¹ RAY E. WAKELEY 

Rural people at the present time are organizing their interests in centers which serve larger areas than heretofore. Trips to the large town and to the city are made more frequently. Broader relationships are formed, which take rural persons outside of the rural community to an increasing extent. Are new functional relationships arising between urban and rural groups? What are the effects of this development on the rural community? Information on these questions was sought by means of a survey of all organizations functioning in Schuyler County, New York, in 1927. The areas served by these organizations, and their membership and activities, were analyzed. An attempt was made to discover whether the concept of the rural community is serviceable in analyzing the organized activities of persons living in this rural area. 

Are present-day special-interest organizations supplanting the rural community? Is the community a result of favorable conditioning among special-interest organizations within a given area? What activities of what organizations vitalize the functioning and the structures of the community groupings found? Do any community groups exist in this area which have community functions in addition to the special-interest organizations within the area? It was the purpose of this survey to. obtain evidence on these questions, which perplex both sociologists and leaders of rural organizations. 

Some theory of the rural community is basic to any study of ruralcommunity relationships. Such a theory does not need to be considered a fixed standard, to be proved or disproved by the facts of the survey, but rather as a hypothesis, or "frame of reference," by the use of which the analysis of rural organizations is made more meaningful.

While there are many concepts of the rural community, they may be conveniently divided into two classes, classes, the geographical and the psychological. 2

The community is the smallest geographical unit of organized association of the chief human activities." THE GEOGRAPHICAL CONCEPT OF COMMUNITY 

The importance of the local area in defining the community was emphasized first by Galpin (1915). This geographical, or ecological, description of community relationships is very definite, and may be called a common-sense explanation of such relationships. 

Certain persons, for one reason or another, are living on contiguous pieces of land. They seek certain services which make it necessary for them to cooperate among themselves or to go to some center or centers where the desired services can be obtained. The centers may be at the same place for a number of persons and a number of services, or they may be different for each service. The persons who regularly or habitually patronize or prefer a certain center, may be located geographically in relation to the place where the center is located. The boundary line connecting the locations of the homes of the persons most distant from the centers who patronize it regularly for any service, determines the service. area of the center for the service under consideration. A combination or a composite of these service areas constitutes the community area. 

The question arises immediately, how many service areas are necessary to determine a community? Authorities who recognize the geographical importance of the service areas differ widely at this point. Some claim that the service areas are so variable in extent that it is impossible to combine them into any semblance of a unity which may be called a community. Others admit the possibility of such a combination, but raise the question, shall the community determined on a service-area basis include the total area included in the areas of all its secondary-service institutions such as banks and high schools, or shall it be determined by the median area for such services? Shall the community, geographically determined, include the areas of the primary-service agencies, such as church and grocery areas, within the larger area, or shall these smaller areas be considered separately? Some authorities feel that the geographical community should include the smallest area within which the people can obtain a majority of their services, and the largest area within which primary face-to-face contact takes place regularly. 

The geographical statement of the community is made in terms of the center, which contains the meeting places of the groups of which it is composed, and of the areas within which services are rendered by these groups. These areas are characterized by the degree of definiteness which their residents have established in their service relationships. 

It is not necessary that all the people in any geographical community area belong to the organizations within that area. If these persons do not belong, the organizations make an attempt to draw them into active participation. Though such a definite attempt may not be made, the influence of the activities of the organizations is present to those who are not members or patrons.

Activity of the people within a geographical community forces personal contact, and brings with it the necessity for the residents in the area to get along together. The extent of the necessary relationships arising out of such contacts defines rather loosely the extent to which psychological considerations enter into the statement of the geographical community. 

THE PSYCHOLOGICAL CONCEPT OF COMMUNITY 

The psychological concept of community is advocated by MacIver (1917) and others. These authorities maintain that the community consists of much more than the people in a geographic area who are relatively self-sufficient because of services received from service agencies maintained by them within the area. It is more than the sum, or composite, of the service agencies and their areas. Psychologically, the community is an area characterized by a common life which is held together by interest in or loyalty to common purposes and common organizations. 

Common interest or common loyalty is not the community. It is a product of the emotions, a result of pleasant relationships. Such pleasant relationships, growing out of pleasurable common activity, become the means whereby loyalty can be further developed. The degree of interest or of loyalty displayed becomes a test of the effectiveness of community processes and a measure of "community consciousness," or solidarity. 

Proponents of the psychological concept of the rural community, among whom are Hieronymus (1917) and Zimmerman and Taylor (1922), claim that the people living in any area constitute a psychological com. munity when, because of common interest, they have subscribed to common purposes until a common loyalty has arisen among them. This common loyalty is the outstanding characteristic of the people of the psychological community, and is recognized and maintained by them. But not all of the integrative sociological processes are cooperative. Conflict, competition, suggestion, imitation, and other processes, also may have an integrative function. Those who would define the community in psychological terms have failed to consider adequately some of the processes involved. 

Psychological technics by means of which the psychological community may be definitely and accurately determined, are lacking. But common loyalty arises from pleasurable common activity in furthering a common purpose. These common purposes and activities are usually expressed through organizations, and the organizations have patronage or membership areas which may be described in geographical terms. Thus the psychological community has geographical implications. However loyal the people of the psychological community may be, they must live some-where, and the geographical aspects of the psychological community are the only ones which at present lend themselves readily to a definite description. The sociological significance of such geographical presentations of the group concept has been well stated by Simmel (Spykman, 1925). 

Proponents of the geographical and of the psychological concept of community agree that the interrelationships between persons are fundamental: that the type, the number, and the degree of excellence of the services rendered within the area are basic to any consideration of the rural community. They agree also that the community is not a static

It is evident that the areas recognized by the proponents of the geographical description of the community are recognized by those favoring a psychological method of description. However, the latter group would not consider these areas as communities until the persons have achieved the development of a recognizable community consciousness. Undoubtedly a majority of rural sociologists find their own position somewhere between the two extremes here presented.

COMMUNITY FACTORS CONSIDERED 

Five factors received first consideration in this study: (1) the number, location, and size of centers in Schuyler County; (2) the number and grouping of organizations and service agencies; (3) the patronage area for each service agency or the membership area for each organization; (4) the number of members in each organization; (5) the community activities promoted by the organizations. The number and the exact distribution of the population, the age groupings, and the sex ratios are important, but lack of data limited the analysis which could be made of these factors.3

Activities of groups are the primary phenomena which give rise to process and structure, and analysis of group activities is a means by which process and structure may be described. It is evident, however, that not all activities have an equal influence on the community, and that some are more useful than others, in the description of communities in Schuyler County. Casual, temporary, or unorganized group activities are less useful in indicating community, though frequently they are useful in indicating social changes. The regular, permanent, or organized group activities are more useful in describing the community, and the analysis of organized groups constitutes the bulk of this study. 

COMMUNITY ACTIVITIES 

Organizations promote not only activities for their members, but also activities of a public or a semi-public nature. These public or semi-public activities are here called community activities, because they are organized to serve all the persons in the particular locality group concerned. Credit for such activities is given to the group or groups, organized or unorganized, which promoted them. 

The more general, public, and widespread the participation, and the greater the cooperation shown by the organization or organizations promoting the activity, the greater is the degree of community indicated. 

Ranked on this basis, there are eight general classes of community activities, as follows: (1) activities promoted by an organization for some. group outside its membership but not including the public; (2) activities promoted by an organization for another organization; (3) activities promoted by an organization for public participation; (4) activities promoted jointly by two or more organizations for their combined membership; (5) activities promoted by two or more organizations for some

METHOD USED 

The method used in this analysis of communities in Schuyler County is essentially the one first employed by Dr. C. J. Galpin and subsequently refined in technic and extended in application by Dr. J. H. Kolb, of the 

University of Wisconsin, Dr. Dwight Sanderson, of Cornell University, and Dr. Edmund deS. Brunner, of the Institute of Social and Religious 

Research. The survey outline for this study contained 162 points concerning which information was desired. Only such information as might be of significance in showing community relationships was included. 

As the study progressed, there was a constant interplay between three factors: the questions in the survey, the theory of the community, and the information obtained in the field. The number of questions was therefore materially reduced, and the social interrelationships stood out more clearly against the background of the data obtained. 

PROCEDURE IN THE FIELD 

The investigator became familiar with the questions and the information was obtained in a conversational manner. All notes were taken in diary form, one note to a small loose leaf, instead of on schedules. This method permitted the preservation of all information pertinent to the problem.

Every center in Schuyler County was visited. Information concerning each agency or organization was obtained by personal canvass except in Montour Falls and Watkins Glen. In these two larger centers, the number of economic agencies was ascertained from Dun's Mercantile Agency Reference Book and only those economic agencies were visited which rendered services directly to farmers. 

Leaders and representatives of each organization and agency visited gave the desired information. This was checked by information given by other leaders in the same center and in other centers until its accuracy was assured.

The smaller unincorporated villages and hamlet centers were visited first. Dwellers in the smaller centers were found to be more familiar with the organizations in their own centers, and with the relationships between their people and the organizations and agencies of the larger centers, than were the residents in the larger centers. Information obtained in the smaller centers was especially valuable in checking and understand-

FINDING THE AREAS 

Areas for the various organizations were determined by the smallest total area which included all active resident members. In each instance, particular care was taken to determine accurately where the boundaries of each area crossed the roads. Maps were used in getting and preserving this information.

Economic-service areas could not be determined on the basis of membership. They include the territory from which local patronage comes regularly. A definite area was determined independently for each specific service. Exactness and care in defining the services was repaid by increased definiteness in locating the corresponding areas. 

Distinctive features of various services Local grocery trade was found to be the best single indication of the general-store area. Groceries are the first commodities stocked, and the last to be sold out when a store discontinues. Other merchandise carried by general stores is often incidental and serves to supply the more general needs of grocery patrons in this area.

Cash groceries are characterized by an entirely different area, much wider in extent, within which the Atlantic and Pacific and Market Basket stores are the determining factors. A number of Red and White cash stores in Schuyler County had more of the characteristics of the general store, and were located in the smaller centers or in the open country. 

Hardware was divided into two classes in order to lend more definiteness to the area for that service. Heavy hardware, consisting of articles such as electric washers, stoves and ranges, large lots of fencing, and electric lighting and water systems, constitutes a specialized class of service characterized by an area county-wide or large in extent and by a tremendous amount of overlapping. Light hardware, consisting of small tools, nails, wire, and similar supplies, constitutes the basis for the hardware areas in this study. 

Banking areas were determined by the local area from which depositors regularly come to the bank. 

High-school areas were determined by the records of pupil attendance, all of which are on file with the district superintendents of schools. The areas so determined were checked by information furnished by the highschool principals and by educational leaders in the various localities. 

Farm-bureau areas were furnished by the manager of the county farm

Areas for each center were then drawn for each of the services investigated. These former areas not only give an additional check on the present areas, but also indicate the extent of some significant changes. 

Areas of marginal farming 

Marginal farming areas were determined (1) by a survey previously made by the Department of Agricultural Economics and Farm Manage ment at Cornell University (Vaughan, 1929), (2) by a road survey of other areas showing similar conditions with regard to farm buildings, condition of the roads, land utilization, and consolidation of farming enterprises, and (3) by unpublished data in the office of the Schuyler County Farm Bureau. Unincorporated-village centers Some definition of the limits of an unincorporated-village or hamlet center had to be made before the village-country distribution of the membership of organizations could be accurately determined. Enlarged maps. of twenty unincorporated population centers were made from post-office maps. These were drawn on cross-section paper to a scale of five inches to the mile. By means of a car equipped with an accurate speedometer, all houses, business places, churches, schoolhouses, and other meeting places were located on these maps. 

Analysis of these maps gave the following definition: An unincorporated village or hamlet consists of any number of houses, located outside an incorporated center, in which the maximum distance between adjacent occupied dwellings is less than one-tenth mile and in which the average density of occupied dwellings is thirty or more per mile of street. The type of each unincorporated center in Schuyler County was determined on the basis of this definition.

Schuyler County is a scenic rural area containing no cities nor large towns. It is a hilly country, whose many forest-covered hills are frequently separated by narrow valleys which yield limited opportunity for a prosperous agriculture.

Seneca Lake and its inlet valley extend southward into the county and make a barrier between the eastern and the western section (figure 1).

EARLY DEVELOPMENT AND ORGANIZATION 

The territory now known as Schuyler County was not inhabited by white men until after 1790. Initial settlements had been made in different parts of the county by 1800. Settlement proceeded rapidly, and 10,411 persons were residing in the area in 1820. The area which was incorporated as Schuyler County in 1854 had a population of 18,519 in 1850. 

Though the actual peak of population was not reached until 1870 (table 5, page 19), Schuyler County was full-grown at the time of its incorporation.

The county seat of Schuyler County was first established at Havana, now Montour Falls. A majority of the county board of supervisors wanted the county seat at Watkins, now Watkins Glen. Long and bitter strife resulted between the two villages, and twenty-three years after the incorporation of the county the county seat was officially transferred to Watkins, where it had been unofficially moved some years previously. 

Peirce and Hurd (1879:555) wrote: "Between the two villages the contention was fierce, and permeated and embittered all relations of the people, political, commercial, and social." Rivalry is still keen between the two villages and some jealousy persists, but the bitterness has been obliterated by the passing years.

The former county buildings in Montour Falls serve useful purposes. 

The former courthouse is now the town hall. The office of the county clerk is now the Episcopal parish house. A former law-office building now houses a library and a museum. A former three-story brick hotel is now an office building for a local manufacturing concern. 

A Methodist Episcopal and a Protestant Episcopal church were built at Catharine in 1809 and 1810, respectively. These two religious groups were among the first to be organized in the county. The first post office was located in Catlin, now Watkins Glen, in 1823. Development was gradual but steady until 1850. 

Village industries flourished and new organizations were started between 1850 and 1870. In Watkins the Masonic lodge was organized in 1853, a county agricultural society in 1855, the Watkins Academy in

1862, the first bank in 1864, the Odd Fellows lodge in 1868, and the library in 1870. Similar development was noted in the neighboring village of Havana, now Montour Falls, and to a lesser extent in other parts of the county.

CHARACTERISTICS OF THE POPULATION5 

The earliest settlers in Schuyler County were predominantly of English and New England ancestry, coming mostly from Connecticut and eastern New York, and a few coming from Pennsylvania. The population has retained its original character. 

The foreign-born are not numerous in Schuyler County. They constitute 3 per cent of the population of incorporated villages, and 4.3 per cent of the open-country population. 

Analysis of data on sex and age distribution indicates an excess of females and of persons 50 years of age or over in the villages, especially in the unincorporated villages. The open-country population contains a larger proportion of children under 15 years of age, and a larger proportion of males.

The population of Schuyler County remained relatively constant from 1850 to 1880 but has decreased steadily since that time (table 5). The loss has taken place on the farms and in the unincorporated villages, since the incorporated centers have made slight gains in population since 1880 (table 7, page 25).

Daily and weekly newspapers

Two weekly newspapers published in Watkins Glen have subscribers at every post office in the county and have a combined county circulation of approximately 2500. Few families subscribe to both papers, because they are owned by competitors who publish the news of the county, carry the advertising of the county-seat business men, and have local correspondents in each village and neighborhood in the county. The only other weekly newspaper with subscribers in Schuyler County is published in Trumansburg. It has subscibers in the Trumansburg mail-service area.

Four daily papers serve 2056 families in Schuyler County. Of these subscribers, 446 live on rural-delivery routes originating in the county.

Marketing of farm products

Opportunity for the cooperative marketing of produce by Schuyler County farmers is very limited. There were 123 farms reporting cooperative sales in 1925,8 and 99 farms reporting cooperative purchases. 

At present there are three milk plants that purchase milk from the dairymen in Schuyler County. These are located, respectively, at Cayuta, Odessa, and Watkins Glen. The milk-shipping stations at Odessa and 

Cayuta are owned by the Dairymen's League Cooperative Association, Inc., and the cheese factory at Watkins Glen buys milk from the Association.

Cooperative purchases consist chiefly of feeds, seeds, fertilizers, lime, and orchard supplies. These are bought mainly through farmer agents of the Grange League Federation Exchange at Reading Center or Odessa, or through the fruit-marketing association at Hector. Intensive effort is being made to sell most of the peaches in the fruit belt from roadside stands.

Beans, the principal cash crop, are sold mostly at the farms to dealers from Burdett, Watkins Glen, and Beaver Dams. Dundee is a marketing place for all kinds of farm produce from the northern part of the townships of Tyrone and Reading. Trumansburg is a strong marketing center for farmers living in the eastern half of Hector Township. 

Commercial poultrymen in and around Odessa ship their eggs individually, most of the eggs going to New York City and vicinity. Smallflock owners must sell to local stores for trade or cash. 

Peddling of produce is done by relatively few farmers, who go mostly to Elmira or to Ithaca. Occasionally a farmer goes regularly as far as 

Binghamton, and one trucker and dealer at Tyrone makes trips to Scranton, Pennsylvania, and to Monticello, New York, with loads of poultry and eggs or of grapes.

Most of the grain grown in Schuyler County is milled at Tyrone, Burdett, Mecklenburg, Odessa, Alpine, and Watkins Glen. feed stores at Hector, Trumansburg, Beaver Dams, and Dundee. Some feeds are sold at the mills, and the Grange League Federation Exchange ships cars of feed to local agents at Odessa and at Reading Center. Mills and feed stores are a factor in determining the groupings of the farmers around the trade centers of the county. The mills are stronger determinants than the feed stores, and have a rather sharply defined semispecialized service which marks the areas from which people come for the special service of milling wheat into pastry flour. Feed areas are difficult to determine because the feed business is divided between the mills and the feed stores. Nearly all feed dealers in Schuyler County will deliver feed at the farm, and much of the feed is delivered by the dealer's truck, thus having little if any influence upon the grouping of the people. 

People tend to do their buying where they do their marketing, but increasing amounts of farm produce are being marketed at the farm. 

Marketing in Schuyler County has little influence upon where people go. 

Those farmers who do market their own produce take it to one of the thirteen shipping stations within the county, but more particularly to one of the six principal marketing centers-Beaver Dams, Burdett, Dundee,

Odessa, Trumansburg, or Watkins Glen. This is in marked contrast to the situation fifty years ago, when the produce shipped out of the county went down Seneca Lake by boat, and the greater part of it was brought in by the farmers and shipped from Watkins Glen.

The only full-time blacksmith shop in the western part of the county is located at Altay. Blacksmiths are working full time at Odessa, Burdett, Mecklenburg, and Watkins Glen. Garage service for Schuyler County residents is centered in the town and the incorporated villages. Special-trade areas 

Good clothes, good furniture, heavy hardware, good shows, and other special trade, take Schuyler County residents to Watkins Glen, Elmira, Ithaca, and Corning in about the order named. Residents of Watkins 

Glen and Montour Falls go to Elmira for most of their out-of-town trading, but many of them go also to Rochester, especially for good clothes which they see advertised in the Rochester newspaper. The selling of good shoes is a specialized service in Schuyler County. There is a shoe store in Watkins Glen which gives excellent specialized service. 

This store meets the specialized demand for fine shoes within the county, and has large numbers of regular customers in Elmira and in Ithaca. 

Mail-order service

Mail-order service is general throughout Schuyler County. It consists mainly of sales of hardware, clothing, automobile supplies, furniture, and groceries, with the amount of trade in each ranking in the order mentioned.

Most of the groceries are purchased through local representatives of a Buffalo firm, and so are not direct mail orders. Local agents of this firm are working at Watkins Glen, Burdett, Montour Falls, Alpine, Valois, Mecklenburg, Reading Center, and Beaver Dams. Odessa is the only incorporated center in Schuyler County not having a local representative of this firm.

Though mail-order trading was rather generally reported and was practiced both in the villages and in the open country, it had little influence on the areas outlined. The greatest influence was reported at Alpine, where there was enough mail-order business in hardware to prevent the defining of a hardware area. More of the residents of Alpine buy their good clothes and furniture by mail, than go to any larger center to purchase them.

Summary 

The definiteness with which the economic-service areas may be determined depends upon the definiteness of the unit of service to be considered, the class or the degree of specialization of the service, the completeness of

the service rendered within an area, the distance to a competitor giving the same class of service, the topographical or other communication features, and the degree to which the people in a service area are bound together as a group. Loyalty or responsibility for other persons seldom enters into the determination of the service areas. Where loyalty is present, the areas are more clearly defined and are more exclusive. 

It is evident that persons go to different centers for different classes of service, but the effects of this are not so readily apparent. At first glance there seems to be no order nor regularity displayed in the relationships arising between buyers and sellers in Schuyler County. This seems particularly true of such service agencies as grocery stores. If a housewife wants ten pounds of sugar she will get it from a store in the nearest center, but if the family is going to attend a moving-picture show in Watkins Glen that same evening the groceries will be bought in that center. This is not always true, because some persons will return from Watkins Glen or some other larger center and purchase their groceries at the local store before proceeding to their homes. It may be stated as a general rule that if purchases of more than one class of service are to be madegroceries and a suit of clothes, for example-the more specialized service will determine the center at which the simpler service will be obtained. 

There is a marked tendency for the hardware, banking, and cash-grocery areas to coincide and divide the county into areas surrounding the incorporated centers. Within or overlapping these larger areas there are a number of smaller areas for economic service. The size and the definiteness of these smaller areas reflect the importance of the smaller places as economic-service centers. 

The specialized-service areas surrounding the towns and the cities are much larger in extent and are less sharply defined than the areas for the secondary services of hardware, banking, and cash groceries. This is particularly noticeable in Schuyler County because of the nearness of Elmira, Corning, Penn Yan, and Ithaca. The only complete center in the county is at Watkins Glen, and its services are not highly specialized. 

Specialized areas coincide closely with the area of distribution of the daily papers published in the various centers. 

Specialized services are not powerful factors at the present time in determining or limiting primary contact. Increased specialization is extending such contact between country and city people, and is the chief integrating factor between the people of Schuyler County and the people of the centers where specialized services are obtained.

Parent-teacher associations

The three parent-teacher associations in Schuyler County are affiliated with the high schools at Watkins Glen and Odessa and with the elementary school at Montour Falls. The areas are the same as for the high schools with the exception of the one at Montour Falls, which is limited to the village since that association is affiliated with the elementary school. Few parents living outside the high-school centers belong to the organization. 

In the case of Watkins Glen, most of the parents outside the town live so far away that their active participation is not expected. Plans are under way in Odessa to encourage parents of rural pupils to attend parentteacher meetings.

Library service 

Five libraries in Schuyler County are listed by the State Library Association. These are located respectively at Odessa, Montour Falls, Watkins 

Glen, Reading Center, and Hector. There are also small local libraries at Mecklenburg and Wedgewood.

These seven libraries contain a total of 16,000 volumes. Of this total, 11,800 volumes belong to the libraries at Watkins Glen and Montour Falls. These two libraries are used by the residents of the two places, to whom their service is free. There are 37 members of these libraries outside the incorporated limits. These usually come in contact with the library through the medium of high-school students. The library at Montour Falls formerly had members in Odessa and its vicinity. Since a library was established at Odessa in 1916, this membership has all been lost. The two larger libraries, at Watkins Glen and Montour 'Falls, give almost no service to farm families.

The smaller libraries, in Odessa and in the unincorporated centers, are used extensively by the open-country population. The Odessa library is free to the residents of Catharine Township, and farmers from all parts of the area use it.

The library hours are few and are more convenient for the residents of the villages; but the village merchants cooperate, and the farmers frequently leave books at the stores, from which the librarian obligingly gathers them up regularly. The free library at Reading Center is used by farmers within a radius of three miles of the village, the area corresponding to the other service areas for the village. The libraries at 

Hector, Mecklenburg, and Wedgewood are small and are relatively unimportant. Residents of Wedgewood go to the private home where the library is located, and serve themselves by getting books at any time. 

Areas for library service in Schuyler County are limited in size and the largest libraries serve the smallest geographical areas. The library area is limited by the area within which library service is free, but in no case is an area larger than the other service areas of the center where the library is located. Library service is not a specialized service in Schuyler County.

HEALTH-SERVICE AREA 

The hospital at Montour Falls was organized after the World War and is limited in its service. It is aided by the Shepard Electric Crane & Hoist, Inc., and is located in a former residence near the factory of that company. This hospital serves Schuyler County and an area to the south including Chambers and Millport. It serves more completely that part of these areas within which the residents get their other services from 

Watkins Glen, Montour Falls, Burdett, and Odessa.

Larger hospital centers outside Schuyler County include parts of that county in their service areas (figure 21). The northwestern corner of the county is served by hospitals at Geneva and Penn Yan, while Bath is the hospital center for the smaller area southwest of Tyrone village. The hospital at Corning serves the southwestern part of the county. The eastern part of Hector Township is served by the hospital at Ithaca. 

The situation in the central and southern parts of the county is more complicated, because the Lehigh Valley Railroad, crossing the county, gives easy access to hospitals at Geneva and at Sayre, Pennsylvania. The 

Sayre hospital is the official hospital for the railroad, and its cases go there. The western part of Hector Township is served by rail connection with Sayre and Geneva and by automobile roads to Elmira and Geneva. 

A case in this area may go to Geneva, Sayre, Elmira, or Montour Falls. 

The southeastern part of the county is served by Elmira and Sayre, and the southern and central areas are tributary to the Elmira hospitals. 

The average distance from the circumference of these hospital-service areas to the center is 32 miles. Thus it is evident that hospital service in Schuyler County is a highly specialized service characterized by a large service area.

PLACE OF RESIDENCE OF MEMBERS OF ORGANIZATIONS 

Further analysis of the membership in 133 organizations (table 15) shows that churches, high schools, parent-teacher associations, home bureaus, and Scout troops located in the incorporated places have the smallest percentages of membership residing outside the center. In these places the outside membership in the various organizations consists mostly of high-school students and lodge members residing outside the center. 

The unincorporated villages have the greatest total number of church members and of lodge, grange, and club members living outside the village center. Unincorporated-village centers owe their importance as religious and social centers to the large number of outside members belonging to the church or to the grange, or to both church and grange. 

Clearly, large numbers of rural dwellers depend upon membership in churches, granges, and lodges for their organized social contact. Church contacts are the most numerous, and the organized social contact of rural dwellers is limited largely to this one form. Rural persons also find contact elsewhere-unorganized contact among themselves, and casual contact with commercialized agencies in the larger centers; but their chief center for organized contact is the unincorporated village, and their chief agency for such contact is the rural church. 

COMMUNITIES IN SCHUYLER COUNTY 

Two fundamental changes in community have come with the increasing development of interest-group organization. Social organization no longer consists in a division of human association into neighborhood, community, town, and city groups. No stereotyped or formalized groupings are present which may be called communities to the exclusion of all other groups. No centers nor areas are self-sufficient. Community is, first, a matter of essential services extended in common to the residents of a given area, and secondly, a matter of functional interrelationships between organizations within a center and its tributary area, or between a center and any other center or centers functionally related to it, which make these services more effective in maintaining any locality group as an entity. 

Community in Schuyler County is a relative matter: a matter of the amount and type of service rendered, and a matter of the amount and type of interrelationship accompanying it. 

Essential services in Schuyler County are divided into four classes: primary, communication, secondary, and specialized. Primary services, which include the general store, the church, the grange, the community club, the elementary school, the garage or the blacksmith shop, the home

bureau, the farm bureau, and the 4-H Club, base their services on primary contact on an intimate, friendly, personal basis. These services are needed frequently, are of relatively small commercial value, and will be procured near at hand if they are available.

Agencies for communication service include the railroad, the post office (with or without rural mail delivery), and the telephone central. These demand a larger amount of business for their maintenance, and persons will go as far as is necessary to procure them. Frequently these services. are located rather arbitrarily: railroads with relation to larger cities to be connected, post offices and rural mail delivery with regard to earlier political prestige or earlier means of communication. They may or may not be efficient in relation to present conditions, but they are all necessary for a satisfactory farmers' market. 

Secondary services include the chain grocery store, the hardware store, the bank, the high school, the lodge, and the physician. The farmers' mill is included also, because of its particular service to the farming population. Secondary services complete the ordinary needs of the population, and these services extend to the limits of regular primary contact and association.

Specialized services include the parent-teacher association, the larger hardware, clothing, furniture, and department stores, and moving-picture shows. These services are needed less frequently, and persons will travel greater distances to obtain specialized service of suitable quality. 

Primary, secondary, and specialized services exhibit a marked tendency to group themselves fairly definitely in relation to one another (table 10, page 27) and to the various service centers in Schuyler County. On the basis of such grouping, three types of centers are indicated: neighborhood centers, primary community centers, and semi-complete and complete community centers. These classes are clearly differentiated by the following characteristics, as shown in figure 9 (page 28).

The neighborhood centers render primary services almost exclusively, and economic, religious, educational, and social services are present in each. If the grange or the community club is lacking, the social life centers in the church or in the school, as in Reynoldsville and Bennettsburg. 

The primary community centers also supply primary services, but are differentiated by the fact that in addition to these they supply communication services and some secondary services. 

The semi-complete and complete community centers have all the service organizations for giving primary, communication, and secondary services. 

Emphasis here is on completeness with regard to communication and secondary services. Specialized services appear in centers of this class. 

When the centers are arranged in a frequency series according to the number of services, the results verify the above classification. The range in number of services for twelve neighborhood centers is 3 to 6, average 5.1, mode 6. The range for ten primary community centers is 8 to 14, average 9.7, mode 10. The range for three semi-complete and complete community centers is 19 to 20. The number of cases is relatively small, but the tendency to divide into the classes outlined seems unmistakable. 

Too few specialized services are included to show the difference between the semi-complete and the complete centers.

COMMUNITY STRUCTURE 

The communities of Schuyler County show a general lack of anything which may be called formal community organization or structure. Leadership is mostly personal in character, with an occasional functioning group of leaders and frequently an organization leading, particularly in the neighborhood and the primary community centers. Although the structural organization of the communities of. Schuyler County often lacks definite form, the identity and life of a community is measured by the number and kind of its community activities. No type of community with virile organizations lacks such activities. 

So long as the communities of the county remain units based on faceto-face association and contact, there will be little need for formal community structure. Coordination of local interests and activities can take place directly by conference of the leaders. Attempts to create an overhead community organization would be ill-advised in so far as unifying the community is concerned, though such an organization might be valuable as a device to direct attention toward and to create interest in community problems or as a means of fostering functional relationships with communities of a different type.

COMMUNITY AND OTHER ACTIVITIES COMMUNITY ACTIVITIES 

Information regarding community activities in Schuyler County was obtained by special questioning of all informants concerning such activities during the fiscal year from July 1, 1926, to June 30, 1927. These activities were relatively few in number, too few to lend themselves to

minute classification, and were grouped in two general classes: general community activities, promoted by an organization for public participation; and organizational community activities, promoted by two or more organizations either for their combined membership or for public participation.11

General community activities are much more numerous than the organizational type, comprising seven-eighths (87.2 per cent) of the total (table 

16). While the general community activities are more numerous in the unincorporated-village centers, the organizational community activities are relatively more numerous in the incorporated villages. 

More than one-half (51.1 per cent) of the community activities in the county in 1926-27 were conducted or initiated by organizations in the unincorporated villages (table 16). The incorporated-village centers rank next, with 17.7 per cent, the open-country centers rank third, with 

16.6 per cent, the hamlet centers are fourth, and the town ranks the lowest. 

The incorporated villages have the largest number of community activities conducted by educational organizations. The town and the unincorporated villages are closely grouped for second and third places, respectively, with the open-country centers and the hamlets last. 

The unincorporated villages have two-thirds (67.8 per cent) of the community activities which are promoted by religious organizations. The hamlet centers, the open country, the incorporated villages, and the town, together comprise the other one-third and rank in the order mentioned. 

The unincorporated-village centers have also the largest proportion of community social activities. The open-country centers and the incorporated villages rank next, with the town and the hamlet centers showing the smallest numbers. 

The organizations vary materially with regard to the number and the kind of community activities which they conduct. High-school, parentteacher, and Scout groups are active in promoting community activities, and so raise the rating of the incorporated centers in which they are located in the number of community activities promoted by educational organizations. Church suppers, sociables, and entertainments in the incorporated centers, especially in the towns, are usually for one church group alone rather than for the public. This places the incorporated centers at a disadvantage in the number of community activities conducted by religious organizations. Fraternal lodges, both for men and for women, discourage public or cooperative activities with other organizations, and this places the incorporated centers at a disadvantage in the number of community activities conducted by social organizations. 

Churches and church organizations are outstanding leaders in the number of community activities promoted. The Ladies' Aid societies alone conduct more than one-third (34.7 per cent) of the total conducted by all organizations. All the churches and church organizations together promote more than one-half (51.5 per cent) of the community activities in

Organized programs of activities are quite as extensive in Montour Falls and in Watkins Glen as in Odessa, but the participation of highschool students living outside these centers is not so high in proportion to their number. DANCING 

Eleven centers in Schuyler County have public dances, and during the year in which this study was made there were approximately 380 public dances held in these centers. The villages, especially the larger unincorporated villages, are the centers within which the dances are held. In five of these centers the local residents do not attend and the entire crowd comes from a distance, often from a near-by city. Three of these five dances are conducted by individuals. The local residents do attend pub-

lic dances in the remaining six centers, and five of these six dances are conducted for and promoted by some organization located in the center. 

Public dances are not included with community activities in this study.

PSYCHOLOGY AND COMMUNITY 

The community which is psychologically self-contained and self-sufficient, and relatively immune to influences outside its area, is not found in Schuyler County. Solidarity is not present to a noticeable degree in a majority of the communities in the county, but it is present in some. 

This solidarity, or "we group" feeling, is closely and directly related to the number of community activities conducted in the communities here. studied. The kind of community activity, and in some cases the presence or absence of community activity, is related to the type of community.

The only complete community in the county has but 5 community activities of an organizational nature, but this number may not be characteristic for centers of this size.

Feelings similar to those which prompt friends to gather for a party or a visit or a picnic, prompt the members of the organizations in the neighborhood to conduct general community activities of a relatively simple nature, including the whole neighborhood. While such activities may or may not be planned to raise money, the fact that all are included is proof of the presence of the "we group" feeling. Activities in the primary community are slightly more formalized, but differ little from those of the neighborhood either in number or in complexity. The semi-complete community shows a marked increase in the number of general community activities, and a still more marked increase in the number of organizational community activities. There is some tendency toward exclusiveness in the churches in this class, as the church groups become larger and more of their activities are for members only. This tendency is still more marked in the complete community, where the lodges also conduct activities for members only and the high-school pupils living outside the center take little part in extra-curricular activities. This, together with the larger size of the complete center, is sufficient to account for a reduction in all classes of community activities in that center. 

Centers of the same type show wide variations in number and in type of community activities. Wedgewood has 31, and is first among the neighborhoods in respect to number. Perry City is second, with 26, and 

Monterey and Townsend are third and fourth, respectively. Wedgewood has a community association and a community hall which form the backbone for its community program. The Friends Meeting House in Perry 

City serves the purpose of a community hall. Monterey is a larger com-

munity than the others, and the churches are at present active in conducting activities for the community. Grange and church lead in promoting community activities in Townsend. Altay has a community hall where social activities are conducted for the community. The community association at Altay is led by persons who are not active in the Baptist Church, which is the leading organization in Altay. Altay is tied with the 

Kayutah-Lake-Cayutaville community in number of community activities, each having 18, which is above the average for neighborhoods in Schuyler County.

Beaver Dam leads the primary communities, with 28 community activities. Tyrone and Mecklenburg follow, with 18 each, and Valois has 16. 

Church and grange are leaders in promoting community activities in each of these primary centers. These activities are still predominantly general in type, as only one-tenth are sponsored by two or more organizations. 

The semi-complete centers each show evidences of community spirit, as is indicated by the number and the character of their community activities. In this type of community the principal difference is the increase in the number of community activities of the organizational type. This may be illustrated by the activities in Odessa, where the high school supports the 4-H Club which gave a program in the grange at Kayutah Lake. 

The community hall in Odessa is owned by the American Legion and was paid for in part by public subscription. The Legion and the churches conduct public exercises about the community Christmas tree at Christmas time. The fire company and the Legion conduct a celebration on Labor 

Day. The school, the Legion, and the churches unite in Memorial Day exercises.

Community activities in Montour Falls are promoted principally by the churches and by Cook Academy. The village has recently acquired a town hall, which furnishes headquarters for the firemen and for the Legion and is to be used for community activities. Much of the leadership in this community comes from the organization of the Shepard Electric Crane & Hoist, Inc., the principal manufacturing establishment in the village. 

The extent of the psychological community is closely associated with the extent of the primary-service areas in Schuyler County. Most communities exhibit characteristics of psychological communities within the borders. of their primary-service areas. Within the secondary-service areas but beyond the primary areas, the sense of solidarity weakens; and in the areas for specialized services, it is practically absent in every case. 

Two contrasting movements are noticeable in the semi-complete communities of Odessa and Montour Falls, both leading to similar results. 

Solidarity is increased in Montour Falls by the fact that the secondaryservice areas have been reduced so that they practically coincide with the primary areas. The primary areas in Odessa have been enlarged so that they nearly coincide with the secondary areas. The 4-H Club, for example, includes members from Kayutah Lake, Catharine, Alpine, and

Cayuta. When an attempt was made to organize a club at Alpine, the members there refused to transfer their membership from Odessa and the new club was not formed.

Psychological conditioning is a factor of considerable importance in determining relations leading toward or away from community solidarity.

BULLETIN 524 

Boys at Alpine attending high school at Odessa enjoy their activities in that connection until they associate Odessa with their good times in such a way that their other activities must center there to be considered satisfactory by them. Persons living in one of the primary centers in Schuyler 

County have been conditioned to dislike the activities of certain other persons, and hence they will not work in any organization in which those persons are members. This difficulty has seriously crippled the work of the grange in this community and has materially hindered the work of the churches and of the home bureau. It operates effectively to prevent community activities of an organizational type, such as church consolidation which is a felt need in this community. 

Leaders in some communities display such strong personal dominance that community activities are hindered. In a large proportion of the communities, social dominance is weak and the residents will not maintain their organizations in the face of strong personal opposition. Cooperative spirit during a period of time is in evidence or is lacking, in proportion to the group dominance or the personal dominance displayed by leaders in the community. When the personally dominant leader uses the group to maintain his dominance, his expressiveness or responsiveness to the suggestion of the members is lost, the solidarity becomes less, and the community solidarity suffers. The leader who considers first the welfare of the group and who is willing to rise or fall as the group develops, is doing the most to increase the solidarity of community groups in Schuy ler County. 

The psychological communities mentioned in this study do not include all the areas in the county. These communities show more than the average evidence of solidarity, and superior ability in getting things done for the community group. While solidarity frequently extends only to the limits of the areas for primary services, there is evidence to indicate that solidarity in these communities may be extended to the limits of the areas for secondary services.

INTEGRATION OF COMMUNITIES 

Neighborhoods and primary communities present the possibility of effectively integrating the services and areas of the semi-complete communities. Smaller but organized communities have at hand a means by which they may be regularly represented in the councils of larger communities: for example, Bennettsburg with Burdett and with Watkins Glen.

Such an interrelationship would strengthen each of the communities involved in it. When the service community becomes centralized, as did 

Montour Falls, it seems probable that it must lose area unless effective means are found whereby persons in the more distant parts of the tributary areas can be effectively represented in the larger center. Farm and village residents need a share in creating and maintaining organizations and institutions which dispense essential services to them. The only means of accomplishing this in Schuyler County at present is by direct membership and by individual participation. This problem is not being adequately solved by means of such individualistic effort, and the organizations do not recognize their opportunities in this direction.

developing out of the necessity for county-seat business. The interest centers, especially the more nearly complete community centers, for the northeastern and the western parts of the county are outside the county; in fact, they are located in three other counties-Tompkins, Yates, and Steuben. Persons living in these border areas-Wayne, Monterey, Mecklenburg, and Searsburg-feel that they are nominally outside of Schuyler County and that they belong in the county where their secondaryand complete-service organizations are centered. Ultimately the political boundaries, now a barrier to functional unity, should be modified to facilitate integration of all types of communities. 

Functional unity is being developed between the four incorporated places in Schuyler County. In 1916 the Elmira College Club began by accepting members from the incorporated places. The Rotary Club and the Zonta 

Club did the same, because of the proximity and the small size of the four centers. Organizations with members in these four centers are locally recognized as county organizations, and this unity is being fostered and encouraged by leaders in all four places. Other sections of the county are considered "outside" so far as county organizations are concerned. 

Contact between the farm people of Schuyler County and the larger town and city centers is increasing. This widening contact indicates the need for a wider functional relationship, the interrelationship of the semicomplete-service community with the specialized or the city center from which its specialized services are obtained. Such relationships have not been established to any measurable extent, and integration practically ceases at the limits of the complete-service community. Such interrelationships as are functioning were established on a county basis, and such arbitrary classification has definite limitations owing to its lack of incidence with natural areas. 

These interrelationships, which must be made functional through organized groups, are hampered in various ways. Most of the smaller centers are not sufficiently well organized to take an active part in the needed integration. The specialized centers recognize no responsibility in developing and strengthening the smaller centers. Leaders in rural communities are distrustful of such alliances, while city leaders do not appreciate rural conditions. The present movement is in the direction of more cordial interrelationships and an integrated organization of rural and urban life in an area which shall center in the cities and which shall be functionally interrelated down to the smallest rural units, the primary community and the neighborhood. Such a change in rural-urban relationships will bring changes to the complete community center because that is the unit in closest contact with city organizations, and hence the unit most affected by changes taking place. Only by and through increase in such interrelationships can any real equality be established between country and city, and by this means our rural and our urban culture may be effectively diffused. 

CONCLUSIONS 

Schuyler County is a typical rural area in the hilly plateau region of southern New York. Three incorporated villages and an incorporated town are located near the center of the county. Small unincorporated villages and hamlets are well distributed over the remaining area.

BULLETIN 524 

Unincorporated places are defined in this study as consisting of any number of houses, located outside an incorporated place, where the maximum distance between adjacent occupied dwellings is less than one-tenth mile and where the number of occupied dwellings averages thirty or more per mile of street. Nineteen unincorporated places were studied, ranging in population from 24 to 233.

The smaller the place, the greater is its proportional loss in population. 

The population of the incorporated places has remained practically stationary since 1900. The unincorporated places have lost population steadily. The population of the unincorporated villages decreased 27.3 per cent, and the population of the hamlets decreased 45.8 per cent, from 

1874 to 1927.

High schools, hardware stores, and cash grocery stores are centering more in the incorporated places, in centers where the schools offer courses in agriculture and home economics. 

Farm-bureau, home-bureau, and 4-H Club units are organized in the small unincorporated villages. Thus the services of the smaller places are increased. Small unincorporated places in areas where farm abandonment is occurring have maintained their importance by serving a larger territory at the expense of other unincorporated villages or hamlets near by. 

Several such hamlets have ceased to exist and are now open country. 

Direct relationship is indicated between abandoned farming areas and lack of organization. Residents in areas where submarginal farming predominates seem unable to maintain any kind of formal social organization. 

The dwellers in these areas frequently do without services which organizations bring, and rely entirely upon informal contact. They sometimes attend special meetings and events of the organizations nearest them. Only in exceptional cases do these persons belong to such organizations or contribute regularly to their support. 

Various economic-service areas can be easily located if some definite service is used as a basis. Cash groceries must be distinct from groceries purchased at the general store. Hardware must be divided into heavy hardware, which is a specialized service, and smaller, less expensive hardware, which is usually purchased locally. The service of grinding wheat for flour must be distinguished from the selling of feed, since feed, though sold by the mills, is sold also at feed stores and in some general stores. 

Persons wanting any specific service, such as cash groceries, will tend to procure it regularly at the same center. If, however, they attend a moving-picture show at some other center, or go there to market produce or for any other reason, the smaller service, such as cash groceries, may also be procured at the larger center. The carrying-out of this practice may be prevented by loyalty to the dealer in the smaller trade center. 

The principal center for organized contact of country people is the unincorporated village, and the agency furnishing the most of such organized contact is the rural church. Unincorporated villages constitute the most important type of service center for country people in Schuyler County. This is indicated by the large number of service agencies in proportion to the population, by the distance to which service areas extend into the country, by the large number of members of organizations in unincorporated villages who reside outside the villages, and by the

THE COMMUNITIES OF SCHUYLER County, New York, 1927 73 

activity of organizations in unincorporated villages in conducting community activities.

The church and the grange are the organizations to which country people usually belong, and the local social contact of these people is limited or ample depending upon the activity program of these two organizations. 

The church is the most important form of social organization in Schuyler County, in point of members reached. Membership in religious organizations as a whole averages higher than does that in either social or educational organizations, and the church is the most active organization in promoting general community activities. 

The concept of self-sufficiency seems valueless in describing community relationships in Schuyler County. No relatively self-sufficient communities were found. The entire area of the county may be included in geographical communities of one of four kinds, on the basis of the approximate incidence of their characteristic interest or service areas. Psychological communities include only those areas showing some exceptional evidence of solidarity, such as numerous or highly organized community activities. Most of the centers can qualify as psychological communities within their primary, or simple, service areas. Areas for school and for local government administration do not coincide with natural social areas found within the county, nor do natural areas coincide with county boundaries.

Communities are mostly quite unorganized in Schuyler County, and the data indicate little need for their more formal organization. It is clear, however, that the concept of community organization may well be broadened to include all organizations and all types of communities. Community organization is any form of organization by means of which community activities are promoted for any locality group. Any organization becomes a community organization in the extent to which it promotes such activities.

Closer functional relationships are highly desirable between all types of communities, and may be more easily developed if there is a sense of solidarity in the locality groups.
