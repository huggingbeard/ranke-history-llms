The Tale Robinson Crusoe. 

THE recent gift from a member of the Yale Library Associates adds to the treasures in the University Library the first edition, in three volumes, of Daniel Defoe's masterpiece, Robinson Crusoe, and further enriches an already great collection of 

Defoe's works and Defoeana.

Over a span years covering the period from 1689, the year which saw the publication of what is probably his first recognized tract, Reflections Upon the Late Great Revolution, Written by a Lay-Hand in the Country, For the Satisfaction of some Neighbours, until 1730, the year preceding his death, when he published An Effectual Scheme for the Immediate Preventing of Street Robberies and Suppressing the other Disorders of the Night, Defoe wrote, usually anonymously, nearly four hundred political and trade pamphlets, essays on the social and economic conditions of his time, moral treatises, poetical satires, and stories of adventure. He contributed to or edited nineteen periodicals appearing from time to time between 1704 and 1729, the most important of which was the Review. Defoe was its sole editor. In its early existence, which began on February 19, 1704, the Review was issued as a weekly newspaper, but when it ceased circulation on June 11, 1713, with Defoe's laconic "exit Review," it was appearing tri-weekly.

In addition, the extant letters from Defoe's pen number close to a hundred. They are addressed chiefly to the Whig and 

Tory leaders, Sidney, first Earl of Godolphin, who became Lord 

Treasurer for a third term in 1702, and Robert Harley, first

Earl of Oxford, at one time Chancellor of the Exchequer; to 

Charles Spencer, third Earl of Sunderland, who rivaled Harley

Up until 1864 when the attention of the late William Lee, one of the greatest of Defoe's biographers, was first directed to the Mercurius Politicus, Dormer's News Letter, and Nathaniel 

Mist's Weekly Journal by the discovery of the letters just men tioned from Daniel Defoe to Charles De La Faye, Defoe's earlier biographers, George Chalmers (1785), Walter Wilson (1830), William Hazlitt, the younger (1840), and John Forster (1855), generally supposed that in 1715 Defoe had written his last political essay. He had by that year suffered three imprisonments and two business failures involving enormous sums, his first imprisonment and his second bankruptcy being traced directly to the writing in 1702 of his best known pamphlet, The Shortest Way with the Dissenters, a masterpiece of irony. After 1715, no longer the political propagandist, Defoe was supposed to have turned from the arena of political and religious strife between Whigs and Tories, High Churchmen and

Dissenters, to a leisure that permitted his active spirit, his wit, and his genius to create the immortal Robinson Crusoe, Roxana, Captain Singleton, and Moll Flanders.

Consequently, though the ninety-nine titles in Chalmers's list were augmented to the "183 certain, and 52 attributed works," of Hazlitt's list, the catalogues of Defoe's works appended to these and the intervening biographies failed to include a quantity of tracts laid at Defoe's door by his contemporaries, Abel Boyer and John Toland among others. Written when its author was fifty-nine years old, The Life and Strange Surprizing Adventures of Robinson Crusoe was published on April 25, 1719, in an edition of, perhaps, twelve or fifteen hundred copies. It bears the imprint of William Taylor at the Sign of the Ship in Paternoster Row, one of the best known and most highly respected publishing booksellers of the early eighteenth century. The truth of the contemporary story that Defoe hawked the manuscript of Robinson Crusoe about the streets of London before he could find a publisher is much to be doubted, for as William Lee remarks: "I think the statement very improbable, as the talents of the author were too well known and appreciated for anything from his pen to go begging." A year earlier Taylor had printed for Defoe A Continuation of Letters Written by a Turkish Spy at Paris, and one may conjecture that the association continued, for several of Defoe's

The immediate popularity of Defoe's masterpiece, which according to tradition brought a fortune for its printer, is indicated by the fact that the eighth of August saw the publication of the second printing of a fourth edition. A fifth edition followed in 1720, and in 1722 William Taylor issued two printings of a sixth edition. There were innumerable piracies, as well as several abridgments, many of these issued in 1719. Robinson 

Crusoe also appeared in a newspaper of the time, The Original 

London Post, or Heathcote's Intelligence, between October 7, 1719, and October 19, 1720, in all probability the first appearance of an English novel in serial form. National popularity soon became universal. In 1720 the work was translated into 

French, German, and Dutch; into Italian in 1731; and subsequently into most of the languages of the civilized world. 

On August 20, 1719, William Taylor published the Second Part, The Farther Adventures of Robinson Crusoe. Perhaps 

Defoe misconstrued the success of Part I, and "the charm that has bewitched the world," as Charles Lamb expresses it, "of that solitary island isolation," and thinking that the public wanted more adventures, took Crusoe back to his desert island and its growing colony, thence into China, and by way of Tartary and Siberia once again to England. Robinson, the boy who ran away from home, returns to London in 1705 an old man of seventy-two.

The Third Part, Serious Reflections during the Life and 

Surprizing Adventures of Robinson Crusoe, a group of moral essays with the addition of the hero's name to help its sale, was issued on August 6, 1720. The "Publisher's Introduction" to this volume, prefacing the text, contains Taylor's own confession to his profits: "Not a dog has wag'd his Tongue at the

Work itself, nor has a Word been said to lessen the Value of it, but which has been the visible Effect of Envy at the good Fortune of the Bookseller." The naïveté of this is at once apparent,

This linking of two great publishing houses was more than one of mere location. At William Taylor's death on May 5, 1724, William Innys and John Osborn, his intimate friends and business associates, were appointed his executors. On the fourth of August, 1724, they sold to Thomas Longman, later to become Osborn's son-in-law, "all the household goods and books, both bound and in sheets, according to valuation" belonging to Taylor; and a contemporary newspaper, the London Journal for 

January 16, 1725, contains this intimate news item: "on Tuesday last (14th) Mr. Innys, an eminent Bookseller in St. Paul's churchyard, was marry'd to the Relict of Mr. Taylor, late of 

Paternoster Row, and worth thirty thousand Pounds."

Less than a month later, on February 3, 1725, occurred the sale of Taylor's copyrights. Entry no. 35 in the Sale Catalogue, dividing the copyright into two parts, reads: "Robinson Crusoe, in 2 vol. 800 and 12mo, with cuts 10£ to be paid [presumably to the author] for every 1000 of the first Part and 10£, 10s. more when every 1000 of the 2d Part is put to printing, and 5£ more when 500 of the 2d vol. are sold; just going to the Press." Entry no. 36 for Serious Reflections names "the whole," a marked copy of the catalogue which has survived informing us that one half of the copyright of the first two parts fetched 15£ 155., the other half 15£, but that the copyright of the Third Part was not sold.

Robinson Crusoe is not a well-printed book. It appeared in an age of book-pirating, inferior type, and careless composition, in a quarter century that had seen the London booksellers twice vainly applying to Parliament for a new Licensing Act to protect their rights. Its text is filled with many typographical errors, such as misprints, transposed type, and broken letters. Indeed, it is these very errors which make copies of the first edition of Robinson Crusoe so interesting bibliographically and assist the collector in detecting imperfect copies. We recall that before the middle of August, 1719, there were six separate printings of Part I. Numbered uniformly throughout, the pages of these editions are practically identical line for line and catchword for catchword. The texts of many rebound copies of the first edition contain leaves inserted from one of the later editions to make the copy complete. It is, therefore, not alone the extraordinary immediate popularity of Robinson Crusoe, resulting literally in its being read to pieces, which accounts for the rarity of the first edition. 

Not many copies of this world-book have survived in as perfect condition as that of the copy which comes to the Yale Library. Probably less than ten exist in libraries, or in private collections, bound in the panelled calf so typical of the period, with the frontispiece and maps of each volume unmarred, and with all the book advertisements intact.

The gift of this scarce and interesting book from a member of the Yale Library Associates adds another rarity to an important special collection in the Yale Library. Of the four hundred separate items now more or less definitely ascribed to Defoe, the Library possesses more than three hundred. Of the nineteen periodicals to which Defoe contributed, the Library has complete runs of eight covering the months during which Defoe was associated with them. Most important among these are the files of Applebee's Weekly Journal, Fog's Weekly 

Journal, the Protestant Post-Boy, and the Whitehall Evening

TOWARD the end of February, 1932, a young dealer from

Germany came to America bringing an unusual selection of rare books to be offered to libraries and collectors in this country. 

Quite unexpectedly, on the day before his departure from Munich, there had been placed in his hands a rare musical manuscript to be sold on commission for the owner. He was introduced at the Sterling Memorial Library and showed his stock -a Troy-Book, medical incunabula, early illustrated books, a specimen of music printing of the fifteenth century, and the Bach manuscript, the Clavier-Büchlein, written by the great Johann Sebastian Bach for the instruction of his eldest son, Wilhelm Friedemann.

At the Yale School of Music interest in Bach is a tradition, one might almost say an article of faith. Few possible acquisitions would have seemed so desirable. The little book was brought over to Sprague Hall and faculty and students were called in to see it, rather with the idea that they should at least be given an opportunity to look upon it once, before it passed into the keeping of some wealthier institution. Such things are not purchased from a slender budget, and with depression increasingly making itself felt there seemed little likelihood of finding a donor for such a treasure. However, one member of the faculty took measures to learn that, while there was no chance of a gift of the whole price from any one individual, a number of people would contribute to a fund, and there were ways of earning a considerable sum. In the end, with the support of the 

Keeper of Rare Books, an arrangement was reached with the dealer, Herr Finkenstaedt, whereby the manuscript was left at Yale to be paid for at the end of a stated time. The transaction was concluded in October, 1932, and the manuscript became the property of the Yale School of Music. 

Gifts came in from the Music School faculty and their

THE GAZETTE 

friends, not only in contributions of money but in concerts and lectures offered for this special purpose. A program of Bach music including a cantata awakened such interest that it has led to the formation of a Bach Cantata Club which, entering upon its second season, promises to become a permanent University organization.

The Clavier-Büchlein is a small oblong volume in a pasteboard binding covered with parchment. Such a blank-book might have been bought from the stationer ready for use, or bound to order. There are one hundred and forty-two pages, ruled by hand with a five-pronged pen. Inside the front cover, on the end paper, is the inscription in a fine italic hand which, in the manner of his time, Bach used for headings, dedications, and the like: "Clavier-Büchlein vor Wilhelm Friedemann Bach. angefangen in Cöthen den 22. Januar Aõ. 1720." Friedemann was then in his tenth year; his own attempt at a signature appears on the end paper at the back of the book. There is testimony to the fact that not only Friedemann but all of Bach's sons were taught from these pages, and no doubt many others, pupils of Friedemann and of later owners. There are signs of hard usage throughout, especially at the beginning-little boys do not always wash their hands before practicing, and the young 

Bachs were no exception. 

On the first flyleaf is a note by a former owner: "Dieses

Buch ist eine Seltenheit, denn es ist von dem gr. S. Bach geschrieben, und aus dem Nachlasse des verstorbenen sog. Clavier- 

Bachs auf dem Pädag. zu Halle, nebst vielen Bachschen und andern Musikalien und Bildern aus der B. Familie von mir im 

Jahre 1814 gekauft worden. [Signed] Kötschau." The "Clavier-Bach" was Johann Christian, a cousin, who had received the book from Friedemann. When in 1845, after Herr Kötschau's death, his library was sold at auction, the Büchlein was acquired by Herr Dr. Krug, of Naumburg, and came down through his family to Herr Siegfried Krug, now of Diessenammersee, near Munich, from whom it was bought for Yale. At the beginning of the Clavier-Büchlein Bach devotes two pages to instruction: the first setting forth the various clefs then in use, with the position of a scale in each; the second explaining the manner of playing the trills and other ornaments-the sign with its name, and the notes written out in full. This page alone is a valuable document, the only record Bach has left to show his own performance of these ornaments; it solves, in part, a most vexing and much discussed problem. Different methods of playing them came into use at a later time and are still prevalent; here is first-hand authority. Above the first page of music, headed "Applicatio," is the reverent ascription, "I.N.I." (In 

Nomine Jesu). This first little exercise preserves the only example that has come down to us of Bach's own fingering. A later number has the fingering noted, but in ink of a different color from that of the score, and in a system that did not come into use until after the time of Bach.

Succeeding pieces, sixty-two in all, are arranged in order of difficulty. They soon demand greater facility than could be expected of any ordinary beginner; as one commentator observes, the pupil must have made progress with seven-league boots. The contents include Preludes from the Little Preludes or Exercises for Beginners, Two- and Three-Part Inventions, and Preludes (but not Fugues) from the Well-Tempered Clavier. In addition to Bach's own compositions, there are two incomplete movements by J. C. Richter, whose identity is not certain; and a partita by G. H. Stölzel. A suite included here, and formerly supposed to be of Bach's composition, is found also in a manuscript of Johann Philipp Telemann, and has been decided on grounds of style to be his work. 

Not all of the book is written in Johann Sebastian Bach's own hand. In the forty-fifth volume of the Bach Gesellschaft edition of the complete works the Clavier-Büchlein is analysed in detail, though not printed in full, the numbers which make it up having been reproduced elsewhere in the set. The editor there

THE GAZETTE 

gives a carefully considered opinion as to the handwriting of each number; there might be a difference of judgment in regard to particular pieces, but the proportion, about two thirds of the book in Bach's autograph, would probably remain. No one has been able to say with certainty who was the copyist who wrote down the other pieces, probably a pupil, surely not Friedemann himself. The compositions at the beginning are written by Bach, very carefully, in an almost calligraphic hand. After the middle of the book the writing becomes more rapid, and the last numbers are set down at high speed with the fluency and economy of effort characteristic of Bach-curved ligatures instead of straight-drawn lines, note-heads and stems made at a single stroke. Corrections and erasures occur here and there, but, for the most part, the manuscript is remarkably clear and legible, a script of great beauty and individuality. An interesting touch appears in a number of places where, to avoid running over onto a second page, Bach has ruled very fine lines in the lower margin and written the final measures in minute notes. 

There is good reason to believe that many of the pieces, especially those of the latter half, were written down here for the first time. Some of the Inventions were afterwards elaborated, and the Preludes as they appear here have a more simple, not to say cruder, form than that in which they stand finally incorporated into the Well-Tempered Clavier. As the manuscript of the Wohltemperirtes Clavier is dated 1722, it would appear that the Clavier-Büchlein was written during a period of about two years.

The intimate association of the Friedemann Bach book, a volume of music written by one of the greatest composers of all time for his eldest and most gifted son, makes an appeal to the imagination which gives it a unique place. Besides this it has a real value for musical scholarship, showing as does no other manuscript, something of Bach's method of composition as well as his way of teaching. Its significance has been pointed out by biographers and editors from Spitta down to Dr. Terry. It is a recognized primary source for authentic readings of the pieces

The Clavier-Büchlein was first published as a whole in 1927, edited by Hermann Keller for the Bärenreiter-Verlag in Kassel, with a change from the old clefs to those in modern use, and other emendations. This charming and useful volume makes no pretense of being a critical edition and, indeed, leaves much to be desired. A reproduction in facsimile would be a welcome contribution to Bach literature, and there is the hope that we may be able to make the work public in this way at some not too distant future.

Bach manuscripts of first importance rarely come into the market. At the time of Johann Sebastian Bach's death very few of his works had been printed. The manuscripts were divided among his sons, and part of them were preserved carefully. 

Friedemann, sad to say, poor and improvident, parted with his one by one and they were scattered. Gradually, however, the greater part of all those still extant have found their way into the Royal Library, now the Preussische Staatsbibliothek, in 

Berlin. There are a few important autographs in other public collections, and a number still in private possession. Many, especially of the vocal works, cannot be traced; a Leipzig gardener was found using pages of cantata parts to shelter his young plants from the sun. Only a few Bach autographs, and those of relatively minor importance, single cantatas and instrumental works, have been brought to America. It is quite within the bounds of restraint to say that the Clavier-Büchlein is the most significant Bach manuscript in any American library.

POLITICAL unrest characterized the post-Napoleonic period in Europe, particularly in the countries along the Mediterranean coast, the rulers trying desperately to strengthen the reins of their restored powers and the liberals attempting to take advantage of the emergency to insert elements of republicanism into the governments. The struggle in Spain. drew the sympathetic assistance of many liberal Englishmen; Byron took active part in the efforts of the Carbonari at Ravenna; and popular opinion towards assisting the Greeks to escape Turkish rule was stimulated throughout Europe by the formation of philhellenic organizations of one sort or another. Insulated England was slow to feel the spark of these movements and scanned all attempts of the left-wing Whigs at concerted sympathetic expression or action with the rabid nervousness of the dominant Toryism. Such, indeed, was the situation there in January, 1823, when Andreas Luriottis arrived to plead the cause of assistance for the struggling Greeks.

When Byron, a youth of twenty-one imbued with the fund of classical lore provided him by Harrow and Cambridge, first visited Greece in 1809-1811, his visit was, as the title of his descriptive poem states, a pilgrimage to the shrines of the past glories of Ancient Greece. 

What little attention he gave in his poem to the contemporary Greeks was filled with youthful wisdom deduced from his actual observations: he found the modern Greeks shiftless and subservient, "Hereditary Bondsmen," and, clearly seeing the futility of thrusting freedom upon any people, he called upon them to arouse themselves. The Curse of 

Minerva, written at the same time, was a rebellious cry at the spoliation of the beloved monuments.

Throughout his literary career Greece was a source of high inspiration to Byron. The Giaour and The Bride of Abydos reflect this interest, and The Siege of Corinth retells an episode in eighteenth century Greek history. Late in 1819, when writing the third canto of Don Juan, Byron inserted, in a horribly burlesqued setting, one of his loveliest lyrics, "The Isles of Greece." The spirit of this lyric is identical with that of the second canto of Childe Harold in its melancholy hopelessness for the present prospect of Greece: "I dreamed that Greece might still be free," but, with a realization of their lack of leaders and the weakness of their native initiative, he adds:

In native swords, and native ranks, The only hope of courage dwells.

101 But Byron was then unaware of the subtle training and organization being achieved in Greece by the Hetairia Philiké and other such organizations. The aborted attempt of Ypsilanti to aid the Greeks early in 

1821 had too much of a foreign, especially too Russian, flavor to gain Byron's enthusiasm or interest. But the internal efforts of the Greeks in the same year, by which they gained almost full sway over the whole Greek peninsula, certainly attracted his attention. In April, 1821, Shelley in Pisa frequently met Prince Alexander Mavrocordato, soon to become the statesman of the Greek revolution, and on visiting Byron at Ravenna in August, the two poets undoubtedly talked at length of the Greeks' prospects. We know that Shelley, while there, read the cantos of 

Don Juan containing "The Isles of Greece," and that in the autumn, back in Pisa, he wrote his Hellas, a curious mixture of factual indignation against the Turks and ethereal republicanism for the Greeks. 

In November, 1822, the Congress of Verona, with its futile parade of pomp and the terrified conservatism of its meager achievement, drew Byron's satiric ire, which crystallized in The Age of Bronze. The Congress rejected the claims of Greece and thus prolonged the Greeks' struggle for independence, but for Byron that did not essentially matter. 

What really counted was that the Greeks themselves were aroused and "Greeks only should free Greece." He warns the Greeks against permitting Russian domination, and a fervent interest in their cause replaces his former melancholy hopelessness.

As Byron was finishing this poem, Andreas Luriottis, in London, was stirring up the liberal Whigs, attempting to obtain loans and to organize a London Greek Committee for circularizing the English people for subscriptions of money to aid the Greeks. His enthusiasm was inescapable and on February 28, 1823, the first meeting of the Committee took place. Edward Blaquière, who had been to Spain and had written lucid accounts of the republican struggles there, volunteered to return to Greece with Luriottis to make a survey of the chances for success, the chief obstacle being a disunited Greece with rival factions struggling for the controlling power. John Cam Hobhouse, Byron's friend and fellow pilgrim on the first voyage to Greece, asked Blaquière and Luriottis to stop at Genoa to talk with Byron, and, after their expressed willingness, he wrote to Byron of the formation of the Committee and of the proposed visit of the envoys.

Arthur Hallam's Centenary: 

A Bibliographical Note. 

THE year 1933 marked an unusual number of important centennials ranging from Chicago's Century of Progress to the hundredth anniversary of the Oxford Movement. The turn of the year brings yet another centennial, that of Tennyson's friend, Arthur Henry Hallam, upon whose grave the poet, in the language of a Victorian reviewer, laid his "scented and amaranthine wreath of flowers," In Memoriam. In

England the London Times Literary Supplement recognized the anni versary in a leading article recalling the day, September 15, 1833, when Hallam died at Vienna. In America the Yale University Library now arranges an appropriate exhibition in memory of January 3, 1834, when those mortal remains, brought "from the Italian shore" by the "fair ship" of Tennyson's musical verses, were buried at Clevedon in Somersetshire, overlooking the Bristol Channel.

THE GAZETTE 

105 To most people Arthur Hallam's name is familiar, but his work and all else that his name stood for a hundred years ago are but vague shadows. When one considers the affection and respect with which, as 

Tennyson wrote, "The man I held as half-divine" inspired his contemporaries, particularly that extraordinary band of his intimates, The Cambridge Apostles, there is a certain irony in the obscurity into which he has fallen. Two factors are responsible for this condition. First, of course, is Hallam's early death at twenty-two, cutting off what his friends confidently anticipated would be a brilliant career. 

Thy leaf has perished in the green 

And, while we breathe beneath the sun,

The world which credits what is done 

Is cold to all that might have been.

But in his short life Hallam produced a considerable body of prose and verse by which posterity could know him. Here the second factor in his present obscurity enters in, for this written record is not easy for the student of Victorian literature to obtain. No library possesses all of 

Hallam's work, and only thirty-seven of his seventy printed poems are in the editions commonly available. 

To remedy this situation an edition of Hallam's writings is in preparation. Meanwhile the Yale University Library honors the memory of the least known of the three nineteenth century men who have been the subjects of great English elegies. Recent Acquisitions in Gothic Fiction. 

UNTIL the present century, Yale's collection of minor English fiction was limited to a few battered volumes on the Linonia and 

Brothers shelves. Librarians ap parently forgot that the light reading of one generation may enrich the historical archives of the next. 

Only in very recent years has Yale attempted to collect the Gothic romances of the late Georgian period-those lurid tales of castles and abbeys, so dear to the sentimental ladies of Jane Austen's day. Harvard was among the first to realize the historical importance of novels which even the British 

Museum and the Bodleian had disdained to collect.

Obscurity is a virtue, among Gothic romances, so it is fortunate that several of our acquisitions are of unknown authorship.
