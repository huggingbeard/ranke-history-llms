Foreword 

Many preventable accidents occur each year in the home, at work, and on the highways. The present war emergency has added another prolific source of danger to life and limb. 

First aid instruction, by giving emphasis to the prevention of accidents and the care of the injured or sick, helps to save lives and contributes to the total war effort. A careful study of this first aid manual will prepare you to serve your family, your community and your country at this critical time. 

This manual is a summary of first aid rules and directions prepared in an easily understandable question and answer form. It will serve as a handy guide and reference for administering first aid to the injured or sick. The contents of this manual are based on the Text-Book of First Aid of the American Red Cross, to which due credit is hereby extended.

SIDNEY COYNE, First Aid Instructor.

FIRST AID FOR SERIOUS EMERGENCIES 

First aid is the immediate and temporary care given to an injured or sick person before the arrival of a physician or where medical aid is not readily obtainable. It is the duty of every individual to be able to render first aid to the injured or sick. It is equally important to be able to direct and assist others in case of injury to one's self. Every home, place of business and automobile, should be prepared for any emergency by having a complete first aid kit on hand at all times.

First aid training is instrumental in eliminating the causes of preventable accidents-ignorance, neglect, haste and carelessness. Accidents can be avoided by being safety conscious and forming correct safety habits. Needless suffering and the loss of time and money will be prevented if each individual observes safety rules, eliminates hazards, and is careful in the performance of his duties either at home, at business, on the highways, or while vacationing.

Unavoidable accidents involving mass casualties may be caused by bombings (and their attendant explosions, fires and release of poisonous gases), or by tornadoes, floods, etc. To minimize the consequences of such a catastrophe, the organized effort of emergency first aid units is required. 

There are five important steps in first aid. 

1. Act promptly to save victim's life and prevent further injury. 

2. Place victim in a reclining position. 

3. Determine nature and extent of injury. 

4. Administer first aid. 

5. Secure medical attention.

To save a victim's life, immediate attention and first aid care should be given for the following serious emergencies in the order named: 

1. Profuse bleeding (hemorrhage). 

2. Stoppage of breathing (asphyxia). 

3. Poisoning.

HEMORRHAGE 

A hemorrhage results when a blood vessel is cut or ruptured. Unless the flow of blood from an open artery or vein is immediately controlled, the loss of blood may be serious or fatal to the victim. Blood from a cut artery comes in spurts, and from a vein in a steady flow. 

There are three types of hemorrhage or bleeding: 

1. Capillary bleeding. 

2. Arterial hemorrhage. 

3. Venous hemorrhage.

The capillary type of bleeding is the least dangerous, while the arterial and venous types of hemorrhage are the most dangerous.

PATH OF ARTERIES TO HEAD, NECK AND ARM (Circles indicate Pressure Points) 

To control minor bleeding from a capillary, apply direct pressure over sterile compress or use a tight bandage. For arterial bleeding apply pressure between the wound and the heart. For venous bleeding apply pressure along the edge of the wound away from the heart.

To control serious bleeding from an artery, apply digital pressure to the proper arterial point. If digital pressure fails to stop the bleeding, apply a tourniquet. As a last resort, it is permissible to apply pressure with fingers directly over the wound in order to stop profuse bleeding.

Digital pressure is applied to: 

1. The temporal artery (just in front of ear, against the skull) to control bleeding from wounds of forehead and scalp. 

2. The facial artery (an inch forward from the angle of the jaw) to control bleeding from wounds of the face below the eye level. 

3. The carotid artery (at the side of the neck near the windpipe) to control bleeding from a cut throat. 

4. The subclavian artery (behind inner end of collar bone) to control bleeding from upper part of arm, the armpit or shoulder. 

5. The brachial artery (inside of upper arm) to control bleeding from the upper arm, forearm and hand. 

Pressure with the heel of the hand is applied to: 

1. The femoral artery (groin region) to control bleeding from the thigh, leg or foot.

Two safe places for applying the tourniquet to control bleeding are: 

1. Around the upper arm about a hand's breadth below the armpit. 

2. Around the thigh about the same distance below the groin.

The tourniquet is always a dangerous device and should be used with discretion. The use of a tourniquet is indicated only if other pressure methods fail to stop the bleeding. The following directions should be carefully observed: 

1. Place a firm pad over the cut artery and then wrap the tourniquet (a flat band or strap being at least one inch in width) twice around the limb. 

2. Tie a half knot, place a six inch stick over it, and fasten with a square knot. 

3. Wind the stick tight enough to control bleeding. 

4. Fasten stick to limb with a thin bandage to prevent it from becoming loose. 

5. Loosen tourniquet every 15 to 20 minutes, and repeat tightening and loosening process until bleeding ceases. 

6. Place sterile gauze compress over wound and bandage snugly.

ASPHYXIA 

Certain accidents, particularly electric shock, gas poisoning and drowning, cause a stoppage of breathing or asphyxia. 

Within a very short time after breathing stops, unconsciousness and death may result. It is therefore urgent for the first aider to apply artificial respiration as soon as possible in order to save the victim's life.

The Schafer Prone Pressure Method is recognized as the most efficient and safest procedure for applying artificial respiration. The first aider induces breathing in the victim by alternately compressing the lungs and releasing the pressure, thus causing air to flow in and out. 

The technique employed in the Schafer Prone Pressure 

Method is as follows: 

1. Lay the victim on his abdomen, one arm extended directly overhead, the other arm serving as a support for the side of the face, so that the nose and mouth are free for breathing. 

2. Kneel and assume a straddling position over the victim's thigh (see figure 1). 

3. Place palms of hands on the lower part of the victim's chest with the little fingers resting over the lowest ribs (see figure 2). 

4. With the arms held straight, swing forward slowly, so that the weight of your body (not more than 60 pounds of pressure for an adult; test pressure on bathroom scale) is gradually brought to bear on the victim. At the end of the forward swing, the first aider's shoulder should be directly over the heel of the hand (see figure 3). 

5. Immediately swing backward in order to completely remove pressure (see figure 4). 

6. Assume original straddling position for two seconds (see figure 5). 

7. Repeat artificial respiration (steps 3 to 6) according to the following rhythmic chant: Place hands; Swing forward; Sit back; And rest.

8. Continue artificial respiration according to this rhythm until natural breathing is restored or until the physician pronounces the victim dead. 

9. After being revived, do not allow the victim to sit up or stand.

POISONING 

A poison is any chemical or drug which is destructive to human life. Accidental poisoning may be the result of ignorance, error or carelessness with respect to the proper use of chemicals or drugs, either externally or internally. 

First Aid Care for External Poisoning 

1. Flush the skin with large amounts of water or other fluid in order to dilute and remove poisonous substance. 

2. Apply soothing oil or salve to relieve painful burn.

First Aid Care for Internal Poisoning 

1. Introduce large amounts of fluid (lukewarm water, salt water or soapsuds) to dilute the poison. 

2. Encourage vomiting by tickling back of throat with the fingers. 

3. Repeat steps 1 and 2 until stomach is well washed out. 

4. Give antidote to neutralize the effects of the poison. 

External poisoning. Water should be immediately applied to the skin in order to dilute and wash away the poisonous substance. If the poison is not freely soluble in water (e. g., carbolic acid), alcohol may be employed over the skin. Soap water is helpful in neutralizing a poison having strong acid properties. Lemon juice or vinegar in water counteracts a poison having strong alkaline properties. After the skin has been thoroughly freed from the poison, it should be covered with a mild oil or salve.

If a chemical solution is accidentally spattered into the eye, wash immediately with large quantities of water. Then put several drops of clean olive oil or mineral oil into the eye. 

An eye wash containing milk is especially valuable for burns caused by alkalies. 

A case of dermatitis venenata resulting from the application of hair coloring should not be treated by a first aider or beautician, but should be immediately referred to a skin specialist or dermatologist.

Internal poisoning. The inhalation of carbon monoxide or other toxic gas into the lungs, or the entrance of a potent chemical into the stomach, may be the cause of internal poisoning. 

An open gas jet or a break in a gas pipe or tubing may be responsible for carbon monoxide poisoning. The victim is readily overcome by this gas without warning. After quickly removing the victim into the fresh air, apply artificial respiration until natural breathing begins. 

Any poison which enters the stomach should be immediately diluted with lukewarm water, soap water or salt water, in order to weaken the poison and lessen its chance of absorption into the blood. The first aider should repeatedly try to induce vomiting by tickling the victim's throat with his fingers. If the fluid vomited is as clear as when swallowed, it is a sign that most of the poison has been removed from the system.

After the stomach has been washed out, the following additional first aid measures may be employed: 

1. Give an antidote. a) For a corrosive poison (strong acid, alkali or bichloride of mercury), give raw eggs or milk. b) For carbolic acid poisoning, give soapsuds and water. c) For iodine poisoning, give a mixture of starch and water. 

2. Give a laxative such as Epsom salts (magnesium sulphate), provided no corrosive poison has been taken.

CIRCULATORY AND RESPIRATORY SYSTEMS 

To render first aid in an intelligent and effective manner, it is necessary to have a familiar knowledge of the circulatory and respiratory systems which govern the vital functions of blood distribution and breathing.

CIRCULATORY SYSTEM 

1. What are the main parts of the circulatory system? 

The heart, blood, and blood vessels. 

2. What is the function of the circulatory system?

To distribute blood to and from all parts of the body. 

3. Describe the main characteristics of blood?

It is a red, salty and sticky fluid. 

4. How much blood is found in an adult weighing about 

150 pounds? 

Five to six quarts. 

5. The loss of how much blood is serious? 

Two pints. 

6. The loss of how much blood is fatal? 

Three pints. 

7. What are the three constituents of blood?

Plasma or serum, white cells, and red cells. 

8. What is the function of the plasma in the blood?

It carries food material, waste products, and the blood cells. 

9. What is the function of the red blood cells?

They impart red color to the blood and carry oxygen from the lungs to the tissues and carbon dioxide from the tissues to the lungs. 

10. What is the function of the white blood cells?

They devour and destroy germs and foreign matter, thus protecting the body against infection. 

11. What causes bleeding to stop?

The formation of a clot or plug at the end of the injured blood vessel. 

12. Where is the heart located?

It lies between the lungs, behind the breastbone, more to the left side than to the right.

13. What is the structure of the heart?

It is a hollow muscular organ divided into two upper chambers or auricles (left atrium and right atrium) and two lower chambers (left ventricle and right ventricle). 

14. What structures are found between the auricles and ventricles? 

Valves which prevent any back flow after a heart beat. 

15. What are the functions of the auricles and ventricles?

The auricles are receiving chambers, whereas the ventricles are pumping chambers. 

16. What are arteries? 

Blood vessels that carry blood away from the heart. 

17. What are veins? 

Blood vessels that carry blood toward the heart.

18. What are capillaries?

Very small vessels which connect the ends of arteries and veins. 

19. In which blood vessels is the pressure high?

Arteries. 

20. In which blood vessels is the pressure low? 

Veins and capillaries. 

21. Which blood vessels carry oxygen and food materials to the cells?

Arteries and capillaries. 

22. Which blood vessels carry carbon dioxide gas and other waste products from the cells? Veins. 

23. Where can the pulse be felt?

Wherever an artery lies close to the surface of the skin. 

24. How and where should the pulse beat be taken? 

Place four fingers (not thumb) along thumb side of the person's wrist; count the number of beats per minute. 

25. At what rate does the normal heart beat? 

72 times a minute for men; slightly faster (80 times a minute) for women and children.

RESPIRATORY SYSTEM 

1. What is respiration?

The function of breathing, including both inhalation and expiration, taking place in the lungs. 

2. What change occurs during inhalation?

Air is drawn into the lungs and the blood is supplied with oxygen gas. 

3. What change occurs during expiration?

Air containing carbon dioxide, water and other waste products, is expelled from the lungs. 

4. Which organs comprise the respiratory system?

Nose, mouth, windpipe (trachea), bronchial tubes, and lungs. 

5. What is the advantage of nose breathing?

The air is warmed, filtered and moistened. 

6. What is the average respiratory rate for a normal adult? 

17 times a minute.

7. What quantity of air passes into and out of the lungs during each normal respiration? 

About one pint. 

8. How much air normally remains in the lungs? 

From four to six times the amount expelled. 

9. What protection is afforded to the top of the windpipe?

A flap, called the epiglottis, automatically closes each time food or liquid is swallowed. 

10. Why should liquids or solids not be given to an unconscious person?

The epiglottis remains open and does not protect the windpipe. As a result, the food gets into the windpipe and chokes the victim. 

11. What controls the process of breathing?

The presence of a certain amount of carbon dioxide in the blood which stimulates a nerve center in the brain. 

12. How is breathing activated by this nerve center in the brain?

Impulses from this nerve center act on certain muscles which increase and decrease the size of the chest. 

13. What may cause a stoppage of breathing?

Any accident (electric shock, drowning, etc.) which paralyzes this nerve center in the brain.

GENERAL FIRST AID 

1. What is first aid? 

Prompt emergency care given to an injured or sick person before the arrival of the doctor. 

2. Why should everyone be familiar with the details of first aid? 

To be able to give prompt efficient help to the injured or direct and assist others in case of injury to oneself. 

3. How should an injured person be handled if the assistant is uncertain as to the first aid to be given? 

Call for medical aid. Keep injured person warm and in a level position. Protect victim from further injury. 

4. How should an injured person be handled if the assistant is certain as to the first aid to be given? 

Place victim in comfortable and level position. Determine nature and extent of injury before administering first aid. Give immediate attention to most serious injury (hemorrhage, asphyxia or poisoning). Call for physician. 

5. What qualities should a first aider possess?

A first aider should keep cool, act quickly, be gentle, and tactfully assume charge of the situation. 

6. What are two main purposes of first aid training?

To prevent accidents; and if accidents occur, to know what to do and what not to do at the proper time. 

7. How can fear in an injured person be overcome?

Make the injured person comfortable. Assure him that the wound is not serious, and keep his head turned away from the injury. 

8. What information should be given when calling for a doctor or an ambulance?

Exact location of injured person, what first aid is being given, and what supplies are available at the scene of the accident. 

9. What are four causes of preventable accidents? 

Ignorance, neglect, haste, and carelessness. 

10. How can accidents be prevented?

By observing safety rules, correcting hazards, and being careful in the performance of one's duties.

11. What may be the causes of unavoidable accidents involving mass casualties?

Bombings (their attendant explosions, fires, release of poisonous gases), tornadoes, floods, etc.

FIRST AID KIT 

1. What is an emergency first aid kit?

A complete assortment of first aid supplies properly arranged in a closed container. 

2. What compresses and bandages should be found in a first aid kit? 

Individual packages of 1 inch compress on adhesive (band-aids). 

Sterile gauze squares (3 in. by 3 in.). 

One square yard of sterile gauze.

Assorted sterile bandage compresses. 

Roll of adhesive (2 in. by 5 yards). 

1 in. and 2 in. roller bandage. 

Triangular bandages. 

3. What other supplies should be included in a first aid kit? 

Burn ointment (5% tannic acid jelly). 

2% tincture of iodine. 

Aromatic spirits of ammonia. 

Scissors, paper cups, and applicators. 

4. What is the advantage of buying iodine and spirits of ammonia in sealed glass ampules?

The potency of the product is maintained until ready to be used. There is no danger of spilling or breaking bottles. 

5. What medicinal supplies should be kept in the home or camp medicine cabinet?

Rubbing alcohol, sodium bicarbonate, boric acid, Epsom salts and calamine lotion. 

6. What safety precautions should be observed in the use of iodine?

Permit the iodine to dry on the skin before applying compress and bandage. Never apply iodine to the eyes, nor reapply twice to the same place.

DRESSINGS AND BANDAGES 

1. What is a dressing or compress?

Material applied over a wound or burn. 

2. What material may be used as a dressing or compress?

Sterile gauze pads, kept wrapped in sealed envelopes until used. Where these are not available, improvised dressings may be made from freshly laundered cloth scorched over heat or flame. 

3. Which materials should never be used as a compress or dressing?

Absorbent cotton, collodion, or adhesive tape. 

4. What should be the condition of the compress or dressing? 

Clean and sterile. 

5. What is a bandage compress?

A sterile gauze pad attached to the middle of a bandage strip. 

6. What is the use of a compress on adhesive (band-aid) ?

It is a dressing for small cuts, scratches, wounds or blisters of the fingers or toe, etc. 

7. What care hould be observed in handling a compress or dressing? 

Keep dirty fingers or objects away from compress. Do not allow it to drag across the dirty skin or slip about on the skin. 

8. What material may be used as a bandage? 

Gauze or cloth material. 

9. How is a triangular bandage made? 

Use a 36 or 40 inch square of unbleached muslin cr similar cloth and cut it diagonally. 

10. What are four uses of bandages?

To hold dressings in place, keep splints in place, control bleeding by pressure, and support an injured arm by means of a sling. 

11. May a bandage be used directly over a wound? 

No; apply sterile compress or dressing first. 

12. Where should knots in bandages be fastened?

Where they are easy to reach and do not cause discomfort to the victim.

CONTROL OF HEMORRHAGE 

1. What is another name for hemorrhage? Bleeding. 

2. What are the three types of hemorrhage?

Arterial, venous, and capillary bleeding. 

3. How can arterial hemorrhage be recognized?

The blood is bright red and escapes in heavy spurts. 

4. How can venous hemorrhage be recognized?

The blow is dark red or purple and flows in a steady stream. 

5. How is capillary bleeding recognized?

The blood oozes from a cut surface. 

6. Which type of hemorrhage is the most dangerous?

Arterial hemorrhage. 

7. Which type of hemorrhage is the least dangerous?

Capillary hemorrhage. 

8. By which two methods can a hemorrhage be controlled? 

Natural method, by the formation of a clot or plug. 

Artificial method, by the application of pressure. 

9. Which type of hemorrhage is controlled naturally?

The blood from a capillary hemorrhage forms a clot within 3 to 6 minutes and thereby stops bleeding. 

10. Which types of hemorrhage are controlled artificially? 

Arterial and venous hemorrhage. 

11. What are four artificial means of hemorrhage control? 

Digital pressure, direct pressure over compress, pressure bandage, and tourniquet. 

12. On which side of the wound is pressure applied to stop an arterial hemorrhage?

On the side toward the heart. 

13. On which side of the wound is pressure applied to stop a venous hemorrhage? 

On the side away from the heart. 

14. What is a digital pressure point?

A place where pressure is applied with the fingers to a main artery which comes close to the surface of the skin and which lies over a bone.

15. Name and locate the six digital pressure points, indicating the area controlled by each artery. 

1. Temporal (near opening of ear)—controls bleeding above eye level. 

2. Facial (under lower jaw bone)-controls bleeding below eye level. 

3. Carotid (side of neck)-controls bleeding from cut throat. 

4. Subclavian (behind inner end of collar bone)-controls bleeding from shoulder and armpit. 

5. Brachial (inner side of upper arm-controls bleeding from arm. 

6. Femoral (groin region)—controls bleeding from leg. 

16. In which type of hemorrhage should the limb be elevated?

In venous hemorrhage, provided the limb is not fractured. 

17. When should a tourniquet be used?

Only when other artificial methods fail to control the bleeding. 

18. Name two safe and convenient places for applying a tourniquet?

Around the upper arm about a hand's breadth below the armpit, or around the thigh about the same distance below the groin. 

19. What is the proper way to apply a tourniquet?

Place a firm pad over cut artery and wrap band twice around limb. Tie a half knot, insert stick, and fasten with a square knot. Twist stick slowly until bleeding stops. Keep stick in place with a light bandage. 

20. What precautions should be observed in applying a tourniquet? 

Avoid using a rough band or too much force. Never cover a tourniquet. Loosen it every 15 to 20 minutes and remove only when bleeding has been stopped. 

21. How can loosening of the blod clot be prevented? 

Do not move the injured part; do not tear a compress from the wound.

22. What is the first aid care for nose bleed?

Place victim in sitting position with head thrown slightly back. Apply cold wet compresses or press nostrils firmly together until a clot forms. 

23. Distinguish between bleeding from the lungs, stomach and intestines. 

Lungs-bright red in color, frothy in appearance.

Stomach-appearance of coffee grounds. 

Intestines-appearance of tarry stools. 

24. What are the symptoms of internal bleeding?

Restlessness, anxiety, thirst, and the typical symptoms of shock. 

25. What is the first aid care for internal bleeding?

Lay victim on back, turning head to one side for vomiting or coughing. Keep victim warm, quiet and comfortable. No stimulants. 

26. When may a stimulant such as tea or coffee be given?

After the bleeding has been controlled. 

27. What bodily condition accompanies all cases of serious bleeding? Shock.

WOUNDS 

1. What is a wound?

A break or cut in the skin or mucous membrane. 

2. List the causes of four kinds of wounds. 

Abrasion-rubbing or scraping off the skin.

Incised wound-caused by sharp cutting instrument. 

Lacerated or torn wound-caused by blunt instrument. 

Punctured wound-caused by penetrating instrument. 

3. How should clothing be removed to examine and care for a wound?

Rip or cut the seams of the clothing at the injured part. 

4. What are three dangers of wounds? 

Serious bleeding, shock, and infection. 

5. What is the cause of an infection?

The entrance and multiplication of germs in the skin, and the lowered resistance of the victim. 

6. What are five signs of an infection? 

Redness, heat, pain, swelling, and pus. 

7. What are two dangers of an infected wound? 

Delayed healing and blood poisoning. 

8. What is the first aid care for wounds in which bleeding is not severe ? 

Prevent infection. Keep dirty objects away. Cleanse away from wound with rubbing alcohol. Apply 2% tincture of iodine with swab. Allow to dry. Apply sterile dressing and bandage. 

9. What is the first aid care for wounds in which bleeding is severe?

Uncover wound to control hemorrhage by the application of pressure. Prevent infection by applying iodine, dressing and bandage, to the wound. 

10. What are the particular dangers of a punctured wound?

Very likely to become infected. The wound does not bleed freely. It is difficult to cleanse and apply antiseptic. The absence of air favors the growth of tetanus germs if present in the wound. 

11. What is the first aid care for a punctured wound?

Encourage bleeding by gently pressing surrounding tissues. Apply iodine with applicator, deep into the wound.

SHOCK 

1. What is shock?

A sudden depressed condition of the nervous system and bodily functions. 

2. What effect does shock have on the circulatory system?

The nerves lose control over the blood vessels, resulting in lowered blood pressure, stagnation of blood in the abdominal area, and an insufficient supply of blood to other parts of the body. 

3. What are the causes of shock? 

Severe injury, profuse bleeding, fear and poisoning. 

4. What are the symptoms of shock?

Pale face having an anxious and frightened expression; cold skin and chills; weakness; nausea; weak and rapid pulse; irregular breathing. 

5. What is the first aid care for shock?

Supply warmth with blankets and heated objects. Lay victim on back with head low and feet high. If conscious, give warm drinks (tea or coffee). If unconscious, place inhalation stimulant (smelling salts) near the nose.

6. How should heated objects be applied to the victim?

Test the temperature of hot water bottles or other heated objects to see that they are not too hot, wrap in cloth, and apply under armpits, between legs, and over abdomen. 

7. When should the victim be kept in a level position? 

In cases of skull fracture or serious head injury. 

8. When is it permissible to place the victim in a sitting position?

When bleeding occurs from the lungs or nose. 

9. When should stimulants not be given?

In cases of hemorrhage, fractured skull, sunstroke, epilepsy, or apoplexy. 

10. When should aromatic spirits of ammonia be given?

After attending to the position of the victim and applying heated objects to the body, and provided no other serious condition prohibits its use. 

11. How is aromatic spirits of ammonia administered to a conscious victim?

Thoroughly mix one teaspoonful in half a glass of water. 

Allow the victim to sip the liquid while supporting his head. 

12. How is aromatic spirits of ammonia administered to an unconscious victim? 

Moisten cloth with spirits of ammonia and use as inhalation stimulant by placing near victim's nose.

BURNS, SUNSTROKE AND HEAT EXHAUSTION BURNS 

1. What are three causes of burns?

Heat (dry or moist), electricity, and chemicals (strong acids and alkalies). 

2. Classify burns according to depth of injury produced. 

First Degree--skin reddened. 

Second Degree-skin blistered. 

Third Degree-deeper destruction involving charring or cooking of the skin. 

3. What dangers are present in every fire?

Carbon monoxide poisoning, suffocation and burns. 

4. How can fires be prevented?

Do not allow inflammable rubbish or liquids to accumulate. Be careful in disposing of partially smoked cigarettes. Observe safety rules issued by Fire Department. 

5. How should burning clothing be extinguished? 

To rescue victim, throw him down and stand at his head; then throw overcoat or blanket over his body to smother the flames.

If victim is alone, he should lie down and roll up tightly in a coat or blanket, leaving head exposed; if no covering is handy, roll over lightly and beat out flames with hands. 

6. How can suffocation be prevented in a smoke-filled room?

By crawling along the floor with a wet cloth or handkerchief over the mouth and nostrils to keep out irritating fumes. 

7. What are the symptoms of burns?

Pain, reddening, blistering or charring, depending on the severity of the burn. Severe shock is present in extensive burns. 

8. What dangers are present in burns? 

Shock and infection. 

9. What are the duties of the first aider in caring for burns?

Relieve pain, prevent infection, and treat for shock.

10. How should clothing be removed from a burned surface? 

Carefully rip or cut it off to prevent breaking blisters or injurying the skin. 

11. When should the clothing not be removed from a burned surface?

If the clothing sticks to the skin. 

12. What is the first aid care for burns?

Cover burned surface with sterile gauze or clean cloth compress immersed into slightly warm solution of baking soda (3 heaping tablespoonfuls to a quart of water). 

Bandage lightly and cover with warm blanket. Keep compress in a moist condition. 

13. How is tannic acid used in the treatment of burns?

Either as a 5% solution, jelly or tea extract, applied as a wet compress. 

14. What substances should not be applied to a burn?

Absorbent cotton and iodine. Oils and greasy ointments should not be used on extensive burns. 

15. What is the first aid care for burns from acids?

Flush the skin with water. (For carbolic acid burn, use alcohol). Treat for burn as in question 12. 

16. What is the first aid care for burns from alkalies?

Flush the skin with water containing dilute vinegar or lemon juice. Treat for burn as in question 12. 

17. How can sunburn be prevented?

By gradual exposure to the sun's rays or sun lamp, and progressive increase in the duration of exposure. 

18. What is the first aid care for sunburn? 

Apply calamine lotion, or tannic acid solution or jelly.

In severe cases, apply wet dressing of Epsom salts (3 tablespoonfuls to a quart of warm water). 

19. How can a beautician prevent scalp and pull burn bruises resulting from faulty permanent waving?

By using protectors, keeping electrical equipment in good condition, and properly controlling the application of heat to the scalp. 

20. What is the first aid care for scalp and pull burn bruises? 

Application of 1% gentian violet jelly to prevent infection and promote healing of the wound.

SUNSTROKE OR HEATSTROKE 

1. What is the cause of sunstroke? 

Prolonged exposure to the sun's rays. 

2. What is the cause of heatstroke? 

Exposure to excessive indoor heat. 

3. What are the signs of sunstroke or heatstroke?

Pain in the head, dizziness, hot and dry skin, rapid pulse, high body temperature, and in severe cases, unconsciousness. ་ 

4. How can sunstroke be prevented? 

Avoid excessive exposure, wear thin clothing, keep head covered, and eat easily digestible foods. 

5. In what position should the victim be placed?

In a lying position, with head elevated. 

6. How is the body cooled?

By a cool bath for about twenty minutes, or cold wet applications. 

7. May stimulants be given to the victim? No. 

8. Why is rubbing of the limbs and trunk important?

Rubbing towards the heart aids the circulation of the blood.

HEAT EXHAUSTION 

1. What is the cause of heat exhaustion?

Direct exposure to the sun's rays or excessive indoor heat. 

2. What are the symptoms of heat exhaustion?

Dizziness, vomiting, profuse perspiration, weak pulse, shallow breathing, and unconsciousness. 

3. In what position should the victim be placed?

In a lying position, with head level or low. 

4. What is the first aid care for heat exhaustion?

Remove victim to a place where fresh air is circulating freely. Treat for shock, apply heat and give stimulants. 

Have victim drink salt water (1 teaspoonful to a pint of water). Call a doctor if symptoms persist.

FROSTBITE AND EXPOSURE TO COLD FROSTBITE 

1. What is frostbite?

Injury produced by the freezing of a part of the body. 

2. How can frostbite be prevented?

Avoid prolonged exposure to cold when fatigued or not well. Wear sufficient clothing and massage extremities of the body. 

3. What are the symptoms of frostbite?

Coldness, pain, loss of sensation, and grayish white appearance of the skin. 

4. What is the first aid care for frostbite?

Thaw frozen part with cool air, water, extra clothing, or by placing it under armpit or between thighs. Do not apply ice or hot objects to frozen part.

EXPOSURE TO COLD 

1. What are the symptoms of prolonged exposure to cold?

Drowsiness, difficulty in body movement, and unconsciousness. 

2. What first aid care should be given to the victim?

Place victim in cool room. If breathing stops, apply artificial respiration. Rub limbs briskly with cool wet cloths. If victim responds, gradually warm the room and give warm drinks.

ARTIFICIAL RESPIRATION 

1. What is asphyxia? 

Stoppage of breathing. 

2. Name six accidents responsible for asphyxia.

Electric shock, carbon monoxide poisoning, drowning, suffocation, exposure to cold, and blows to nerve centers (head, neck, spine, solar plexus, etc.). 

3. What is the first aid care for asphyxia?

Artificial respiration and treatment for shock. 

4. What is artificial respiration?

Alternately compressing the lungs and releasing the pressure, thus allowing air to flow in and out. 

5. What is the most efficient and safest method of giving artificial respiration?

The Schafer Prone Pressure Method. 

6. When should artificial respiration be started? 

Immediately upon discovering that the victim has stopped breathing. 

7. Where should artificial respiration be carried on? 

At the nearest possible point to where the victim received the injury, and away from any source of danger. 

8. In what position is the victim placed?

On the stomach, with one hand extended upward and the other hand being bent to serve as a support for the side of the face. 

9. What is the position of the first aider before compressing the lungs?

The first aider kneels and assumes a straddling position over the victim's thigh. The arms are extended forward with the palms of the hands on the small of the back and the fingers resting on the ribs. 

10. What is the position of the first aider while applying the pressure?

As the first aider swings forward, the arms are kept straight and the shoulders come directly over the heels of the hands. 

11. How much pressure may be applied to an adult victim? 

Not more than 60 pounds.

12. In what rhythm does the first aider apply and release pressure to the lungs?

The following chant is repeated while going through each count: (1) Place Hands; (2) Swing Forward; (3) Sit Back; (4) And Rest. 

13. At what rate should artificial respiration be applied? 

17 to 25 times per minute; this rate corresponds to the natural rate of breathing. 

14. What supportive measures should accompany artificial respiration?

Keep victim covered and warm, both during and after applying artificial respiration. 

15. Why should the victim be kept lying down and not be allowed to stand up? 

To prevent strain or collapse of the heart. 

16. When may a stimulant (warm tea or coffee) be given?

After consciousness returns. 

17. When should the victim be moved?

If the victim is exposed to extreme weather conditions or possible further injury. Apply artificial respiration while the victim is being moved. 

18. When should artificial respiration be discontinued? 

When the victim resumes breathing, or is pronounced dead by the physician.

ELECTRIC SHOCK 

1. What are two causes of electric shock?

Electric current or lightning passing through the body. 

2. What safety rules should be observed to prevent electric shock? 

Keep all electrical equipment in good condition. Protect exposed parts with insulators. Avoid handling electrical cords or devices with wet hands. 

3. What are the symptoms of severe electric shock? 

Victim is blue; pulse is weak; stiffness; burns may be present; asphyxia and unconsciousness.

4. What steps should be taken when rescuing a victim from a live electric wire?

First turn off the current; then stand on a dry board and with a dry pole, remove victim from contact with the electric wire. 

5. What is the first aid care for electric shock?

Apply artificial respiration and treat for shock and burns. 

CARBON MONOXIDE POISONING 

1. Name three sources of carbon monoxide gas.

Manufactured gas used for heating or illumination, smoke from fires and stoves, and automobile exhaust. 

2. What are five common causes of carbon monoxide poisoning? 

Open gas jet, extinguished flame from open gas jet, leak from gas pipe or tubing, inadequate supply of air to burning fuel, and the running of the motor of an automobile in a closed garage. 

3. What are the symptoms of carbon monoxide poisoning? 

Dizziness, throbbing of the heart, skin blue or cherry red in color, inability to walk, asphyxia and unconsciousness. 

4. How should the first aider go about the rescue of a victim from an atmosphere of carbon monoxide? 

Turn off gas meter or otherwise discontinue the supply of gas; wear mask equipped with air hose. 

5. What is the first aid care for carbon monoxide poisoning?

Remove victim to room free from gas, and keep comfortably warm. Apply artificial respiration.

FIRST AID FOR POISONING 

1. What are three causes of poisoning?

Excessive strength or improper use of chemicals, either externally or internally. Eating decomposed or contaminated food. Contact with plants such as poison ivy. 

2. Name six chemicals which may be responsible for poisoning? 

Phenol or carbolic acid, lysol, bichloride of mercury, iodine, strong acids, and alkalies. 

3. How can chemical poisoning be prevented? 

Do not use unlabelled bottles; keep bottles containing poisons in a safe place, away from children's reach. 

4. What are symptoms of internal poisoning?

Sudden illness after eating, drinking, or taking medicine. 

Pain in stomach, nausea, vomiting and cramps. Mouth or tongue may be burned or stained. Odor of poison may be present on breath. 

5. What is the first aid care for internal poisoning?

Dilute and wash out contents of stomach until most of the poison has been removed. Encourage vomiting by giving an emetic (soapsuds, salt water, or lukewarm water) and by tickling throat with the fingers. Repeat vomiting until fluid is as clear as when swallowed. 

6. What should be administered to the victim after the stomach is well washed out?

Give large dose of Epsom salt, except if there is extensive corrosion of the digestive tract. 

7. What is a good antidote for a corrosive poison? 

If the poison taken was an acid, alkali, or bichloride of mercury, give soothing drinks of milk and eggs. 

8. What are the symptoms of exposure to poison ivy?

The skin becomes red and swollen and itches violently, followed by the formation of blisters. 

9. Why should the skin in a case of ivy poisoning not be scratched?

Infection is started, and the poison is spread from one part of the body to another.

Poison ivy, poison oak and poison sumac, cause a severe inflammamation of the skin, and the formation of small blisters 

10. How is a case of ivy poisoning treated?

Immediately wash the skin with soap and water, followed by the application of alcohol. Apply thick soap paste, allow to dry and remain overnight. The application of calamine lotion containing 2% phenol is soothing to the irritated skin.

WAR GASES 

1. List six groups of war gases and give an example of each. 

Tear gases or eye irritants—chloracetophenone gas. 

Sneeze gases or nose irritants—adamsite gas.

Choking or lung damaging gases-phosgene gas. 

Blister gases-mustard gas. 

Paralyzing gas-hydrocyanic acid gas. 

Screening smokes—burning of phosphorus. 

2. What protection is available against war gases?

Gas masks, gas-proof clothing, and gas-proof shelters. 

3. How is the presence of tear gases or eye irritants recognized? 

By their odor; either like fly-paper or apple blossoms. 

4. What effects do tear gases produce in the victim? 

Temporary burning pain in the eyes, copious tears, and spasm of eyelids. 

5. What first aid care should be rendered to a victim of tear gas?

Remove victim from contaminated air and expose to action of wind. Do not rub or bandage the eyes. In severe and lasting cases, wash eyes with either cold water or weak boric acid solution. 

6. How is the presence of sneeze gases identified?

Appearance of coal smoke, yellowish cloud or grayish smoke cloud without odor. 

7. What are the effects of sneeze gases on the body? 

Temporary but severe effects. Aching pain in head, face, throat and chest. Sneezing, coughing and vomiting. Occasionally partial paralysis of limb. 

8. What is the first aid care for a victim of a nose irritant gas? 

Flush victim's nose and throat with weak solution of baking soda (sodium carbonate). If vomiting occurs, give victim baking soda solution to drink. 

9. What are the effects of lung damaging gases on the body?

In low concentrations-headache, coughing and soreness in lungs. In higher concentrations-throat spasm, blueness of the face, increased pulse and breathing rate. 

Exertion may cause sudden collapse without warning.

10. What is the characteristic odor of lung damaging gases?

Pungent and disagreeable odor resembling new cut hay or moldy hay. 

11. What precautions should the first aider take in treating a victim of a war gas?

Prevent contamination. Wear gas mask to prevent inhaling gas. Use rubber gloves in handling the victim.

Wash gloves and hands frequently with the proper chemicals. 

12. What first aid care should be rendered to a victim of a lung damaging gas?

Treat as for shock-keep victim lying down, cover with blankets, and give tea or coffee. Do not give artificial respiration nor permit victim to stand up or walk. Transport on a stretcher. 

13. What are the odors of blister gases?

Lewisite gas-odor like geraniums, then biting. 

Mustard gas-odor like garlic or horse-radish. 

14. What are the effects of blister gases on the body?

Extremely strong, persistent and dangerous. Burning of eyes and skin. Pain in chest and stomach. 

15. What immediate care should be given a victim of blister gas? 

Wash off exposed area with soap and running water, followed by the application (do not rub) of cloths moistened in either benzine, kerosene, alcohol or ether. Apply a paste of bleaching powder (chlorinated lime) and water to the skin. Wash it off with alcohol every five minutes. Wash eyes with cold water. Transport on a stretcher. 

16. What precaution should be taken in the use of either benzine, kerosene, alcohol or ether?

The fumes of these solvents are explosive. Therefore, avoid sparks, cigarettes or flames, during the application of any of these liquids to the skin. 

17. Why should the victim's clothing be discarded?

Blister gas clings to the clothing; it should therefore be removed for decontamination and fresh clothing supplied to the victim.

18. What care should be taken by the first aider to prevent contamination?

Avoid inhaling the gas. The hands and gloves should be frequently washed with hot water and soap. 

19. How is the presence of paralyzing gases detected? 

The odor resembles bitter almonds or rotten eggs. 

20. What are the effects of paralyzing gases on the body?

Mild headache, unconsciousness, convulsions, and asphyxia. 

21. What first aid care should be given to a victim of paralyzing gas?

Remove victim to fresh air, apply artificial respiration if needed, treat for shock, and transport on a stretcher. 

22. How are screening smokes produced?

By the burning of phosphorus from an exploding bomb. 

23. How is the presence of burning phosphorus recognized?

Acid, acrid or suffocating odor resembling burning matches. 

24. What are the effects of burning phosphorus on the body? 

The smoke is harmless. Should the phosphorus particles strike the skin, however, severe burns are produced. 

25. Why should the burned surface be kept immersed under water?

Because phosphorus particles start to burn again upon exposure to the air. 

26. How are the phosphorus particles removed from the skin?

If hot water is available, immerse wound in order to melt the phosphorus. If only cold water is available, immerse wound and pick out particles with tweezer. 

27. What substance will coat the phosphorus and prevent it from burning the skin?

The application of a 2% to 5% solution of copper sulphate to the wound. 

28. What should be done after the phosphorus particles have been removed? 

Care for like an ordinary burn.

FOREIGN BODIES 

1. What foreign substances are irritating to the eyes? 

Particles of dirt, dust, hair or coal; chemical solutions spattered into the eye. 

2. What care should be given the eye irritated by a small foreign body? 

Don't rub. Close eye. Grasp the upper lid and pull down over the lower lid. Have the victim roll eye up. Brush out particle with the corner of moist cotton swab. 

HOW TO REMOVE FOREIGN BODIES FROM UPPER EYELID 

Grasp central lashes with two fingers of right hand, and pull down and away from eyeball. While the victim is looking down, place smooth end of match stick on eyelid about half an inch from its edge, and press downward. After locating the foreign body, remove it with the corner of a clean bandage or handkerchief moistened with clean water or boric acid solution. 

3. What care should be given the eye splashed with a chemical solution?

Wash the eye with large quantities of tepid water until the chemical is thoroughly diluted and removed. Place several drops of clean mineral oil or olive oil into the eye, cover with sterile compress, and secure medical aid. 

4. What are the signs of a foreign body lodged in the throat? 

Blue face, gasping for breath, and violent coughing. 

5. How can a foreign body be removed from the throat? 

Try to remove object with finger. If necessary, invert victim and slap sharply between shoulders. Apply artificial respiration if breathing stops. Call for medical aid if these attempts prove futile.

6. How can a splinter be removed from the nail? 

Scrape the nail thin. Cut out a small V shaped piece over the splinter and remove with sterile tweezer. After bleeding stops, apply iodine, compress and bandage. 

7. When should a first aider not attempt to remove a foreign body?

A doctor should be summoned if the object is buried deeply in the tissues. 

ANIMAL AND SNAKE BITES 

1. What disease may be transmitted by a dog or cat bite? 

Rabies or hydrophobia. 

2. How is rabies spread?

By the saliva of a rabid animal. 

3. How can dog bites be prevented? 

By keeping your dog muzzled; and not playing with strange dogs. 

4. What is the first aid care for an animal bite?

Remove saliva by immediately washing wound under running water. Dry wound with clean gauze, apply iodine and allow to dry. Cover wound with compress and bandage. 

5. Is rabies curable?

No. 

6. What preventive treatment is 100% effective?

Pasteur or vaccine treatment given by a physician. 

7. When should the Pasteur treatment be given?

In every doubtful case, particularly if the victim received a bite in the region of the face. 

8. When is the victim not in danger of getting rabies?

If the animal does not develop rabies within a period of ten days. 

9. How much time is required for rabies to develop after a person has been bitten? 

About forty days. 

10. Should the animal responsible for the bite be released?

No. Shut up the animal in a secure place, or tie him with a chain; then notify the Health Department.

11. Name two poisonous snakes found in the United States.

The coral snake and the pit-vipers (rattlesnake, copperheads and cotton-mouth moccasins). 

12. How is the coral snake identified?

Black, red and yellow bands, encircle the body. 

13. How are pit-vipers recognized? 

They have flat, triangular heads, thin necks, and stout bodies. By means of two hollow fangs, they inject venom into their victims. 

14. What kind of wound is produced by the pit-viper snake? 

Two small punctured wounds. 

15. What kind of wound is produced by the bites of nonpoisonous snakes?

Series of scratches, or a horseshoe shaped row of teeth marks . 

16. How can snake bites be prevented?

Keep the lower extremities protected with leggings or boots. Do not poke hands into hidden places. 

17. What are the symptoms of a poisonous snake bite?

Pain, swelling and shock (weakness, nausea and rapid pulse).

First Aid Care for Poisonous Snake Bite 

18. What is the immediate first aid care for a bite by a poisonous snake? 

Keep victim in a flat and quiet position. Apply tourniquet around limb above bite. Make cross-cut incisions (½ by ½ in. and 8 to 4 in. deep) with sterile knife, at each fang mark. Apply suction with mouth or cup for a period of one-half hour. Treat for shock, but do not give stimulant. Call for physician to administer antivenin injection.

19. Why is the tourniquet applied?

To prevent the snake venom from being carried by the veins to the heart, thereby causing death. 

20. Why are incisions made over the fang marks?

To encourage bleeding and thereby wash out the snake venom. 

21. What is the purpose of suction?

To draw out the venom from the wound. 

22. What is the first aid care for bites from non-poisonous snakes?

Treat as for ordinary wound to prevent infection. 

23. What is the first aid care for insect bites? 

Remove the "sting" if present. Relieve pain by applying baking soda paste or compress moistened with ammonia water. Relieve itching by the application of calamine lotion.

INJURY TO BONES 

1. What is a fracture? 

A break in a bone. 

2. What is a simple fracture?

A broken bone in which no wound extends through the skin. 

3. What is a compound fracture?

A broken bone in which the wound extends through the skin. 

4. What is the danger of a simple fracture?

Improper handling may result in a compound fracture.

5. What is the danger of a compound fracture?

Hemorrhage, infection, and shock. 

6. What are three causes of fractures?

Direct violence, falls, and automobile accidents. 

7. How can fractures be prevented?

Keep wires and objects out of the way to prevent tripping over them. Keep floors dry to prevent slipping. Use secure ladder for climbing. 

8. What are the symptoms of a simple fracture? 

Snap of the bone, pain, deformity, and loss or limitation of movement. Swelling and discoloration occur later.

9. What are the symptoms of a compound fracture?

Hemorrhage and the symptoms found in a simple fracture. 

10. What is the first aid care for a simple fracture? 

Send for doctor or ambulance. Prevent further damage by keeping victim in a lying position, warm and comfortable. Apply splints only if victim is to be moved or transported. 

11. What is the first aid care for a compound fracture? 

Check bleeding with tourniquet. Treat wound with iodine compress and loose bandage. Care as for a simple fracture. Summon medical aid. 

12. What is a splint?

Firm supports made of wood or metal to keep in place and protect an injured part. 

13. What can be used as a substitute for a splint?

A folded and firm newspaper or magazine. 

14. What parts of the body can be used in place of a splint?

The opposite leg can be used as a splint for the injured one by tying them together. The injured arm can be splinted against the chest with a snug bandage. 

15. What is the required length for a splint?

It should be long enough to extend beyond the joint above and below the fracture. 

16. What is a sling?

Bandage (narrow or broad) suspended in a loop from the neck to support the forearm and hand. 

17. When should a sling be used?

Apply narrow sling after splinting a fractured upper arm. Apply broad sling after splinting fractured forearm, for fractured collar bone or finger, and for fractured elbow found in a bent position. 

18. What are good substitutes for a sling?

Sleeve pinned to clothing or lower edge of coat or shirt turned up and pinned in place. 

19. When should the arm in a sling be slightly elevated? 

If there is an injury to the hand, lower forearm or collar bone.

20. What is traction?

Lift and gentle pull maintained at the end of a broken limb until the splint is tied in place (see figure, page 59). 

21. What are the benefits of applying traction?

It prevents further damage to surrounding tissues, relieves pain, and reduces shock. 

22. When should traction and splints be applied to a fractured limb?

If an assistant and necessary supplies are available, and if the injured person has to be moved or transported. 

23. What is immobilization?

Application of splints and bandages to render broken bone fragments immovable. 

24. What are the causes of a fractured skull?

A severe blow or hard object striking the skull, or the skull striking a hard object. 

25. What are the symptoms of a fractured skull?

Bump or cut on head, bleeding from ear or nose, unequal size in the pupils of the eye, and unconsciousness. 

26. What is the first aid care for a fractured skull?

Keep injured person warm and in a lying position. Apply cold cloths or ice bag to head. Apply light compress and bandage over scalp wound. Give no stimulants. 

27. What are the symptoms of a fractured lower jaw? 

Pain and difficulty in moving the jaw, drooping mouth and bleeding gums. 

28. What is the first aid care for a fractured lower jaw?

Support lower jaw with palm of hand and apply cravat bandage as for chin, cheek and ear bandage. (See page 28). 

29. When should the bandage on a fractured lower jaw be removed?

If the patient begins to vomit, remove bandage, support jaw, turn head to one side; when vomiting ceases, reapply bandage. 

30. What are the signs of a fractured collar bone?

Fractured ends can be felt along collar bone, injured shoulder is lower than the other, and the victim is unable to raise arm above shoulder.

31. What is the first aid care for a fractured collar bone?

See illustration and explanation below. 

32. What are the signs of a broken neck?

Pain in the neck and inability to move the fingers. 

33. What are the signs of a broken back?

Pain in the back and inability to move the feet or toes. 

34. What are the signs of a fractured thigh?

The limb is shortened, foot is turned outward and cannot be raised. 

35. What is the first aid care for a fractured thigh?

See illustration and explanation on page 59. 

HOW TO APPLY AN ARM SLING 

Narrow Arm Sling-Support wrist and hand with narrow cravat, and tie both ends together at side of neck. 

Broad Arm Sling-Bend arm at right angle. 

Place point of triangular bandage under elbow of injured arm, allowing one end to hang over the shoulder and the other to hang down. Puck up loose end, carry it over other shoulder, and tie both ends together at side of neck. Pin point to sling.

FIRST AID CARE FOR A FRACTURED COLLAR BONE 

Preparation: Triangular bandage and narrow cravat. 

Procedure: Bend arm at right angle. Apply broad arm sling, keeping hand elevated and fingers exposed. Immobilize upper arm by tying cravat bandage to side of arm.

FIRST AID CARE FOR A FRACTURED ELBOW 

If arm is traight, apply one padded splint to palm side of arm, and fasten with three cravat bandages, above and below and at the wrist and hand. 

If arm is bent at elbow, follow the procedure as for a fractured collar bone.

FIRST AID CARE FOR A FRACTURED UPPER ARM 

Preparation: One padded splint, two bandages, and narrow cravat. 

Procedure: Bend arm at right angle. Fasten splint to outside of arm with two bandages, above and below the fracture. Apply narrow arm sling.

FIRST AID CARE FOR A FRACTURED FOREARM AND WRIST 

Preparation: Two padded splints, two cravat bandages, and broad arm sling. 

Procedure: Bend arm at right angle. Place one splint at the back and the other at the front of arm and wrist. Apply one bandage below elbow, and the other at wrist and hand. Support with broad arm sling.

APPLICATION OF MANUAL TRACTION AT END OF LIMB 

Traction is a steady and gentle pull exerted at the end of the broken limb and maintained until the splint has been fastened in place with bandages. The application of traction prevents further damage to surrounding tissues, relieves pain, and reduces shock in the victim.

Preparation: Two assistants. Two padded splints and four cravat bandages.

Procedure: One assistant straightens limb and applies traction while the other assistant inserts bandages and fastens two splints to limb. If no splints are available, apply smooth padding between thighs, and tie feet, legs and thighs together.

INJURIES TO JOINTS AND MUSCLES 

1. What is a dislocation?

Injury to a joint and surrounding tissues resulting in the bone getting out of place. 

2. What are the causes of a dislocation? 

Blow, fall, or violent muscular exertion. 

3. What are the symptoms of a dislocation?

Pain, deformity, limited movement, swelling and shock. 

4. What is the first aid care for a dislocation?

Call for doctor; place injured part in comfortable position, cover joint with cold wet cloths, and treat for shock. 

5. What is a sprain?

A temporary dislocation in which the joint immediately springs back in place. 

6. What causes a sprain? 

Muscular exertion, lifting, and falling. 

7. What are the symptoms of a sprain?

Pain, swelling, difficulty in moving joint, and discoloration of the skin. 

8. What is the first aid care for a sprain?

Apply cold applications (wet compress or ice bag) and keep injured part elevated. For the wrist, place limb in a sling. For the ankle, have the victim lying with support under the leg. 

9. What is a strain?

Injury to a muscle or tendon as result of severe exertion. 

10. What are the symptoms of a strain? 

Pain and stiffness. 

11. What is the first aid care for a strain?

Rest injured part and keep victim in a comfortable position. Apply heat to relieve pain and gently massage the limb in an upward direction to help the return flow of blood through the veins. 

12. What is a bruise?

A minor injury caused by a blow which breaks the capillaries under the skin. 

13. What are the symptoms of a bruise?

Pain, swelling and discoloration. A wound may or may not be present.

14. What is the first aid care for a bruise?

Immediately apply ice or cold compresses to reduce the swelling, relieve pain, and prevent discoloration. Care for open wounds if present.

UNCONSCIOUSNESS 

1. What is unconsciousness?

A condition in which the victim has lost all sensory contact with the external world. 

2. What special examination should be given an unconscious person?

Look for signs of bleeding, asphyxia or poisoning. Examine head for injuries, notice the size of the pupils of the eyes, inspect the lips for burns, and smell the breath for characteristic odors. 

3. Name three types of unconsciousness in which the cause of the condition is not determined. 

Red unconsciousness, white unconsciousness, and blue unconsciousness. 

4. Name the chief symptoms and first aid care for red unconsciousness. 

Chief symptoms-red face and strong pulse. 

First Aid Care-Keep victim warm, quiet, and in a lying position, with head slightly raised. Apply cold applications to the head. Give no stimulants. 

5. What are the chief symptoms and first aid care for white unconsciousness? 

Chief symptoms-Pale face and weak pulse.

First Aid Care-Keep victim warm, and in a lying position. Apply external heat. Give inhalation stimulant (smelling salts) if there is no bleeding or head injury. 

6. What are the chief symptoms and first aid care for blue unconsciousness? 

Chief symptoms-Blue condition of lips and finger tips and stoppage of breathing. 

First Aid Care-Apply artificial respiration and treat for shock.

7. Name seven common causes of unconsciousness.

Apoplexy, skull fracture, shock, hemorrhage, fainting, poisonous drugs, and exposure to extreme heat or cold. 

8. Name three conditions in which red unconsciousness is present. 

Apoplexy, sunstroke, and head injuries. 

9. Name three conditions in which white unconsciousness is present. 

Shock, heat exhaustion, and heart failure. 

10. Name three conditions in which blue unconsciousness is present.

Drowning, electric shock, and carbon monoxide poisoning. 

11. What is the cause of apoplexy?

Rupture of small artery in the brain, formation of clot which presses against the brain, causing unconsciousness and paralysis. 

12. What are the symptoms of apoplexy?

Red face, strong pulse, pupil of one eye larger than the other, and breathing somewhat similar to snoring. 

13. What is the first aid care for apoplexy? 

Same as for red unconsciousness; see question 4. Call for doctor. / 

14. What may be the cause of fainting?

Absence of food or air, severe emotional strain, sight of blood or injury, or fatigue. The immediate cause is an insufficient supply of blood to the brain. 

15. How can fainting be prevented?

Keep victim lying down or bend the head forward between the knees until the person feels better. 

16. What are the symptoms of fainting?

Dizziness, pale face, weak pulse, shallow breathing, and unconsciousness. 

17. What is the first aid care for a case of fainting?

Loosen clothing, keep victim lying down either with head lowered or lower limbs elevated, sprinkle face with cold water. If still unconscious, place ammonia inhalant or smelling salts to nose. If conscious, give warm stimulants (tea or coffee) by mouth.

18. What is the cause of epileptic fits? 

The cause is not known. 

19. What are the symptoms of an epileptic fit?

The symptoms appear suddenly: pale or blue face, convulsions of head and limbs, and loss of consciousness. 

20. What is the first aid care for a victim of an epileptic fit?

Prevent further injury by having the victim lie down, place a pillow under the head, and insert a firm object (wood wrapped in cloth) between the teeth. Give no stimulants, and leave victim alone until convulsions cease and consciousness returns. 

21. What are the symptoms of heart failure?

The victim may show fainting characteristics and complain of pain in the heart. The victim may be conscious and manifest heart pains and shortness of breath. 

22. What is the first aid care for heart failure?

Keep victim warm, quiet, and in a lying position. Sit up if there is difficulty in breathing. Give stimulant if conscious. Call for doctor.

METHODS OF TRANSPORTING A VICTIM 

1. When is transportation the first consideration of the first aider?

When it is necessary to rescue the victim from fire, drowning, etc., or to prevent further injury by falling objects, extreme weather conditions, etc. 

2. When is transportation the last consideration of the first aider?

After first aid measures have been applied to stop bleeding, reestablish breathing, or remove poison. 

3. What precautions should be observed in transporting a victim?

Handle gently and do not move unnecessarily. 

4. Should the patient's body be kept covered during transportation?

Keep body warm to prevent it from being chilled; keep body uncovered if face is red and body is hot. 

5. In what position should a victim of a head injury be transported?

Level position on the back, or on the side if the back of the head is seriously injured. 

6. When should the patient's head be lowered while in a level position?

If the injured person shows signs of white unconsciousness, shock, or heat exhaustion. 

7. When should the victim's head be raised while in a level position?

If the injured person shows signs of red unconsciousness, sunstroke, or head injury. 

8. When may a victim with a fractured limb be moved? 

After splints have been applied and limb has been immobilized. 

9. What may be used to transport an injured person? 

Chair, wide board, door, stretcher, or blanket. 

10. In what position should a victim of a broken neck be transported?

Do not pick up victim but keep him flat on his back. 

Protect head and neck while rolling or sliding the body onto a door or board, and during transportation.

11. In what position should the victim of a broken back be transported?

Several assistants, acting as a unit, roll the body towards them so that the face lies downward on the board or door.
