FOREWORD 

Bacteria play a tremendous role in our life on this earth. 

Not only is this true, but it is also true that bacteria have been playing this role for centuries and will still play it for centuries to come. 

Bacteria cause disease! Yes, true. But this is not the most noteworthy of their activities. In agriculture, with which we are most concerned, their importance far exceeds the wildest thoughts. 

In the soil bacteria are continuously at work. They break up the complex organic molecules into forms available to plants for food. Nitrogen, carbon, sulphur, phosphorus, are all rendered available to the plant by bacteria. Many processes such as retting of flax, curing of tobacco, the fermentation of silage are bacterial. 

Our life is the better for the understanding of these minute organisms. Disease, too, is better understood. Water, milk and foods themselves, are less a menace with our increased knowledge of these bacteria.

But we must understand them. We must learn to see them, to grow them, to study them. We must watch them in their daily task. 

Such is bacteriology: fundamentally important to all our lives, but especially important to agriculture.

Sterilization is more prompt where large vessels initially contain a few drops of wator.

When storilizing EMPTY plugged test tubes, flasks, etc., reverse them so that the stoppers are downwards, in order to insure more rapid and complete evacuation of contained air. Plugged test tubes, plugged flu.sks, etc., when not inverted, should be loosely

covered to prevent entrance by dripping of undesired water of condensation. 

Sterilization is accomplished more quickly and dependably when a large number of small filled flasks are used in preference to a small number of large flasks. Large flasks should be only partly filled to insure most rapid and thorough penetration of heut. 

After closing, or partly closing the valve, the steam pressure will rise rapidly and must be controlled so that it does not reach the blow-off point of safety valve. The temperature will rise slowly until the optimum is reached. Its rate of ascent will be influenced by the initial temperature and mass of the load and by the thoroughness of the air evacuation. Any marked increase in mass and decrease in the initial temperature of the load from that of a known or control load will add considerably to the time necessary for completion of the sterilization process, as will retention of air should the evacuation jet be occluded. 

After the optimum temperature has been maintained for the period of time essential for the thorough penetration of steam and complete destruction of the pathogenic organisms, or for completo sterility (as found by previous experiment), cut off the source of neat and allow steam pressure to recede. The use of an alarm clock or "Interval Timer" is helpful for control of the optimum period.

Should the steam pressure not be carefully controlled as suggested and, as a result, the safety valve is allowed to lift, water will be primed into the sterilizing chamber through the steam shaft duo to sudden relief of steam pressure and, as a consequence, the violent ebullition of the water in the generator. 

Liquid culture media will also boil ovor and scaled ampoules containing volatile liquids may explode. This must be avoided. 

Do not employ either the safety valve or the exhaust valve as a means for adjusting the optimum pressure. This method is conducive to priming and much steam is wasted. THE OCULARS 

There is but little difference in the oculars which you will use in this course and those you have used in previous courses. It is well to point out here, however, that "a compound microscope" is nothing more than two simple microscopes, one mounted above the other. The objective first magnifies the object and the ocular then magnifies the image which the objective has made. One might say, using terms current in radio parlance, that we have two stages of amplification (or magnification). The total magnifications which the objects and oculars give are correct only if we have a tube length of 160 mm. In order to have a certain amount of adjustment for this tube length, microscopes usually have a sliding tube within the body which is marked in millimeters. To obtain the best results this should be drawn out to 160 mm. whenever the microscope is in use. This tube length is sometimes varied when certain calibrations are made with the microscope. A final admonition would be to always keep BOTH EYES open when using the microscope. It lessens fatigue.

THE HYDROGEN IONS 

In bacteriology, as in all of the biological sciences, we are interested in acidity and basicity. Bacteria, for instance, are dependent upon an optimum condition of acidity. Neutral solutions of nutrients are most favorable although fermentations may change the reaction to either side of the neutral point. Frequently bacteria reach certain acid values and then cease to grow. Although titrametric methods were found useful in the early days, the bacteriologist has, with the chemist, turned to more modern ways of determining the acid and basic properties of fluids.

Acids, bases, salts, and many complex molecules, such as dyes, and organic substances, dissociate into ions when they dissolve in water. 

A certain fraction of the amount dissolved in ionized. Thus water itself can be expressed as dissociating into H ions and OH ions: thus H₂° → (H) (H) + (OH) The undissociated mass of water is placed as a denominator in the equation-

The H ion concentration, or pH, would be difficult to determine but for the invention of a colormetric method. As the name suggests, the

pH is "indicated" by the colors of certain indicators. A series of these is employed. Each indicator changing from the acid color it displays to the basic coler at a definite place in the pH scale. It is an exceedingly simple procedure when once it is mastered. 

One cannot think of hydrogen ions without thinking of a closely allied subject: buffers. There are, in biological solutions, numerous substances which really are salts of weak acids or bases. On the addition of H ion to such solutions, they combine with the salt, thus forming acids which are much less dissociated. The addition of the H ions make very little change in the concentration of the ions in solution. These weak acid salts are buffers. Acitates, carbonates, acid phosphates are all buffers. So is peptone. Clark and Lubs* illustrate this very aptly when they say that "if we add to 1 liter of perfectly pure water of pH 7.0, 1 c.c. of 0.01 normal HCL, the resulting solution will be about pH 5.0 and very toxic to many bacteria. If, on the other hand, we were to add the same amount of acid to a liter of standard beef infusion medium of pH 7.0, the resulting change in pH would be hardly appreciable. The power of certain solutions to resist change in reaction was ..... likened., to a "tampon". The word was adopted by Sorenson, and in a German rendition of his paper it became "puffer" and thence the English "buffer". There has been some objection to this word so applied, but it has now acquired a clear technical meaning and is so generally used that it should probably be retained. By buffer action, then, we mean the ability of a solution to resist change in pH through the addition or loss of acid or alkali."

To prepare pH standards by the drop ratio method.

The colormetric determination of the hydrogen ion concentration has one disadvantage; it is difficult to carry in ones mind the color that the various indicators display at the different points on the pH scale. The drop ratio method is used extensively and involves the use of a weak acid and weak alkaline solution. To tubes containing an acid solution decreasing amounts of indicator are added while increasing amounts of indicator are added to alkaline tubes. Certain pairs of these tubes when observed one behind the other against a light source give the intermediate colors of the indicator which correspond to predetermined pH values. The table given on another sheet gives the pH values for each combination of tubes. Only tubes having 10 drops (9-1, 8-2, etc.) of indicator between them are to be used.

BACTERIAL MEDIA 

Until it became possible to grow bacteria more or less at will, outside the bodies of animals and plants, very little was accomplished in bacteriology. The search for a suitable medium for grewth of bacteria has been an important and interesting one. Bacteria need food even as you and I.

Their protoplasm is similar to ours and the elements the bacteria need are those which we also need. 

One basic fact is easily understood; bacterial food should be in solution because it is only by osmosis that bacteria get their food within their bodies. Solutions of materials containing C, H, O, N, Fe, Cl, P, S, Na, K, Ca, etc., are essential.

Bacterial media, although they vary in their minuter details, have certain basic classes of substances which will be discussed. It

PEPTONE is the name given to a soluble digest of protein material. contains proteoses, peptones, amino acids, smaller quantities of salts of Nu, K. Of cellular erigin, it contains fragments of cytoplasmic, and nuclear substances. Rich in nitrogen, it is found in a majority of media.

BEEF EXTRACT, as the name implies, is made from beef extracted in water. 

It contains soluble nitrogen compounds of a non-protein sort. 

It is used in place of beef infused with water which formerly was a common procedure.

CARBOHYDRATES are frequently added media. They offer a more available source of carbon and, since bacteria ferment their substances differentially offer further an opportunity to classify bacteria. Although the word carbohydrates is employed, other substances of like nature are used; starches, alcohols, etc.

SALTS are sometimes used to buffer media, to maintain balances of one sort or another. Common salt is frequently used in physiological concentrations for obvious reasons.

DYES AND STAINS AND INDICATORS are added either for their bacteriostatic effect or for their ability to display evidence of changes of acid-base equilibria or in oxidation-reduction equilibria. 

AGAR is added to fluid nutrient media to harden or stiffen them. Then added in concentrations of 1%-1.5%, it swells or "dissolves".

It is stiff at incubator temperatures and is "melted" by boiling. 

Solutions of agroan be cosled to 40°C before they solidify. It offers no food source, but is merely used for its stiffening properties.

GELATINE is also added to media for the same reason as is agar. It is fluid ut incubator temperatures, but is still useful because bacteria liquify it. 

HYDROGEN IONS are important. They are not "added" in the sense that the afore mentioned are added, but their control is essential.

HOMEDIA ARE USED IN THE ISOLATION METHODS 

The use of solidifying agents for culture media, such as gelatin and agar made possible the easy isolation of bacteria from mixtures. Several isolation procedures have been developed in bacteriology all of which are somewhat different in their characteristics. These methods may be called (a) quantitative plate method (b) loop dilution method (c) streak plate (d) serial dilution. (a) Quantitative plate method. 

If one has a tube containing a fluid with many bacteria, 1 c.c., this fluid is transferred to a bottle or flash containing 99 c.c. of sterile water. If a c.c. of this is then transferred to a plate and agar added, we have the original bacterial mass diluted 100 times. This can be extended so that dilutions by tens can be made up to 10,000,000 or more. The details are difficult of verbal instruction so a demonstration will be given. · 

Follow the operation of the instructor. Much lost time will be avoided by paying close attention to every detail. A sheet is issued showing the steps and the method of marking the plates. (b) Loop dilution.

Where one is not concerned with quantitative information and wishes merely to isolate organisms, the loop dilution is employed. With this method, agar tubes are melted and cooled to about 45°C. After flaming, the loop is touched to the surface of the culture of the organism to be isolated. The loop is now successively introduced into each of 3 or 4 agar tubes. It is agitated in each tube slightly, this agitation removing successively fewer bacteria as it is inserted in successive tubos. The agar is now poured into sterile petri dishes. These are dated and labeled and placed in the incubator. A modification of this method would be to place 

1 or 2 c.c. of sterile water in 3 to 4 petri dishes. The procedure then is to transfer bacteria to the water of the first dish, flame the loop and then transfer some of the now contaminated water to the water in a second dish and so on, flaming between each transfer. (c) Streak plate.

This method of isolation is not conducive to very pure cultures, but it is used a great deal mevertheless. First a sterile petri dish is taken and sterile agar added directly to it. This is allowed to harden. 

The material to be studied is now lightly touched with the needle or loop. 

Several streaks are now made over the surface of the hardened agar and the plate incubated. The result will show that the first streak has heavy growth, the next less and by the time the third or fourth streak is made, isolated colonies are frequently found. Study the sheet showing a picture of this. 

(d) Serial dilution. 

If it is suspected that an organism is present in large numbers but with some contaminating types, the serial dilution method is used. By running the dilutions well up, the contaminant is diluted out and left behind. The organism originally present in greater numbers is now found pure.

STAINS AND THE STAINING PROCEDURE 

Until one has seen a bacterial suspension under the microscope with its lack of detail and poor definition, one cannot quite realize what a tremendous boon to bacteriology the use of dyes has been. The increasing knowledge on dyes available to the bacteriologist has helped in ways impossible to describe. *It is safe to say, nevertheless, that the use of stains revolutionized microscopic technic.

Without the use of dyes very little advancement could have been made. The use of dyes both for textiles and for bacteria are theoretically the same. Just as certain dyes are best for wool and stain cotton only faintly, so we have dyes which are selective for certain parts of the cell, the bacterial cell as well as tissue cells. The chemistry and physics of dyeing is still not well understood and it would seem best not to fog the real issue with a vapor of words which in the last analysis might be wide of the truth.

The first dyes were of vegetable or animal origin, such as indigo and carmin. Later "aniline dyes" were used, the basic ingredient being benzene. Benzene itself is colorless. If, however, three of the hydrogen atoms are replaced by three NO2 radicals one obtains tri nitro benzene. 

Even this, although it is highly colored, is not a dye. It has no affinity because it cannot be dissociated into ions. If one more hydrogen is now replaced, this time by a -OH radical, picric acid is formed, we now have a dye or a "stain" as the bacteriologist calls the dye. Picric acid is soluble, it is slightly dissociated and hence can form salts with alkalies. 

The tri nitro benzene is called a chromogen and the NOz radicals are called chromophore groups. The -OH radical is given the term auxochrome and some such linkage is necessary in all dye compounds. 

ACID AND BASIC DYES. 

**Generally the basic dyes are salts of a colorless acid, such as hydro-chloric, sulfuric, oxalic or acetic acid. Likewise the acid dyes are their sodium, potassium, calcium or ammonium salts. Occasionally the basic dyes are the free bases. When a basic dye which is ordinarily in the form of a salt comes into commerce as the free base, it is customary to use the word "base" immediately after the name of the dye. Thus "basic fuchsin" indicates a salt of fuchsin with a colorless acid, while "fuchsin, base" indicates fuchsin itself, not combined with an acid.

CERTIFIED STAINS. 

Since stains are so important to the biologist, there was considerable difficulty during the war to maintain a supply, Germany having a virtual monopoly. Dyes were soon manufactured here, but were of inferior quality at first. So serious did the situation become that a Commission on Standardization of Biological Stains was appointed, whose business it was to standardize and to certify the stains on the American market. The re is no reason now for getting an inferior stain. A bottle with a label bearing the guarantee of the Commission and with the signature of H. J. 

Conn is sufficient evidence of quality. The bacteriologist of today would do well to always buy certified stains. They cost but little more. 

Biological Stains, second edition - page 9.

SOLUBILITIES OF STAINS. 

*The amount of a dye that will go into solution in either water or alcohol depends upon the amount of mineral salts prosent. If a dye contains a large percentage of sodium chlorido, for instance, a saturated solution will bo of considerably lower ctual dyc content than if the dye were free from salt; the sodium chloride prevents the solvent from taking up as much of the dye as it would normally. 

It is plain that the only way two staining solutions can be mado identical if different batches of stain are used is to make them up on the basis of the weight of actual dye present in the stain employed. This can be done only if the manufacturer has cooperated to the extent of printing the actual dye content of cach batch of stain on the container in which it is sold. At present the Commission is issuing its certification only if the dyc content is printed on the label. In this way it is hoped that eventually all stains on the market will be so marked; and then when staining formulae are readjusted so as to call for definite quantitics of actual dyo, the preparation of staining solutions will be put on a morc scientific basis.

GENTIAN VIOLET, methyl violet and crystal violet are practically the same except that gentian violet is an ill defined mixture of dyes, with methyl violet a little better. Although many formulae call for gentian violet, it is safe to say that crystal violet may be substituted since it is a definite chemical compound. These two stains are specifically described since they are both important and confusion often arises when they are used.

STAINS IN MEDIA. 

Many bacterial stains when used in culture media have a profound effect either by preventing the bacteria from growing (BACTERIOSTASIS) or actually killing them. Gentian violet is often used in high dilutions for its bacteriostatic effect. There is a direct relation between the Gram reaction (Q.V.) and the effect of this dye in the medium. 

A still further use of stains in culture media is as an indicator of the amount of oxygen present. Litmus, methylene blue and a few other dyes, turn colorless when the normal oxygen tension is lowered. The bacteriologist finds much use for this reaction.

METHOD OF STAINING 

The actual process of staining is simple. A cover slip or microscope slide is used. It must of necessity be clean. A drop of water is placed on the slide. To this is transferred, with sterile needle, some of the material to be examined. There should not be too much nor too little. 

The slide is now dried. The film is "fixed" by passing the slide or slip through the flame with the film side down. 

The stain is now poured or dropped on the slide and then washed off. The slide is now dried, or blotted, and examined. Although simple enough in theory, too frequently the student gets films too thick or too thin. Practice is necessary, as in all things, to attain any degree of success, but the above directions are sufficient. Certain stains like the 

Gram stain of course require more work, more care. Directions for stains like these should be followed carefully.

The importance of staining in bacteriology lies not only in the greater ease of observation which the SIMPLE STAINS give, but also in their use as a diagnostic aid. Two types of stains come under this head: 

1. Differential stains such as the GRAM STAIN. This stain works on the principle that some bacteria when stained with crystal violet will retain the stain even when washed with alcohol. Others lose the original stain but are COUNTER STAINED after the first stain is removed. It is performed as follows:

---

Stain 1 minute with the gentian violet solution; wash in water; immerse in iodine solution for 1 minute; wash in water and blot dry; decolorize in 95% alcohol for 30 seconds with gentle agitation; cover with counter-stain for 10 seconds. Then wash, dry, and examine with oil 

The organisms retaining the original stain are designated as 

Gram (positive), those not as Grom - (negativo). 

2. ACID FAST STAIN. 

Some bacteria stain with difficulty. By vigorous means, usually by heating, these bactoria can be made to take the stain. When once stained, however, these organisms are difficult to destain. They can be washed with acid alcohol and still retain the stain. They are acid fast.

The organism causing tuberculosis is acid fast. The Ziehl Neelsen Carbol 

Fuchsin method is used. *The Ziehl Nelson method is: carbol fuchsin with gentle steaming for 3 to 5 minutes or cold for 15 minutes; wash in water; decolorize in 95% ethyl alcohol containing 3% by volumo concentrated hydrochloric acid until only a suggestion of pink remains; wash in water; counterstain with saturated aqueous methylene blue or Loeffler's methylene blue; wash and dry. 

Tho acid fast organisms are rod, the others are blue.

BACTERIAL CHARACTERISTICS 

In the study of bacteria we are at once confronted with difficulties. It should be perfectly obvious to the student just what these difficulties are. In the first place bacteria are very minute. In the second place they are very monotonous in their morphology (there are only 3 type forms and with little variation in these). The bacteriologist is therefore forced to study, for a more intimate knowledge of bacteria, their phynology as well as their morphology. It would be much like the botonist, forced to study with every plant the changes in the environment of that plant every time he studied the plant. It is a big job, but one that is necessary since by the environmental changes bacteria are classified. next few pages of this manual are written to acquaint the student, (1) with the various important characters that should interest the bacteriologist, and (2) with the means available for studying these characters. 

They will be classified under the heads - morphological, physiological, and cultural characters. It will be necessary to make constant reference to this subscribed material. Study it now for ease in later reference. 

Morphological Characters 

Under this heading will be found discussion and directions for study of the obvious visible characters. 

The Size of Bacteria. 

Bacteria are roughly 1/1000 of a millimeter in length, or in diameter, as the case may be. They vary in size of course just as a race of men or of plants. They vary in size at various ages, in various media, under various conditions of heat, cold. But the size of bacteria is an important item in their classification. It is obvious that a new unit of measure must be used since it would be inconvenient to speak of an organism being 1.25625 of an inch, etc.

The new unit is the micron (called "mu" - the Greek) or a length of 1-1000 of a millimeter. The eyepiece micrometer which has already been calibrated is used. The calibrations were made in millimeters. 

These can be changed to microns by multiplying by 1000 (.0025 m.m. becomes 

2.5M). In measuring bacteria at least 25 cells should be measured and the average taken.

The Spore. 

One of the most distinguishing of the physical characteristics is the spore. It was first observed by Cohn and studied by Koch. It is supposed to be formed by a gradual accummulation of the chromatin material. accumulated mass then assumes a hard impervious mass resistant to chemical and physical agents out of all proportion to the resistance of the same cell in its vegetative state. The spore is formed during stress and is not a regular part of reproduction. It is difficult to stain and is usually observed as a non-staining portion of an otherwise stained cell. 

The process of spore formation is termed SPORULATION, while the reverse is termed GERMINATION. Germination takes place when the spore is placed in an environment conducive of growth. The spore and the ability of an organism to form it, is a very important fact to be determined in our study of bacteria. Usually but one spore is formed in a cell. 

It is also formed inside of the cell, and is therefore called an endospore. It may or may not cause swelling and may be at the middle or at` ends.

Oxygen Relations 

It would indeed be a strange animal or plant that could grow without air. Yet Pasteur observed growth without air, - in the bacteria! 

So revolutionary was the thought that severe criticism was leveled at this interesting Frenchman. It is an interesting phenomenon and one that is constantly in operation. Dung piles, destruction of leaves and wood under water, septic tanks, all give evidence of this growth without air. This ability of bacteria to grow without air oxygen is not a characteristic common to all. Hence, it becomes of considerable diagnostic value. 

A few terms are in order here. The words ANAEROBE and AEROHE 

are used to describe bacteria that grow without air and with air respectively. Two other qualifying words are applied to these two they are STRICT and FACULTATIVE. The first word is applied to both of the above terms and when so applied serves to indicate that that organism is strictly one or the other and cannot tolerate an intermediate condition. The second term, facultative, when used in conjunction with either anaerobic or aerobic, indicates that an organism prefers that condition, but can tolerate conditions other than what is described as strict. 

We will not study anerobes as such to any extent, but a knowledge of the oxygen requirements of bacteria is necessary for classification purposes. *In their relation to free oxygen, organisms are generally classified as strict aerobes, facultative anaerobos, or strict anaerobes. 

It must be remembered, however, that there is no absolute distinction between those groups; and recent work is completely modifying our understanding of the subject. Methods for studying the matter will undoubtedly have to be radically modified in the future; and those now in use must be considered merely provisional. 

Provisional Method 1. Determine by noting the presence or absence of growth in open and closed arm, respectively, of a fermentation tube containing glucose broth. Care must be taken to use tubes of media from which the dissolved oxygen has been recently driven off by heating. In case of gas production, this test is of comparatively little value, because bubbles of gas may carry the sediment up with them; hence if an organism produces gas from glucose, the test should be made in the presence of some other sugar which it attacks (i.e. produces acid) without gas formation -- provided a sugar on which the organism has such an action is known. The presence of some sugar available to the organism should always be provided, however, inasmuch as anaerobic bacteria ordinarily require carbohydrate as a source of energy.

Golatine, added to a nutrient solution, gives a stiff, solid medium upon which colonies grow. Koch first made gelatine. But it was fluid at incubator temperatures, and also bacteria seemed to "liquify" it. Agar is now used for the stiffening agent. Fermentation of Carbohydrates. 

As mentioned heretofore, carbohydrates (including starches and alcohols) are added to media. Bacteria ferment some of these substances but not all. Further, a single bacterium will ferment certain ones leaving others untouched. By its selective fermentation of sugars and starches it can be told apart from others.

The fermentation reactions are detected by the pH of the medium after the organism has grown. Sometimes Andrades indicator is used.

The important consideration in regard to the medium is that a peptone be employed containing tryptophane, which is not always present in bacteriologic peptones. Peptons are ordinarily digests of lean meat; but for the indole test a casein digest is apparently more satisfactory. Chromogenesis or the Development of Pigments. 

Most bacteria produce a color when grown on culture media. 

Frequently the color is gray or a light brown which very nearly coincides with the color of the medium. Then again the colors are brilliant: reds, yellows, greens, blues and oranges prevailing. The color an organism displays is characteristic for that organism; hence its use in classification. 

The pigment is sometimes in the cell, an endochrome; sometimes outside the cell, an exochrome. The pigments are variously soluble in water, alcohol, chloroform or are insoluble. The solubility, however, is of less interest as a classification factor. Very little is known of the chemistry of pigments due to the difficulty of isolation. 

Pigment formation seems to be correlated with (a) the presence of oxygen (b) the food present, (c) the temperature of incubation. Hence there would be little, if any, pigmentation in a stab culture although it would develop on the surface; a high temperature of incubation would suppress the formation of pigment and starches and carbohydrates are frequently conducive to pigments. The tendency for pigment formation must be noted. 

Milk 

Since milk is an ideal natural food for bacteria as well as for humans, it is much used in growing bacteria. The reactions which the bacteria initiate are used for diagnostic purposes. An indicator is usually added to the milk. This serves the two fold purposes of (a) telling the acidity and (b) the state of reductionof the milk. Some indicators such as litmus and methylene blue are illustrative of this. There has recently been a tendency toward the use of bram cresol purple, which gives a reasonable idea of the pH but does not show reduction. Milk is considered as neutral, alkaline, and as weak or strong acid. Where there is a curd formed by the acid, it is considered as "acid coagulating". Peptonizing describes a more or less complete digestion of the casein.

A study of the lethal action of heat upon bacteria. 

The action of any lethal agent upon any bacteria is in general more or less regular. Whether it be heat, chemical, or drying, the action is similar. The killing of bacteria does not take place at once but, in an ideal case, follows the equation of a monomolecular reaction. When it is considered that death is in reality a chemical reaction (the donaturization of the protein) the above statement is not hard to understand. Theoretically at least, the lethal action would be a regular change. In other words, the same per cent of bacteria would be killed in each unit of time. 

Plotted, this gives a curve with a sharp decline at first, but sloping off regularly. If the log of the numbers are plotted, the points fall on a straight line. This is ideal, but due to varying age of the organisms, the individual resistance, and other factors, the lethal curve does not follow the ideal curve. In this exercise it is intended to plot such a curve for ideal conditions and superimpose upon this a curve for actual recorded data.

To determine the phenol coefficient of various substances. 

Although it is well enough to know of the fact that a given substance will have a germicidal action, it is even bettei to know just how offective that substance is in relation to a known standard germicide. Such a determination is made, using phenol as the standard. This is called the phenol coefficient and requires a bit of care and thought in its operation. Briefly, dilutions of phenol are made and similar or greater dilutions of the unknown as well. 

A test organism is added to equal amounts of each of the se dilutions and the germicide so diluted is then allowed to act for a period of time during which loop amounts are inoculated into broth. The results are determined by the presence or absence of growth in these sub-cultures. The phenol coefficient is then calculated. It is a figure representing the degree of dilution of the weakest dilution of the unknown that kills the test organism in 2 minutes divided by the figure representing the weakest dilution of phenol killing in the same time. This is also calculated for the 15 minutes interval. The coefficient is the mean of these two figures. The test is run under strict rules governing the age of culture, the kind of culture, amount of inoculum temperature, kind of broth and many other things. 

It should be pointed out that although the actual run is but of 15 minutes duration, the preparation for the run is apt to take considerable time. Two students will be assigned a given germicide. It is suggested that one run the test and the other label the tubes.

The Gas Ratio 

It was Theobold Smith who pointed out the difference in the ratio between the amounts of CO2 and H2 formed by these two organisms.

Another test of somewhat doubtful value is the Voges Proskauer Test (V.P.). Indole Test.

It is recommended that the coli-gerogenes group be considered as including all gram negative non-spore-forming bacilli which forment lactose with gas formation and grow aerobically on standard solid media.

THE BACTERIOLOGY OF MILK 

Milk is an important human food. It is used in the form of milk as well as in the form of its products, i.c., butter, cheese, cream, ice 

Since it is a splendid human food, it is also a splendid bacterial food. A complete telling of all of the story is impossible here. One must know how to determine the numbers of bacteria in milk, but more interesting than any quantitative study is the qualitative where the organisms are studied as they appear in milk and create the profound changes that they do. Milk spreads disease, sad to relate, through bacteria. How to keep them out, how to kill them if they do get in, is an interesting story.

One cannot grasp understandingly the interplay of the bacteria in milk unless one understands first the bacteria normally found in milk. 

Quite generally the bacteria in milk are classified according to their reactions in the milk. There are five reactions which are distinctly characteristic: acid coagulating, acid, inert, alkali and peptonizing.

The student will hear frequent mention of the so five types of reaction. 

They will be discussed in the lecture room and seen in the laboratory. It must be understood that there are at times no sharp lines separating these These are borderline types, usually involving a combination of the last with some one of the first four. Furthermore, the distinction between the first two is one of degree largely and the same is true of the second two.

Of the five types the last three will be discussed first. They will be studied as groups, no one organism being considered. This is not truo of the acid-forming group. Hero, since they are so important, specific organisms will be studied. The acid coagulating group embraces the lactic organisms, the lacto bacilli and other organisms having specific characteristics such as ropiness, etc. The colon group, too, might be studied under this heading, and of course this group is of great interest to the dairy bacteriologist.

In the studies of these organisms, certain reactions should be noted in all cases:

Appearance of colony on plain agar. 

Appearance of colony on milk powder agar. 

Appearance of plain broth tube.

Appearance of lactose tube and pH. 

Appearance of litmus milk. Cdor. Flavor. 

Microscopic features.

Litmus milk is used for certain reasons. It has been suggested that brom cresol purple milk be used since sharper readings of pH can be made. 

The advocates of this medium maintain that it is not decolorized, but this very failure of decolorization is one of the most important reactions at the disposal of the dairy bacteriologist. With litmus milk, however, one can determine not only the acid or alkaline reaction produced, but can tell as well the ability of the organism to reduce the milk. The rapidity of this reaction, whether it takes place before or after acid formation, is a very important bit of information.

Only one period of incubation, and one temperature is regarded as standard, 48 hours at 37°C. Piles of plates should be separated at least one inch and ventilation should be provided in incubators. If among the different dilutions there are plates containing from 30 to 300 colonies these should be counted and the number, multiplied by the dilution, bo reported as the final count. All colonies on such plates should be counted, and the numbers from the different plates averaged. 

If there are no plates within these limits, the one that comes the nearest to 300 is to be counted.

Counting shall be done with a lens, and all recognizable colonies included. A lens magnifying 2 diameters (or what the opticians call a

6. Common Sources of Error in Counts. 

Agar plate "counts" per c.c. are to be rogarded as estimates of numbers rather than as exact counts, since only a portion of a cubic centimeter is used in preparing the plates. As such they are (like all estimates) subject to certain well known and recognized errors whose size can be largely controlled by the care taken in the analysis. Among these errors are: (a) Failure of some of the bacteria to grow because the incubation temperature, or the composition, or reaction of the medium, is not suitable. (b) Inaccuracies in measurement of the quantities used. (c) Mistakes in counting, recording data, computing results and the like. (d) Incomplete sterilization or contamination of the plates, dilution waters, etc. The possible errors caused by those things makos it highly important for all routine laboratories to follow carefully a standard procedure.

The latter form of expression shall be considered an abbreviated method of saying "an estimated count of 2,000,000 colonies per c.c. as obtained by standard methods." Moreover, analysts shall be careful to avoid giving a fictitious idea of the accuracy of the standard plate count.

A study of manurial contamination of milk as indicated by the sporogenes test.

The scroones test is based on the characteristic milk reaction produced by certain anaerobic spore-forming bacteria which are widely distributed in nature and which are particularly common in fecal material. 

In characteristic reaction the casein is coagulated and the curd torn by gas within 24 hours at 37°C. The whey is usually quite clear and the odor of butyric acid is noticeable. When the milk in a test tube is covered with a paraffin plug the latter is usually forced up almost to the top of the tube and sometimes entirely out of the tube.

The obvious advantage of using the spores of B. enteritidis sporogenes as an index of contamination lies in the fact that pasteurization does not destroy them, and therefore does not interfere with the determination of their presence in milk. Furthermore, they are not believed to vegetate in milk under normal conditions, and vegetative cells do not sporulate in the presence of the fermentable sugar in milk; so the sporogenes test with both fresh and old milk, provided it is still sweet, should give the same results. 

The test has consequently become known as a "nonmultiplying test". Should it prove to be reliable in guaging the cleanliness of production and be relatively accurate, it would be extremely valuable in milk-control work.

DIRECTIONS: Analyze a sample of ice cream following the directions as given below.

Method of Sampling 

Liquids. Cream, milk, skim milk or ice cream mix should be sampled by means of a sterile sampling tube. 

If the liquid to be sampled is in a vat, the contents should be agitated thoroughly, then sampled by means of the sterile sampling tube in at least six places (proferably more) in the vat. 

Liquid samples taken from the spout of the homogenizer or freezer should be obtained by quickly passing the bottle under the opening at periodic intervals during the discharge so as to get representative samples of the material delivered from the machine. 

Solids. Sugar, gelatin, and skim milk powder should be thoroughly mixed with a sterile spatula and a portion placed in a sterile sample bottle.

Frozen ice cream should be sampled with a sterile butter trier, after removing the surface inch from the point to be sampled. The core thus removod should be cut into short pieces with a sterile spoon or spatula and placed in a sterile bottle.

Measuring the Sample 

The sample of ice cream may be either weighod or measured. 

With ice croom it is frequently difficult to expel all the air,with the result that one 10 cc. volume may contain more or less ice cream than another. Variations in the viscosity of the mix and in cleanliness of the pipotte may cause more or less of the mix to adhere to the glass. The gravimetric method has the disadvantage of being more tedious and more time-consuming, but, in general, gives more accurate and less variable results. The preference for the gravimetric or volumetric method will depend on the nature of the analytical work. 

For ordinary plant control work, where routine check is being made on the quality of the ingredients and the final product, the volumetric

If the sample to be analyzed is frozen ice cream, it should be melted and the fir expelled by heating in a water bath at 45°C. (113°F) for fifteen minutes. If a higher temperature is used for melting the ice cream, it is likely to injure the organisms, and lower temperature is likely to necessitate a sufficiently long exposure to permit noticeable growth of the bacteria.
