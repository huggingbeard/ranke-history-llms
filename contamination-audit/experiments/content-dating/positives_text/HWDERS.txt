FOREWORD

TUNA! TUNA!

TUNA! TUNA! TUNA! Magic words! Thrilling words! Words bringing back days at Avalon when the little village seethed with excitement! Days when men rushed this way and that, recklessly bidding for a launch—any kind of a boat! Eager hands waving five, ten, twenty dollar bills for a single flying fish!

Hollow eyed, weary men, with bandaged hands, glumly nursing their wounds on the porch of the old Metropole Hotel. The pebbly beach lined with eager tourists scanning the sea-excitedly awaiting the return of every boat in the hope of glimpsing another of these royal fish.

Clusters of men arguing-waving arms-shaking heads -recounting tales of defeat-of long hours of battle-of bruised hands and aching limbs of great, bronze, surging bodies of broken rods and snapped lines.

Great days were those when King Tuna reigned supreme-long before Broadbill or Marlin were ever thought of. And just between ourselves-King Tuna still so reigns. 

Don't let anybody try to persuade you otherwise. Broad- 3

bill are tough and strong and wary. Marlin are splendid spectacular fighters. But, my friends, there is no fish that swims that even approaches this same old King Tuna! Listen!

In the summer of 1918 tuna were scarce. They usually are. Scarce, that is, to all but one angler, the late W. C. 

Boschen. Day after day he and Farnsworth came in with one, two, even three splendid fish-ranging in the hundred thirties and forties. Nobody knew where they were finding them -nor did they tell, being a close-mouthed pair. As far as that is concerned, so are most anglers. Half the fun of fishing is to "get 'em when they ain't"—to paraphrase a wellknown baseball slogan.

Many a hopeful angler aroused himself early in the morning to slip out after that smooth-working team-hoping to surprise them in their hunting, or rather fishing grounds. 

But it was no use. The "Mabel F" was too fast-Farnsworth too foxy-to be trapped. The mists of the horizon invariably engulfed the "Mabel" only to surrender her again in the afternoon with one or more shining fish piled on her stern. 

Most everybody was certain that these tuna were out in the San Clemente Channel. But where? That was the burning question! That channel embraces considerable ocean. A thirty foot boat cannot be seen very far. 

Stealing off a week end from a variety of activitiesperhaps you may remember that most of us were pretty well occupied during that summer of 1918-I slipped over to Avalon for a couple of days' fishing. I secured Enos Vera as boatman.

The first day was empty. We worked San Diego Bank -on and off Silver Canyon-Salto Verde. Not a sign of tuna did we see.

5 The next day, Sunday, we joined the procession of "monkey see, monkey do" and prepared to chase after

Boschen and Farnsworth. Grasping what advantage we could, we started early, going around the East End in the hope that they would be going in that direction. Sure enough, just after we had passed Seal Rocks, the "Mabel" came purring along, flicked the white of her wake contemptuously at us as she went by, and disappeared into the haze. That was that. 

Still, there might be a chance. We knew what her course was, at least at the time she passed us, and while the little "Pirate" was hopelessly outclassed as far as any chase was concerned, nevertheless, if we kept plugging along, we might stumble upon them somewhere out there. It would be the blindest sort of luck-but in fishing one never can tell.

So, edging a little off shore we kept on along the southerly side of the island until we were abreast of Salto Verde. There, we headed out into the channel in the general direction of the West End of San Clemente. For an hour or so we chugged alongjust looking-the wind being wrong for kite flying.

About ten miles out I saw Enos stiffen and peer ahead. 

I threw the glasses on the horizon there. I stiffened too! 

What we both saw were birds-lots of them! Not doing much -just cruising around and settling again. But they were not going anywhere, evidently loafing, hanging around waiting for something. 

Enos kicked the throttle open. "Humph!" he grunted. "Them birds she look good! You see birds like them-tunny she around somewhere! Pretty good sign!"

By the middle of July in 1924 tuna became a scarce article around Catalina Island. There had been good fishing earlier in the seasonbig fish and lots of them. But, by the time I speak of, the migrations seemed to have passed on, leaving a few mavericks, devastated schools of bait, and pleasant memories.

Now your dyed in the wool tuna fisherman does not take kindly to memories! They are all right enough during the winter-but during the season-in summer, well, that is different. Then he wants tuna-and lots of them!

So it was, that as fish became scarce, tempers became short around the Tuna Club. Life-long friends quarreled over nothing-snapping and snarling-passing one another up with just a grunt. Sometimes with not so much as that! 

It is a tough game-this chasing over rough water day after day. It is bad enough when fish are plentiful! When they aren't—it is just plain hell! It takes a good man to do it-and stay with it. You might be surprised to know how few there are who can.

Jump and I had been fishing together for a week or so. 

That is, he was fishing-I was acting as boatman. His son was sick or had hurt himself-or something. Anyhow, I had rashly volunteered to run his boat for him until Rob was able to carry on again.

From time immemorial sailor men have come to port with tales of unknown monsters seen upon the face of the deep. Perhaps some of those sailors were liars-certainly all were not. XXNXX WHAT IS THERE about fishing? What makes it what it is? 

Is it catching fish? Not entirely. Because they are good to eat? No! Because there is always the chance of landing a big one? To a certain extent-yes; but only to a certain extent. Because it is hard work-gruelling, painful labor out under a broiling sun? No! Because of the elemental thrill of personal conflict? Yes-undoubtedly that has a lot to do with it--but there is still something else. 

Well, then, what is it? It is the little things-the thousand and one unimportant incidents-elusive and vague when taken each by itself-but when welded together which go to make up the whole. I am firmly convinced that deep down in the heart of most of us it is these little things-the background of the sport-that draw-that hold us. Furthermore, I am likewise of the firm conviction that it is not the lust to kill.

ALL FISHERMEN are queer but sea anglers are the queerest of their breed! Whenever and wherever they foregather, their talk invariably turns to fish-to the thing uppermost in their minds to the thing they dream about-plan for-and upon which they spend more money than they can afford.

Speaking of big fish-nine out of ten anglers will confess or lay claim-to this monster or that which he has hooked and lost. It has become an axiom that "the biggest one got away"! We laugh about it—using it as showing up the lying proclivities of all anglers. But is it exaggeration? 

I decline to admit that any angler is unduly untruthful.

Nor is such belittling the child of over-modesty.

I give you my word that right here within a radius of a hundred miles from the end of the San Pedro Breakwater, there have been, perhaps are at this moment, and most certainly will be, great fish-the equal of any in the Seven Seas!

I know for I have seen them-have hooked them-have attempted to fight them-and have been soundly whipped by them. So have my friends. So have others who are not my friends.

Of these fish I intend to speak in the following pages.

or, perhaps, guessing, the weight and size of fish. That is a most dangerous pastime. Attempting to judge the weight of a fish when he is in the air, or in the water for that matteror is any place but on the scales—is an exceedingly tricky undertaking.

In the first place it is human nature to exaggerate. Innocently, perhaps, we all do it-and always will. Nextone naturally wants it to be larger than it is—the wish being father to the thought. Again-to properly judge weight, one must have ample time for observation- an opportunity which rarely presents-and, likewise, to be enabled to secure a bird's eye view-one wherein girth, length, bulk, can be seen together, weighed one against the other, and calmly judged. A fast moving fish—and most of them are moving plenty fast when we see them-rarely affords this chance-not to mention the inevitable distortion of objects seen in the water. 

Take swordfish, for example, varying as they do in relative dimensions-in structure-in thickness versus length or visa versa. If I never realized these things before, I learned them when I took my first broadbill swordfish.

WE HEAR a lot about that lone wolf of the Seven Seas, the broadbill swordfish. Some of what we hear is true-some only partly true and most of it pure moonshine. Why anyone should feel called upon to draw upon his powers of imagination as he relates tales of encounter with these great fish I have not the faintest idea. Certainly, to my mind, they offer plenty of opportunity for dramatic presentation of stories concerning them without adventuring into the realm of inventive genius.

To begin with I must confess an abysmal ignorance as to any real knowledge of them. True enough, I have hunted chased and sworn at them for some fifteen or sixteen years.

It took me eight years to land my first one! I have fought them for varying lengths of time-thirty seconds to nine hours - have operated boats for others who fought them -have, myself, gaffed them from three minutes after they were hooked up to four or five hours. Naturally I have striven to learn something about them, applying thereto whatever 147

modicum of intelligence an all wise Providence has seen fit to bestow upon me. The sum total of that effort has been practically nil. Today, after all those years, I only suspect,

I do not know, why they act as they do-where they come from or where they go!

Of course, over such a period, one is bound to form certain theories-to come to certain conclusions based upon observation. For instance I am not afraid of them-nor do 

I think anyone else need be if reasonable caution is exercised. Potentially dangerous-they have the strength, the weapon, wherewith to inflict damage, injuries and death.

But I do believe that that danger is more or less nullified by the equipment we use in fishing for them. In fact—after hours on one of the brutes-hours during which I suffered acute physical pain without making the slightest impression on the fish-I have almost prayed that it might attack the boat-try to come aboard-anything-just so long as we might have a chance to throw a gaff into him or that I might be relieved of the torture! But my prayers remained unanswered.

Entirely aside from any element of danger involved in hunting them, they do possess such other characteristics as will give one all the adventure one seeks. In the first place, if any fish is an individualist-it is the broadbill swordfish! 

I never saw any two that acted alike that fought alikethat pursued the same tactics. The broadbill is the only fish I ever observed that seemed really to think-to try to outsmart you. Others fight furiously, stubbornly, but more or less blindly, frenziedly striving for freedom. A broadbill, on the other hand, impresses one as being coldly calculating. 

Truly they are in a class by themselves-something apart from the common ruck. Not that they are defiantly game as are tuna and marlin. Their resistance is more the resistance of craft-of well planned strategy than of intestinal forti-

tude. When they are whipped-and realize it-they will come in as meek as Moses. But they take some whipping!

Add up their calculating intelligence, their tremendous strength, their unbelievable vitality, their scarcity, their reluctance to take a bait-or perhaps, it may be pure canniness, and the aspiring angler finds himself nibbling a cake of sufficient sweetness without adding the spice of personal danger.

As a matter of fact I believe it is just this which holds men to the pursuit of broadbill. This and the lure of the chase the long, wearisome days in the glare of sun and sea-under the lash of wind-eyes forever glued to the face of the waters-the knowledge that just over the horizon's edge may be those two weaving fins. Yes-those are the things which hold us to it-not the mere glory of a successful kill. Just between ourselves, the actual fighting of a broadbill swordfish-providing that you do fight him, not just hold on-is pure, unadulterated hell! Ask Grey-Adams- 

Pillsbury-Martin-Howard-Thomas. They will tell you.

In July, 1917, I was fishing with Capt. Walker in his "Leona". We were after broadbill. Boschen's and Adams' feats had fired me with an overwhelming ambition to take one of these great fish. Two disastrous previous encounters had strengthened this. Tuna and marlin were all very well -but broadbill was pie a la mode! I have acquired more sense since! So it was that, scorning tuna, we spent our days running about the ocean seeking fins.

BETWEEN the great current drifts lies dead water. In it collect an amazing variety of things. There are patches of floating seaweed, the great bull kelp, boxes, planks, driftwood, wreckage, bottles, paper cups, orange peel, crates, barrels, queer wicker baskets bearing scrawled Chinese characters, sometimes bunches of rotten bananas thrown over from some vessel inbound from the tropics. There are tiny, diving ducks, always in pairs and resembling the fins of a swordfish when seen from a distance. They duck and bob, busy about their little affairs-fluttering awkwardly away at the approach of a boat. Sea birds of every description rest upon the surface or perch upon floating pieces of wood, balancing precariously upon their bobbing roost. It swarms with sea life-tiny deep-water minnows, strange stuff like horse hairs, Portuguese men-of-war, jellyfish, spawn, algae. Over and across it, through and around it range predatory swordfish, sharks, and sometimes tuna. In it there may be found certain alleged humor-some of it will be sad-all of it is meant to be human. 

Perhaps all fishermen are queer. Certainly it is a queer conglomeration of humans that goes to make up the angling fraternity of Avalon. Queer anglers-queer boatmen--good fellows all and sportsmen-but filled with odd quirks, strange philosophies-seeming to take a different attitude toward life the moment they set foot in a boat in the quest of big fish. Men who throughout their lives have been big and broad-gauged, who have fought and pounded their way to the top through the breadth of vision, suddenly become old maidish, petty, nagging. Others, dubbed by friends and families as snarling, fault-finding tyrants, metamorphose into genial, laughing good fellows. The spell of wind and sea and sun seems to work strange magic. Under it man becomes something different from what he ordinarily is. Perhaps it is the true self coming out-perhaps not. Quien sabe?

Take the Tuna Club itself. Probably there is no more famous fishing organization in the world. It is the parent of every sea angling club that is. Its rules and regulations are copied wherever there is sport fishing. One would think that a group of men, sailing the wide blue sea in search of such thrilling adventure as is offered by this King of Sports, would develop the finest spirit of good-fellowship that could be conceived. Do they, though? They do not! Hot quarrels arise over the most trivial of things. There is the story of W. C. Boschen, perhaps the greatest angler the Tuna Club-the world-has ever known. 

Year after year Boschen made the long trek from New York to Avalon. His particular pets were broadbill. Day after day, week after week, month after month, he pursued their wearying chase across tumbling seas, under a burning sun. But he loved it. He loved the sea and all her moods—he loved the wind-he loved the sky. He loved the island-its rugged peaks, its ever changing colors, its broken coast, its sleepy coves. His blood leaped at the very thought of those sinister, weaving fins. 

With him day after day was his boatman George Farnsworth. Through good weather and bad-through good luck and bad-the two were together out upon the face of the waters. Now-broadbill are not weaklings they will take their toll from any man. Particularly will they take it when, for years, such a practice is followed. That constant stabbing, boring, of the rod-butt is bound to do things to you which are not good. Ultimately the reckoning was demanded of Boschen. There was a winter when his health was bad-when he suffered constant pain. At last even he surrendered-submitting to an examination by certain world-famed specialists. The verdict was a sentence of death. Boschen was doomed!

He took it standing up-just as he took everything else that came his way. "Can I fish one more summer?" he asked. "Perhaps but you will probably die the sooner!"

So-Boschen came back to his beloved Catalina, to his swordfish-and for the last time. What were his thoughts that summer, no one ever knew. Each day as he saw the island in some certain phase or some particular cloud effect- -or saw or worked one of his pets-he knew it was for the last time. Each day brought the end closer-each day passed, closed that page of life's ledger. But he confided his secret to no one-except George.

IN THE beginning I am going to place myself on record by declaring that I do not like light tackle. Not that anybody cares a hang whether I do or not-but merely to give added emphasis to the experiences which are to come through the following pages. When I say I do not like it, I mean for fish such as tuna, running over seventy-five pounds, marlin or broadbill. To my mind it is entirely out of place to use it consistently on these fish.

There are various reasons for my prejudices. First-I was brought up among the school of heavy tackle anglers. 

From them I received my training, hints as to tricks of the trade, suggestions. From among them I formed my models -those whom I aspired to emulate. As light tackle came into use I probably absorbed some of their prejudice-treating it as a freak, a stunt, a circus performance-far removed from the basic fundamentals of good angling. Secondly-I have taken my most unmerciful lacings when using light tackle beatings which left me discouraged, humiliated, ashamed and angry. There is not the shadow of a doubt but 211

www. that those same whippings were entirely my own fault. Nor is there the shadow of a doubt but that I will always fall short of even approaching mastery of the art-even though I should live to be a hundred. Things which we cannot master —which we do not understand—we fear and dislike. That is precisely my case in the matter of light tackle. 

Let me make myself very plain. I have not the slightest quarrel with anyone who does like it. I am quite settled in my conviction that each one of us is the best judge of what he wants to do-and should be permitted to do it quite unhampered by criticism from others. Neither do I desire to precipitate any argument over the matter. There has been quite enough of that over the last fifteen years or so. Personally, I am thoroughly tired of it. Personally, I have been impaled upon both horns of the controversy. Among my friends, living and dead, are some of the greatest heavy tackle men in the game. Also among my friends are some of the greatest experts in light tackle. 

Light tackle is sporting-very much so. You can't afford to make any mistakes when using it. You must possess perfectly co-ordinated mind and muscles. You must know beyond guess work the precise strain which you can put on your rod, your line, under every situation which presents. 

To be really great-you must have a sort of sixth sense which telegraphs you ahead of time what the fish is going to do. These qualities you must have-if you are going to whip your fish-not run him to death!

To one who is skilled, experienced, surfeited upon heavy tackle, and when there are lots of fish around, I say by all means go out and test your skill, your brain, your wits. To the beginner I earnestly urge that he first gain experience and knowledge of the ways and tricks of fish, before resorting to light.

TACKLE SPECIFICATIONS AND RULES 

"The following tackle specifications and rules are the result of over thirty years experience. Strict adherence to both the spirit and letter of these rules is vitally necessary in order that the Tuna Club may maintain the high standards set by its founders and retain the enviable reputation it now enjoys. In fairness to all, involuntary infractions of these rules can be no more condoned than can willful violations. 

Members should be sure that they understand the rules and that their tackle complies with the specifications. The underlying spirit of angling should be that it is a sport in which the skill of the angler is pitted against the instinct and strength of the fish and that the latter is entitled to an even chance for his life.

TACKLE SPECIFICATIONS 1. HEAVY TACKLE-Rod to be of wood, with usual mountings, and shall consist of butt and tip and shall not be shorter than 6 feet 9 inches over all. 2. LIGHT TACKLE-Rod to be of wood, with usual mountings, and shall consist of butt and tip and shall be not shorter than 6 feet over all. Butt shall not exceed 14 inches in length. Tip shall be not less than 5 feet in length and shall weigh not more than 6 ounces. Line shall not exceed standard 

9-thread linen line and shall have a maximum breaking strain, when dry, of not to exceed 26 pounds.

By way of a little more detailed explanation, a sea angling rod consists of two pieces-butt and tip. The former, in the case of heavy tackle, is about two feet long-in light tackle the butt cannot exceed fourteen inches. The tip, in every case, is of one piece, without joints. Guides are placed in order that the line, when under strain, will not drag along the side of the tip. You see, for the sake of greater leverage, all reels and lines are on top of the rod, not underneath. Hand grips are permitted and do not count against the weight of the rod. They may be of any material one desires-although ordinary rubber hose or soft twine are the general favorites. In fighting a big fish such a grip is almost necessary. One's hands become cramped to a degree that makes it a very painful-frequently impossible-

process to close them around the slender rod. At times like this a grip of some kind is like unto the mercy of the Lord!

Twenty-four, nine and six thread line can mean nothing at all to the unitiated. For that reason I shall try to illustrate. 

Twenty-four, or heavy tackle, is somewhat larger than the lead in an ordinary lead-pencil. Nine is about the size of such leads, while six is smaller. Lines are made of the finest linen flax of the size known to the trade as No. 50. In each classification there are the number of threads that the line is called. All lines, whatever their size, are of three strands tightly twisted. Great care being put into their manufacture, naturally they are of considerable strength-a strength which increases somewhat when they become wet. 

To my mind, line is the most important part of one's fishing gear. It is subject to terrific strain during the course of a battle with a big fish. Just to show that this is true-if you will let out a thousand feet of twenty-four thread line behind your boat and with nothing on it, not even a hook, then run the boat ahead at about two miles an hour, you will have a fearful job to reel it in! Many times in the preceding pages you have read of kites. It sounds incredible-ridiculous-for grown men to fish with kites, and yet that is about the only way you can take a blue fin tuna in our waters. Tuna are very wary. 

They will sound instantly if any disturbance caused by the boat reaches them. Therefore it becomes necessary to use some means to enable the placing of the bait into the school before the fish are aware of the boat's proximity. Hence the kite. 

An ordinary square silk kite of 28 inches, 32 inches, or 36 inches, depending upon the force of the wind, the stronger it is the smaller the kite, is run out with about three or four hundred feet of line. Generally, nine thread is used.

At the end, this is stranded, one single strand being tied to the fishing line at varying distances above the leader, again

depending on the force of the wind. A hundred to two hundred feet of fishing line is then run out and the boat's course adjusted in order that the bait may skip along parallel to it. 

The action of the flying fish bait is very natural—much like that of a crippled fish trying to clear the water. It skips, jumps, skitters and splashes, creating quite a fuss on the surface. When the fish strikes, the ensuing jerk breaks off the kite line where it is tied to the fish line and the kite flutters down to the water. Generally you have time to run over and pick it up before settling down to the fight. There are dozens of methods of tying the kite line-but I usually just tie it in any kind of knot. If it is properly stranded and the loose strands peeled back for about a foot and cut offit will break at the right time. Kites are mostly used in tuna fishing. They can be used for marlin but it is difficult to hook those fish on a kite strike. A tuna hits furiously, while a marlin is more gentle and inclined to let loose the moment he feels any drag. 

We use flying fish for bait for the main reasons that they troll better, stand abuse better, and are easier to handle.

For tuna I weave the hook through the fish's belly, down near the tail so that the barb itself is about by the tail lobes. 

To the hook is a short wire, about nine inches. This goes up through the gills and out the mouth, there being an eye in its end. Then fish line sewed though the head and mouth and into the eye holds the bait firm under the constant beating it gets skipping along the surface. 

For marlin, I sew up the mouth, leaving a short loop which I weave over the hook a couple of times. This leaves the action of the bait in the water free and natural. It is also well to sew the eyes close to the head-otherwise they pop out and start the bait spinning with the resultant twisting of the line.

With broadbill, I like to use the same method that I do

444444 for marlin with the exception of sewing up the eyes. We use barracuda or mackerel for broadbill bait for the very simple reason that they show up better, make a greater flash in the water.

As I have already said—tuna hit the bait with a furious smash. Marlin, on the contrary, more often than not, take the bait rather gently, swimming away with it crosswise in their mouths for fifty feet or so. Then they seem to pause a moment to swallow it. That is ordinarily the time to strike them. However, let no one set these things down as hard and fast rules. Changes of method must be adopted as occasion seems to demand. Fish change their tactics just as do human beings. The main thing is to successfully set the hook! It doesn't matter much how you do it.

We always feed broadbill a lot of line before striking. 

We do this partly in order to hook them deep-partly be cause they are too wary. It is my firm conviction that when we see broadbill on the surface, and that is about the only chance we have to fish for them, they have already fed and are simply loafing around under the warm sun. If my contention is correct it would seem that that is the reason they are so reluctant to take a bait. My own experience is that one out of seventeen fish worked will bite. Average seeing two fish a day and some little idea of the vicissitudes of broadbill hunting may be had!

I have fought a broadbill as hard as I knew how for twenty minutes with the fish apparently well hooked and fighting back. Suddenly the line went slack and I reeled in my barracuda intact, except for being pinched in its middle! 

That swordfish just swam around with it in his mouth precisely as a puppy would pull on a rope or rag, and when he got tired of the game-let it go! 

Fishing chairs in well equipped boats spin on spindles precisely as do office chairs. This permits the angler to face

in any direction as he follows the gyrations of his fish. On the chair seat, or under it, is a socket fitting the butt of the rod, thereby giving a firmer grip on it. Heavy tackle fishing is done sitting down. Light tackle and three-six should always be handled standing up.

Probably any other questions will be found answered in the foregoing Tuna Club rules. And now, I've tried to cover the high spots of technicalities. To those who are not familiar with angling technique I hope this chapter will prove readable and of some service.
