POST-WAR PLANNING IN PROBATION AND PAROLE 

EXPECTED INCREASE IN CRIME 

HREE MONTHS AGO it was reported by the Century Foundation that one hundred and ten public and private agencies are engaged in some form of post-war planning. The rapidity with which these agencies are created makes it impossible to predict how many of them will be functioning at the termination of the war. It is not known whether any of these post-war planning organizations are interested in the problem of crime, its prevention and the treatment of convicted criminals. 

However, crime and delinquency have been major social problems following the cessation of each war in which the United States has participated. As a matter of fact, following the Civil War it was reported that burglars and bank robbers infested New York City and that a number of resident gangs "could muster a thousand or more members."

In the past, the methods used in attempting to solve post-war problems of crime have usually been negative. It has been found that crime usually receives widespread publicity after a war and as a result pressure is placed upon legislators to increase the penalties for crimes. Usually attacks are made upon the modern and more humane methods of dealing with criminals and delinquents-the suspended sentence, probation and parole. 

After the last war, for example, the charge was made that the misuse of probation was responsible for the increase in crime and for the breakdown of the administration of criminal justice, with the result that the Law 

Enforcement Committee of the American Bar Association recommended at its annual meeting "the withdrawal from trial judges of the power to admit to probation.' 

The statements being made by speakers at annual meetings or conferences of workers with delinquents and criminals, regarding the post-war problems of crime are far from optimistic. It is apparently accepted that after the war there will be a major increase in crime and more prisons will have to be built to care for the increased prison population. It is asserted that it should now be determined whether "maximum security prisons" should be erected, at an estimated cost of $5,000 for each cell built, or whether "medium security prisons", with cells costing $1,500 would meet the need. 

Is the building of new prisons the only possible solution to the crime problem or can this problem be met by intelligent planning and the development of preventive measures?

For two hundred years, with the exception of brief intervals, the treatment of criminals has been based neither upon sound philosophy nor objective thinking. Post war planning in the correction field is obviously needed but no intelligent plans can possibly be made unless a study is conducted both of the practices followed, and the philosophy underlying the existing State system of correctional care. This study of theories and [3] 582428

practices should be made by individuals who are objective and trained in the field, and who are interested in the entire problem. It should be accepted by probation and parole officials that the results of any post-war planning for probation and parole will have no positive results unless both systems are considered as units of the State system of correctional care. 

There still exists in America sublime faith in the enactment of laws to solve social problems. The enactment of social legislation does help, but the history of the field proves that adequate social legislation is merely a beginning. Some workers in the field of crime have been caught with the phrase, "streamlining the Government" and are urging the consolidation of probation and parole, or the consolidation of probation departments. Even if these proposed measures are followed through, they will not solve the existing situation. The major issue which sooner or later must be faced, if any real progress is to be made in the correctional field, is the conflict which exists between theory and practice, and that for several centuries crime control has been a matter of expediency rather than planned thinking.

The roots of the present correctional system and all the modern devices for treating criminals lie buried in English history. The conditional pardon, upon which probation and parole are based, was being used in England as early as the seventeenth century, not primarily for the welfare of the prisoner, but because of Colonial expansion and the need for man power. 

The choice of the convicted criminal was limited. He either agreed to be transported or the death penalty was imposed. The conditional pardons granted included high sounding phrases such as, "the pardon is granted to make these criminals useful to the commonwealth," but provisions were also made for the imposition of the death penalty if the prisoner refused to leave England or if he returned to England prior to the time fixed by law. 

The transportation of criminals to America terminated at the outbreak of the Revolutionary War and England was again faced with the problem of what to do with convicted criminals. The problem was further complicated by an outbreak of crime in England with the result that a serious crisis arose in the administration of criminal justice. Pardons and commutations of sentence were granted so freely that it was charged that the whole legal structure was weakened. The King was petitioned for "a speedy and due execution of the law, both as to capital punishment and transportation." Faced with a crime wave, with the unsanitary hulks used to house criminals overcrowded, the Pitt government, which had no interest in the welfare of criminals, had to make a choice: Either to use Australia, recently discovered, as a refuge for the thousands of American Royalists who had returned to England and were starving, or for the reception of transported criminals. The decision, based upon expediency, to use Australia for convict settlements, was announced by the King in 1787. 

These historical statements would probably be of no interest to workers with delinquents and criminals, if, from these emergent measures, we had not later developed another use for the conditional pardon, the shortening of sentence for good behavior and work willingly performed, and the concept of the ticket of leave which in the United States, later developed into parole.

In America following the termination of the war for independence, although Jefferson agitated for the abolishment of the death penalty, it was the Quakers who stressed the need for humanizing the methods of treating criminals. It was a New York State Quaker, Thomas Eddy, known as the 

American John Howard, who convinced Governor Clinton to recommend to the legislature a major reduction in the number of crimes for which the death penalty was imposed, and in 1796 this legislation was enacted and provisions were made for the erection of State prisons. After the prisons were built, the problem arose of what to do with criminals during impris onment and after release, and a hundred and forty-seven years later, the same problem confronts us.

PROGRESS AND TREATMENT OF CRIME AND CRIMINALS HAS BEEN SLOW AND UNCERTAIN 

Progress in the treatment of delinquents and criminals during nearly a century and a half has been slow and uncertain. There have been brief periods of progression and long periods of retrogression. How slowly changes have occurred can be judged from the fact that it was eighty years after the first prison was built before the idea was accepted that reformation is the primary objective of imprisonment. The direct result of this new concept was the establishment of the Elmira Reformatory in the last quarter of the nineteenth century. Following the adoption of the reformatory system, agitation was begun to initiate reformation sentence, or the so-called indeterminate sentence. It was, however, hundred and twelve years before a law was finally passed making it mandatory for judges to impose indeterminate sentences upon individuals never before convicted of felony, and another thirty years passed before provisions were made for the imposition of indeterminate sentences on all convicted felons. 

The first parole law relating to State prisons was enacted in 1889 but no provisions were made for publicly salaried parole officers until 1908. 

In that year four parole officers were appointed and this number was not increased until 1926. 

In 1901 provisions were made to place adult offenders on probation and in 1903 this procedure was extended to juvenile delinquents. After forty years, at least a quarter of the counties in this State, if not more, are without the services of publicly salaried probation officers, and in a majority of counties where some provisions have been made for probation service, the number of probation officers serving the courts is inadequate. 

Children's Courts were established in most states at the beginning of the present century. In New York State, however, it was not until 1921, following the passage of a constitutional amendment, that legislation was enacted providing for the establishment of a State-wide system of Children's Courts. It is doubtful if uniformity of procedure can be achieved, or if effective socialized treatment can be given to the children passing through these courts, because skilled case workers serving the courts are too few in number, and some courts are without case work service. Despite the intent of the law, in some of these courts the proceedings are criminal in nature.

NEED FOR FACTUAL INFORMATION FOR POST-WAR PLANNING 

Before any post-war planning can be done for the correctional field, factual information should be gathered regarding the inequalities of sentences and the relation of sentences to prison programs. Nearly a hundred and fifty years ago a law was placed on the statute books providing that "punishment for the crime shall be apportioned to the different degrees of crime" and the length of the sentence was made dependent upon whether the accused was convicted of a felony in the first, second, or third degree. 

The inequalities of sentences imposed upon convicted criminals has been the subject of many discussions, but no constructive results have yet been achieved. The fine distinctions written into the law have made it possible for caught criminals to bargain with the courts regarding the crime or degree of crime to which they will accept a plea of guilty. The result of this bargain counter justice or copping pleas, is that there is no relation between the crime as charged in the indictment, and the crime to which the accused finally pleads guilty. It has been conservatively estimated that not more than thirty per cent of the men and women convicted of felonies stand trial and are convicted by a jury of their peers, but rather, accept pleas of guilt to lesser crimes or degrees of crime. 

The punishment prescribed for specific crimes has not been constant but has fluctuated. The severity of sentences and the lack of it depends upon changing social conditions. During the so-called crime wave of the twenties, real first offenders, individuals never before convicted of crime, who were found guilty of armed robbery, received sentences as high as fifty-seven and a half years to life. Today, individuals guilty of equally serious crimes may receive sentences of one year and three months to two years and a half. It has been accepted that there is need for specialized institutions and that classification of prisoners is of fundamental importance, but the classification of prisoners and provisions for specialized institutions remain mere objectives to be achieved. Specialized institutions have been provided but efforts to have them function as such have been defeated due to the types of individuals committed to these institutions by the courts. 

In the first report of the Superintendent of Elmira Reformatory, he complained bitterly of the "hardened and habitual criminals being committed" to the newly created institution for reformation. The same complaint is being made today. The selection and commitment of offenders to the 

Elmira Reformatory has been and still is, a matter of chance. In one year, of three hundred ninety-five real first offenders eligible for reformatory treatment, fifty-four per cent were sent to Elmira and forty-six per cent committed to the prisons of the State. A prisoner's age, his crime conviction, and the seriousness of the offense, are not the governing factors in the selection of the institution to which he shall be committed. Just as no selection is made of the type of offender to be sent to Elmira, it is again apparently mere chance that determines whether a convicted felon will be sent to a State prison or to a city or county institution. Just why one individual becomes a State charge and another convicted of an equally serious crime, becomes the responsibility of a local community, still remains an unsolved mystery.

It is essential that the public be re-educated to a realistic picture of the criminal and the complex social and moral problems which the majority of men and women confined in prison present. Lombroso, the Italian criminologist, created the myth of the born criminal, and except for the individuals who draw cartoons for magazines and usually depict prisoners as replicas of Lombroso's criminal man, it is accepted that the Lombrosian theory has been exploded. It is equally important that the theory built up by well meaning leaders in the field, for public consumption, that all prisoners upon reaching the prison gates, demand the program of rehabilitation so well advertised, should also be exploded, for it is just as mythical. Some prisoners do take advantage of the education and vocational opportunities offered and profit from them, while others do nothing to prepare themselves for their return to the community. In prison parlance, "they sleep through their sentences." Nevertheless, our prison programs and parole are based upon the idea of reformation. 

Originally good time allowance, or the reduction of sentence, which makes it possible for prisoners to appear for parole consideration at an earlier date, was to be granted only on the basis of definite achievement in the institution. This idea has been forgotten. It is now automatic for good time to be granted and no time is lost unless the inmate presents a serious behavior problem.

Parole too, was to be granted as a privilege to selected prisoners who, within their physical and mental limitations, had profited from their incarceration and could be regarded as safe risks in the community, but this has changed. Parole today is considered by prisoners to be a right and not a privilege, and they feel it should be granted when they have served the required amount of time according to the sentence imposed. Prisoners no longer feel that they have to earn parole. Those who are not released at the earliest possible date now frequently resort to the courts in an effort to force the Parole Board to release them despite their unsuitability for return to community life. 

What should be the basis of parole? Has too much stress been placed upon selection for parole and not enough upon supervision? Can prisoners be selected for parole on the basis of standards established by theoretical criminologists? Can prison inmates be selected for parole on the basis of prediction tables? If so, what should be done for prisoners who cannot be included under these standards-the blind, the tubercular, the aged and the crippled are they to be left in prison until their sentences expire and then released with no plans formulated for their return to society? 

If parole is to be limited only to those for whom success can be predicted, what other exits should there be from prison? Should concepts of parole be enlarged so that it might ultimately become the social service department of the correctional system?

Probation for children and parents who become involved in domestic problems is generally accepted, but probation as a means of treatment in cases of adults convicted of felonies is still not generally accepted. During a period of twenty years there has been little or no increase in the number of persons convicted of felonies and placed on probation. Why has no

8 progress been made during the last two decades in the use of probation as a means of treating adults convicted of felonies? One reason is that the use of probation in selected cases has apparently not been stressed by those interested in this type of treatment, but there are undoubtedly other reasons which can be discovered only through study and research. Is its uneven development due to the rigid interpretation of the principle of local responsibility, which no longer exists in the field of public welfare? If so, why is probation the primary responsibility of local communities, when it is accepted that other units of the correctional system are the responsibility of the State? When tried under favorable conditions, probation has proven its effectiveness as a method of social control, but can its use be increased until the State assumes additional control and responsibility for its development and its standards? 

The first juvenile court was established more than forty years ago and its informal procedures, based upon chancery proceedings, have vitally affected all phases of correctional treatment. It was originally assumed that if a delinquent child appearing in juvenile court was sympathetically dealt with, his delinquent career could be terminated. For this reason it was stressed that the record of the child's appearance in court should be kept confidential and should not be used against him. 

It was soon recognized, however, that something more than sympathy was needed, but long before the Children's Courts could function effectively in dealing with juvenile delinquents, they were loaded down with a wide variety of unrelated duties. In some states the courts were given jurisdiction over mothers' allowances, the placement of children in foster homes, and the care of the physically handicapped children. As early as 

1914 Thomas Elliot pointed out in his book, "The Juvenile Court and the Community," that most of the functions performed by Children's Courts could be done as well, if not better, by other social agencies, thus leaving to these courts the problems for which they were established, the supervision and treatment of juvenile delinquents. 

Healy in 1915 published the results of his study of one thousand individual delinquents who had appeared in one court. In his book, "The 

Individual Delinquent", he stresses that multiple factors are responsible for delinquent behavior and that if treatment programs are to be followed, it is important to know what happened to John Jones as a child, in attempting to understand his behavior as an adult. However, in the treatment of adult criminals who have a previous history of juvenile delinquency, there is seldom available information regarding the treatment given by the court and, frequently even the records of the case are not available. 

During the years major changes have been made in public welfare laws. 

In most states legislation now makes it possible for the Public Welfare Commissioners to handle the problems of dependency and neglect and they have personnel trained and equipped to handle their clients. However, no such provisions have been made for the staffs attached to the Children's Courts and too many lack adequate personnel or personnel trained to deal with the diverse and complex problems presented by delinquent children.

NEED FOR CRIME PREVENTION 

• • More than a hundred years ago newspapers were reporting and I quote, "the lamentable extent of dishonesty, fraud and other wickedness among our boys and girls, shocks the nation. Large numbers of these youngsters belong to organized gangs of thieves and cut-throats and are in the regular employ of old criminals who teach them the trade. . . Half the number of persons actually convicted of crime are youths who have not reached the age of discretion." Similar comments are being made today and according to the press and radio, there has been an increase in juvenile delinquency since the war. Public opinion is focused upon this problem but specific information and definite plans do not seem to be forthcoming and workers in the correctional field do not seem to have any constructive program to meet the problem. The statistics available relating to the delinquency rates are notoriously unreliable. In England the problem of juvenile delinquency received similar publicity during the early days of the war. A comparison of the statistics available from England and in New 

York State show that more girls appeared in one Children's Court in New York State during a peace time year, without arousing any comment, than appeared in all the courts of England and Wales during a war year. 

Crime prevention programs are a definite need but as yet no determination has been made of the form these programs are to take. Has the State any responsibility for crime prevention and if so, is there a need for a 

State Crime Prevention and Research Bureau? What are the functions which should be performed by such a Bureau?

Probation and parole officers who conduct investigations and supervise persons placed under their care, are aware that in practically every com munity, rural, and urban, there are areas where moral risks are high and it is from these areas that an undue proportion of delinquents and criminals are recruited. Sociologists have stressed "situational factors" as causes for anti social behavior. They have made it possible for us to add to our vocabularies such fine phrases as "areas in transition" or "interstitial areas" but in more virile terms, what is meant, of course, is "rotten neighborhoods" or "slum districts". It is generally accepted that there are individual factors responsible for anti social behavior but it must also be accepted that community conditions play an important role in creating delinquents and criminals. Citizens must accept responsibility for the conditions prevalent in a community which breed delinquency and crime. 

We must seriously consider the extent of interest which can be aroused on the part of the average citizen in any program limited to crime prevention. Our war time experience has shown that hundreds of thousands of private citizens with diverse interests and representing widely different economic and social groups, can be aroused to recognize the necessity for protecting their communities from possible dangers resulting from the war. We know that effective organization and intelligent planning has been accomplished and that these volunteers have given freely of their time. 

Is it possible that in planning an after-war program, crime prevention as such, could be de-emphasized and the stress placed on community protection? Is it too much to hope that the interest and imagination of some

of these volunteers might be aroused in a peace time program of community protection? Some of them might find as much satisfaction in working for decent housing, adequate recreational facilities and the abolishment of moral risks in their communities, as they now find in their present activities.

NEED FOR TRAINED PERSONNEL 

Administrators of public agencies dealing with delinquents and criminals are aware of the need for staff members who are equipped with the personality, training and stamina to handle the problems encountered in their complex and difficult jobs. It is recognized by most administrators that the lack of progress in the field and the existing lack of coordination in procedures is due to the limited trained personnel available. 

From time to time it has been suggested to schools of social work that training programs for workers with delinquents and criminals should be established, but if the head of any of these schools were to ask what would constitute the content of such a training program, and what assurance could be given to men and women interested in training for the field, that jobs would be available upon the completion of their course, no one with authority could answer these fundamental questions. 

Are there possibilities for recruiting after the war, individuals into the field of correctional care? If so, can the proper state and local officials be convinced of the need for trained personnel and will they cooperate with professional schools of social work and administrators by providing scholarships and nominal salaries during a period of internship in correctional agencies?

It appears to be a futile gesture to establish new methods and procedures for programs of correctional care until we have personnel trained to carry out the plans.

It is high time that all parts of the State system of correctional care should be critically evaluated and that plans for changes should be based upon factual knowledge and not upon wishful thinking.
