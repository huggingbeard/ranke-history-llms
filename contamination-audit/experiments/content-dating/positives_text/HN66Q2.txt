PREFACE 

The following advantages were aimed at in writing this Outline of Geometry: 

Earlier introduction of powerful theorems.

A sequence (not only of single theorems, but of natural groups of theorems) which would make the logical succession more obvious, and reduce the burden of memory. 

Occasional study of logical patterns for their own sake, with examples not merely geometrical; and especially the exhibition of "indirect proof" as proof of the contrapositive of the given theorem.

Emphasis on the idea of Order in a geometrical figure. 

The use of algebra and of trigonometric ratios whenever clearness or brevity of demonstration could be thus promoted. 

Using numerical approximation as an easy and sound method of teaching "limits." It is not expected that pupils should learn to reproduce the argument of P1, but that it should be discussed in class, not only at the beginning, but again whenever the "incommensurable case" presents itself.

The table of contents will indicate what theorems of solid geometry will be available at different stages of plane geometry.

CHAPTER I MEASURING DISTANCES, ANGLES AND ARCS 

The word geometry means literally land measurement. One of the ideas underlying our study of geometry is the actual measurement, direct and indirect, of definite objects or diagrams. 

Another idea not quite so ancient is the idea of convincing proof. Thus in geometry we begin by accepting as true a group of statements and then showing how other statements follow from these. 

First comes the direct measurement of distances and angles, and then the indirect measurement of things that cannot be measured directly. This is practical and very old. Thousands of years before 

Christ, direct and indirect measurements were made of rather complicated objects, and some of the records of such measurements, in astronomy for example, are of interest to us today. 

All practical measurements are approximate; for example, after the United States Coast Survey had measured a certain angle with the greatest care and skill, the measurement number recorded in degrees could be written decimally as 71.730456.000078. That means that the angle is not less than 71.730378 degrees, or more than 

71.730534 degrees. In geometry we study figures in which the lines. and angles are supposed to have absolutely accurate measurement numbers.

In this country the standard of length is a metallic bar in Washington, one meter long. The inch and the foot are defined in terms of that bar, by saying that one meter = 39.370 inches, and that one foot 12 inches. The numbers 39.370 and 12 are here theoretically exact, like the measurement numbers of geometry.

APPROXIMATIONS AND IRRATIONAL NUMBERS 

A rational number is a number which can be exactly expressed in figures; as a whole number, a fraction, or ratio of integers. Examples are 5, 1.3, 2/3, 101/100. An irrational number is one which cannot be expressed in figures. Examples are √2, 1/√ 5, π.

Without irrational numbers we cannot find measurement numbers for all distances; for example, the diagonal of a square one inch each way does not correspond in length to any number that we can express in figures. We can get numbers that are too large and numbers that are too small, but we cannot get any number, expressed in figures, that exactly corresponds to the length of this diagonal. 

For that reason men have invented the new kind of number called irrational. That remarkable idea occurred first in geometry. 

Working With Approximate Numbers 

In multiplying and dividing approximate numbers, if the data are of three-figure accuracy the results will not be reliable to more than three figures; if the data are of four-figure accuracy the results will not be reliable to more than four figures; etc.

While logarithms may be used to save labor in a long set of multiplications and divisions, it is often convenient to abbreviate these operations by another method, namely, by omitting unnecessary columns of figures at the right. 

Square roots may be extracted to any desired degree of accuracy by the following steps: 1. Find the nearest square root that you can easily see. 2. Divide. 3. Average the divisor and quotient. 4. If necessary take this average for a new divisor and proceed as before. 5. When you have gone far enough, check by multiplication.

These methods of computation furnish clear illustrations of the fact that approximations can be found with a progressively

In our study of geometry we accept certain general statements and use them to prove other statements which we call theorems.

We have thus far two definitions and a theorem proved by means of them. These three statements have served to make clear and definite the notion of irrational numbers as lengths of straight lines; they apply also to the measurement numbers of all sorts of measurable figures.

Angles are measured directly with protractors which are marked off in units. Just now we want to show how arcs are measured indirectly by first measuring angles. We begin with statements about straight lines, angles, and circles. 

A3. Two straight lines meet if at all in one point only. 

D4. On any straight line, points that lie all on one side of a given point of that line are said to lie in the same RAY, of which the given point is the ORIGIN. 

D5. The DISTANCE of any two points is the straight line having these points as ends. 

A straight line is generally designated by the letters that mark any two of its points; but the expression "the straight line AB" may mean any one of three different things, namely: (1) The distance (often called the line-segment) having A and B for ends. (2) The ray having A for its origin and containing not only the points between A and B, but also every point in line beyond B from A. It is a straight line with only one end, namely, the origin A. (3) The endless straight line AB containing every point in line with A and B whether between A and B or not. A distance has a measurement number but a ray and an endless line are not measurable figures.

A Relation Between Arcs and Angles 

P2. In any circle, or in equal circles, if two central angles are equal, their arcs are equal.

Plan of proof: Superposition. 

P3. In any circle, or in equal circles, if two arcs are equal, their central angles are equal. 

Plan of proof: Superposition. 

Converse Statements 

In theorems P2 and P3 the same figure is referred to, namely two central angles each intercepting an arc. In P2 the hypothesis is that the angles are equal, the conclusion is that the arcs are equal. 

In P3 this hypothesis and this conclusion change places but are not otherwise altered. Such statements are said to be converse; each is the converse of the other. 

When you have two converse theorems each requires a proof; but every definition is supposed to include its own converse. Thus the definition,—“If one straight line makes equal adjacent angles with another straight line, the lines are perpendicular",- may also be stated as follows: "If one straight line is perpendicular to another, they make two equal adjacent angles".

CHAPTER II CONGRUENT TRIANGLES 

What is Geometry?

Geometry has for its subject matter the shape and the measurement of objects. Not only of separate objects, but of groups of objects,― that is to say, the position of one object in relation to other objects.

The shapes that we discuss in geometry are called figures. 

They are not irregular or indefinite, like the shapes we actually see and handle, for they are as we make them in our minds; they are ideal figures. We imagine them perfectly smooth, with sharp and fine edges, lines, and corners. We make drawings of them, which agree as closely as possible with the descriptions that identify them, so that we can keep clearly in mind the details of shape; but the drawings are less perfect than the ideal figures they represent. 

These drawings represent particular figures; but those figures are only samples of the millions of figures answering to the same descriptions. The statements we make about one particular figure then are made also about every figure that can be described in the same way as the sample. They are general statements about a whole class of figures.

Of all these statements there are some that we assume to be true. Others that are just as obvious, and others again that are not obvious at all, we prove true if the statements we assume are true. Thus we include a great multitude of statements, some rather intricate, some previously known, and many of very great importance, in one system of exact knowledge, all built upon a few simple foundations.

At first we study only figures that can be laid upon a flat surface, which we call in geometry a plane, and even then we confine ourselves entirely to figures that can be made with circles and straight lines. In this restricted field we learn to appreciate the certainty of geometrical proofs. 

It was by studying plane geometry that men first learned to understand about multiplying fractional numbers; and it is still by

studying such simple figures as triangles and circles, and by approximating to their measurements, that we can learn to understand about the strange numbers called irrational; numbers that can be described in words, and represented by symbols, but never exactly written down.

The Next Thing to Do 

We have already reminded ourselves about some of the simplest details of shape: What do we know about a plane? A circle? 

An angle? How does right-and-left come into our work in Chapter I?

Our next task is area measurement, which is necessarily indirect. 

Everybody knows that the area of a rectangle is the product of length and breadth. People knew that before they could use iron. 

The area of an irregular figure, and especially of a circle, is more difficult; and even for the rectangle we must make sure that the rule holds whether we can or cannot find an exact expression in figures for the length or the breadth.

For this purpose we need to study further the details of shape. 

This chapter and the next will be devoted to this preparatory work.

What we need, then, is the following statement: If two lines meet in two points they are not both straight lines. Is this last statement really different, except in wording, from the one before?

Proportion 

An equation between two ratios is called a PROPORTION, if it is stated in terms of the four quantities whose ratios are in question. 

In old times these proportions were solved verbally; and some of this antiquated algebra still survives and has given us a few mathematical phrases. Thus the words "x and p are proportional to y and z" were used to express the fact that we state by the two equations x my and p = mz when we do not say what number m stands for. In the form of a "proportion" this would be y/xz/p or x/y = p/z; the four quantities in the proportion being called its TERMS. The terms that begin and end the proportion are called the EXTREMES; those in between the MEANS. Again, the last term would be called a FOURTH PROPORTIONAL to the other three in their order. In the proportion a/p = p/b, p is a MEAN PROPORTIONAL between a and b, and b is called a THIRD PROPORTIONAL to a and p. 

In this last proportion p² = ab suggests a square equal to a rectangle, and thus indicates why the mean proportional is also called a 

GEOMETRIC MEAN.

Note on Converging Approximations

Until we came to P37, the converging approximations we used were decimal numbers, because our decimal system of writing numbers makes that method most convenient, and it could easily be applied to straight lines and polygons. We can, however, divide a unit not only into tenths but also into any other subdivisions, and we might have used halves or fifths or sevenths were it not for the superior convenience of decimal numbers. 

In P37 it is simpler, and just as sound, to approximate by halves; that is, to make each succeeding margin of error less than half of the one before it.

The Words "Limit" and "Approach" When we first discussed converging approximations we took decimal approximations, both upper and lower, to the rational fraction 4/3; that served as illustration for the fact that any number, rational or irrational, can be determined if we can get converging approximations to it.

A number so determined is often spoken of as the limit of one of the two series of approximations. It is the upper limit of the lower series as it ascends, or the lower limit of the upper series as it descends. Thus 4/3 is the upper limit of the series 1.3, 1.33, 1.333, etc.; or 4/3 is the lower limit of the series 1.4, 1.34, 1.334, etc.

The verb to approximate is the same word as the verb to approach, except that the latter word has been filtered through the French, while the former comes more directly from the Latin original. 

In fact the French say "valeurs approchées" when they mean "approximate values." When we say the variable nr²sin (180°/n) "approaches" the area of the circle "as a limit" as n is repeatedly doubled, the word "approach" has a technical meaning, not the same as when we say that a ship is approaching the harbor. It sometimes happens that we can find precisely the "error" of an approximation, that is, the difference between the approximation and its limit. This is the case with the series 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, 15/16 of which the limit is 1. In many cases, however, the value of the limit is not known except by its approximations, and the "error" is merely known to be less than what we have called the margin of error. This is the case with the area of the circle, or with any irrational number. 

When we speak of a series of numbers approaching a limit it should not be supposed that the series must be entirely an ascending series, or entirely a descending series; it might be an "oscillating" series, that is, with one term greater, the next term less, and so

on.

There is, for example, a famous series of fractions of which the squares approach 2 as a limit. It was known before Euclid's time, but he furnished the proof for it. This is the series 3/2, 7/5, 17/12, The squares of the terms are 9/4, 49/25, 289/144, ; and the "errors" are +1/4, 1/25, + 1/144, 41/29. 1681/841 - 1/841.

NOTE ON LOCUS PROPOSITIONS 

In every locus proposition there are two theorems involved. 

When one of them is proved it is shown only that the hypothesis of that theorem is a sufficient condition for the conclusion; then the second theorem is to show that the same hypothesis is a necessary condition for the same conclusion. Homely illustrations, as well as geometric statements, will serve to make this distinction clear. "If I am rich, I can pay my poll-tax." Being rich is a sufficient condition here, but not a necessary one; because if I am not rich, I can still very likely pay my poll-tax. "If Benjamin is a native of the United States, he can be President." The condition there is necessary, but certainly not sufficient. In order that a quadrilateral may be a parallelogram, it is sufficient but not necessary that all its angles should be equal; it is necessary but not sufficient that one side should be parallel to another side; it is necessary and sufficient that the diagonals bisect each other; and it is neither necessary nor sufficient that two of the angles should be right angles. 

When any theorem is proved, it is shown that the hypothesis that the conclusion is a sufficient condition for the conclusion, or is a necessary condition for the hypothesis. Proving is the same thing as proving ; the two statements are, as we say, contrapositive.

43 In every locus proposition there are four statements to consider; they are as follows: (a) This is our kind of point. (b) This is not our kind of point. (c) This point is in our figure. (d) This point is not in our figure. 

What we have to prove is that (a) is a necessary condition, as well as a sufficient condition, for (c). This is usually done by proving that if (a) is true (c) is true, and then that if (c) is true (a) is true; two theorems, one the converse of the other. Either of these theorems could be replaced by its contrapositive, and it is quite usual to make that change for the second theorem; that is, after proving that if (a) is true (c) is true, we prove that if (b) is true (d) is true.

Locus theorems are of great educational value because they return with each theorem to the distinction between necessary and sufficient conditions and to the equivalence of contrapositive statements. The fact that these two ideas are precisely the things that make locus questions seem difficult is sure testimony that this practice is badly needed. It is in this work also that one is often required to recognize differing statements when their verbal. expression seems almost identical; for example, in finding the locus of points less than two inches and more than one inch from a given point Q, the locus must be seen to be the bounded region without its boundary; whereas if the word "less" in the description of the locus were changed to "not more," a part of the boundary would'thereby be included. 

Definitions are different from theorems not only in not being proposed for proof, but also in presenting conditions necessary as well as sufficient. Thus, the definition of a parallelogram tells us not only that a quadrilateral is a parallelogram if its opposite sides are parallel, but also that if the opposite sides are not parallel the quadrilateral is no parallelogram. On the other hand, the theorem that if a triangle has a right angle, the square of one side is the sum of the squares of the other two sides does not tell us that if the triangle has not a right angle, the equation of the squares is not true. That requires a separate proof, and even in the unusual statement here given should be recognized as the converse of P22. 

As we have seen, it is included in P24.

This theorem could also be stated in either of the following ways:

In choosing which of the four "answers" you will prove, it is necessary to remember that if any hypothesis is sufficient to prove a certain conclusion, that conclusion has to be a necessary condition for the hypothesis. CHAPTER XI PARALLEL AND PERPENDICULAR LINES AND PLANES 

Since we are now to study figures that do not lie all in one plane, we have to make diagrams that show different planes. Our diagrams cannot show them unless they have edges. For that reason our pictures of planes are the flat surfaces of certain objects, like blocks or pyramids, or pictures of what might be a flat card or a sheet of window glass.

However, the plane is not limited in extent, as our pictures seem to indicate. Any point, no matter how far away, is in a given plane if it is in line with any two points of that plane (A18). We have already had in mind, when we discussed parallel lines, the idea of an unbounded plane.

Since a plane is determined by any three of its points that are not in line, the plane may be named by the letters of three such points. The three letter name is unmistakable. Sometimes, when there is no danger of confusion, a single letter may be used, preferably a Greek letter. The practice of naming a plane by two letters is not advisable.

NOTE ON THE PRISMOID FORMULA 

The formula of S23 is of much practical use, for example, in computing the filling required for an embankment, or the masonry for a dam. Cross sections are measured at regular intervals and the entire body of material is considered as a succession of prismoids. 

If the embankment starts and ends with flat surfaces, and the areas of the sections (including the end areas) are successively A, B, C, D,... to N, with an interval of h feet, the first two portions will have for volume 2h(A + 4B + C)/6, and the next two 2h (C + 4D + E)/6, and so on (S22), and the number of sections will have to be odd. If it should not be odd, the end portion can be treated as a separate prismoid, and its volume separately measured. 

When the prismoids are added up we get for the total volume h(A + 4B + 2C + 4D + 2E+...+ N)/3. The coefficients 1, 4, 2, 4, 2... 1 in this expression for volume are sometimes called "Simpson's Multipliers," and the formula containing them "Simpson's Rule." It is often used for finding the area under an arbitrary curve, just as it serves here, not merely for a volume, but also for the area under its curve of sections.

WHERE SIMILARITY COUNTS 

The strength of muscles in an animal depends on the number of muscular fibers, and this depends on sectional area; the weight of an animal depends on its volume. $29 makes it clear that a large animal will necessarily be less agile than a small one, and thus explains why a moose cannot leap as a small deer can; why a grasshopper can jump into the air though an ostrich, with its powerful legs, cannot; why a man, in proportion to his size, is far less efficient physically than an ant; and finally why there is a limit to the size of living things, since weight increases as the cube of the height, while strength increases as the square.

The loss of heat in an animal body depends upon its surface area; the ability to generate heat depends upon the volume of the heat-producing tissues in the body. If an adult human being loses 

64 units of heat in a given time, his body has to produce 64 units in order that he may live in comfort; if a baby is one-fourth as tall as the adult, he will lose only 4 units of heat in the same time, but his body tissues can produce only 1 unit of heat. The loss of heat is proportional to the square of the length, the production of heat to the cube. For this reason, doctors say, babies need to be warmly wrapped.

The lifting power of an aeroplane depends on areas, its weight on volumes; what of it?

NOTE ON REGULAR POLYEDRONS 

Theorem $47 implies that the sum of the face angles of any convex polyedral is less than 27 (D77). Therefore there cannot be more than five "regular" polyedrons. A regular polyedron is defined as convex and bounded exclusively by equal regular polygons; and since to bound a polyedron there must be at least three

faces at every vertex, we are restricted to polygons with angles less than 2π/3. The only suitable regular polygons, then, are the regular triangle, the square, and the regular pentagon, with angles π/3, π/2, 3/5, respectively; we could have three, four, or five regular triangles at one vertex, but no more than three squares or three regular pentagons. So with triangles, we have the tetrahedron, the octahedron and the icosahedron, with squares the cube, and with pentagons the dodecahedron.

It should be noted that while any prism is a polyedron, the only regular prism that is a regular polyedron is a cube; and that while every triangular pyramid is a tetrahedron, the only regular tetrahedron is the regular triangular pyramid that has lateral edges and base edges all equal.
