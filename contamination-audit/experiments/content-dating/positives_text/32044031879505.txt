Federal Power Commission INTRODUCTION 

This report is similar to the Federal Power Commission October 1955, publication bearing the same title. The forecasts extend to the year 1980 as previously, but historical data have been advanced one year in order to make the information current. The tabular data are presented in the same detail as in the earlier publication, while two charts showing past and projected future load growth have been added. To satisfy the many types of inquiries pertaining to the previous report and to assist the numerous individuals and groups desiring to study load forecasting methods, including teams of foreign power engineers visiting the United States, a portion of the text of the 1955 report relating to scope and procedures has been repeated in the current issue, and some background information has been added to better familiarize the reader with the Commission's work and the methods used.

SCOPE OF REPORT The 

The smaller areas for which data are included in this report are called power supply areas. Although there are 48 power supply areas at the present time, the borders do not follow state lines except in a few instances. boundaries of the power supply areas are not permanently fixed but are adjusted when necessary to reflect changes in utility system service areas. Basically the power supply areas (see map on page 21) are made up of the territories served by several substantially interconnected utility systems operating as an essentially self-sufficient group independent of adjacent systems. In summarizing power supply area data, isolated systems are grouped with the systems to which they would most likely be physically connected for coordinated operation and economy. As interconnecting transmission lines are built or systems merged, changes occur in the boundaries of affected power supply areas; and because of the rapid expansion that has been going on in the electric utility industry, systems have become more closely integrated with each other for reasons of economy and reliability of service, so that in some instances utility pools cover very extensive areas. For this reason power supply areas are grouped into power supply regions, eight in number, and power problems are frequently studied on a regional basis particularly when a broad picture of the power requirements and supply situation over the entire country is desired. This report is fundamentally regional in scope and all the tables are arranged to give figures by the eight power supply regions. The power supply area data shown are included because of the many requests received for information on areas smaller than the regions. 

The coverage of the report is the same as last year, and thus the figures pertain to the entire electric utility industry including both large and small systems, public and private. Data for nonutility plants having their own generating capacity are also given.

The viewpoints of such parties are very important to the regional offices in their judgment of how fast power markets will expand in the future. However it has been the general practice of the Federal Power Commission staff not to release its economic analyses for the reason that undue authority might be attached to them and because they might mistakenly be regarded as official government forecasts. The Commission is primarily concerned with electric power and is interested in the related economic aspects only as a means to an end in the development of its load projections. 

PREVIOUS FORECASTS 

The continued expansion of the nation's economy has for the most part induced those concerned with future growth of power requirements to raise their sights. Experience shows that predictions for the future are more strongly affected by current than by past conditions. During the present period of prosperity and rapid expansion in electric power use the country's electric utilities have become increasingly more optimistic with respect to the future. Each month the major utility systems, representing some 97 to 98 percent of the industry total, to the Federal Power Commission giving their estimates of anticipated loads over the next few years. Summarizing the reports, it is found that the forecasts were raised every month from January 1955, to September 1956, the latter being the latest month for which information was available at the time of this writing. 

Back in January of 1955 the major systems of the country estimated their combined December 1958, peak would be 116,409,000 kilowatts, and by August of 1956 the figure had been raised to 122,976,000 kilowatts, an increase of more than 6½ million kilowatts. On the other hand, back in 1953 and 1954, at a time when the economy was operating below its growing potential, estimates of future electric loads were being revised downward. Thus, it is found that in January of 1953 the utility forecasts of the December 1954, peak amounted to 92,250,000 kilowatts, and nearly every month thereafter the figure was lowered until December 1954, became history and the actual peak proved to be 85,580,000 kilowatts, nearly 7 million kilowatts lower than originally estimated. 

As the preceding observations illustrate, it is difficult to foresee the near-term future; and it is obviously still more difficult to foresee the longterm future. For example, the extraordinary population increases that the nation - 3 -

has been undergoing since World War II were not fully anticipated by anyone prior to 1950. Most people who make predictions of things to come rely on the Bureau of the Census for estimates of future population growth. The Census reports generally give several series of projections based upon rates of growth experienced during different periods in the past, and the forecaster is allowed to choose the series he believes preferable. In most instances, it appears, the high series is selected. Back in 1947 the high Census series showed a United States population of 185 million for the year 1975 whereas the most recent Census study gives a high of 228 million for 1975. This factor alone would increase load forecasts nearly 

25 percent. Some years ago the Federal Power Commission was inclined to use the median Census projections rather than the high. For the past several years, however, leaning has been toward the higher series, and the regional estimates of population used herein total 222 million for 1975 as against the high series figure of 228 million given in the latest release of the Bureau of the Census. 

Most long-term projections made in the past appear to be proving themselves low as the years unfold. Of the numerous long-range estimates made by various authorities just prior to the Korean War, one was far above the others, predicting that the nation's electric utility energy requirements would reach 657 billion kilowatt-hours in 1960. While the accuracy of this estimate will not be known until the year 1960 is history, present indications are that the projection will prove to be low by some 100 to 150 billion kilowatt-hours. Of course it must be said in favor of the prognosticators that none of the pre-Korean estimates assumed wartime conditions, and there is no question that the Korean War and the defense program which followed have had a very important effect on the nation's economy. A substantial part of the plant expansion that has taken place since the war has been for defense purposes, and much of the electric utility expansion program has likewise been necessary to meet defense needs. Consumption of electric power at production facilities of the Atomic Energy Commission alone now amounts to some 60 billion kilowatt-hours annually, and how much additional power is required in the mining and manufacturing of some of the high quality materials needed by atomic plants is not known. 

Quite aside from the military program the nation's population, as previously stated, has been growing at a rapid pace, and employment and income have been high. In addition the desire of the people for material goods has continued unabated. In spite of record-high incomes consumers have gone heavily into debt resulting in further stimulus to the economy. As a result over-all consumption of electric power is approaching three-quarters of a trillion kilowatt-hours per year and seems destined to continue to rise in ever increasing amounts. During 

1955 nearly 12 million kilowatts of generating capacity were added to the electric utility resources of this country, the greatest annual expansion in history. This compares with an average annual increase of less than 1 million kilowatts during the 1930-40 decade and less than 3 million kilowatts in the 1940-50 decade. 

PRESENT FORECASTS 

The industry is very optimistic about the years ahead, with many of its electric utility representatives seeing a doubling of electric loads every 10 years indefinitely into the future, but, the Federal Power Commission estimates indicate a lower rate of growth. Somewhat contrary to the oft-expressed statement that loads have doubled every 10 years in the past, the 10-year rate of growth. actually has fluctuated widely. In the early 1900's the rate of growth was much greater than later on, and loads sometimes doubled in a period of as little as four years. Considering the period 1920 to 1955, the lowest 10-year rate of increase was between 1928 and 1938, being equivalent to a doubling in 22 years. The highest 

10-year rate of growth to the end of 1955 was between 1933 and 1943 from depression to war -- and was equivalent to a doubling in seven years. When the final figures for 1956 become available, it may be found that the period 1946 to 1956,

starting as it does in the post-war reconversion period, experienced a somewhat faster rate of expansion than occurred between 1933 and 1943. Therefore, it does not appear practicable to forecast loads merely on the basis of rates encountered in the past.

Assuming a doubling every 10 years in the future, as some estimators do, electric power use would reach astronomical heights in a relatively short time. By the end of this century the growth in one single year would, on the latter assumption, amount to nearly twice the entire growth that occurred during the first 75 years of the electric industry's history (1879-1954), a period of farreaching achievement. With such an increase in electric power use, the nation's utilities would have to place in operation each year about 200 million kilowatts of additional generating capacity compared with the all-time record to date of 12 million kilowatts for the year 1955. 

While most planning does not extend into the future more than about 25 years, it has been found necessary in some instances to make power projections beyond the end of this century, thus it does not seem amiss to contemplate the result of assuming a constant rate of growth for the long-term future. It is found that many factors of the economy are being projected at nearly constant rates in studies analyzing prospects for the future. Graphical extrapolations on semi-logarithmic paper may be partially responsible for some of these projections. 

Unless the scales used in plotting past data are carefully selected, factors which have shown substantial growth variations in the past may appear to have followed a fairly straight line course. It then may not seem unreasonable to extend such graphs into the future along the same straight line. If a semi-logarithmic scale is used in so doing, exceedingly high figures can result because of the very nature of such a scale. For this reason it is generally wise to complement logarithmic charts with rectilinear charts. Graphs 1 and 2 of this report, explained further on, illustrate the two types. 

The Federal Power Commission forecasts show the nation's electric power loads increasing from 103 million kilowatts in 1955 to 335 million in 1980 (Table A). The latter represents an increase of 7 percent, over the 312 million kilowatts shown in the last report. To meet these loads, the electric utility systems must have not only generating capacity equal to the load but an added increment for reserves in order to assure continuity of service. In the past the amount of reserve capacity has varied considerably, being as high as 44 percent of the load requirements in 1939 for the country as a whole and as low as 5 percent during 1948. In 1955 the reserves were somewhat more than 11 percent. REGIONAL PATTERNS 

All areas of the country have been participating in the remarkable industrial expansion that the nation has been enjoying since World War II, and from the standpoint of electric power consumption all areas have been experiencing greater growth in the past few years than during any other time in the past. 

Electric power is considered to be a very good indicator of economic activity, but in reviewing the past, many divergent trends and cross-currents are found which make it difficult to analyze the relationship between power use and economic growth. This results from the fact, first, that the over-all economy consists of a number of heterogeneous segments including such activities as mining and manufacturing, farming, construction, wholesale and retail trade, plus numerous other categories essential to a well-rounded economy. A few indicators of economic activity, incidentally, are given on a regional basis in Table E, page 18. Secondly, electric power consumption is broken down into a number of categories such as farm, residential, commercial, industrial, and others. Common denominator of both economic activity and power use is population and the things that motivate the population.

The amount of electricity people use varies considerably throughout the country for a number of reasons. Average consumption per capita is currently running in the neighborhood of 4,000 kilowatt-hours per year. The figures for 

1955 show that Region VII in the Northwest was the highest with a consumption of 8,100 kilowatt-hours per capita, while the Midwest-Mountain Region VI, with a use of only 2,200 kilowatt-hours, was the lowest. Region VI has very little industry as compared with other areas, but on the other hand Region VII is not a typically industrial area either. Moreover, Region I in the Northeast which has more industry than any other region by far had a per capita use of only about 3,000 kilowatt-hours in 1955 and was next to Region VI at the low end of the list. The reason for Region VII's high use lies not only in the number of industries it has - 6 -

-- 
The of the type that requires huge amounts of power in the manufacturing process such as aluminum refining plants but also in the availability of large amounts of low-cost hydroelectric power from the Columbia River, and the attractive rates. for residential consumers. Region VI, on the other hand, is basically a farm area, having an income from farm operations much larger than that of any other area. high income from farming does not, however, represent unusual prosperity since it results from the fact that a sizable portion of the people are engaged in farming. 

From the standpoint of income per farm there are other sections of the country which rank higher and others which rank lower. Region VIII in the Pacific Southwest, with its high-yield irrigated farms, and the Midwest-Great Lakes Region IV, with its prosperous dairy and livestock farms, have higher average farm income. than Region VI; but Region III in the South and Region V in the Southwest, in spite of the fact that they are now the fastest growing areas in the country in terms of electric power use, both have low incomes per farm. The latter areas have a good deal of industry, however, whereas Region VI has comparatively little. 

Thus with its average electric use per farm and per residential home about the same as for the nation and with small power use by industry, Region VI has a lower per capita use of electricity than other sections of the country. 

Region III in the South has experienced the fastest growth in power consumption of any of the regions during the last 10 years. Use of electricity in its homes is very high next to that of Region VII and a number of homes, particularly in the area served by the Tennessee Valley Authority, have electric heating. The TVA also serves some exceptionally large power-consuming industries and supplies a huge amount of power to facilities of the Atomic Energy Commission. Nearly one-half of the industrial electric load in Region III is accounted for by such nuclear plants. Since no further expansion of nuclear loads is contemplated at present, the rate of growth of total electric power requirements in the region for all purposes is not expected to be as great in the future as in the past. Incidentally, whenever industrial plants requiring unusually large amounts of power locate in a particular area, a substantial rate of increase in total electric energy requirements generally results. Such increases should be borne in mind when comparing future rates of growth with those of the past. Regions II (Ohio Valley-Great Lakes) and IV (Midwest-Great Lakes) are areas which, along with Region III, have participated in nuclear energy expansion, and Region II will soon be affected by a number of aluminum plant expansions as well. Industrial loads of this type point to one of the difficulties encountered in forecasting load growth on a regional basis and particularly for still smaller areas such as power supply areas, data for which appear in Table C, page 15. 

Region VIII in the Pacific Southwest has outrun all other regions by a wide margin from the standpoint of population gains. In spite of this its electric energy needs have been increasing, for some time, only at about the same rate as the nation's. Nevertheless the area's industrial energy sales have been growing in importance as compared with other sales classes. A number of industrial companies have been expanding in the West and such expansions together with others being contemplated will help to round out the industrial economy of the region. 

Value added by manufacture is still below that of the nation as a whole on a per capita basis and as the region catches up and as its population continues to increase, electric power needs are expected to rise without too much falling off in the rate of growth for many years to come. The forecasts presented in this report give Region VIII a greater rate of growth than that of any other region.

-- sales including that part of farm sales represented by the farm home have shown a larger rate of growth over the long run than any other class, and represented nearly 25 percent of the total energy sold by electric utilities in the country during 1955. Nevertheless this percentage is only about one-half of that accounted for by the industrial sales group which, while having experienced a longterm loss insofar as its percentage of the total is concerned, has lately been holding its comparative importance fairly well. World War II increased the relative size of the industrial load at the expense of most other categories, but the post-war reconversion period brought it back to its normal position, and the atomic energy program plus the huge aluminum expansion program have helped to keep industrial sales at about 50 percent of total utility sales. It should be pointed out with respect to the industrial group that, in addition to the large quantity of power which is purchased from electric utilities, a substantial amount of power is generated by industry in its own plants (see Table B, page 15). Taking this into consideration, it is found that total industrial use of electric power in 1955 was some 58 percent of combined consumption of electricity for all classes of use exclusive of energy losses.

The only changes of particular note over a long-term period have been a gradual increase in the relative magnitude of residential sales and an accompanying gradual decrease in the relative size of the railroad and railway category along with industry. The railroad and railway classification, which is intended to include only power for electric propulsion purposes, has gradually but consistently been losing out, having slipped over the past 30 years from 10 percent of total utility sales to a mere 1 percent. Except for the latter group, however, recent annual growth has been distributed among the various categories in such proportions that their percentages of the total have been holding fairly steady for the past four or five years.

Residential The homes of this country probably represent the greatest potential market available for increasing electric power sales. There are close to 49 million farm and nonfarm homes, nearly all of which now have central station service and are therefore customers of the nation's electric utility systems. There are only about six million other electric utility customers of all kinds and most of these fall in the commercial classification. While total industrial energy consumption, including that supplied from industry's own plants, is nearly three times. as great as residential consumption, there are but a few hundred thousand industrial customers altogether. Average use per industrial customer is, of course, much higher than average home use. However, the typical fully electrified home, of which there are relatively few today, consumes from 20,000 to 25,000 kilowatthours per year, whereas the average home uses less than 3,000 kilowatt-hours. 

With rising population and a resultant growth in the number of homes, together with increasing use per customer, the residential classification is expected to become still more significant and may at some time in the distant future even overtake the industrial class. 

It is anticipated that residential use will increase from 2,690 kilowatt-hours per home in 1955 to 7,000 in 1980. The latter figures represent an average for both farm and nonfarm homes. Sufficient information is not available at present to determine whether the farm home uses more or less electricity than the nonfarm home. It would seem that the farm home has the greater potential need for electricity, particularly for such uses as water heating, cooking, and refrigeration, which are among the top consumers of electric energy in the home today. 

- 8 -

However, because of lower incomes and generally higher costs for electricity, the farm home may use less than the nonfarm home on the average. 

The number of nonfarm-residential customers is expected to increase from about 40 million in 1955 to approximately 62 million in 1980. These figures exclude any apartment or other type homes which the utility companies include in the commercial classification. There are possibly as many as two or three million homes in this category at the present time. Combining use per customer and number of customers, the forecasts show nonfarm-residential consumption increasing from 109 billion kilowatt-hours in 1955 to 434 billion in 1980. 

In making the forecasts of energy use per home, some 20 separate appliances were studied and the increases in the saturations of each of these appliances throughout the forecast period were estimated. The results indicate that electric water heaters, air coolers, ranges, refrigerators, television sets, deep freezers, and clothes dryers will be the most important appliances from the standpoint of energy consumption, and in the order given. These appliances will account for nearly three-quarters of total home use in 1980 exclusive of lighting and electric heat. Lighting is estimated at an average of 725 kilowatt-hours per customer in 1980, and 1,450 kilowatt-hours are allowed for electric heating. It is assumed that some 14 percent of the homes will have complete electric heat by 1980, either of the resistance or heat pump type. This may seem small when it is considered that there are now, in certain sections of the country, communities in which most of the homes under construction are being equipped with electric heat. However, it is the average condition for the country as a whole which must be considered and it is felt that electricity will not be competitive with other fuels, on a price basis, in most areas of the country during the period of estimate. Although customer preference will play a part, the major consideration will be price. 

In spite of the promotional work that has already been done by some electric utilities, there are many aspects of the electric heating problem that are still being investigated, so that it is difficult, at this stage, to estimate how much should be allowed for electric heating in load forecasts. 

With regard to new uses of electricity which will be developed in the future it is helpful to look back over the past for guidance. While a multitude of new appliances have been placed on the market over the years, the main uses of electricity in the home have continued to be for lighting; television and radio; preserving, preparing, and cooking food; pumping and heating water; washing and drying clothes and dishes; ironing; and cleaning, heating and cooling the home. 

Most of the major appliances now on the market were invented many years ago, but they have been improved over the years and their acceptance by the public has broadened. Although new appliances do not always add to electric energy since they sometimes substitute for equipment already on hand, it seemed prudent to make a generous allowance in these long-range forecasts for increased electric consumption due to new developments in the future. On the other hand some applications may be expected to become more efficient -- lighting and cooking, for example and other scientific advances, such as the replacement of radio and television. tubes by transistors, may tend to offset in part the bright outlook for new uses of electric energy. Farm

In making the electric load projections for farms it was assumed, as previously pointed out, that the average farm home would use the same amount of electricity as the nonfarm home. Total use per farm includes, in addition, the power consumed by the great variety of equipment now available including milking machines, milk coolers, incubators, brooders, grinders, hoists, pumps (including irrigation pumps), hay driers, cleaners, and many other devises. The Department of Agriculture has made a number of excellent studies of present and potential use - 9 -

of electricity for a variety of different types of farming operations in scattered areas of the country, and these studies as well as conferences with the Department of Agriculture staff, including staff of the Rural Electrification Administration, were of great value in preparing the farm projections. In addition, considerable material was supplied regarding use of electricity on farms served by the REA Co-operatives. Individual studies of the need for power over a 10 year period in the future have been made by the REA systems, and although such systems serve only about one-half of the farms in the country, the studies were helpful in determining trends. 

The advantages of electricity in farm operations are well recognized and in many instances it can be demonstrated that electricity pays for itself by increased production, improved quality, and reduced labor costs to say nothing of added convenience. There has been considerable effort by various organizations to call the farmers' attention to the kinds of electric equipment available and the benefits resulting from their use. Consumption of electricity for farm operations has been increasing but the potentials for growth still remain large. Of course it should be borne in mind that not all of the equipment utilized by farmers is electric. Farm mechanization has accomplished wonders in increasing production and improving working conditions, but most of the farm machines turned out by our factories are powered by liquid fuels rather than electricity. 

While electric use per farm is expected to increase in the future, the over-all rate of growth for the country will not be as large as for other classes of use nonfarm-residential, for example because the number of farms is not expected to grow. The long-term trend in the number of farms has been declining, as has farm population, although size of farm and output per farm has increased. Leveling off tendencies in the decline are possibly appearing, and it is assumed that the number of farms in 1980 will not be much lower than today. 

Total use of electricity on farms is expected to increase from 26 billion kilowatthours in 1955 to about 59 billion in 1980. These figures include 8 billion kilowatt-hours for irrigation and drainage pumping in 1955 and 15 billion in 1980. Commercial

Electric energy sales to commercial customers represent about a sixth of total utility sales. While this is small as compared with the industrial portion, it nevertheless represented some 81 billion kilowatt-hours in 1955. This amount was consumed by 6 million customers having an average annual use of 13,363 kilowatt-hours, which is five times the average residential usage. Very little detailed information is available with respect to the commercial category. The lack of information probably results from the complexity of this group consisting as it does of so many kinds of stores, offices, restaurants, hotels, garages, gasoline stations, depots, warehouses, hospitals, schools, churches, clubs, societies, museums, stadiums, and even small manufacturing plants in some instances -- not to mention the many homes included in the commercial group. The Federal Power Commission selected a particular area some years ago for studying commercial energy consumption but it would not be practical to apply this study today to the entire country. The Building Research Advisory Board recently completed an investigation. of load growth in certain commercial buildings, and a number of electric utilities. provided information from which summarized conclusions were derived. However, the investigation was not of large enough scope to be of much assistance in projecting commercial loads on a regional or country-wide basis. 

The Federal Power Commission has available for each utility system in the country a long-time record of commercial energy sales, revenues and number of customers, and the Edison Electric Institute periodically publishes such information summarized by states and for the country. The Commission staff, in making its projections, summarizes available data by power supply areas, compares the infor- - 10 -

mation with population and income data, and extends past trends on a judgment basis. The load forecasts anticipate an increase in number of commercial customers from 6 million in 1955 to more than 9 million in 1980. Annual use per customer is expected to grow from 13,363 kilowatt-hours to 28,500 kilowatt-hours during the same period, resulting in a total commercial consumption of 272 billion kilowatthours in 1980 as compared with 81 billion in 1955. 

Industrial 

With respect to the very important industrial classification it had been hoped that the full results of the 1954 Census of Manufactures and the Census of Mineral Industries would be available at the time the Federal Power Commission. load forecasts were being prepared. No complete information on power in manufacturing has been prepared since the 1947 Census of Manufactures and very little information has been published on power for mining operations since the 1939 Census of Mineral Industries and the World War II canvasses conducted by the Federal Power Commission. The Census Annual Survey of Manufactures series have been of some use to the Federal Power Commission staff, but the 1954 data will provide a wealth of new material necessary to bring previous studies up-to-date. 

The Federal Power Commission each month canvasses those industrial establishments which have their own generating plants as well as railroads and and railways, government facilities and certain other concerns having generating capacity of their own. In 1955 total nonutility generation, as shown in Table B, page 15, amounted to 82 billion kilowatt-hours of which more than 79 billion was industrial that is, manufacturing and mining. This represented about a fourth of the total electric power used by industry. While generation by industry for its own use has not increased as fast as energy purchased from the electric utilities, it has nevertheless been growing steadily. Because of their very size some industrial plants can afford to install big, efficient units and generate their own requirements. The fact that transmission losses, normally associated with utility generation, are largely eliminated is an item in favor of self-generation. Other industries have need for substantial amounts of heat or steam in the manufacturing process, and the incremental cost of providing some additional heat and steam for generating at least a portion of the electric energy needs is often sufficiently low to warrant such generation. Among the manufacturing industries the prime metal manufacturers, particularly aluminum and steel, have large amounts of generating capacity. Chemical plants and pulp and paper plants also generate considerable quantities of electric energy in their own stations. These three major industries primary metals, chemicals, and pulp paper account for roughly 75 percent of total industrial generation. The respective quantities for the three in 1955 were 26, 18, and 16 billion kilowatt-hours. 

In connection with the rapid industrial expansion that has been taking place over the past few years, all regions of the country seem to have participated at least that is true for the eight Federal Power Commission regions. Chemical plants have been located in nearly all areas, but probably mainly in Regions I, II, and V. Primary metal facilities and factories producing metal products and machinery have gone up in Regions I, II, and IV but also in other sections of the country including Region VIII. Aircraft expansions have occurred in Regions V, VII and VIII, and missile developments in VI and VIII. Paper facilities have been installed in Region III and expanded in Region VII. Numerous electronic plants have been put in operation in Regions I and II. Food processing factories have been built in Regions IV and VI and farm equipment plants, as well, in Region IV. Shale oil and uranium developments have helped Region VI. Further aluminum refining expansion has occurred in Region VII, and additional auto assembly plants have been located in Region VIII. The construction industry has been booming and as a result cement plants have been constructed all over the country. - 11 -

The preceding discussion of industrial developments is in no wise complete and it is possible that major omissions have been made. Also there have been many small plant expansions which are rarely noted, except possibly in publications strictly local in circulation. Actually, the average industrial establishment is quite small. When the Federal Power Commission was making detailed studies of industrial energy requirements during World War II, it found that 21,000 establishments out of a total of around 250,000 accounted for better than 90 percent of over-all requirements. The large majority of plants have loads of less than 100 kilowatts. On the other hand, a good many plants have loads of 20,000 kilowatts and more. Demands of the biggest steel factories may run 200,000 kilowatts; the biggest aluminum refinery in the United States requires about 400,000 kilowatts; and the biggest atomic energy plants require 2,000,000 kilowatts. 

From an electrical energy requirements standpoint the primary metals industry is the largest even larger than the atomic energy facility group. The aluminum refining plants, which use some 30 billion kilowatt-hours annually, are the biggest power users in the metals group. Chemicals are next to metals in appetite for electricity, and paper and pulp mills also require tremendous amounts of power.

Year after year industry makes more power available to each worker as output, increases. Back in the Census year 1929 the average production worker, including those engaged in both manufacturing and mining, used about three kilowatthours every hour he worked. In 1955 he used four times as much or approximately 12 kilowatt-hours every hour. Naturally the amounts vary depending upon the type of industry, with workers in atomic energy and aluminum refining plants, for example, using much more than the average. Leaving out atomic energy plants, the typical worker used 10 kilowatt-hours per hour of work, and eliminating aluminum refineries as well the amount drops to 9 kilowatt-hours. In

As important as electric power is, however, it represents for the most part a small portion of the total cost of production. In 1953, the latest year for which information is presently available, cost of electricity in manufacturing represented about two percent of the total cost as measured by those items included in Bureau of the Census data on value added by manufacture. paper and allied products industries the amount was close to five percent and in tobacco plants it was less than one-half of one percent. The latter figures represent the range for the major industry groups. Going to individual industries, the amounts would be higher in some and lower in others. Although cost of electricity is generally a small portion of the total, it may represent a substantial figure when compared with profit margin, and is therefore of concern to industry in selecting plant locations. Of still greater importance is power availability, for without adequate supplies of electric power no area can expect to attract industrial developments.

It is obvious that industry will spare no effort to use more electricity if output can be increased by so doing. New applications for electricity in industrial operations are being found every day. Electricity is used not only by motors, furnaces, electrochemical processes, and electrometallurgical processes but also for infra-red curing, ultrasonics, precipitrons, static devices, numerous high-frequency applications, and even in nut-cracking which is now being accomplished by means of an electric charge. Electricity, needless to say, will never replace the human brain, but with increasing automation in industry it appears to be coming. pretty close to doing so in certain operations. 

Altogether industry is expected to buy about 808 billion kilowatthours from electric utilities in 1980 as compared with 257 billion in 1955.
