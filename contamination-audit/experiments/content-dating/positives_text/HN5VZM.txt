FOREWORD 

"Swim and you won't sink!" This advice, given by an old sea dog to the Navy recruits, applies to all who would learn to swim. Swimming will keep you from sinking. Know that and follow the directions in this book, which inspire confidence as well as give definite instructions.

This series has assisted thousands of men and women, as well as boys and girls, actually to swim with no other aid than these lessons. 

Master them and your muscles will be trained to a response even before you go into the water. Your confidence will be established and from that point you will be able to move in the water under your own power. That is swimming.

Miss Margaret Hamilton, by her skill in directing, has taught thousands to swim in an incredibly short time.

CAN SWIM The best way to learn to swim is just to make some kind of a start. The most difficult part for many people is that of making a beginning, so just make up your mind that you are going to learn and then get at it, even if it's dry land swimming. -9-

When once you have practiced bubbling in a basin and floating in the bath tub, perfected your crawl stroke supported by the family ironing board, and made long, graceful, sweeping back strokes along the dining room rug, you may progress to diving off the foot of the bed on to the mattress, or you may take to some of the nearby lakes and pools to try out your indoor skill! 

Open your mouth and inhale, drawing the air down into the deepest parts of your lungs. Close your mouth and hold your breath for four or five seconds, then exhale through your nose. 

In all of the breathing exercises you have had, if you have taken voice training, you have learned to breathe correctly by inhaling through the nose and exhaling through the mouth.

In swimming the breathing is just reversed. When water gets into your mouth, you can spit it out, but when just a little water collects on the tiny -10-

After you have practiced this method of breathing until you do it comfortably and rhythmically, you have taken your first step toward swimming and are ready to progress to the water. If there is no pool or lake at hand, you do not have to stop here, for the next part of the lesson can be taken. 

Just get a basin of water, inhale through your mouth, put your face in the water, and exhale through your nose, bubbling the water. Try to exhale evenly, without jerks or puffs, and when your lungs are comfortably empty, raise your head and inhale again through your mouth. Repeat until you can do this ten or fifteen times without strain or hesitation.

If you are not yet interested enough to have hunted up any more water than your wash basin holds, then you'll want to practice dry land floating. The first and easiest is the face float. Lie flat on the floor or bed, face down, arms extended straight ahead, feet together, - 13-

and head raised a little. Inhale deeply through your mouth, lower head to floor, resting its weight on your forehead, and open your eyes. 

Hold your breath as long as possible, then exhale slowly and evenly through your nose. This is the correct form and procedure for the face or "dead man's" float, and it is the easiest and simplest way of keeping your equilibrium in the water. 

Raise your head so that you are looking forward and notice the strain on the muscles in the small of your back and the pressure of your feet on the floor. So far you have been swimming with the position of your head such that it would be possible to take only short dashes if you were in the water, as your face would be submerged. You remember how your feet pressed on the floor when you tried raising your head while you were learning the face float. If you - 25-

were in the water this would mean that your feet would sink if you raised your head to breathe. 

Take a pencil in your hand, holding it in a horizontal position. Raise the point very slightly and notice that the eraser end goes down in exact proportion. Although your spine is more flexible than a lead pencil, yet when you attempt to raise your head in the crawl, it stiffens up and acts just as the pencil does and your feet go down.

Now take the pencil and roll it in your fingers so that the side which is down is up. This can be done without changing its horizontal position. Thus can your head be rolled upward allowing you to breathe, without making your feet go down. Don't you wish you were in the water?

The next stroke you are to attempt is the reverse crawl or racing back stroke.

YOUR BODY IS A BOAT 

Now your face is up, and when you progress to the water, you will enjoy being able to breathe and watch where you are going. The weight of your head is on the upper part of the back of your neck, and your chin is tipped down against your chest so that you are looking toward your feet. This position of your head allows you to ride the water like the prow of a boat, pushing the water in waves to the side instead of splashing it up over your face and interfering with your regular breathing. 

Your arms reach backward and pull in just the opposite manner of the face crawl. Some prefer pulling the arms up at the sides (with the palms out and the little fingers up) until reaching the chest, when they are swung back over the water as the reach above the head is made, while others prefer a lifted swing from the end of the pull back into the water above the head. 

Whichever way seems to be the most -33-

natural and give you the best results in the pull is for you to judge and adapt to yourself, as the important part is the relaxation of your arms and the ease with which they work. The kick is made with knees relaxed and power coming from the hip, with a slight accent upon the upward stroke of the extended feet. 

You will find this one of the speediest of strokes. For women, an excellent hip exercise is just to float on your back and practice this foot kick. When combined with the constant reaching above the head and pressing the water toward the feet, it adds a twist and becomes a strenuous hip, abdominal and shoulder exercise. 

1. Keep your chin tipped. 

2. Keep your arms relaxed. 

3. Avoid throwing water in your face as your arms swing by. 

4. Accent upward stroke of kick. 

5. Breathe deeply and regularly.

Take your side float position on your right side. Draw your left hand up along your left side with your thumb touching your side and your fingers pointing toward your feet. At your chest, push your hand across toward your right armpit with fingers leading, then take a long sweep, with your palm down, across the front of your body back to the starting position, and rest. 

Take this in four counts. 1-up! 2across! 3-push to position! 4-rest! 

Keep your thumb dragging against your body throughout this stroke.

BREAST STROKE REQUIRES COORDINATION 

The breast stroke is an excellent stroke for floor practice, as its coordination is difficult and should be fixed in your mind before trying it in water. 

Take your face float position. Stretch your neck forward, resting the weight of your head on the tip of your chin, and place the backs of your hands together with your thumbs down. Pull your arms directly out to the side with your elbow straight, then bend your elbows and bring your hands together -47-

on your chest. The palms are turned outward and the hands slide forward until the elbows are straight and the arms extended in front of you again.

It is most important to count for this stroke, using four counts. 1-pull arms sideward. 2-bend elbows. 3-slide hands forward. 4-rest and float with arms extended ahead of you. Your feet use the same frog kick that you learned with your resting back stroke-up, out, together, and rest. 

To combine the arms and legs in four counts, take your starting position again. 1-pull arms sideward, keep legs still. 2-bend elbows and knees.

If you are still a dry land swimmer, it is about time to try your skill in water, and a depth of about three feet is preferable for a beginning. Walk with your hands under water and extended a little ahead of you, so as to be able to steady yourself and regain - 52

your balance should you slip or step into a hole. In practicing your floating and your strokes, always walk out and then head in toward shallow water so you will not be in danger of getting beyond your depth before you are able to take care of yourself. 

To start your face float, bend your knees, extend your arms forward on the water, put your face in the water, and glide forward by straightening your knees and pushing gently with your feet. To get on your back from a standing position, stoop down until your shoulders are under water, get a deep breath, lay your head back on the water, arch your back and straighten out. Keep breathing!

To regain a standing position from a face float, raise your head, pull sharply downward with both arms, draw both knees up against your stomach, then lower your feet together and stand up. 

Do these three things all together. — 55-

From the back float position, the quickest and most dependable method for a beginner is just to turn over on your face and get up.

The correct and more difficult recovery is to sit up in the water, draw your knees up against your stomach, force your face down into the water in front of your knees and, swinging your arms upward and backward over the water, scoop forward just as if you were picking up a big log, as you shoot your feet down and take a standing position. If you stagger backward, swing your arms and scoop again until you steady yourself. 

If you are going to use any artificial aids, a small wooden plank, three feet long, one foot wide, and one and onehalf inches thick, smoothed off and oiled, is one of the most simple, inexpensive and useful devices. OFF THE DIVING BOARD 

If you have ventured into the water and practiced your last lesson; if you have had the thrill of feeling the water buoy you up as every muscle relaxes and you paddle lazily along in the sunshine then you've already thought how much fun it would be to dive. 

It is fun, and you can make a start right at home. You can learn some of the fundamental points which, if practiced, will later become parts of even 

-59-

the graceful swan dive or the agile jackknife. But if they are not practiced, they will lead to those flat dives which smack both you and the water. 

The most important part of diving is the position of your head and, for the beginner, it must be held well down with your arms over the tops of your ears, your hands stretched forward to guide you and to protect your head.

Your feet are always together, with toes pointed, for a good entry into the water.

Get on both knees in a low chair and sit back on your feet, your face down on your knees and arms stretched forward. Let the weight of your head pull you forward until you unfold enough to rest your hands on the floor. Practice until you get the feeling of the part that your head plays in pulling you forward, and the necessity of your arms staying forward to protect it.

When trying this in the water, be -60-

OFF THE DIVING BOARD 

sure that you do not try to jump or to force yourself. Just relax and unfold as your head pulls you forward and you take a slanting drop. To come up from under water, just arch your back so that your shoulders and arms are reaching up toward the surface. If you have no board or steps to practice from, get on your partner's back and push off. You may progress from this to pushing off with one foot from a kneeling position. 

When standing and diving, be sure that the water is at least six feet deep. 

Stand with arms straight above your head and, keeping both knees straight, tip your trunk forward and downward, raising one leg backward and upward as your head pulls you on into the water. Do not jump-just tip. 

Now, if you want to spring, stand on the board or some support above the water surface, with your arms straight forward at shoulder height, palms down, head erect. Swing arms slowly -63-

downward and backward as you bend your knees, then as you swing your arms forward toward the water, lower your head down under your arms and, by pushing with your toes, lift your feet together into the air, knees straight, and enter the water as if you had to go over a hurdle in front of you without touching it. 

1. Go slowly! 

2. Keep your arms extended ahead of you to guide and protect. Always have palms downward and, if necessary, lock your thumbs to keep your arms from spreading as you enter the water. 

3. Never dive into water until you have investigated to see that the depth is sufficient. 

4. The position of your head is the key to all diving. 

5. Exhale through the nose and bubble slowly if the water gets in your nose. 

6. Go slowly! -64-
