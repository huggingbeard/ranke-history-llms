PREFACE AND ACKNOWLEDGMENTS 

The power and scope of the district attorney in Pennsylvania and the nature of the work of the office has its authority so scattered throughout the various statutes and common law that a newly elected district attorney, and even one who has been long in office, may frequently have need of a book at hand wherein can be quickly obtained authoritative information necessary for an emergency decision. The handbook herein presented is a compilation of such legal authority used in the course of the conduct of the office. 

Too much credit cannot be given to the men who have done so much to make this book possible. To 

John E. Flynn, David E. Groshens and Victor J. 

Roberts, assistant district attorneys, I owe an immeasurable debt for the work they have done. Their meticulous and careful work has been reflected, I feel, in the pages of the handbook. We were insistent upon the applicability of cases to the authority for statements made, and I am deeply grateful to them for their careful labors in preparation of the material. 

The section dealing with magistrates is borrowed extensively from the ably prepared pamphlet written by Guy DeFuria, assistant district attorney of Delaware County under the direction of the district attorney of that county, William B. McClenachan. The chapter devoted to coroners is taken, practically unchanged, from a treatise on that subject prepared by David E. Groshens, assistant district attorney. Gilbert P. High, a former assistant district attorney, also prepared material that is included in the book. 

It will be noted that the Index is large. This was felt necessary because if a manual is to have any practical [iii]

I feel that there is a need for the book among lawyers engaged in criminal trial work, magistrates, coroners, and district attorneys. If it succeeds only in permitting the correct answer to one of those three o'clock in the morning calls from a distressed police officer engaged in solving on the scene a knotty problem, or, from a sleepy but conscientious magistrate who has before him a decision as to a person's liberty, it will have accomplished much, and we who have prepared the handbook will be satisfied.

INTRODUCTION 

A district attorney is a good public servant so long as he remembers the quasi-judicial character of his office. He should oppose the improper conviction of a citizen and zealously prosecute the law-breakers. He should keep clearly in mind that he is the sole protection of the innocent against tyrannical abuse of police power and the most efficient source of power to curb lawlessness and corruption. He should never allow his influence to be exerted as an arm of any political party but should consider it his responsibility to be divorced from partisanship as completely as the court. 

A fearless, fair, honest district attorney compels good government: a dishonest one makes good government impossible. [v]

HANDBOOK FOR DISTRICT ATTORNEYS IN PENNSYLVANIA CHAPTER I HISTORY OF THE DISTRICT ATTORNEY'S OFFICE IN PENNSYLVANIA 

Originally, all criminal investigations and prosecutions within the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania were conducted by the attorney general or his duly appointed deputies. The office of the district attorney, as such, was unknown at common law and did not come into existence until after the various courts of the state had been organized and was not an independent separate office until nearly seventy-five years after the independence of the United States.

In the early days of the Commonwealth, while Pennsylvania was under the Crown, through the charter to William Penn, it is probable that Crown officers conducted the criminal affairs of the Commonwealth. In the royal charter granted to Penn on March 4, 1681, and in the conditions agreed upon by William Penn on July 11, 1681, the criminal proceedings followed those of England, and possibly the criminal docket was handled by an officer similar to the Crown officer. 

(Charter and Laws of the Province of Penna., Ed. 

1879.) 

Very early in Pennsylvania history, however, we find the attorney general mentioned as the prosecuting officer for the people. The provincial fee bill, passed 

November 27, 1700 (2 Stat. at L. 137, 138), provided for the payment of a fee to the attorney general by the prisoner. Thus, the title "Attorney General" was [1]

in current use in 1700 (Report of Crimes Survey Committee, 1926), although as late as 1847, Justice Gibson said, in Commonwealth v. Burrell, 7 Pa. 34 at 38, "That the name 'Attorney General' was used proves nothing; for, as we have no master of the crown-office, it was necessarily used in all cases, just as it will have to be used in all cases under our statute.'

It is thus intimated that the title of attorney general need not necessarily have been created prior to the date when it appears in reports. It may have been merely an accepted designation for the state's legal officer or, as suggested by Justice Gibson, a substitute for the title of "Crown Officer." However, even in 

1764, before the Articles of Confederation of 1777, we find the term "Attorney General" used: "Moved on the part of the defendant to oblige the attorney general to bring on the trial, or discharge the defendant. The court said they would not force the Crown to bring on the trial nor discharge the defendant from bail without some appearance of oppression." King v. Haas and others, 1 Dall. 9.

The title and office of district attorney were created by the Act of May 3, 1850, P. L. 654, Section 1, wherein it is provided: 

The qualified voters of each and every county in the state, shall *** every three years *** elect one person, learned in the law, who has been two years admitted to the bar, and who shall have resided in the county for which he is elected for one year next preceding his election, who shall be called the district attorney of said county;"

This Act of 1850 marks the first change of the control of the criminal proceedings from the attorney general to a local officer of the county. The Act of 

1850 provides that the person who is elected "shall be called the district attorney."

Certain parts of the act were repealed and amended in so far as they concerned different counties by the Acts of 1852 and 1853, but the entire statute was reënacted throughout the Commonwealth in the Act of 

April 26, 1883, P. L. 15. Of course, this followed the

Constitution of 1874 which made the district attorney's office a constitutional office. In 1874, the Constitution, in Article XIV, Sec. 1, provided for the establishment of certain county offices, as follows: "County officers shall consist of sheriffs * district attorneys, and such others as may from time to time be established by law. * 

The Act of May 2, 1929, P. L. 1278, Art. III, Secs. 

245 and 246, provides: "At the municipal election preceding the expiration of the term of the district attorney of any county, and quadrennially thereafter, the electors of such county shall elect a person to hold the office of district attorney, from the first Monday of January next succeeding his election, for a term of four years, and until his successor is elected and qualified." (Sec. 245.) "The district attorney shall be a person learned in the law, who has been qualified for admission to practice as an attorney in the Supreme or Superior Court of this Commonwealth for at least one year and six months preceding his election, and who shall have resided in the county for 2

which he is elected for one year next preceding his election. "No district attorney shall be eligible to a seat in the Legislature, or to any other office under the laws and Constitution of the State, excepting an office or commission in the militia of the State, during his continuance in office." (Sec. 246.)

The statutes are singularly lacking in any definition of the duties of a deputy attorney general, as well as of the attorney general himself, although as has been seen, the latter seems to have been a recognized officer prior to 1700. The power of the district attorney is that of deputy attorney general, limited by the boundaries of his election district, viz., the county. The hereinafter enumerated duties of the attorney general are the only statutory prescription of the obligations of the district attorney, as provided by the aforementioned Act of 1850.

There seems to be little difference between the early powers exercised by the attorney general and those of the present district attorney in so far as their respective criminal duties and responsibilities are concerned. Since all of the power of the district attorney originates with the powers and the duties of the deputy attorney generals, we must examine the general duties of the attorney general, as determined by the Acts of Assembly and historical precedent, to ascertain what the district attorney may and may not do. 

Of course, the Department of Justice, over which the attorney general presides, conducts all of the legal business of the Commonwealth. This office, then, differs from the district attorney's office in that the district attorney is solely involved with criminal matters. Solicitors for the county and the township perform that branch of the attorney general's function which concerns collection of taxes and legal advice to

The localizing of the office was probably made necessary by the increase of population, which in the United 

States jumped from 3,929,214 in 1790 to 23,191,876 in 1850, with Pennsylvania continuously the second largest state in the United States, and also by the congregation of masses of the population in many widely separated communities. It was physically impossible for a state officer to assume the detailed watchfulness so vital to criminal prosecution and moral welfare when the towns and cities were growing so rapidly on so scattered a frontier. Therefore, the delegation of the criminal power of the attorney general to local attorney generals, called district attorneys, was ac complished by the aforesaid act. 

In this connection, it is interesting to note that the president judge in the district having jurisdiction of any criminal proceeding in the Commonwealth may request the attorney general to intervene and to send an attorney to supersede the district attorney in any county, to investigate, prepare and bring to trial the case to which he may be assigned. (Act of April 9, 1929, P. L. 177, Art. IX, Sec. 907.) This act appears to indicate that the supreme authority to investigate and to prosecute crimes rests with the attorney general. The only prerequisite for the attorney general to supersede the district attorney in the investigation, prosecution and trial of a case in any county in the 

Commonwealth, is the written request of the president judge of the county having jurisdiction of the criminal proceeding, directed to the attorney general, requesting the appointment of a special attorney to represent the Commonwealth, because, in the judgment of the president judge, the case is a proper one for the Commonwealth's intervention. Though the attorney ap-

pointed by the attorney general supersedes the district attorney, yet "He shall take the oath of office required by law to be taken by district attorneys, and shall be clothed with all the powers and subject to all the liabilities imposed upon them by law." 

(Act of April 9, 1929, P. L. 177, Art. IX, Sec. 

907, 71 Purdon Pa. Stat. Ann., Sec. 297.) 

In Commonwealth v. Lehman, 309 Pa. 486, 492 (1932), the court said: "Prior to the Act of May 3, 1850, P. L. 654, 16

P. S. Section 1691, the attorney general was represented in each county by his deputy who conducted criminal prosecutions; by that statute the office of district attorney was created and that officer was charged with the performance of the duties theretofore performed by the deputy attorney general. Thereafter, the prosecutor was elected instead of appointed, but the power of general supervision vested in the attorney general over the performance of a district attorney's duties in the county was not taken away; that power remained, and it is matter of general information that the power has been exercised from time to time when necessary." 

However, the attorney general may supersede the district attorney without proceeding under this act, and hence, without any request by the president judge, In Commonwealth ex. rel. Minerd et al. v. Margiotti, 325 Pa. 17 (1936), it was flatly held by the Supreme 

Court that the attorney general may, upon his own motion, supersede any district attorney whenever in his judgment such action is necessary for the proper enforcement of the criminal laws of the Commonwealth.

7 Hence, it is obvious that the attorney general is the supreme authority in the Commonwealth over public legal affairs and particularly over the enforcement of the criminal law. Since the district attorney is now charged with the performance of the duties formerly performed by the deputy attorney general, it follows that, within his own county, he wields the same authority that the attorney general has over the Commonwealth at large. It would consequently appear that the district attorney could, in case of necessity, supersede any police chief, magistrate or lesser criminal authority if, in his opinion, this was necessary for the proper investigation of crime or for the proper enforcement of the criminal law.

The Legislature and the Constitution do not prescribe the powers of the district attorney. He has the powers of the crown offices and the attorney general except where his authority has been specifically limited by legislation or by decisions or by natural county boundaries.

his election or appointment and who is a resident of such county. CHAPTER III DUTIES AND RESPONSIBILITIES OF THE DISTRICT ATTORNEY A. General Power to Investigate Crime 

As the Supreme Court has pointed out, in Commonwealth v. Lehman, 309 Pa. 486, at 491 (1932), "As the Constitution does not prescribe the duties of the district attorney, it has been held that the Legislature may regulate the performance of the duties of the office. ***'' 

Since the Legislature and the Constitution do not prescribe the powers of the district attorney, he must have the powers of the crown offices and the attorney general except where his authority has been specifically limited by legislation or by decisions or by natural county boundaries. It is obvious that the district attorney can have no authority beyond the county borders within which he was elected. Within that county he represents the people and the affairs of the Commonwealth. He, subject to the general supervision of the attorney general, is the supreme authority in all criminal matters, including offenses affecting the morals and safety of the people. 

He has the power: 

1. "To investigate any violations or alleged violations of the laws of the Commonwealth which may come to its notice; 

2. "To take such steps, and adopt such means, as may be reasonably necessary to enforce the laws of the Commonwealth." 

(Act of April 9, 1929, P. L. 177, Art. IX, Sec. 

904; 71 Purdon, Sec. 294.) [ 29 ]

And, of course, by an earlier Act, the district attorney has power to: 

66*** sign all bills of indictment, and conduct in court all criminal or other prosecutions in the name of the Commonwealth, or when the state is a party, which arise in the county for which he is elected, and perform all the duties which now by law are to be performed by deputy attorney generals, and receive the same fees or emoluments of office; Provided, said district attorney shall in no case whatever have authority to enter nolle prosequi in any criminal case, either before or after bill found, or to discharge a prisoner from custody, without first having obtained the approbation of the court in writing." (Act of May 3, 1850, P. L. 654, Sec. 1.) 

1. Certain special powers designated: 

Certain specific sources of difficulty which the district attorney has express power to investigate may be enumerated as follows: 

(a) Dance halls. (Act of May 11, 1927, P. L. 968, Sec. 4; 53

Purdon, Sec. 19084.) 

(b) Enjoin as a nuisance the use of premises for illegal sale of liquor. (Act of Nov. 29, 1933, P. L. 15, Sec. 608 as amended; 47 Purdon, Sec. 744-608.) (c) Desertion and nonsupport. (Rotan's Petition, 23 Pa. Dist. 110.) (d) Juveniles. (Act of June 2, 1933, P. L. 1433, as amended 

June 15, 1939, P. L. 394; 11 Purdon, Secs. 243268.) (e) Protecting public justice and morals.

One of the most useful powers of the district attorney, and yet used too infrequently, is the power to

prevent any acts which tend to corrupt public morals or shock the public conscience. Under the authority to protect public justice, a district attorney has the power to see that all public officials keep within the bounds of public ethics. Many public prosecutors cry, "What can I do?" The answer is clearly set forth by all the authorities as the power to indict any person who acts contrary to public morals. A broad term and a powerful instrument for the public good. 

The question arises, what is "public morals?" The common law is broad enough to punish a public official who conducts himself in an improper manner. The charge is usually malfeasance in office, or obstructing justice. As was said in 16 C. J., p. 65: "In addition to these specific crimes, the common law is sufficiently broad to punish as a misdemeanor, although there may be no exact precedent, any act which directly injures or tends to injure the public to such an extent as to require the state to interfere and punish the wrongdoer, 

injuriously affecting the public safety, health or comfort, or tending to injuriously affect the morals of the community or shock its sense of decency; and acts constituting or directly tending to an obstruction or a perversion of public justice or of the administration of the government.' 

In Commonwealth v. McHale, 97 Pa. 397, 409 (1881), the court quotes with approval the following, from 

Bishop's Criminal Law: 

"The government requires its subjects to do more than simply abstain from attempting its overthrow. It requires them to give, when called upon, their active assistance to it, and at all times to refrain from casting obstructions in the way of its several departments and functions. Therefore, every violation of these duties, being sufficient in magnitude for the law to regard, is criminal."

And at p. 410: "We are of opinion that all such crimes as especially affect public society are indictable at common law. The test is not whether precedent can be found in the books but whether they injuriously affect the public policy and economy. "It needs no argument to show that the acts charged in these indictments are of this character. 

They are not only offenses which affect public society, but they affect it in the gravest manner. An offense against the freedom and purity of elections is a crime against the nation. It strikes at the foundations of Republican institution. Its tendency is to prevent the expression of the will of the people in the choice of rulers and to weaken the public confidence in elections."

It would seem from the above that if the character of the offense charged in the particular indictment is such as to shock the public sense of decency and would tend to weaken the public confidence in popular government it is obviously indictable at common law. 

Where the act which tends to injure the public morals is performed by a public officer, the officer may be indicted for the common law offense of malfeasance in office. Com. v. Miller, 94 Pa. Super. 499 (1928). (f) Jury tampering or embracery.

A district attorney may, on occasion, be confronted with the unpleasant experience of having a defendant acquitted because of jury tampering. Embracery is not sufficiently punished in Pennsylvania because it may well become the lesser of evils for a defendant and the temptation is too great to avoid. The law is becoming well defined as to the power to prosecute for this crime, and its artful guises can all be covered by a prosecutor who follows the cases. In order to aid in the protection of a district attorney against the use

of embracery to avoid convictions, a general survey of the situation is here related. 

Embracery is defined in the Act of March 31, 1860, P. L. 382, par. 13 (reënacted, Penal Code, June 24, 1939, P. L. 872, Sec. 308, 18 Purdon, Sec. 4308) as follows: 

"If any person shall attempt to corrupt or influence any juror in a criminal or civil court, or any arbitrator appointed according to law, by endeavoring, either in conversation or by written communication, or by persuasion, promises or entreaties, or by any other private means, to bias the mind or judgment of such juror or arbitrator, as to any cause pending in the court to which such juror has been summoned, or in which such arbitrator has been appointed, or chosen, except by the strength of evidence or the arguments of himself or his counsel during the trial or hearing of the case, he shall be guilty of a misdemeanor, and on conviction, be sentenced to pay a fine not exceeding five hundred dollars, or suffer an imprisonment not exceeding one year, or both, or either, at the discretion of the court."

Referring to the historical note on the above Act found in 18 Purdon, p. 30, the offense under the Act of 1860 "is that known at the common law as embracery, which consists in any attempt whatsoever to corrupt or influence a jury in a case beforehand, or in any way to incline them to be more favorable to the one side than to the other, by money, promises, letters, threats or persuasions, except only by the strength of the evidence and the arguments of counsel, in open court, at the trial of the cause. 

In view of the foregoing, it seems appropriate at this point to refer to several cases in which the common law offense of embracery is defined. 

"It is said in the 3d volume of Bacon's Abridgement, page 785, 'Embracery is defined in general

to be an attempt by either party, or a stranger, to corrupt or influence a jury, or to incline them to favor one side by gift or promises, threats or persuasions, or by instructing them in the cause, or any other way except by opening and enforcing the evidence by counsel at the trial, whether the jurors give any verdict or not, and whether the verdict be true or false. Also, it is an offence of this kind for a stranger basely to labor a juror to appear and act according to his conscience, or for any person to labor a juror not to appear. 

Com. v. Crans, 2 Clark (Pa. Law Journal Rep.) 

441, 456. 999 

See also, Com. v. Kauffmann, 1 Phila. 534. 

And in Com. v. McBride, 14 Pitts. L. J. (Old Series, Vol. 31) 482 (1884) it is said: "The evidence tends to show the defendant, in the interest of the prisoner Malone, who was about to be tried for murder, visited many of the jurors summoned and asked their politics, religion and views on temperance. To some he said he was a court detective, to others a sheriff's officer, and that there was some difficulty about that particular jury which caused him to do this. To others he spoke of the particular case about to be tried. 

Others he urged to appear and serve. * * * The wrong, if any, is not in the mere gathering of the information, but it is in the defendant, in the interest of Malone, seeking it of the jurors. 

The statute defines embracery to be the 'attempt to corrupt or influence any juror in a criminal or civil court *** by endeavoring, either, in conversation or by written communication, or by persuasion, promises or entreaties, or by any other private means, to bias the mind or judgment of such jurors *** as to the cause pending in the court to which such juror has been summoned.' 

The questions addressed to the jurors as to their views on religion, politics and temperance, would to many men serving on juries seem like appeals ** *

to them to aid prisoners of like religion, politics or views on temperance. To some men the representations that a court detective was asking such questions would have a tendency to cause them to lean against the Commonwealth, especially if they were of the same religion, politics and views on temperance as the prisoner. They would believe that the district attorney wished to exclude them because of their views on these subjects from the jury. Judge Parsons, in his opinion in the case of Commonwealth v. Cranch, 2 Clark, 441, quotes this from Bacon's Abridgement: 'Also, it is an offense of this kind-embracery-for a stranger basely to labor a juror to appear and act according to his conscience, or for any person to labor a juror not to appear.' He makes similar quotations from Viner's Abridgement and Coke on Littleton. *** If the Defendant knew they were jurors, and knowingly used language calculated to influence their verdict, the offense is complete." 

More recent cases indicate that the following statements, when addressed to a juror, furnish ground for an indictment for embracery: "If you can do anything for the traction company it will be appreciated.” 1 

Houser's Case, 57 Pa. Super. 43 (1914). 

"the defendant 'asked me to do what I could for 

Alderman Kline * * * and use my influence; that 

Herr was a bad actor. * * * He was in the slot machines.'"' 

Com. v. Riley, 113 Pa. Super. 77 (1934).

The most troublesome question raised in embracery cases formerly revolved around whether or not solicitation to commit embracery is a crime. This question has been settled in Pennsylvania by the case of Com. v. Wiswesser, 134 Pa. Super. 488 (1938), in which the court held conclusively that it was, saying (p. 494): "There are few offenses that more directly affect the public economy and welfare than em-

bracery. 'Embracery is an offense striking at the very foundation of civil society': 20 C. J. 496.

It is a grave and serious offense tending to undermine the very foundations of organized society and government of which the administration of law and justice is an essential part. "We again hold that a solicitation to commit a serious misdemeanor harmful to the public peace or the public welfare or economy is a common law offense in this state and that only such misdemeanors as by their nature make it unreasonable or illogical to treat them as a separate crime are excluded as an object of solicitation. In Smith v. 

Com., supra, the Supreme Court gave a number of practical reasons for excluding a solicitation to commit adultery or fornication. "Our conclusions are supported by many cases in other jurisdictions, as a reference to 35 A. L. R.

They are also indictable where their object is interference with public justice.' "There is not any sound reason why solicitation to commit embracery should not be an indictable offense and there are strongly persuasive reasons for holding to the contrary. The law has always condemned any improper contacts with citizens called for jury duty, and if the evil is to be corrected the law should reach that interference at its earliest stage. B. Prepare All Criminal Prosecutions for Trial 

It seems clear that if the trial in court of a criminal prosecution is to be successful, the district attorney's preparation of the case must of necessity include everything from the initial investigation of the nature of the offense, with the apprehension and arrest of the guilty, to the final presentation of the Commonwealth's evidence of the record of the convicted man awaiting sentence by the court. 

1. Assign cases for trial: 

The function of the district attorney should include preparation of the trial list and assignment of the cases to the various courts. Because of his knowledge of all the circumstances, the district attorney is better able than the court to determine the length of time that the trial of a case will take. He likewise is better informed as to the availability of witnesses. This system is now used to advantage in several counties of the Commonwealth. (Cf. Moschzisker, Trial by 

Jury, Sec. 118.) 

2. Incur expenses of prosecution: 

Many times a district attorney may find himself confronted with the opposition of officials who do not, for one reason or another, wish the district attorney to incur expenses in connection with the prosecution of crime. The purse strings can become a barrier to efficient prosecution so as to prohibit a district attorney from properly functioning. On such occasions, the public generally cannot understand why the district

attorney did not do this or that and he is impotent to proceed. The law is adequate in granting the power to the district attorney to incur "necessary expenses, provided he insists upon a court interpretation of the few statutory words of his authority. A discussion of this power of the district attorney may be helpful here. 

The Act of June 29, 1923, P. L. 973, Sec. 1, provides that all necessary expenses incurred by the district attorney in the investigation of crime and the apprehension and prosecution of persons charged with or suspected of crime are to be paid by the county upon approval of the bill of expense by the district attorney and the court. There is no provision for approval by the controller. 

In Commonwealth ex rel. v. Irvin, 110 Pa. Super. 

387 (1933), a special attorney to represent the Commonwealth was appointed by the Attorney General, on the written request of the president judge. The compensation was fixed at $750 by the Attorney General. 

The bill was approved by the county commissioners, and also by two judges of the court. The county controller refused to pay the bill. 

The controller's answer set forth that the appointment was not necessary and he further denied that the Attorney General had the right to require the county to pay the bill. The court reasoned that the necessity of the appointment was a matter to be decided upon by the president judge. It further said that in Commonwealth ex rel. Greer v. Wertz, 251 Pa. 241, it had decided that when two grand juries had recommended and approved alterations to the court house and when their recommendations had been approved by the court, that the controller had no discretion to refuse to advertise for bids on the ground that he did not approve the expense or necessity of the work. 

The court went on to say that the discretion vested in the controller is not an arbitrary but a legal one, and when he refuses to act it must be made to appear

that the case was within his discretion and that his discretion was exercised in obedience to law. He cannot substitute his discretion for that of the court. 

The court then concluded that the discretion for the necessity of the appointment of a special attorney was vested in the Attorney General. It concluded by saying that since the controller did not question the regularity of the appointment, there was no merit in his objection on the ground of necessity. Hence, the bill was ordered paid. 

In Pipa v. Kemberling, 126 Pa. Super. 289 (1937), (aff'd 326 Pa. 498), the Superior Court decided that the county is not liable for the compensation paid to a special prosecutor, but rather that this expense must be borne by the state treasury. The court distinguished 

Commonwealth v. Irvin (supra) by saying that the decision in that case had merely been to the effect that the county controller may not refuse to pay a bill which the court had approved and the county commissioners had ordered paid, even though the action of these officials had been erroneous. In short, although in the first case the controller's contention that the county should not be held liable was correct as a matter of law, yet he could not be allowed to raise this question because the bill had been approved by the officials in whom the sole discretion was vested by the legislature. 

A lower court case directly in point is In re Noakes et al., 24 D. & C. 39 (1935). The district attorney of

Cumberland County ordered autopsies on three bodies in the investigation of a murder. Bills totaling $997.75 were presented by the various doctors, etc., and approved by the district attorney. The county commissioners refused to pay the bill, and in their answer they pointed out that under the Act of 1931, P. L. 60, it is provided that no county of the sixth class shall be liable for the payment of more than $25 as the cost of an autopsy in any case being investigated by the coroner.

It was held that the commissioners must pay the bill. The court said that the district attorney had approved the bill and that under the Act of 1923, P. L. 

973, he had authority to incur bills in the investigation, apprehension and prosecution of crime, that this autopsy was as a result of an investigation by the district attorney and not by the coroner, and hence, that the court felt that it must order the bill paid. 

Clearly then, it would seem that the district attorney is authorized to incur any bill which he feels necessary in the investigation, the apprehension or the prosecution of crime. He is the person who is to decide upon the necessity of incurring the expense. The only check upon his discretion is the provision that the court must approve the bill. The court could only refuse its approval in the event that the expenditure was clearly improper. However, once the court has given its approval, the controller must pay and he can no longer question either the advisability, necessity or the legality of the bill presented. 

3. Sign all bills of indictment: (Act of May 3, 1850, P. L. 654, Sec. 1; 16 Purdon, Sec. 3431.)

It has been held that the fact that the signature of a district attorney to a bill of indictment is not written with his own hand, but by an assistant, is not a ground for quashing the indictment, where it appears that the signature was affixed by the express direction of the district attorney, who in person laid the bill thus signed before the grand jury and by other unequivocal acts avowed his individual and official responsibility therefor. Commonwealth v. Brown, 23 Pa. Super. 470 (1903). However, in so deciding, Judge Rice pointed out that the court should not be understood as commending the practice or as holding that the duty of signing bills of indictment imposed upon the district

attorney by law may be performed by a clerk or deputy under a general authorization by the district attorney to sign his name.

A district attorney may draw a bill of indictment charging a higher grade of crime than that charged by the committing magistrate where there are facts to support it. Nicholson v. Commonwealth, 96 Pa. 503 (1879). 

When a bill of indictment is presented to a grand jury, the district attorney should see that the names of all witnesses who are to be called before the grand jury, are endorsed upon the bill. Failure to do this may result in the bill being quashed. *** where a grand jury is considering a formal indictment laid before it by a district attorney it may not swear and examine any witness whose name has not been 'marked' thereon by the district attorney." Com. v. Brownmiller, 137 Pa. 

Super. 261, 268 (1939). 

4. Submit all bills of indictment to the grand jury:

The district attorney is not to try the case before the grand jury but only to present all of the evidence most favorable to the Commonwealth and to give to the jurors such benefit of the law as they may request. 

Commonwealth v. Bradney, 126 Pa. 199 (1889); Maginnis's Case, 269 Pa. 186 (1921).

During the deliberations of the grand jury, the district attorney should withdraw. However, if, in fact, he is present, this of itself is not sufficient to invalidate the indictment. It has been held that in the absence of any participation of the district attorney in the deliberations of the grand jury or of effort on his part to influence their finding, his mere presence in the jury room during their deliberations is not ground for quashing the indictment. Commonwealth v. Bradney (supra). Likewise, it has been stated, by way of dictum, that the district attorney's presence during the

voting by the grand jury would not of itself invalidate the indictment, although the grand jury always has the right to request the district attorney to withdraw during their deliberations if he attempts to remain. 

Commonwealth v. Hegedus, 44 Pa. Super. 157 (1910). (a) Presence of stenographer in grand jury room: 

At times it is necessary to have a stenographer in the grand jury room to take the notes of testimony for the use of the district attorney. Originally, in accordance with the traditional view of preserving absolute secrecy in the grand jury room, it was held to be ground for quashing the bill of indictment if an unauthorized person, such as the district attorney's stenographer, was in the grand jury room while testimony was being taken. Commonwealth v. Kositchy, 17 Dist. 778 (1907). However, the Superior Court, in an opinion by Judge Rice, has taken a more practical view, and it is now held that the mere presence in the grand jury room of a stenographer, employed by the district attorney, under a statute authorizing him to employ a stenographer "as an assistant in his office," and the taking down in shorthand by such stenographer, for the use of the district attorney, the evidence upon which an indictment is returned, does not invalidate the indictment, in the absence of anything tending to show that the stenographer was present when the grand jury deliberated or voted upon the bill, or that the stenographer participated in the proceedings in any other way than by taking the notes of testimony, or that the accused was injuriously affected thereby. Commonwealth v. Hegedus (supra).

It has likewise been held that the testimony before the grand jury may subsequently be used at the trial before a petit jury to discredit a witness. In Commonwealth v. Gordon, 92 Pa. 216 (1880), it was held that on the trial of a case before a petit jury, the foreman of the grand jury could be called to discredit the tes-

timony of a witness by disclosing how the witness testified before the grand jury. This case has been relied upon by the Superior Court in more recent times to hold that a witness in a trial before a petit jury may be impeached by interrogating him as to how he testified before the grand jury, and if his testimony is inconsistent a grand juror may be called as a witness to contradict him. Commonwealth v. Fotti, 93 Pa. 

Super. 365 (1928); Commonwealth v. Carr, 137 Pa.

Super. 546 (1939). 

(b) Presence of assistant district attorneys in grand jury room: 

The Act of May 16, 1929, P. L. 1794, Sec. 1, 16 Purdon, Sec. 3433, provides: "From and after the passage of this act, the district attorney, or any assistant district attorney, in third class counties of this Commonwealth, may be present during the presentation of all matters to the grand juries in their sessions, in third class counties of this Commonwealth, to lay before them matters upon which they are to pass, to aid them in their examination of witnesses."

It might be argued that the use of the word "or" in the act instead of "and" grants the right to be present only in the alternative-i. e., either the district attorney or any assistant, but not both at once, may be present in the grand jury room. This act has never been before any court for interpretation or construction.

However, the most reasonable view of the Legislature's intention would seem to be that it meant to grant to all the assistants a privilege coextensive with that which was formerly limited to the district attorney and the first assistant district attorney (by a Luzerne County decision construing the Act of June 10, 1911, P. L. 868). In the Governor's approval of the

Act of 1929 he recognized that its purpose was to set aside the effect of Commonwealth v. O'Donnell, 21 Luz. 

66 (1920). In that case it was decided that the provision in the Act of 1911 vesting the first assistant district attorney with all the duties, powers and privileges of the district attorney "in the absence of the district attorney from the jurisdiction, or when the district attorney is unable to perform the duties of his office through sickness," had the effect of allowing the first assistant to attend the sessions of the grand jury only in the absence or sickness of the district attorney and that it did not accord any such privilege to any other assistant. Therefore, when the Legislature, with the intention of setting aside the effect of this decision, says, in the Act of 1929, flatly and without qualifications, that the district attorney or any assistant may be present during the presentation of all matters to the grand juries, the privilege thus extended to any assistant district attorney would not seem to be dependent on the presence or absence of the district attorney or of any other assistant.

But even independently of any statute, the view has been taken in one Superior Court case that the mere presence of an additional assistant district attorney and his making remarks in the grand jury room are harmless and entirely proper. Commonwealth v. Garletts, 81 Pa. Super. 271 (1923). (c) Presence of detectives or other unauthorized per

It seems clear that to allow the presence of detectives in the grand jury room is at least a very dangerous practice, even if not necessarily a fatal irregularity in every case. In Commonwealth v. Carlucci, 48 Pa.

Super. 72 (1911), the Superior Court made the pronouncement that while the mere presence of an unauthorized person in the grand jury room during the taking of testimony is not necessarily and under all

circumstances fatal to the indictment, its effect depending upon the circumstances of the particular case, yet it is competent for the lower court to receive evidence which would justify it in sustaining a motion to quash; and hence it was held that the appellate court has no right to find that the quashing of an indictment upon such ground is based on legally insufficient reasons. (The report of the same case in the lower court, 15 Luz. 167, shows that the county solicitor, as well as detectives and other interested parties, were present and conferred with the jurors both inside and outside of the grand jury room.)

And if a detective or any other member of the district attorney's staff is also a witness in the case, it will be fatal to the indictment to allow him to be present in the grand jury room at any time other than when he is testifying. 

In Commonwealth v. Stetzka, 26 Dist. 460 (1917), aff'd 69 Pa. Super. 15, it was held that only one witness may be present before the grand jury at a time. 

If a witness be sworn and examined by the grand jury and then remain while another witness is sworn and examined in the same case, an indictment based on such evidence will be quashed. An interpreter may be appointed by the court to act for the grand jury, but if the interpreter is also a witness in the case, the indictment will be quashed. 

5. District attorney's bill: 

A district attorney, with the permission of the court, may prefer an indictment before the grand jury without a preliminary hearing or previous commitment of the accused. This may be done even after a previous indictment by the same officer has been ignored by a grand jury. The employment of this procedure, however, can only be justified by some pressing and substantial necessity. Rowand v. Commonwealth, 82 Pa. 405 (1876).

6. Notification of time and place of trial:

The district attorney of the county wherein the preliminary hearing was held shall, before the time fixed for the trial, mail postal card notice or notices, of the time and place fixed for such trial, to at least one of the persons whose names appear upon the transcript, to the address or addresses given thereon. (Act of 

July 10, 1935, P. L. 643, No. 228, Sec. 2; 19 Purdon, Sec. 30.)

This requirement is supplementary to the duty of justices of the peace, imposed by Section 1 of the same statute, by which they must ask any person held for the action of the grand jury to give the names and addresses of any persons whom he or she desires to be notified of the time of the trial and must note the same upon the record and make this a part of the transcript returned by the justice of the peace. 

7. Power to exhume:

The district attorney has an inherent power and a positive duty to exhume dead bodies when he believes in his own best judgment that such is advisable or necessary. In Commonwealth v. Grether, 204 Pa. 203 (1902) the court said: * * "The rights and duties of the coroner were not involved in the examination of the body, which was made at the instance and under the direction of the district attorney for the purpose of being able to submit to the jury * evidence*

Such evidence it was the right as well as the duty of that officer to procure, if it existed, without regard to anything the coroner may have done or omitted to do. ** *"" 

The right to exhume is so necessary a part of the office of district attorney that the cases which treat of it dismiss the objections to such a power with language which indicates it would be a neglect of duty for

the district attorney to fail to exhume when to do so would permit better presentment of the case. 

8. Procedure in change of venue:

When a change of venue has been granted a defendant by the court (either lower or appellate), the district attorney of the new venue is responsible for the trial of the case, either directly or by appointment of a special prosecutor. 

Commonwealth v. Deutsch, 72 Pa. Super. 298 (1919).

(3) Practice and procedure. 

The application for the requisition must be made by the district or prosecuting attorney for the county or district in which the offense was committed, and must be in duplicate, original papers or certified copies thereof.

The following must appear by the certificate of the district or prosecuting attorney: (a) The full name of the person for whom extradition is asked, together with the name of the 5

(d) That the person named as agent is a proper person, and that he has no private interest in the arrest of the fugitive. (e) If there has been any former application for a requisition for the same person, growing out of the same transaction, it must be so stated, with an explanation of the reasons for a second request, together with the date of such application, as near as may be. (f) If the fugitive is known to be under either civil or criminal arrest in the state or territory to which he is alleged to have fled, the fact of such arrest and the nature of the proceedings on which it is based must be stated. (g) That the application is not made for the purpose of enforcing the collection of a debt, or for any private purpose whatever, and that if the requisition applied for be granted, the criminal proceedings shall not be used for any of said objects. (h) The nature of the crime charged, with a reference, when practicable, to the particular statute defining and punishing the same. (i) If the offense charged is not of recent occurrence, a satisfactory reason must be given for the delay in making the application. 

In all cases of fraud, false pretenses, embezzlement or forgery, when made a crime by the common law, or any penal code or statute, the affidavit of the principal

complaining witness or informant that the application is made in good faith, for the sole purpose of punishing the accused, and that he does not desire or expect to use the prosecution for the purpose of collecting a debt, or for any private purpose, and will not directly or indirectly use the same for any of said purposes, shall be required, or a sufficient reason be given for the absence of such affidavit. 

Proof by affidavit of facts and circumstances satisfying the executive that the alleged criminal has fled from the justice of the state, and is in the state on whose executive the demand is requested to be made, must be given. The fact that the alleged criminal was in the state where the alleged crime was committed at the time of the commission thereof, and is found in the state upon which the requisition was made, shall be sufficient evidence in the absence of other proof, that he is a fugitive from justice. 

If an indictment has been found, certified copies, in duplicate, must accompany the application. 

If an indictment has not been found by a grand jury, the facts and circumstances showing the commission of the crime charged, and that the accused perpetrated the same, must be shown by affidavits taken before a magistrate (a notary public is not a magistrate within the meaning of the statutes), and that a warrant has been issued, and duplicate certified copies of the same, together with the returns thereto, if any, must be furnished with the application. 

The official character of the officer taking the affidavits or depositions and of the officer who issued the warrant must be duly certified. 

Upon the renewal of an application, for example, on the ground that the fugitive has fled to another state not having been found in the state on which the first was granted, new or certified copies of the papers in conformity with the above rules must be furnished.

In the case of any person who has been convicted of any crime, and escapes after conviction or while serving his sentence, the application may be made by the jailor, sheriff, or other officer having him in custody, and shall be accompanied by certified copies of the indictment or information, record of conviction, and sentence, upon which the person is held, with the affidavit of such person having him in custody, showing such escape, with the circumstances attending the same.

No requisition will be made for the extradition of any fugitive except in compliance with these rules. 

At the Interstate Extradition Conference, held in New York, in August, 1887, it was resolved by the representatives of the several states "That it is the sense of this conference that the Governors of the demanding states discourage proceedings for the extradition of persons charged with petty offenses, and that, except in special cases, under aggravating circumstances, no demand should be made in such cases." 

Requisitions will not issue in cases of fornication and bastardy, desertion (except under special and aggravated circumstances), nor in any case to aid in collecting a debt or enforcing a civil remedy, nor in cases in which the offense is of such trivial character as to leave a doubt as to the issuing of a mandate thereon, by the executive of another state or territory; nor in a case of seduction, until an indictment is found and the relations of the parties clearly established, so as to leave no doubt that the case is one of seduction, and not of fornication and bastardy. 

Requisitions will not be issued on petition alone, but the copies of the record and affidavits required by the preceding rules must in every case be furnished; and this regulation will be applied with special strictness in all cases where the charge is cheating, obtaining money by false pretenses, embezzlement and the like.

C. Prosecute All Criminal Charges Regularly Returned 

1. Prosecutions by private counsel:

If any district attorney, within this Commonwealth, shall neglect or refuse to prosecute, in due form of law, any criminal charge regularly returned to him, or to the court of the proper county; or if, at any

stage of the proceedings, the district attorney of the proper county, and the private counsel, employed by the prosecutor, should differ as to the manner of conducting the trial, it shall be lawful for the prosecutor to present his or her petition to the court of the proper county, setting forth the character of the complaint, and verify the same by affidavit; whereupon, if the court shall be of the opinion that it is a proper case for a criminal proceeding or prosecution, it shall be lawful for it to direct any private counsel, employed by such prosecutor, to conduct the entire proceeding, and where an indictment is necessary, to verify the same by his own signature, as fully as the same could be done by the district attorney. (Act of March 12, 1866, P. L. 85, Sec. 1; 16 Purdon, Sec. 3432.) 

However, special counsel should not be permitted actively to participate in conducting prosecutions except in extreme cases, it being the duty of the district attorney to do so. If one is so appointed, it is the duty of the court and the district attorney to see that he observes the rules of conduct required of the regular prosecuting officer. Commonwealth v. Cicere, 282 

Pa. 492 (1925). 

2. Trial of cases: 

In the conduct of his office, the district attorney should ever remember that he is not a persecutor but a quasi-judicial officer who represents the people of the Commonwealth. He should be as zealous in guarding the rights of the accused as he is in seeking punishment of offenders. Convictions are no test of the ability of the district attorney, for as the Supreme 

Court said in Commonwealth v. Williams, 309 Pa. 529, 535 (1932): "The district attorney is a quasi-judicial officer, representing the Commonwealth, which seeks no victims, but only justice; that, since he is invested with these grave responsibilities, he should, at all

times, conduct the Commonwealth's case fairly, present it in an impartial manner, and avoid seeking to influence the jury by arousing their prejudices. * * *11 

In the trial of cases, his duties and responsibilities have been plainly stated in Commonwealth v. Stein, 103 Pa. Super. 198 (1931), as follows: 

"The Commonwealth's officer should not seek to inflame the jury by referring to matters not in evidence, or by drawing deductions from the evidence which are not strictly legitimate or fairly warrantable." 

A district attorney may: (a) draw all legitimate deductions from the evidence; (b) impress the jury with the importance of a just verdict; (c) point out the consequences of an erroneous one.

D. To Advise and Assist Coroner 

Because the coroner's office is so deeply imbedded in the early common law, and, since the district attorney's office functions closely with this office a separate chapter has been devoted to the subject. Chapter V of this handbook treats in detail the relationship of the coroner's office with the district attorney's office. 

E. To Advise Local Magistrates and Police Officers 

Every phase of the closely knit activities of the public prosecutor's office, from the time of the offense up to the sentence of the criminal, must be within the full control and disposition of the district attorney, if his office is to function to the best interests of the people. 

A district attorney is entirely dependent upon the coöperation of the local township and borough police as well as the various magistrates. No matter how

honest or efficient a district attorney and his staff may be, the link in the administration of the criminal law upon which he is most dependent in order to properly conduct his own office is the minor judiciary. The magistrates in counties other than the first class are the officials who are responsible in the greatest degree for the ultimate success or failure of the prosecutor's office. Not only in the handling of trials but in the preparation of transcripts and the taking of bail does the magistrate conduct the vital phases of a successful district attorney's work. So vital is the proper handling of the cases by magistrates for the district attorney that it is thought necessary to specifically cover the situations raised in a separate chapter of this book. 

Chapter IV relates in detail the problems of tran scripts, bail, and other matters relating to the joint offices.

F. Vacancy or Removal of District Attorney 1. Filling of vacancies: 

If any vacancy shall occur, either by death, resignation, removal from office or from the county, or otherwise, the judges of the court of the common pleas shall supply such vacancy by the appointment of a competent person to fill the office during the balance of the unexpired term. 

247; 16 Purdon, Sec. 247.) 

2. Removal of district attorney for misconduct: 

(a) Misdemeanor in office: 

(1) Nature of offense: 

If any district attorney shall, wilfully and corruptly, demand, take or receive any other fee or reward than such as is prescribed by law, for any official duties required by law to be executed by him in any criminal

59 proceeding, or, if such district attorney shall be guilty of wilful and gross negligence in the execution of the duties of his office, he shall be guilty of a misdemeanor in office, and, on conviction thereof, be sentenced to pay a fine not exceeding one thousand dollars, and to undergo imprisonment not exceeding one year, and his office shall be declared vacant. 

248; 16 Purdon, Sec. 248.) 

(2) Procedure and disposition: 

Upon complaint in writing, verified by the oath or affirmation of the party aggrieved, made to the court in which any district attorney shall prosecute the pleas of the Commonwealth, charging such district attorney with wilful and gross negligence in the execution of the duties of his office, the court shall cause notice of such complaint to be given to the district attorney, and of the time fixed by the court for the hearing of the same. 

If upon such hearing the court shall be of opinion that there is probable cause for the complaint, they shall hand over or commit the district attorney to answer the same in due course of law. If the court shall be of the opinion that there is no probable cause for such complaint, they shall dismiss the same with reasonable costs to be assessed by the court. 

(Act of May 2, 1929, P. L. 1278, Art. III, Sec. 

248; 16 Purdon, Sec. 248.) (3) Appointment of special attorney to prosecute district attorney:

If any district attorney is charged according to law with any crime or misdemeanor before, or bound over or committed by, any court to answer for wilful and gross negligence in the execution of the duties of his office, it shall be the duty of the court to appoint some competent attorney thereof to prepare an indictment against such district attorney, and to prosecute the

same on behalf of the Commonwealth until final judgment. Such attorney shall be paid by the county for his services a reasonable compensation to be fixed by the court. 

249; 19 Purdon, Sec. 249.) 

(b) Conviction of other crimes: 

If such district attorney shall be convicted of any crime, for which he may be sentenced to imprisonment by separate or solitary confinement at labor, in addition thereto, his office shall be declared vacant by the court passing such sentence. 

(Act of May 2, 1929, P. L. 1278, Art. III, Sec. 

249; 16 Purdon, Sec. 249.) 

(c) Effect of disbarment: 

A district attorney cannot be removed from office because of disbarment as an attorney or by virtue of disbarment proceedings. Snyder's Case, 301 Pa. 276 (1930); see also Maginnis's Case, 269 Pa. 186 (1921).

CHAPTER IV  IN CONDUCT OF OFFICE 

As was indicated in Chapter III, subdivision E, page 57, the magistrate's office is one with which the district attorney must be in ever constant communication and cach should coöperate with the other. Neither can function properly without the aid and help of the other. Discussed herein are some of the main points of mutual interest to the magistrate and the district attorney.

It should be understood that the following points are only discussed from a broad cursory standpoint and do not pretend to be exhaustive. A recent book of instructions and suggestions to justices of the peace and aldermen in Delaware County, prepared by Assistant District Attorney Guy G. deFuria under the direction of William B. McClenachan, Jr., February 

15, 1938, is an excellent treatise on the subject and everything used here concerning magistrates has been largely taken from that source. It is quoted more fully because frequently a district attorney is called upon by conscientious magistrates for information regarding these all-important subjects and the district attorney may have need of a ready reference.

A. Issuance of Warrants of Arrest 

1. Complaint under oath:

It is the duty of a magistrate to issue a criminal warrant as soon as a sworn complaint, charging the violation of the criminal law is made to him. But no right [61]

or duty to issue a warrant exists in the absence of a complaint under oath, as the law specifically provides: "No warrant ✦✦✦ to seize any person shall issue without describing them as nearly as may be, nor without probable cause, supported by oath or affirmation, subscribed to by the affiant." 

Const., Art. I, Sec. 8. 

The complaint furthermore, must charge the commission of a crime at a certain time and place. It is sufficient if the complaint gives the accused fair notice of the charge against him and the time and place where it was committed. Com. v. Carson, 166 Pa. 179 (1895). 

The complainant or prosecutor must assume the responsibility of the charge and must state in the complaint that he believes the charge to be true or true to the best of his knowledge, information and belief, although the name of the informant need not be stated. 

Com. v. Green, 185 Pa. 641 (1898).

Warrants issued for acts which are not crimes are void and render both the arresting officer and the magistrate liable. Magistrates sometimes mistakenly issue warrants charging "suspicion" or "suspicious character." There is no such crime in our law and the warrant is void. If the name of the accused is unknown, this fact should be stated in the complaint and he should be described by a statement of his personal appearance, his occupation, age, place of residence or other pertinent circumstances. Address of John C. Bell, 13

Dist. 815, 819 (1904). The desire to protect the community or to bring a criminal to justice may urge the apprehension of someone on a charge of this nature or on a blank or imperfect complaint and warrant, but the public is best served by strict enforcement of the criminal law, regardless of the need of the moment.

2. Arrest without warrant: 

Where a person has been arrested without a warrant, the accused should be taken immediately to a magistrate and a sworn complaint made against him.

In all cases the complaint under oath should be filed with the magistrate before the preliminary hearing. 

3. Warrant on Sunday: 

A magistrate may not issue a warrant on Sunday unless the offense charged is a felony or breach of the peace. A breach of the peace means a disturbance of public order by an act of violence, or an act likely to produce violence, or an act which by inciting terror or fear, disturbs the peace and quiet of the community. 

Com. v. Orlick, 26 Del. Co. 107 (1936). 

4. Search warrants: 

The same provision of our Constitution forbidding the issuance of a warrant without a complaint under oath, requires that before a magistrate may issue a search warrant, there must be presented to him a complaint in writing, and under oath, describing the place to be searched and the articles to be seized, as nearly as may be. Const., Art. I, Sec. 8. The complaint and the warrant should name or describe the accused and the place as definitely as possible. Search warrants may be issued where there is specific statutory authority as in the following cases: Liquor violations, search and seizure of stolen goods, gambling establishments, indecent literature, seizure of articles to procure abortion, etc. The officer seizing the articles must give a receipt for them to the owner or person in possession and should immediately deliver the articles to the office of the district attorney. The return to the search warrant should be attached to the transcript.

5. Who may serve warrant:

The warrant may be directed to and served by any proper person. Com. v. Keeper, 1 Ash. 183; Com. v. 

Warden, 8 Dist. 159 (1898).

A private citizen need not do so but an officer is bound to act when so requested. However it is suggested that this be done by a constable or police officer whenever possible.

B. The Hearing 

The hearing before the magistrate should be held as soon as possible and concluded as soon as possible. 

A continuance should not be granted except for a good reason noted in the docket and record of the case. 

The accused should be present at all stages of the hearing, unless he is a fugitive, in which case this fact should be noted on the transcript. 

The testimony of the prosecutor should be heard first, followed by the testimony of other witnesses for the prosecution. The accused should be given the right to cross-examine by himself or attorney.

The accused need not himself testify and his silence may not be considered evidence of guilt. Com. v. Zorambo, 205 Pa. 109 (1903).

The Act of May 14, 1915, P. L. 499, makes it the duty of the magistrate, if the accused shall so desire, to hear the testimony of the accused and any witnesses in his behalf, except in prosecutions for murder, manslaughter, arson, rape, mayhem, sodomy, buggery, robbery or burglary. In these serious cases it is in the discretion of the magistrate to hear testimony for the defense. Cr. Surv. Rep. 172. As noted hereafter, the magistrate must enter into a full hearing in surety of the peace and assault and battery cases. 

In prosecutions other than for summary conviction offenses, the purpose of the hearing before the magis-

trate is to ascertain whether the testimony for the prosecution, with any explanation offered for the defense, makes out against the accused a prima facie case of guilt. "Prima facie" means at first view, or at first glance. The magistrate is to take the testimony for the prosecution as given. It is not his province to pass upon the credibility of the witnesses as this will be for the jury. Respublica v. Davison, 4 Yeates 125 (1804).

C. Cases Which Should Be Disposed of by the 

Magistrate

Great expense and needless inconvenience is caused by the return to court by magistrates of cases of trifling importance which may, and should be, finally disposed of by the magistrate. If a complaint has been properly filed and a warrant issued, whether or not the accused is held for court has nothing to do with the magistrate obtaining his costs. Where a prima facie case is not shown, the accused should be discharged, and a transcript setting forth the facts, sent to the controller for the collection of costs. In every proper case, they should be paid. Brown v. Luzerne Co., 36 D. & C. 638 (1939). 

1. Surety of the peace:

The law specifically provides that in surety of the peace cases, the magistrate must make an effort to induce the parties to compromise their differences. 

Com. v. Hooks, 23 D. & C. 206 (1935). If the case is returned to court, the transcript must show: (a) That the magistrate suggested propriety of compromising the differences of the parties. (b) That the magistrate entered into a full hearing and investigation of the facts. (c) That the prosecutor was in actual danger of being hurt in body or estate.

(d) That the accused made threats maliciously and with intent to do harm.

If the case is not returned, the magistrate may place the costs on either prosecutor or defendant or may divide them. (Act of March 18, 1909, P. L. 42, 19 Purdon, Sec. 24-26.) 

2. Assault and battery:

No case of simple assault or simple assault and battery should be returned to court, unless the magistrate may not otherwise dispose of it after a full hearing and suggestion of settlement, and the magistrate is satisfied that the prosecution is reasonably well founded. 

In these cases the magistrate is required to enter into a full hearing, and the transcript to show this. 

Furthermore, the law provides, in an effort to have these cases disposed of by the magistrate, that the costs may be placed on either the prosecutor or the defendant or divided between them. (Act of May 27, 1919, P. L. 306, 19 Purdon, Sec. 21, 22.) While this act also provides that the magistrate may impose the costs on the county, this should be done only under impelling circumstances. 

3. Assault and battery by automobile:

Strictly speaking, there is no such offense as assault and battery by automobile. The offense is assault and battery whether committed by fist, brick, stone, club or automobile. At every term of court, there are cases returned by magistrates which are not properly criminal cases at all. Not every automobile collision in which some person is injured should result in the driver or drivers being held for court on a criminal charge. The great majority of these cases are entirely of a civil nature, and the parties should be relegated to that

forum for the redress they seek. Before a case of this nature is returned to court, the magistrate should be satisfied not only that someone was injured but that the accused was guilty of gross negligence, wantonness or maliciousness in the operation of his automobile. 

The Superior Court in Com. v. Coccodralli, 74 Pa. 

Super. 324 (1920), held that one who wilfully drives an automobile on the public streets at a rate of speed or in a manner which involves a reckless disregard for the safety of other persons lawfully using the streets, and by so doing causes injury to another, is guilty of assault and battery.

D. Cases Which May Be Settled 

The law provides that some cases may be settled by the parties involved before the magistrate. Unless the law permits the settlement of a criminal charge by some provision of the criminal code, settlement thereof by the magistrate is a misdemeanor in office. The pertinent statute is the Act of March 31, 1860, P. L. 

427, Sec. 9, as amended by Act of April 11, 1929, P. L. 

514, Sec. 1, which provides that the magistrate, before or after hearing, unless the case has been returned to court may permit settlement of the following cases: 

1. Larceny of goods worth less than one hundred dollars; 

2. Assault and battery, except when upon or by any officer or minister of justice, or when charged to have been done with intent to commit a felony; 

3. Any other misdemeanor, except when committed by or upon any officer or minister of justice, provided: (a) The misdemeanor is one which has caused personal injury and damage to the complainant and is one for which the complainant may bring civil suit;

(b) The complaint does not charge the misdemeanor to have been committed with intent to commit a felony; (c) The misdemeanor is not an infamous crime.

Where the misdemeanor is a crime against the complainant solely as one of the public, or where the accused is charged with intent to commit a felony, or where the misdemeanor is an infamous crime, under no circumstances may settlement be permitted. Infamous crimes are offenses which involve the element of falsehood, and include all offenses which have a tendency to injuriously affect the administration of justice by the introduction of falsehood and fraud. 

Com. v. Schambers, 110 Pa. Super. 61 (1933). Infamous crimes include forgery, embezzlement, perjury or subornation of perjury, bribery, malfeasance, conspiracy to interfere with justice, etc. 

The charge of driving an automobile while under the influence of intoxicating liquor may not be settled because it is not a misdemeanor for which the prosecutor might have brought civil suit. No felony may be settled before a magistrate except the charge of larceny of goods worth less than one hundred dollars. 

The approval or disapproval of the settlement lies in the discretion of the magistrate. Com. v. Radzinowicz, 39 Pa. Super. 173 (1909). Cases involving known criminals should not be settled. The magistrate's docket should show all the facts. Where cases are settled in accordance with this act, the magistrate must look to the parties for his costs. If the transcript has already been sent in, the magistrate should immediately notify the district attorney in writing of the settlement.

E. Bail

The right to bail is entirely statutory and unless the power to admit to bail in criminal proceedings is inherent in the judicial office or is expressly authorized by statute, it does not exist. Com. v. Hinsdale, 85 Pa. 

Super. 238 (1925). The bail bond must be in writing.

The right to be released on bail is one of the rights of freemen secured to the accused in Pennsylvania by statute and constitution from the earliest times. The 

Act of March 31, 1860, P. L. 427, Sec. 7, 19 Purdon,

Sec. 51, which is now in force, provides that all persons charged with any crime or misdemeanor shall be admitted to bail, except such persons precluded from being bailed by the constitution. Bail is an absolute right of the defendant excepting in capital offenses and the proper officers are bound to admit to bail when so requested. Com. v. Keeper, 2 Ashmead 227 (1838).

The Act of April 9, 1915, P. L. 76, Sec. 1, 19 Purdon, 

Sec. 54, 55, provides that the arresting officer upon the application of any citizen must issue a certificate stating the name of the prisoner, the charge upon which he is held, and the amount of bail demanded, if the same has been fixed. Refusal to do so constitutes a criminal offense. 

1. Magistrates may take bail: 

Justices of the peace and aldermen are authorized by the provisions of the Act of March 31, 1860, P. L. 

427, Sec. 7, 19 Purdon, Sec. 51, to take bail in any criminal case except the following: Murder, manslaughter, arson, rape (including statutory rape); mayhem, sodomy, buggery, robbery, burglary. In these cases, the magistate must hold the accused for court without bail. A recognizance taken by a magistrate for one of these offenses is void. Com. v. Phillips, 2

U. S. Law. Mag. 316.

2. Warrant by another magistrate:

From the wording of the Act of 1860, it would appear that a magistrate may take bail in a proper case, although the proceedings are pending before another alderman or justice of the peace. However, the Act of 

April 21, 1915, P. L. 145, Sec. 1, was passed specifically to give the right to magistrates in the City of Philadelphia to release on bail persons committed for a hearing by another magistrate. The question arises, therefore, why it was necessary to pass the Act of 1915 if under the Act of 1860 all magistrates already had that power. The unwritten legislative inference would seem to be that except in Philadelphia, this right does not exist. In the absence of a decision otherwise, the better practice would seem to be for magistrates, except in the cases of real hardship, to refuse to take bail where the proceedings are pending before another magistrate.

This does not apply to cases where the warrant is issued in one county and served in another. In such case, any magistrate may take bail conditioned for the appearance of the defendant in the court of the county where the warrant issued. Of course, as in all cases where the magistrate is asked to take bail, the offense must be one which is bailable before a magistrate. The transcript and recognizance should be sent immediately to the clerk of the court of the county where the offense was committed and the warrant issued. (Act of March 

31, 1860, P. L. 427, Sec. 3, as amended May 2, 1899,

P. L. 173, Sec. 1, 19 Purdon, Sec. 3.) See Opinion of Atty. General, 27 D. & C. 462 (1936). 

3. Security: 

The power to fix bail, accept the recognizance and permit the substitution thereof, is a judicial power, and a magistrate has the right to exercise reasonable discretion with respect to all matters in connection

71. therewith. Com. v. Miller, 105 Pa. Super. 56 (1932).

The power is an important one and must be judicially exercised. Its abuse is a violation of the oath of office since magistrates "become criminally liable if they fail to require sufficient bail. It is their duty to inquire into the solvency of the surety no less than to inquire into the sufficiency of the complaint." Sadler, Criminal Procedure, 186.

The statutes specify only "sufficient sureties" and give no other instructions as to the nature of the security, excepting to authorize cash bail. Cash bail is permitted by the Act of April 7, 1921, P. L. 118, Sec. 1, 19 Purdon, Sec. 73, providing for a deposit in cash with the magistrate in lieu of bail in all criminal prosecutions wherein the magistrate has the right and authority to take bail. When cash is accepted, it becomes the immediate duty of the magistrate to return the money to the clerk of court. Failure so to do renders the magistrate liable for the full amount received. 

See Cummings v. Mecleary, 24 Del. Co. 499 (1934). A certified check is not cash.

It is suggested that unless approved corporate bail is offered, the magistrate should require: (a) Actual production before him of a deed to the bondsman for real estate in the county of jurisdiction; (b) A sworn justification by the bondsman, disclosing all liens, judgments, mortgages, etc., against the property; (c) That there be sufficient equity in the property to constitute good security.

The magistrate should remember moreover, that the person becoming bail must be one competent to contract. A minor should not be accepted as bail, nor a married woman. The law specifically provides that a

married woman may not enter into a contract of suretyship, which is exactly the nature of a bail bond. It is for this reason that a husband and wife may not offer real estate held in both names as security. The bail bond would be void as to the wife and the security would be beyond the reach of process. Com. v. Matyiewicz, 17 Pa. C. C. 154 (1895).

However, a widow or divorced woman may become bail, provided the security is in her own name. If real estate is in the names of husband and wife, and one has since died, the survivor being the sole owner, is competent to offer the property as security. 

4. Bail on Sunday:

The Act of March 24, 1937, P. L. 105, Sec. 1, 19 Pur don, Sec. 58, provides that in all bailable criminal cases, including desertion or nonsupport and surety of the peace, bail may be, and if offered, shall be accepted and entered on Sunday. Formerly, there was some question about the validity of bail taken on Sunday. Now, in any case in which a magistrate is authorized to take bail, the entry of bail on Sunday is proper. 

5. Automobile accident death cases:

In cases of accidental death, especially in automobile collisions, the problem arises as to the disposition of the driver or drivers. In practice the driver is arrested or surrenders himself to the police. He is confined in the police station until the magistate holds his next hearing or unless the police officer in charge voluntarily permits him to go on his promise to be present at the hearing. After the hearing, regardless of the circumstances of the accident, there is nothing for the magistrate to do except to hold the defendant for the action of the coroner, or in a clear case, for the next term of court. The prisoner can terminate his imprisonment pending the coroner's inquest only by being

admitted to bail on petition for a writ of habeas corpus because magistrates cannot take bail in manslaughter cases. The result of this procedure is that often a driver who without blame has accidentally caused the death of another, is likely to spend some time in jail awaiting the holding of the coroner's inquest. However, no one should be held for the coroner or for court in a manslaughter case, or for that matter in any case, unless a complaint or information charging some specific crime has been made against him. In the absence of such complaint or information, the justice of the peace or alderman has no right to hold the defendant in jail or otherwise, nor to issue a warrant.

Conner v. Com., 3 Binney 38 (1810). In these cases it is suggested that the magistrate wait until after the coroner's inquest before holding his hearing, as the facts developed may warrant discharge of the accused without returning the case to court. 

6. Taking bail after transcript returned to court: 

The magistrate is seemingly given authority to take bail in a proper case after the defendant has been held for court and the transcript returned, provided it is before the beginning of the term to which the defendant has been held to appear. Com. v. Lamar, 32 Pa. Super. 200 (1906). The practice, however, is a poor one for a district attorney to encourage because of the conflict with clerks of courts. The magistrate's recognizance must be conditioned for the appearance of the defendant at the term "to be holden next after the taking thereof." (Act of May 22, 1722, 1 Sm. L. 

131, Sec. 4.) 

7. Taking bail after defendant committed: 

If the defendant has been committed to jail, the magistrate may take bail and issue a discharge for the defendant, provided the next term has not been reached. Com. v. Basendorf, 153 Pa. 459 (1893).

8. Taking bail for continued hearing:

The magistrate may not only hold to bail for appearance at the next term of court, but in cases where he has power to take bail, he may also take bail conditioned for appearance before him, from time to time, pending the examination and conclusion of the preliminary hearing. Sadler, Criminal Procedure (2d ed.) 

164. Continuances for mere delay should be refused and the preliminary hearing concluded as soon as possible.

F. Material Witnesses 

The power of committing witnesses to prison on the ground that they are material, in default of bail, is one frequently misunderstood and abused. The early 

Act of 1 and 2 Phillip and Mary, 1554, gives the right to justices of the peace and coroners to bind all witnesses by recognizance or obligation "as do declare anything material to prove" the offense. The Act of 

March 31, 1860, P. L. 427, Sec. 56, 19 Purdon, Sec. 651, however, restricts this old Act clearly and prohibits the commitment to prison of any witness willing to enter into his own recognizance to appear, unless the case is triable exclusively in the Oyer and Terminer and unless a positive oath is made in writing, setting forth sufficient reasons or facts to induce the firm belief on the part of the magistrate that the witness will fail or refuse to appear at the trial. Unless, therefore, the case is exclusively triable in the Oyer and Terminer and unless such an affidavit is made, the magistrate has no authority to exact more than the recognizance of the witness as bail. The offenses which are triable exclusively in the Oyer and Terminer are: murder, manslaughter, treason, sodomy, buggery, rape, robbery, arson, mayhem, burglary, concealing death of bastard child, kidnapping to extort money, and certain second offenses. (Act of March 31, 1860, P, L. 427,

Sec. 31; Act of July 2, 1901, P. L. 605, Sec. 1, 17 Purdon, Sec. 391, 393.)

The following portion of the opinion of President 

Judge Arnold in the case of In re Engelke, 26 Pa. 

C. C. 363 (1902), at p. 365, is informative on this subject: 

"The procedure to hold a witness to appear and testify is regulated by the penal code above quoted, which provides that a positive oath shall be made, reducing to writing and signed by the deponent, setting forth sufficient reasons or facts to induce the firm belief on the part of the judge, magistrate or alderman that any witness will abscond, elope or refuse to appear upon the trial, before bail can be demanded or the witness committed. This oath must be returned to court with the transcript in order that the judge as well as the magistrate may determine its sufficiency, if an application is made for the discharge of the witness, or, if necessary, the party who made the oath may be sent for and examined."

G. Return of the Transcript 

As soon as the hearing is terminated, the magistrate should prepare and file his transcript, in cases where the accused is held for court, with the district attorney or clerk of courts. The law provides that this must be done immediately in cases of desertion and nonsupport and surety of the peace. In cases of felony, the magistrate is given five days in which to return his transcript. Act of June 11, 1885, P. L. 110, 42 Purdon, Sec. 722. While there is no specific provision in other cases, it is the duty of the magistrate as stated in many decisions to prepare and file his transcript immediately so that the district attorney may proceed with preparation of indictments and for trial. 

The Act of May 8, 1854, P. L. 678, 42 Purdon, Sec. 721, provides that the original recognizance or bailpiece

must be returned at least ten days before the next criminal term. Failure of the magistrate to do so may cause needless delay and the detention of persons who might otherwise have their cases heard and disposed of on pleas of guilty and otherwise.

CHAPTER V THE OFFICE OF CORONER IN RELATION TO THE DISTRICT ATTORNEY 

By reason of the mutual dependence of the district attorney and coroner, it seems advisable to comment on the powers and duties of the coroner at some length. It is hoped that the following discussion will answer questions which may involve the district attorney by reason of an exclusion of a member of the bar from asking questions at the coroner's inquest, primary authority in dealing with deaths, and the effect of a coroner's jury decision.

A. History of Coroner's Office in England 

To understand the coroner's office of today it is necessary to know of its origin and evolution. 

Jervis, "On the Office and Duties of Coroners," the leading English treatise on the subject, wrote in the third edition, in 1866, at page 2: "The office of Coroner is of so great antiquity, that its commencement is not known. By some it is said to be coeval with that of Sheriff, and to have been ordained, with the later office, to keep the peace when the earls gave up the wardship of the county; but it is doubted by others, and, as it seems, with reason, notwithstanding, the great authorities to the contrary; for, according to Sir Edward Coke, the sheriff was more ancient than the division of England into counties by King Alfred, and existed in the time of the Romans in this country as an officer of the consul, at which period we find no allusion to any officer whose duties corresponded with those of the Coroner. But, whatever, may have been the commencement of the office, it [77]

is evident that Coroners existed in the time of Alfred, for the king punished with death a judge who sentenced a party to suffer death upon the Coroner's record, without allowing the delinquent liberty to traverse. This officer is also mentioned in the charter granted by King Athelstan to the monastery of St. John of Beverly, A. D. 925."

In the reign of Richard I, we find the first undoubted reference to the coroner's office. Richard spent much of his time abroad in the Crusades, and left domestic affairs to Hubert Walter, Chief Justiciar of England, who, in 1194, issued instructions to the itinerant justices, known as the Articles of Eyre, in which reference is made to the coroner's office. At any rate, by 

1215, the office had become so offensive to the subjects, that Chapter 17 of Magna Charta forbade them to hold pleas of the crown. Jervis on Coroners (3d ed.) 

31. In 1276, some of the Coroner's duties were defined by the statute "De officio Coronatoris," 4 Edw. 1, st. 2, which forms the basis of practice even today. 

Jervis indicates the importance of the office of coroner in connection with suicides. The crime of selfmurder, or "felo de se" "is wisely and religiously considered by English law as the most heinous description of felonious homicide; for, as no man hath power to destroy life, but by commission of God, the author of it, the suicide is guilty of a double offence; one spiritual, in evading the prerogative of the Almighty, and rushing into his presence uncalled for; and the other temporal, against the king, who has an interest in the preservation of all his subjects. 

This offence, therefore, is ranked by the law amongst the highest crimes, making it a peculiar species of felony, a felony committed on one-self. 

4 Bl. Com. 189. "What punishment, apportioned to the enormity of the offence, can human laws inflict upon one

who has withdrawn himself from their reach. 

Upon his body they are inoperative; they can only act upon that which he has left behind, his fortune and his reputation. One main object of punishment is example; and the infliction of an ignominious burial, and the forfeiture of property, may deter others from sacrificing their reputation, and the welfare of their family, by so wicked and desperate an act. "It seems clear that a felo de se forfeits all chattels, real and personal, which he has in his own right; all chattels real which he has in right of his wife, or jointly with her; all bonds and other personal things in action belonging solely to himself; all entire chattels in possession, to which he was entitled jointly with another on any account except that of merchandise, and a moiety of such joint chattels as may be severed. *** The forfeiture is not vested until the self-murder be found by inquisition, which ought regularly, as we have seen, to be done by the Coroner super visum corporis. " 

Jervis on Coroners (3d ed.) 144, 145. "Besides legal forfeiture, this offence incurs an ecclesiastical forfeiture of the Christian rites of the church, * * *. It was formerly usual for the

Coroners to grant a warrant, directing the constables and church-wardens of the parish where the inquest was held, to cause the body of a felo de se to be buried in some public street or highway, which was usually complied with by burying the party in a public cross road, and by driving a stake through his body." 

Jervis on Coroners (3d ed.) 148, 149. 

As late as the reign of George IV, the unseemliness of this practice was partially remedied by an act which provided (4 Geo. 4, c. 52): "it shall not be lawful for any Coroner * * * to issue any warrant or other process directing the

interment of the remains of persons against whom a finding of felo de se shall be had, in any public highway, but such Coroner *** shall give directions for the private interment of the remains of such person felo de se, without any stake being driven through the body of such person, in the churchyard or other burial ground of the parish or place in which the remains of such person might, by the laws or customs of England, be interred, if the verdict of felo de se had not been found against such person; such interment to be made within twenty-four hours from the finding of the inquisition, and to take place between the hours of nine and twelve at night; proviso, (s. 2), not to authorize the performing of any of the rites of Christian burial on the interment of the remains of any such person 

The office of coroner early lost something of its original dignity. The office came in for masterful satirization at the pen of Shakespeare in the churchyard scene in Hamlet.

1. Kinds of English coroners: 

(a) The coroner's virtue officii were the Lord Chief Justice of the Court of King's Bench, who, by virtue of his office, was supreme Coroner over all England, and the other justices of the King's Bench were Sovereign Coroners. (b) The coroners by virtue of charter, commission, or privilege were of four kinds: 

1. The coroner of the verge, or the king's household. The verge was a movable jurisdiction of twelve miles in radius from the place where for the time being the king held his court. 

2. The coroner of the admiralty. He was appointed by the lord high admiral and had sole jurisdiction on the high seas. 

3. Other coroners appointed by the king. 

4. Coroners of franchise. (c) The coroners by virtue of election. There were usually four such coroners in each county. Their jurisdiction was limited to the county for which they were elected.

2. The powers and duties of the English coroner: 

(a) Conservators of the peace:

Coroners in England become magistrates by virtue of their election and appointment. They are empowered to cause felons to be apprehended, and to bind any person to the peace who makes any affray in the presence of the coroner. (b) Triers of pleas of coroners:

Before Magna Charta, coroners conducted trials of pleas of the crown. Their power in proceeding to trial and judgment in criminal matters was taken away in 1215. (c) Inquests of death:

In the reign of Edward I (1272-1307) the coroner's duty of taking inquests was regulated and defined by the statute De officio Coronatoris, 4 Edw. 1, st. 2, which enacts: 

"That the Coroner, upon information, shall go to the place where any be slain or suddenly dead or wounded; shall forthwith" call a jury and inquire of them upon oath, "whether they know where the person was slain, whether it were in any house, field, bed, tavern, or company, and if any and who were there." "Likewise it is to be inquired who were and in what manner culpable, either of the act or of the force; and who were present, either men or women; and of what age soever they be (if they can speak, or have any discretion). "And how many soever be found culpable by inquisition, ***, they shall be taken and delivered to the sheriff, and shall be committed to gaol; and such as be founden, and be not culpable, shall be attached until the coming of the justices; and their names shall be written in the Coroner's rolls.

"If it fortune that any man be slain in the fields, or in the woods and be there found; first, it is to be inquired, whether he were slain there or not.

And if he were brought and laid there, they shall do so much as they can to follow their steps that brought the body thither; or of the horse which brought him, or cart, if perchance he was brought upon a horse or cart. It shall also be inquired, if the dead person were known, or else a stranger; and where he lay the night before. "And if there be any who are said to be guilty of the murder, the Coroner shall immediately go into their house, and shall inquire what goods they have, and what corn they have in their grange; and if they be freemen, he shall inquire how much land they have, and what it is worth yearly; and further, what corn they have upon the ground. And when he has thus inquired upon everything, he shall cause all the corn and goods to be valued, and the land to be extended in like manner as if they should be sold incontinently; and thereupon they shall be answerable before the justices for all; and likewise of their freehold, how much it is worth yearly, over and above the service due to the lords of the fee; and the land shall remain in the king's hand until the lords of the fee have made fine for it. "And immediately upon these things being inquired, the bodies of such persons being dead, and slain, shall be buried. "In like manner, it is to be inquired of them that be drowned or suddenly dead; and after it is to be seen of such bodies, whether they be so drowned, or slain, or strangled, by the sign of the cord tied straight about their necks, or about any of their members, or upon any other hurt found upon their bodies; whereupon they shall proceed in the form aforesaid: and if they were not slain, then ought the Coroner to attach the finders, and all others in company.

"Also all wounds ought to be viewed, the length, breadth, and deepness, and with what weapons, and in what part of the body the wound or hurt is, and how many be culpable, and how many wounds there be, and who gave the wounds; all which things must be enrolled in the roll of Coroners. "Concerning horses, boats, carts, etc., whereby any are slain, that properly are called deodans, shall be valued, and delivered unto the towns, as before it is said.'

Those provisions of the statute pertaining to seizure of goods by the coroner and deodands have been repealed.

This statute is merely directory, and in affirmance of the common law, and does not restrain the coroner from any branch of his power incident to his office at common law. Thus, the coroner ought to inquire of the death of all persons who die in prison. 

"An inquest of death can be taken by a coroner super visum corporis only; and if there be no view the inquisition is void.

It is clearly laid down by all the books, that a Coroner has no authority to take an inquest without a view of the body 

"Both the coroner and the jury must view the body at the first sitting of the inquest; and it is desirable that they should view it at the same time; for the inquisition proceeds upon the view of the body lying dead, and the oath ought regularly to be administered by the former to the latter, super visum corporis, in order that the jury may have the benefit of the Coroner's remarks upon the appearances which the body exhibits ***"" "The view must be such as will be calculated to afford information to the coroner and the jury.

"*** In truth, the body itself is part of the evidence before the jury. * * * So essential is the view to the validity of the inquisition, that if the body be not found, or have lain so long before the view, that no information can be obtained from the inspection of it, or if there be danger of infection by digging it up, the inquest ought not to be taken by the Coroner, ***, application should be made, in such cases, to the magistrates or justices authorized to inquire of felonies, who, without viewing the body, shall take the inquest by the 

"The Coroner's inquest is to ascertain truly the cause of the party's death, and is rather for information of the truth of the fact, than for accusation; it is not so much an accusation on an indictment, as an inquest of office to inquire truly how the party came to his death. An inquisition is, nevertheless, an indictment within the meaning of the 24 & 25 Vict. c. 100, s. 6 * * *.'' (d) To inquire of other felonies:

There was considerable authority holding the right to take an inquest of breakers of houses, rape, prisonbreach and arson. The case of Reg. v. Herford, 29 

L. J. Q. B. 249 disposed of that authority by holding that the office of Coroner, with respect to felonies, is limited to cases of homicide on the view of the body. (e) To inquire of treasure troves: 

By 4 Edw. 1, "a coroner ought to inquire of treasure that is found," for treasure troves, if the owner could not be found, went to the king. 

(f) To inquire of royal fishes: 

These were the whale and the sturgeon, which, when thrown ashore or caught near the coast of England became the property of the king by virtue of his pre-

rogative and in recompense for his protecting the shore from pirates and robbers. The coroner was charged with protecting the royal interest in such fish. (g) To inquire of wrecks: 

Goods cast ashore from wrecks at sea were forfeited to the crown. (h) In appeals of felony: 

Before the statute, 59.Geo. 3, c. 46, which completely abrogated this mode of prosecution, coroners were empowered, by the statute of 4 Edw. 1, to receive an appeal of any felony or maihem in the county court, upon the plaintiff's finding sufficient pledges to the sheriff for the prosecution of the suit. (i) To take the confession and abjuration of felons: 

This was a renunciation of England, a species of self-imposed banishment, under oath never to return to the kingdom except by permission. Coroners were anciently authorized to allow abjuration of the realm to confessed criminals, as a means of saving their lives. The privilege of sanctuary was abolished by statute in the reign of James I. (j) In outlawry:

Outlawry is a punishment inflicted on a person who is guilty of a contempt and contumacy, by refusing to be amenable to and abide by the justice of that court which has lawful authority to call him before it. It subjects the offender to heavy forfeitures and attainder. The entry of judgment of outlawry was by the coroner. (k) To execute process:

The coroner has also certain ministerial authority to execute process in certain cases where the sheriff can-

not act. (Act of May 2, 1929, P. L. 1278, Art. 3, sec. 

59, Act of July 9, 1901, P. L. 614, sec. 1, cl. 15.) 

3. Coroner's inquest in England:

The proceedings of the coroner at the inquest and the scope of his inquiry are more or less matters of unwritten law, and therefore varied from time to time. 

We have considered the subject-matter of the inquisition above, and the next logical step is a consideration of the manner in which the inquest was taken. (a) Time of inquest:

The coroner should proceed to hold his inquest forthwith upon receipt of information of a violent, or unnatural death. No precise definite time can be specified. The body is part of the evidence, and it is essential that it should be in such a state that information may be derived from the inspection of it. A delay of seven months was held too late. The inquest must be held within a reasonable time after death. (b) Who may attend the English inquest:

It has been the subject of much controversy, whether the inquest is of a public nature, or whether it is an ex parte and secret proceeding, analogous to that before the grand jury. It is clear in England from the decision in Garnett v. Ferrand, 6 Barnewall & Cresswell 611, 626-627 (1827); 108 Eng. Rep. K. B. (Full

Reprint) 576, 582, that the coroner has the power of excluding, not only particular individuals, but the public generally. The case just cited was action of trespass against coroner for assaulting the plaintiff, and about turning him out of a room where he is about to take an inquest. Plaintiff had not been summoned as witness or juror. Defendant alleged plaintiff entered for purpose of taking notes, and had been asked to leave. Held, coroner not liable. (Unanimous decision.)

"It is argued on the part of the plaintiff, that the Court of the coroner is a public court. That it is and ought to be open to the entrance of all His Majesty's subjects, or at least of so many as the place will contain; and it is averred, and not denied on the record, that on the occasion in question there was room for the presence of the plaintiff. 

The court was assembled for an inquest on the view of the body of a person then lying dead. Now it is obvious that such an inquiry ought for the purposes of justice, in some cases be conducted in secrecy; it is a preliminary enquiry, which may or may not end in the accusation of a particular individual, it may be requisite that a suspected person should not in so early a stage be informed of the suspicion that may be entertained against him, and of the evidence on which it is founded, lest he should elude justice by flight, tampering with witnesses, or otherwise. *** It was admitted in the argument, that secrecy and exclusion may be proper and necessary when charge and accusation begin. It is obvious that this may begin as soon as the evidence begins. Cases also may occur, in which privacy may be requisite for the sake of decency; others, in which it may be due to the family of the deceased. Many things must be disclosed to those who are to decide, the publication whereof, to the world at large, may be productive of mischief without any possibility of good. Who then is to decide whether privacy be necessary or proper? We answer, the coroner, and the coroner alone, and that the propriety of his decision cannot be questioned in an action ***. Even in cases where absolute privacy may not be required, the exclusion of particular persons may be necessary or proper. Who then is to decide upon this? We again answer the coroner, and the coroner alone, and that the reason of his decision cannot be tried in an action. It will be, in many cases, impossible that a proceeding should be conducted with due order and solemnity, and with the effect that jus-

tice demands, if the presiding officer, whether he be judge, coroner, justice or sheriff, has not the control of the proceeding, and the power of admission or exclusion according to his own discre tion. *** The power of exclusion is necessary to the due administration of justice." Garnett v. 

Ferrand, supra, at 626.

It is doubtful in England if the coroner has the right to have a person who is in custody, charged with the homicide in respect of which an inquest is being taken, brought before him for the purpose of identification by witnesses to be examined on such inquest. 

Jervis on Coroners (3d ed.) 250-251. (c) Witnesses at the English inquest: "It was formerly doubted whether a coroner was bound or ought in all cases to examine all the witnesses, as well against the interest of the crown as for it. It was admitted to be the duty of the coroner, in cases of felo de se, to examine the witnesses as well against the king's interest as for it; for in that case no one was to be condemned to death, but the fact only was to be inquired into. And in one case, an inquisition of felo de se was quashed, and the coroner reprehended, because he refused to admit witnesses to prove that the party was non compos mentis, but shut them out, and only took witnesses for the king.

And it seems now to be agreed, that the Coroner's inquest must in all cases hear evidence on oath, as well for the party accused or suspected, as for the crown, if it be offered them; because the proceeding is not so much an accusation on an indictment, as an inquisition of office to inquire truly how the party came to his death, which cannot be ascertained unless all the witnesses who know anything of the death be examined." 

Jervis on Coroners (3d ed.) 265-266. 

---

(d) Right of parties interested in English inquest to the benefit of counsel before the inquest: "There is nothing in the position of counsel to except them from the power of exclusion vested in the Coroner, and if they are allowed there is no reason why any other advocate skilled in the law should not be permitted to be present at the Coroner's proceedings. If any advocate has a right to be present, a fortiori, the party suspected (for there can be no accusation until the inquiry has terminated), may be present also; and undoubtedly the Coroner may, if he thinks fit, exclude even persons suspected. If the party suspected or interested has a right to the assistance of counsel, it is impossible to say that those of opposite interest have not an equal right to have the presence and assistance of some legal adviser on their behalf; and if one may attend why not several. If an advocate has a right to be present, he may obtain such information as may tend to frustrate the administration of justice, by knowing who the persons are who are likely to be accused and the evidence by which that accusation may be supported. 

"There are undoubtedly many occasions in which counsel have attended before the Coroner when holding an inquest; *** but it by no means follows as a consequence that their admission on some occasions would confer a right to be present at all. If it be not left to the fair discretion of the 

Coroner whether he will or will not admit counsel to be present, that privacy which is due, not only to the ends of justice, where the inquiry may terminate in accusation, but also in many cases to the feelings of the family of the deceased, cannot be maintained. This exclusion may in some cases operate as a temporary grievance, but it is obviously a greater that the party suspected should be excluded; yet that may be done if the Coroner think proper; and it is better that there should

be some hardship suffered in the individual case than that the public should sustain a great detriment. "On the other hand, it is obvious that there are many cases in which it may be expedient for coroners to admit the presence of persons learned in 

In fine, the power of exclusion, even of the public generally, ought to be regulated by a due regard to the circumstances of each particular case, and should in no instance be arbitrarily or improperly enforced."

B. The Office of Coroner in Pennsylvania 1. History of coroner's office in Pennsylvania: 

From the establishment of the earliest English settlement in Pennsylvania, it has always been the practice to hold an inquisition over the bodies of those suddenly dead.

A proper understanding of the office and duties of coroners in Pennsylvania requires a knowledge of the English law prior to 1680, for several of the old English statutes are still in force in Pennsylvania, and it follows that the English common law, as well as statute law, is applicable in Pennsylvania, except in so far as it is altered to meet the form of our government, and the social conditions of our people. 

The county coroner is the only kind of coroner whose office was found necessary in Pennsylvania, and the number of coroners for each county was reduced from four (as in England) to one.

The office of coroner in colonial Pennsylvania underwent very definite changes. Most of the ancient English duties were not needed in the New World. The coroner's duties in regard to appeals were never transplanted to Pennsylvania. His jurisdiction over wrecks of the sea, sturgeons and whales naturally atrophied. 

Treasure trove in a new land played an insignificant

part, title thereto going to the finder. Deodand, forfeiture, outlawry, confession and abjuration in Pennsylvania, if they existed at all, have long been forgotten. 

The coroner has lost his ancient characteristic of collector of the king's revenue and has become in our Commonwealth the county officer whose duty it is to investigate the cause of death of those who come to a sudden and violent end. He is now an officer engaged in the administration of justice. In transplanting the office to a new country the duties as an agent of the king's exchequer have been sloughed off. 

2. The powers and duties of coroners in Pennsylvania: 

"One of the most striking differences between the powers of the coroner in England and those of the same officer in Pennsylvania, is that in the latter he has no power as a committing magistrate.' 99 

Woodward, Office and Duties of Coroners in 

Pennsylvania, 157. 

In Walker v. McKean County, 15 Dist. 577 (1905) it is stated (at 578): "He originally acted as a committing magistrate but that power he does not have in Pennsylvania." 

In Coroner's Duties, 11 Phila. 387 (1875) it is stated: "He is also a committing magistrate, and it is his duty, if the jury find any person guilty of homicide, to commit that person to prison, without bail, to await his trial. The finding of a coroner's inquest, in the old English law, was equivalent to the finding of a grand jury."

In Philadelphia, Allegheny, and several other counties, the coroner sits as a committing magistrate. In

other counties of the state, the coroners do not issue a warant, or hold for court. They leave the arrest and commitment to the police, district attorney and magistrate. The McKean County case, or as it is sometimes called, the “rural rule," is more in conformity with the history of the coroner's office in Pennsylvania, which, as we have seen, has been one of constant diminution of authority. The City or "Philadelphia rule" makes the office of some political importance. It gives the coroner great power in important cases. Judge 

Sulzberger in Coroner's Duties, 20 Dist. 685 (1911), undermines the importance of the office of coroner in 

Philadelphia, but a practice once started requires more than the advisory opinion of a good judge to overthrow it.

*** the coroner's jury does not try an issue of fact, but investigates a fact, there is no matter before it asserted on the one side and denied on the other; the duty of the coroner's jury is to investigate circumstances and declare what truth the evidence before them establishes. * * The coroner has long since ceased to be in Pennsylvania what in England he always has been, to wit, in the strictest sense, a court. of the coroner upon matters of law. * * * Until they agree the inquest is incomplete and therefore not a valid inquest; the coroner, therefore has the right to set the jury aside for refusing to agree and to hold another inquest. In the Reports of the American Bar Association, Vol. 54 (1929), at pages 548-563, there is a paper en titled: "The Coroner's Office: Its Proposed Aboli tion," by Dr. William C. 4. The coroner's inquest in Pennsylvania: "The object of the inquest in Pennsylvania is different from the object thereof in England prior to the settlement of this country. There the questions of deodands and forfeitures played an important part; here the matters relating to the king's revenue have no application." 

Woodward on Coroners, 180.

The object of the Pennsylvania inquest was defined by Mr. Justice Mercur in County of Lancaster v. Mishler, 100 Pa. 624, 627 (1882) to be: "to seek information, and obtain and secure evidence, in case of death by violence or other undue means. 

Judge Henderson, in Miller v. Cambria County, 29

Pa. Superior Ct. 166, 168 (1905), said of the object of the inquest: "An inquest is held to secure information and develop evidence in case of death resulting from felony or criminal negligence."

The Court of Common Pleas, No. 2, of Allegheny 

County said in a per curiam opinion in Ralston's Petition, 9 Dist. 514, 515 (1900) of the object of the inquest: "The only object and result of his proceedings is to furnish prompt information which may guide the officers of the law in apprehending and prosecuting those who appear to have been connected with a supposed homicide, and in discovering the evidence by which it may be investigated."

President Judge McClean of Adams County, in Witmore's Case, 3 Dist. Rep. 699, 700 (1894), said of the object of the inquest: "The object of an inquest being to detect and aid in the punishment of crime *** It was a plain case of suicide, and an inquest was not necessary." 

In Burn's Case, 5 Pa. C. C. R. 549, 552 (1888) it is said:

CC* ** * Where life has been feloniously de stroyed, as evidenced by wounds or other circumstances, it is important to secure such evidence of

identity and description of the cause of and manner of death that may be available on the trial of the party charged with the felony.” 

Perhaps the best working definition the Courts have given us on the objects of the inquest, appears in 

Walker v. McKean County, 31 Pa. C. C. R. 664, 666 (1905): "The purposes of an inquest are, First, to ascertain the cause of death; 

Second, whether or not anyone is criminally responsible for the death; 

Third, if anyone is criminally responsible to procure evidence in order that the person responsible may be brought to justice. Whenever the coroner has reason to suspect that a death was due to other than natural causes, and there are grounds for suspicion that someone is criminally responsible, it becomes his duty to act, and he is the only judge of the necessity of the inquest." 

Under the Pennsylvania decisions the duties of the coroner are unquestionably to gather evidence and information for the Commonwealth in homicide cases. 

As a corollary of this well-established purpose of the inquest, an individual as such can have no interest in the inquest. 

In McFadgen v. Chester County, 10 Pa. C. C. R. 124, 128 (1891) it is said: "The only reason given by the coroner for holding the inquest, aside from the suddenness of death, was that Mr. Latch" (with whom deceased lived) "'wanted an investigation; he wanted protection.' It, however, is no part of a coroner's duty to hold inquests for the protection, or vindication of individuals, ***. As we have already said, the object of an inquest is to detect and aid in the punishment of crime; ***."

In Woodward on Coroners, at page 181, it is stated: * The verdict of the coroner's jury has come in this state to have less and less weight until now it has no legal effect, ***, the jurisdiction of the coroner today extends only to the investigation of cases where the crimes of murder or manslaughter may have been perpetrated. 

Cases where the crime of involuntary manslaughter may have been perpetrated are comprehended within the jurisdiction of the coroner. 

Miller v. Cambria County, 29 Pa. Super. 166 (1905). 

5. Coroner's inquest procedure in Pennsylvania: 

(a) Preliminary: 

There is very little authority on the point in Pennsylvania, and from inquiry made to all the coroners of the Commonwealth, there is little uniformity. "When the coroner is notified that one has been slain or is suddenly dead he should go forthwith to the place where the body is and make his preliminary investigation. When he has determined that an inquest is necessary he should summon his jury, setting a day for the inquest as soon as may be after the death, and the view should be held, if possible, while the body is in the same position and other circumstances as when the person died. 

The inquest should if possible be held at the place where the body is found, but this does not mean at the very particular spot where the body is but in general at the place. The coroner's inquest must be super visum corporis and if the body be not found there can be no inquest. 

"It is not necessary that the whole evidence be presented while the body lies before the jury." 

Woodward on Coroners, 208-209.

(b) Duty of the district attorney to assist coroner. 

The coroner or justice of the peace in his absence may exhume a body if requested by the district attorney in order to make an investigation.

Pickett v. Erie County, 19 W. N. C. 60 (1886). 

In Com. v. Grether, 204 Pa. 203, 206 (1902), one of the few Supreme Court cases on the whole subject of coroners, it was held that the district attorney may exhume the body of one slain and make his own examination and that the evidence so procured was admissible on the trial for murder "without regard to anything the coroner may have done or omitted to do." 

Two years before the Grether case was decided, President Judge Yerkes of Bucks County in Grove's 

Inquest, 26 Pa. C. C. R. 449, 452 (1900) wrote: "The true remedy for abuse of the coroner's office is its absolute abolition. The original purpose of investigating crime through it is better ac complished in the district attorney's office * * *.'' 

The right and duty of the district attorney to assist the coroner is set at rest in a convincing manner by no less an able jurist than President Judge Sulzberger in Coroner's Duties, 20 Dist. Rep. 685 (1911), as follows: 

"Opinion given upon request, to John W. Ford, Esq., Coroner of Philadelphia County. 66 'Sulzberger, P. J., Aug. 10, 1911.-Your letter of yesterday, requesting me to put in writing my opinion of the relation between the functions of the district attorney and of the coroner in homicide cases, has been received, and, following the precedent established by the late President Judge 

Thayer (11 Phila. 387), 'I have no hesitation in expressing my opinion in regard to the question which the coroner has propounded to the court.'

"While the office of coroner is provided for by the constitution, neither that instrument nor any law passed in pursuance thereof gives any satisfactory statement of his powers. It has been the habit to attribute to him certain common law powers, without any clear apprehension of their source or extent, or of the question whether these, if they ever existed, have been modified by subsequent legislation. "The statute of 4 Edward I, ch. 2, passed in 

1276, is entitled 'De officio coronatoris.' 'Of what things a coroner shall inquire.' In substance, it empowers him to inquire as to where a person was slain and who were there, who and in what manner were they culpable of the act or force, and who were present. Whoever is found culpable is to be delivered to the sheriff and committed to gaol, and whoever is not culpable 'shall be attached until the coming of the justices.' "This statute is one of those reported by the judges as in force in this state. From its terms it is seen that, under it, the coroner had no power to discharge any one, whether culpable of the death or merely present at it. That power is reserved to the 'justices' of the King's bench. Quite in accordance with this is the general principle of the old English law that the Lord Chief Justice of the 

King's bench is the chief coroner of the Kingdom.

Under our law, the justices of the oyer and terminer are, by virtue of their office, superior committing magistrates, and as such, whenever they choose to exercise the function, outrank the coroner or other committing magistrate. "Our modern Pennsylvania law has materially modified the old English law. In Philadelphia, for instance, we have an admirable and efficient police department, which in a large measure does the initial work that anciently in England devolved on the coroner. We have also a state system of district-attorneys, whose function it is to represent the commonwealth whenever a crime has been

committed, and especially when a homicide has been perpetrated. "In order that these several arms of state police power shall work together harmoniously, their several functions must be wisely coördinated. The district-attorney's duty to prosecute homicides would be seriously interfered with if the police department, the magistrates or the coroner had the legal right to acquit a person charged. And although the acquittal has no force in law, it is always an obstacle, more or less powerful, in the prosecution of a man for an offence of which he cannot be convicted unless all reasonable doubt is absent. "When the coroner carefully investigates the cause of death and ascertains either that it is due to natural causes or to violence, and, if the latter, who produced it or was present at it, he has done his whole duty. In performing it, he should be careful to hear evidence and not to accept as a substitute for it mere hearsay or the written opinion of an absent physician, or, indeed, the testimony of any one who is not duly sworn and subject to the cross-examination of the commonwealth, which is a party and which normally demands a commitment whenever the circumstances indicate a prima facie case. "By way of summing up these thoughts, I answer your implied questions categorically: "1. The coroner should never hear a homicide case without the presence of the district-attorney. "2. The district-attorney has the power to cross-examine the witnesses. "3. If, on a careful review of the testimony, the district-attorney deems it his duty to declare that there is no evidence to hold a person charged, he must expressly so declare to the coroner, and on such express declaration the coroner should discharge the person accused. Under no other circumstances should the coroner discharge a man accused of homicide.

"During the whole progress of civilization the taking of life has been deemed the most serious act which can be committed by a man. The dreadful injury to the victim, to his family and to the state, is obvious. That the man who perpetrates such an act, even by accident or misfortune, is in a serious position has always been recognized. It is, therefore, a perversion of thought to exhibit a nervous apprehension concerning the mere inconvenience to which an innocent homicide may be subjected. He is concerned in a dreadful misfortune and has no right to complain of the comparatively trivial inconvenience to which he is in consequence subjected. Least of all is it proper for the police, the coroner, the district-attorney or other magistrate to exhibit undue sensitiveness for the perpetrator. Their function is to represent the commonwealth and its citizens who have been injured. "The place for hearing both parties and ascertaining the very right of the matter is in open court, where a jury, carefully selected, presided over by a judge learned in the law, will see to it that no innocent man shall suffer." (c) The coroner in his discretion may exclude whomsoever he sees fit to the end that justice may be accomplished.

There was considerable doubt as to whether the coroner's inquest was to be considered a matter of public inquiry or merely a secret investigation, analogous to the ex parte proceeding before the grand jury. There are no Pennsylvania decisions directly in point. The real basis of the privacy or publicity of the inquest depends in part on the object of the inquest. We have seen the object of the inquest in Pennsylvania, to use the Supreme Court's language in Lancaster County v. 

Mishler, supra, is "to seek information and obtain and secure evidence in case of death by violence or other undue means.”

From the language used in this and other cases, it appears very clearly that the Commonwealth has a vital interest in the inquest. If the object of the inquest is prejudiced by the attendance of the public generally, or any particular members of it, we submit that the coroner should have, and does have, exclusionary power.

It is perfectly obvious that in some cases it would be ruinous to the Commonwealth's interest to permit the perpetrators of murder, who are at large, to know all about the evidence in the possession of the Commonwealth. Such a revelation would result, if, in every instance, the inquest must be public. "Bearing in mind the purpose of the inquest is not to accuse, we may fairly say the coroner has power to admit or exclude from his inquest according to his own discretion." 

Woodward on Coroners, 213. 

Jervis on Coroners, 248.

The coroner, having power to exclude, has power to admit persons to his inquest subject to reasonable rules to secure the ends of justice. The inquest is one of the Commonwealth's proceedings in its general obligation to administer the criminal law. It is a preliminary proceeding which develops information, which, in the hands of parties whose interests are adverse to the Commonwealth, may be used to defeat justice. 

Why would a person instrumental in a human death desire to have an accurate report of the inquest? The answer is obvious. He learns who are the witnesses he may have to face later, and what they may testify to at his trial. It may be used to build up a defense, or to contradict the Commonwealth's witnesses when they testify weeks or years later. 

At the inquest, the Commonwealth cannot require the accused to reveal his hand. Why at the inquest,

which binds nobody, should the criminal have the right to be present and have reported all that may be used against him?

We have seen above that in England the coroner has the right to forbid the taking of notes at an inquest. (d) The coroner may prevent counsel for interested persons from taking active part in his inquests. 

The leading English authority, Sir John Jervis, holds that the admission or exclusion of counsel is a matter for the discretion of the coroner in the particular case.

This undoubtedly was also the law in Pennsylvania until the passage of the Act of April 2, 1923, P. L. 106. 

That act provides as follows: "That any person learned in the law, who has been admitted to practice as an attorney in any court of record of this Commonwealth, shall hereafter be entitled to practice as an attorney in any proceeding before any magistrate, justice of the peace, coroner, burgess, alderman, mayor, or in any court not of record, with the same powers and privileges to which he shall be entitled as a practicing attorney in the court of which he is already a member."

It would appear at first blush that this act might have been designed to allow counsel for interested persons to participate in coroners' inquests. However, it is submitted that if such were the purpose of the act, then it would undoubtedly have been declared unconstitutional for a number of reasons, one of them being that the title was not sufficiently broad to have put anyone having an interest in the office of coroner on inquiry concerning the provisions in the act pertaining to changes in practice before the coroner. This act was first tested in Montgomery County in the following manner: A coroner was conducting an inquest

and counsel for an interested party appeared with a stenographer and began to make notes of the proceedings. The coroner directed the stenographer to leave and threatened to eject him if he failed to do so. The stenographer left and following that a petition for a declaratory judgment was filed and the court was requested to interpret the act. The court held that if the act were to be interpreted as giving counsel for interested parties the right to actively participate in an inquest, it would undoubtedly be unconstitutional. The court further held that the coroner was within his rights in excluding the stenographer, on the ground that the act only permitted an attorney to do those things at an inquest which his client had the right to do for himself. In short, the act did not change or diminish the discretion of the coroner except that it prohibited him from arbitrarily excluding an attorney from an inquest when he would not have excluded the attorney's client. Bernard v. Dettre, 50 Montg. 235 (1933).

The above case is the only one in Pennsylvania which interprets the Act of 1923, P. L. 106. It would appear that the reasoning in the case was sound and that the act in question has very little effect upon the discretion of the coroner. As the court there pointed out, the Act of 1923 is merely declaratory of existing law and was apparently designed to terminate certain usurpations of power by justices of the peace, some of whom had been exhibiting a tendency to curtail the activities of attorneys appearing before them; the statute does not operate either to enlarge or to diminish any of the powers of these subordinate judicial officers. Although counsel for a client would appear to have the right to be present at an inquest where his client's presence is necessary, it likewise would seem that counsel has no right to have a record of the proceeding made nor has he any right to actively participate in the inquest itself.

(e) Neither a party interested in an inquest, nor his counsel may offer and submit evidence as a matter of right.

It is the coroner's duty to hear all the relevant evidence bearing on the subject matter of the inquest. If a witness is suggested to the coroner, he should be permitted to testify provided his testimony is relevant to the inquiry, and not merely cumulative. He hears such witnesses, not as the witness for an interested party, but as a witness who may shed some light on the questions before the jury. The coroner may call whomsoever he pleases, in whatsoever order he pleases. 

Under his discretionary powers he may call one witness before the jury at a time, so that the testimony of such witness may not be heard by others who testify later.

If any party interested in the inquest may as a matter of right offer and submit evidence, the inquest will be turned into a preliminary trial, and vitiate any good or benefit which might have come to the Commonwealth as a result of the inquest. (f) Neither any interested party nor his counsel has the right to examine and cross-examine witnesses at the inquest.

In Bouvier's Law Dictionary, Rawles, 3rd Ed., Vol. 

I, page 733, cross-examination is defined as: "The examination of a witness by the party opposed to the party who called him, and who examined or was entitled to examine him in chief.” 

In Words and Phrases, Vol. II, page 1762, it is said: "The examination of a witness by the party producing him is denominated the 'direct examination'; the examination of the same witness upon the same matter by the adverse party, the 'crossexamination.""

McLain v. Commonwealth, 99 Pa. 86, 97 (1881), definitely denies the right to cross-examine. In the 

McLain case defendant was indicted and tried for murder. At the trial he offered a report of the testimony of one James Cox, given at the coroner's inquest, Cox being ill and unable to attend court. The testimony of Cox had been taken stenographically. This offer was objected to as incompetent, and because there was no cross-examination by the Commonwealth; objection sustained; exception. Verdict of guilty. On appeal defendant specified as error the rejection of 

Cox's testimony before the coroner.

Mr. Justice Mercur, speaking for the Supreme Court of which Sharswood was a member, said at page 97: "The action of the court rests on firmer ground. 

The hearing before the coroner was not between the parties to the issue in which the evidence was offered. No issue was there formed between the 

Commonwealth and the plaintiff in error. An inquiry there takes a broad range. It is not to ascertain the guilt of any particular person, but of every person that the evidence might implicate. technical rules restrict or control the admission of evidence. No cross-examination of witnesses is had. The coroner's discretion marks the line where the evidence of a witness shall begin, and where it shall end."

No 

In assembling witnesses, a coroner must be guided by his own knowledge, acquired at first-hand or brought to him through the police or otherwise. In a well-organized office, police officers detailed to duty there, or other competent assistants, acting under the coroner's directions, see that the necessary witnesses are summoned and that no important witness is overlooked. They should indicate to the coroner the facts concerning which each witness is to testify. There is, however, ordinarily no systematic examination, crossexamination, and redirect examination of witnesses at

a coroner's inquest, for there are no parties to the proceeding and therefore no one has the legal right to ask questions, except the coroner, the district attorney and possibly the jurors. It rests within the sound discretion of the coroner as to who shall and who shall not ask questions and what questions shall be asked. The strict rules of evidence are not ordinarily followed; in fact, ordinarily they cannot be, for the coroner does not know them. The latitude of questioning indulged in by the coroner and his jury is calculated to bring out facts that may be useful in developing a criminal prosecution later.

CHAPTER VI CONCLUSION 

The district attorney in a county is thus the chief of all criminal proceedings in the district from which he has been elected, but owes the same responsibility to the attorney general of the Commonwealth as the local police chiefs and magistrates, in their criminal proceedings, owe to him. The most interesting phase of the powers and duties of the district attorney's office arises in connection with his power over local police chiefs and magistrates in the criminal field. It is obviously essential to the successful investigation and prosecution of crime that there be one responsible party in any district for the trial and disposition of criminal offenses against the state. If every local township police chief were able to exclude the district attorney and his staff, or hinder his efforts, there could be no successful law enforcement or prevention of crime within the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania.

Sometimes local authorities have tried to prevent the functioning of the district attorney, but without success. While there is no statute or decision in Pennsylvania specifically providing that the district attorney supersedes the local police authorities, yet it must of necessity be true because of the clear mandate of the Legislature and the Constitution that the district attorney shall be the supreme authority in all criminal matters. It is an implied power, of necessity. 

This handbook has been compiled with the thought that it may be of some aid in solving the practical problems which daily come to the attention of a prosecuting officer. It too frequently has been the custom of those charged with the prosecution of crime to judge their own success or failure upon apprehension of the [109]

culprit or the number of convictions obtained. Neither of these two tests should be the gauge of success. In the apprehension of criminals, the success or failure of the district attorney should be determined by how carefully he investigated the crime; or, as Hans Gross states in his work on Criminal Investigation: "An investigating officer may be said to do well in proportion as he is able to reduce the number of inquiries in which the necessary basis of proof cannot be secured." (1934 Ed., p. 36.)

As to the number of convictions, the Supreme Court has, times without number, vehemently denounced the attitude on the part of a Commonwealth's officer that securing a verdict of guilty was a triumph. Mr. Chief 

Justice Frazer said, in Commonwealth v. Williams, 309 Pa. 529, 535 (1932): "The winning of a verdict should be a hollow reward to the advocate who has brought it to pass by appeals to a jury's prejudices and not by the strength of the case presented."

Finally, it should be remembered that the office is important and if properly administered is certain to lead to criticism because of the very nature of the disciplinary character of the office. The criticism should not deter the district attorney in doing his duty when he is right. The obligations of the office require a recognition at the outset that the office will not enjoy popular favor, except by accident.
