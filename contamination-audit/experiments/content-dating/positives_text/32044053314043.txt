TALK BY FRANCIS HRUSSELL DIRECTOR OF THE OFFICE OF PUBLIC AFFAIRS DEPARTMENT OF STATE BEFORE THE CONFERENCE OF UNITARIAN MINISTERS. 

WASHINGTON, D. C. 

OCTOBER 13, 1947 AMERICAN FOREIGN POLICY 

332 Edgar Allan Poe in one of his critical essays remarks that the clearest subject may be over-clouded by mere superabundance of talk and that when a topic is thus circumstanced, the readiest mode of dealing with it may be to forget that anything has previously been said on the subject. That is a rather cavalier way of dealing with some of our American columnists but I confess that occasionally it is tempting. 

I was asked a few weeks ago whether American foreign policy was not really determined in Moscow. What the questioner was asking, of course, was whether this country was not merely reacting to moves by the Russian Government rather than pursuing a policy of its own. I replied in the best State Department tradition by saying, "Well, yes and no."

Upon reflection, I am inclined to think that the answer was accurate and meaningful. Perhaps as good a way as any of embarking this evening upon a discussion of American foreign policy might be to spell out what I meant by that answer. 

Does this country have a foreign policy? Immediately, in trying to give an answer to that question, I find myself faced with a problem which, I am sure, is not unknown to ministers. 

That is how to state a constructive, positive set of policies, without seeming dull and trite. All of us, in that respect, have something in common with the housewife who must occasionally wonder what quirk in the order of things brings it to pass that the divorcees get all the publicity. 

With your sympathy and understanding, therefore, in the risk which I shall be taking of being dull, I am going to spend the next two minutes and forty seconds in attempting to summarize American foreign policy. Our over-all objective, of course, is to create international conditions in which people everywhere, including ourselves, can work toward a better life. There are three ways, speaking broadly, by which we try to do that. 

First, we are endeavoring to do everything possible to achieve peace and security. After the first world war our foreign policy was clear cut and definite. It was to have nothing whatever to do with any world-wide organization that might be set up to accomplish that purpose. In the very middle of the war that we have just finished, however, this country invited its major allies to join it in discussions to set up the United Nations. Our first and foremost objective in international relations is to build strong and effective international institutions

-2- institutions. 

We have put forward a plan for assuring that atomic energy will not be used for destructive purposes. The atomic energy authority that is contemplated would be one of the most forward-looking steps in the long history of international human relations.

We have offered to Russia, Great Britain, and France a pact to last forty years which would insure that Germany never again would become a menace to the peace of the world. 

In this hemisphere we are putting our own house in order through an Inter-American system that is rapidly growing in vitality.

Those are some of the policies, not all by any means, that we have followed to try to guarantee that the world will not again be visited by war or even by the threat of war. 

The second broad field is that of our economic foreign policies. Here our premise has been that this country cannot long enjoy a high standard of living in a world that is stricken with chaos and poverty. We cannot expect the world economic boat to sink, at one end and remain floating tranquilly at the other. Our effort has been to bring into full strength as rapidly as possible the Economic and Social Council and the various specialized agencies that would make possible progress in solving problems of food, labor, finance, and trade. In addition to this, we have contributed a not unworthy percentage of our national production to relief in Europe and Asia. Recently we suggested to all of the countries of Europe -- a suggestion which sixteen of them have acted upon that they cooperate in an effort to bring to bear all of the available resources for helping Europe out of the chaos and poverty resulting from the war. This effort has come to be known as the Marshall Plan. 

The third broad area of our foreign policy has to do with things of the human mind and spirit. We are promoting the work of the United Nations Educational, Scientific, and Cultural Organi zation. Mrs. Roosevelt has ably and energetically represented us on the Commission on Human Rights. We have a cultural exchange program that is as active as possible within the funds which Congress has allotted to it. And finally, we have a policy, recently affirmed but as old as this country itself, of supporting free peoples everywhere who are attempting to maintain their freedom, and of encouraging those peoples who are struggling to establish their own freedom and independence. 

Now the thing that I would like to stress is that all of these policies have been and will continue to be the backbone of our foreign relations, regardless of Russia and the Russian problem or any other special problem. They constitute, in my opinion, a dynamic and constructive foreign policy and one on which your servants in the Covernment are working literally day and night.

Why is there a Russian problem? As an illustration of our long-standing problem in getting onto a friendly basis with the Russians, the experience of our Minister to Russia in 1851 is illuminating. Dispatches from the minister, Neill S. Brown, to the "Honorable Daniel Webster, Secretary of State" included such statements as the following: "Secrecy and mystery characterize everything. Nothing is made public that is worth knowing.. 

"The Government of Russia is consistent. It promises no freedom and gives none. 

And again. ".. "...This government possesses to an exquisite degree the art of worrying a foreign representative without giving him even the consolation of an insult." 

And finally: "As proof of the extent to which censorship is carried, I may mention that the last message of the President of the United States was not regarded in all its parts as a safe document for Russian readers and came into their hands scathed with the censor's knife...."

Add to this set of traits the fact that the Russian Government is controlled by leaders of a world movement, the essential characteristic of which is that it is dedicated to the overthrow of all non-communist governments by all available means and it is not difficult to see why a problem exists. 

But more specifically, how did the present state of our relations with Russia come to pass? I found it of considerable interest a few evenings ago to read again an article that appeared in the Saturday Evening Post of April 10, 1943, reporting on the attitude and views of President Roosevelt at that time as personall3 given to the writer. The portions which I found of particular interest were summed up in the following: "For the third time since Napoleon, the nations have a chance to remake the structure of the western world....The President does not blink the power consequences of a United Nations victory. He knows that the defeat and disarmament of the Axis will leave a power vacuum in Europe from the limits of the Soviet Union in the east to the English Channel and in Eastern Asia. With Germany reduced and France in ruins, Russia becomes the only first-rate military power on the Continent....The riddle at the heart of postwar planning in the western democracies is what use the Kremlin proposes making of such power....There is a growing awareness in Washington that Russian successes in the 817837 field have not produced greater warmth in Moscow toward the

I have a feeling that that article will be read by historians with a great deal of interest. For one thing it foretold with great accuracy more than two years before the event the general shape of the United Nations. Organization. For us here this evening, I think that it is helpful in illuminating two facts. 

The first was our determination in this country to do everything we could to bring about a friendly, cooperative relationship between the western democracies and Russia. Second was the uncertainty or apprehension as to whether Russia would reciprocate such an attitude. 

Parenthetically, I think that we must agree once and for all to clear the atmosphere of references to the fact that thirty years ago we attempted an abortive invasion of the Soviet Union. We did, but we did the same to Canada, and Great Britain has twice invaded us and many times invaded France. These events of the past have not in other cases been barriers to the pursuit of common interests in a new day.

The determination foreshadowed in the Post interview to make every possible effort to demonstrate our desire for friendliness and cooperation was energetically and wholeheartedly pursued by this country. As former Under Secretary 

Acheson pointed out, it is no exaggeration to say that no country has attempted to win the confidence and cooperation of any other country to a greater extent than we did during and after the war and until it became clear that that policy was not being reciprocated by the Russians but that, on the contrary continuation of it would jeopardize world peace and stability. It is worthwhile recalling some of the efforts to extend the hand of friendship to the Russians that were made by President Roosevelt and after his death for more than a year and a half by President Truman. For instance: From the moment that

Hitler invaded the Soviet Union, we supplied to the Russians, to a degree previously unknown in history, military and technological information. We fought the war on a purely military and non-political basis with respect to Russia; we did not attack through the Balkans, as we were urged to do in order to exclude Russia from western Europe. We struck across the Straits into 

Normandy. During and after the war we supplied a truly collosal amount of economic and financial assistance to the Soviet Union. 

Our Lend-Lease contribution for the defense of Russia totalled eleven and a quarter billion dollars. Our contributions to

Russian relief through UNRRA after the war equalled another quarter of a billion dollars. Our sales on terms of a thirty-year. credit, after the war, totalled another quarter billion dollars.

With respect to the atom secret, we have made an offer to share the knowledge which we have obtained on condition only that adequate safeguards of international security are established.

This is an impressive record of effort. Despite subsequent developments and despite criticism which this policy has received in this country, it was a wise effort from at least two points of view: First it made a vital contribution to the winning of the war. Secondly, it placed beyond all question our willingness and purpose to join in organizing a foundation of trust throughout the world as a basis of peace. 

In this latter respect the effort appears to have failed, at least temporarily. But it has not wholly failed. It has put our purposes so far beyond suspicion that they have withstood in many areas of the world even the most determined propaganda. 

Now, what about the other question that was raised by 

President Roosevelt in 1943 namely, his fear that Russia might discontinue her policy of cooperation with the western powers as soon as the war was over and revert to a policy of promoting world-wide revolution and chaos?

Let us briefly review the record on that. There was, for example, no reciprocity of information by the Russians during the war. On the contrary, there were frequent and increasing restrictions on every form of communication. 

The Soviet government refused to discuss any of the outstanding economic questions between the two countries. 

There was complete frustration by the Soviet Government of the Yalta Agreement for common action between the three powers

-6- in assisting the countries of eastern Europe to free democratic institutions and reconstruction. This policy culminated in the complete seizure of the governments of Poland, Rumania, Bulgaria, and Hungary by Soviet directed forces. 

The Soviet Union has encouraged obstructionism and truculence in the governments of Poland, Rumania, Yugoslavia, and Bulgaria, amounting in the Greek episode to aggression. It has thwarted all efforts to carry out the agreement for the occupation of Germany, Austria and Korea.

For almost eighteen months the Soviet Union obstructed the efforts of foreign ministers to negotiate the Italian and Balkan peace treaties. And the same methods have made impossible the formulation of the Austrian and German treaties. 

Even in the case of the agreed decision to organize Germany as an economic unit, the Soviet Union has observed the same. obstructive tactics and has put almost insurmountable obstacles in the way of European recovery.

The Soviet Union has declined to participate in practically every one of the United Nations specialized agencies such as 

UNESCO, FAO and the others.

In the United Nations the Soviet Union has used international machinery as an instrument of violent propaganda. 

The work of the Military Staff Committee and the formulation of agreements to provide military forces; upon which so much of the effectiveness of the United Nations depends, has been brought to a complete standstill by Soviet obstruction. 

The work of the Atomic Energy Commission of the United Nations has made no progress in a year for the same reason. 

On many occasions the Soviet Union has utilized the veto in the Security Council to make United Nations action impossible, in clear contradiction to the understanding upon the basis of which the veto was included in the United Nations Charter. 

The Soviet Union has either engaged in or inspired military action or guerrilla warfare, or threats of warfare, against Iran, Greece, and Turkey in an effort to greatly expand, through the seizure of strategic world points, the area of Russian domination. Finally, there has been, since the end of the war the most intense communist propaganda both within the Soviet Union and in foreign countries directed against the United States. This country is daily being pictured as war-mongering, imperialistic, reactionary, fascist, and striving for world domination. This program has culminated recently, and logically, in the formation of a new Comintern directed against the economic rehabilitation of Europe. 

How do we sum up this effort? And what do we conclude from

-7- from it?

We conclude. 

I think, that it is not the result of mere perversity. It is the result of a combination of several ingredients. One of these is the historical objectives of 

Russian governments and the traits and characteristics which Russians for generations have shown in their dealings with other peoples. Another is the need of a police state to have an aggressive foreign policy, a policy which pictures danger from some foreign enemy. A third is the discovery of a new evangelism to which is added discipline. The evangelism is communism which, even to those who do not believe it, furnishes the essential elements of respectability. The discipline is the communist part a discipline which can make foreigners and a foreign country blind devotees of Soviet objectives. 

To be more specific, we might say that Russian policy can be summed up in this way:

First, in areas contiguous with their own where their force is overwhelming and can be used without risk, the policy is to subjugate the contiguous areas by force and then consolidate them into the Soviet system through the use of the 

Communist party.

Second, in non-contiguous areas, the policy is to weaken and, if possible, overthrow the government through the use of the Communist party and create another satellite country, Third, to achieve Communist domination in still other areas, the policy is to out-sit the democracies and, when they become occupied with other matters, such as the economic recession the Soviet Union is counting on, or when democratic. forces become tired, to move in and take control. 

Fourth, to probe wherever there is weakness but to make no trial against strength. Fifth, to use international organization only for their own purposes and to become involved in no international organization which is not susceptible of such use. 

Now if this analysis is true, how do we moet the problem which it presents?

Moscow?" And for another reason. That reason is that unfortunately aggressors are almost always able to pick the time and place and circumstances of their aggression. I take it we repudiated it when we signed the Charter of the United Nations. 1 In meeting the Russian problem we must have both négative and positive policies. On the negative side, first, we must not get jittery. We must not allow ourselves to be duped into believing that the Russians, who emerged only a little more than a quarter of a century ago from a medieval form of government, represent the "wave of the future" while the western democracies, who have for centuries been hard at the business of evolving the infinitely intricate ideas and patterns of progressive democracy, have suddenly become cruel, intolerant and barbarous. 

Secondly, on the negative side, we must not expect a quick solution or a showdown. This will be a long affair. It will take emotional stability and steadfastness. We must not believe that the fundamental problem between us and the Soviet Union, which is the inherent expansionism and aggressiveness of communism, is soluble by the simple expedient of an afternoon of talking with any of the men in the Kremlin. Not only is the push of foreign Communist parties a weapon of great value in Russian eyes but it is something which the Russians cannot stop. 

For this idcological evangelism is the fig leaf of respectability which covers a brutal dictatorship. Without Communist ideology, Stalin is no different from Peter the Great or Ivan the Terrible. This Communist ideology not only furnishes the allies which the Soviet Union has abroad but gives it the essential element of respectability. The Russians could not drop it if they wanted to, and quite obviously they would not want to.

These are the negative guides, but they are of vital importance.

There are positive guides also. The most important is the most

most obvious. We must heed and follow the ancient advice, "Physician, heal thyself." This country cannot deal with the problem we have been discussing if we allow ourselves to fall here at home into political division, economic recession or social stagnation. We must continue to move forward in all of these fields. We must continue to be united in foreign policy. 

We must realize that the solution of our own internal economic difficulties is a matter of essential self-preservation and we must realize that the best answer to Soviet propaganda is social progress at home.

We must steer a middle course between the two extremist schools of thought: those on the one hand who would appease 

Russia, and those on the other who would engage in strong-arm moasures, including possibly the use of the atomic bomb. Either appeasement or hot-tempered action would aggravate, certainly not cure the problem. It is rather through adherence to principles that we shall find the solution. I personally have no doubt that the democracies of the world will produce minds in sufficient numbers that are astute enough to see the essentials of our problem and with intellectual guts enough to see us through it to our greatest hope, a lasting and progressive peace.
