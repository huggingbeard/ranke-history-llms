TAXATION FOR PUBLIC EDUCATION IN VIRGINIA 

Annual Address by Christopher B. Garnett, President, to the Virginia 

State Bar Association, Roanoke, Va., August 3, 1944

INTRODUCTION 

The general assembly, at its session in 1944, passed a joint-resolution providing for the appointment of a commission to study the advisability of a sales and use tax, the method of distribution and division between the Commonwealth and localities, as a means of financing public schools, and for the relief of real estate. It also passed another joint resolution providing for the appointment of a commission to make a thorough and complete study of free schools in Virginia. 

The legislative advisory council was directed to make a study of the entire field of State and local taxation. 

The creation of these commissions, the work which they are doing, and the activities of the legislative advisory council have stirred up in the State of Virginia a great interest in, if not anxiety in regard to, the subject of tax legislation. There is a strong demand in the State. for increased appropriations for public schools, both for those of primary grade and for high schools. It is also anticipated that, when the war is over and our boys at the front come back home, they will demand, and will be entitled to, increased opportunities to attend. the various State-supported institutions offering education above the high schools. Plans have already been made to increase the facilities at these institutions. This will demand increased appropriations, both capital and annual. From what source will these funds be derived?

As an aid to the proper solution of this question it is the purpose of this paper to review the history of taxation in Virginia for public education.

NO APPROPRIATIONS FOR PUBLIC EDUCATION IN COLONIAL TIMES 

It is probably well known to most of this audience that there was no such thing as education at public expense in Virginia prior to the Revolution. The attitude of those who controlled the destinies of the Colony of Virginia toward public education is exemplified by the attitude of Sir William Berkeley and Lord Culpeper, while they were 

Governors of the Colony.

In 1670 twenty-three "enquiries" were addressed to the Governor of Virginia, Sir William Berkeley, by the Lords Commissioners of Foreign Plantations. Sir William Berkeley's reply to these "enquiries" are found in 2 Henning's Statutes at Large, page 511, et seq.

POLL TAX AN UNFAIR SYSTEM OF TAXATION 

One reason why no attempts were made in the Colonial legislatures to make provision for public education was the unfair and burdensome method of taxation which existed from 1619 to 1775, which put the burden of the government on the poor, while no provision was made for their education or social improvement. During this long period the Colonial Government was supported partly by duties on imports of selected articles and exports, mostly of tobacco, but almost entirely by poll taxes, which consisted of so many pounds of tobacco upon tithable persons. The number of pounds varied from 1 pound in 

1619 to 47 pounds in 1684; 10 pounds in 1775; 14 pounds in 1777..

Tithable persons were usually white male persons above the age of 16 years and Negro males (and sometimes females) above the age of 16.

As shown above, the poll or capitation tax was the principal source of revenue, and, as no property tax was imposed, the rich paid exactly the same as the poor, and the result was a great and unequal burden was placed upon the poor people to support the government of Virginia while the large planters were practically escaping taxation. 

BACON'S REBELLION CAUSED BY BURDENSOME POLL TAX 

One hundred years before the Revolution, the imposition of the poll tax was so burdensome that it was one of the moving causes of Bacon's Rebellion. Gyles Bland, one of the leaders of Bacon's Rebellion, on the eve of the rebellion wrote: 2 2 Hen. 518.

They complain that great taxes are imposed upon them every yeare, by wayes very unequall, laying them heavily, by the poll, whereby the poorer sorte are in the hardest condition.

Bland suggested that all inequality might be eliminated by adopting a land tax which, he stated, seemsto be the most equal imposition and will generally take off the complaint of the people, although perhaps, some of the richest sort will not like it, who hold greater proportions of land than they actually plant. 

The King's commissioners agreed in theory but thought the imposition of tax "impracticable there." 3

After Bacon's Rebellion was suppressed, Bland was one of the leaders to whom Governor Berkeley refused a pardon and he was executed as a traitor, because he had the audacity to urge that taxation for the support of the government should be graded according to the wealth of the taxpayer and not collected by the poll.

PROPERTY TAX INSTITUTED IN 1775 

Although a tax on land was resorted to as a temporary emergency during the French and Indian wars, it was not until July 1775 that the convention which met at Richmond made a revolutionary reform in taxation when it adopted an ordinance providing not only for a poll tax but also for a tax "for defense" upon lands "of four shillings for every hundred acres of land and after that for a greater or lesser quantity," and a tax upon coaches and carriages. With this beginning in 1775, a permanent system of taxation of lands and of tangible personal property was soon adopted by the 

Commonwealth of Virginia.

MODIFIED POLL TAX-PURPOSE UNDER CONSTITUTION OF 1902 

After this system became firmly imbedded in the tax system of Virginia, the State no longer relied upon the poll tax as its chief means of support for the government, but a modified form of poll tax was continued and is contained in our present constitution. The constitution of 18529 provided for a capitation tax equal to the tax assessed on land of the value of $200 to be levied on every white male inhabitant who had attained the age of 21, and one-half of the capitation tax was applied to the promotion of education in primary and free schools. By the constitution of 1869 10 the general assembly was authorized to levy a poll tax not exceeding $1 per annum to be applied exclusively in aid of public free schools. 

Under the constitution of 1902, as amended in 1928, the general assembly is required to levy a State capitation tax of not to exceed $1.50 per annum on every resident of the State not less than 21 years of age except those pensioned by the State for military services, $1 of which is to be applied exclusively in the aid of the public free schools, 50 cents to be returned to the county or city in which it was collected. This capitation tax, however, is not a lien upon, nor can it be collected by legal process from property exempt under the poor debtor's law.

The constitutional provision with reference to the elective franchise and qualification for office provides that a citizen of the United States, having the necessary qualifications of age and residence, may register, provided he has paid his State poll taxes assessed against him for 3 years next preceding that year in which he offers to register, or, if he of against him 1, $1.50 in satisfaction of the first year's poll tax assessed

Thus it is clear that, under the constitution of 1902, the purpose of assessing a poll tax is not to raise revenue but to restrict the elective franchise and, while the motive assigned was to prevent fraud in elections, the real purpose was to prevent the Negro from voting.

ACT OF 1796 NULLIFIED BY A PROVISO 

Jefferson, in 1779, drew a bill for the purpose, but he was so far ahead of his time that he could not get support in the legislature for even a meager beginning of public education. In 1796, however, the legislature passed an act to establish public schools.12 This act provided for the election in each county of three aldermen, whose duty it was to carry out the subsequent provisions of the bill, namely to divide the county into sections so that they might contain a convenient number of children to make up a school. The householders of every section were to meet and choose the most convenient place for building a schoolhouse. Provision was made for the subjects to be taught and for the salary of the teacher, with the expense of building and repairing the schoolhouse to be defrayed by the inhabitants of each county in proportion to the amount of their public assessments and county levies. Attached to the bill, however, was a provisothat the court of each county, at which a majority of the acting magistrates thereof shall be present, shall first determine the year in which the first election of said aldermen shall be made, and until they so determine no such election shall be made, and the court of each county shall annually, until each election bc made, at their court in the month of March, take this subject into consideration and decide thereon.13 In 1816, Jefferson, having in mind this proviso, wrote the following: 

The experience of twenty years has proved that no court will ever begin it. 

The reason is obvious. The members of the Court are the wealthy members of the counties; and as the expenses of the schools are to be defrayed by a contribu tion proportioned to the aggregate of other taxes which everyone pays, they consider it as a plan to educate the poor at the expense of the rich.14 

Only one county ever established a public school under the provisions of the act.15

EFFORTS FOR PUBLIC EDUCATION IN CONVENTION OF 1829-30 

The cause of public free school education in Virginia, since the time when Thomas Jefferson and a few of his contemporaries attempted the impossible and failed to arouse among the people of Virginia support for public education by taxing themselves, has been a long, strenuous, and difficult one.

When the constitutional convention of 1829-30 met in Richmond, it had among its membership some of the most brilliant minds in the Commonwealth. The delegates included John Marshall, of Richmond; John S. Barbour, of Culpeper; Benjamin Watkins Leigh, of 

Chesterfield; Alexander Campbell, of Brooke; Philip P. Barbour, of 

Orange; John Tyler, of Charles City; James Madison, of Orange; 

James Monroe, of Loudoun; William H. Fitzhugh, of Fairfax; and 

Littleton W. Tazewell, of Norfolk.

There were three questions upon which the members differed and usually the differences were based upon sectional interests. The first question was as to the method of representation in the legislature. 

The eastern part of the State had a majority and wanted to retain it by giving the geographical districts a fixed number of representatives. 

The western section wanted representation to be based on the white population. The second question was the question of suffrage. Here the people of the western portion of the State strove to secure a provision in the Constitution abolishing property qualifications for suffrage and providing for free, white manhood suffrage. In both cases the East was successful in preventing such reforms. As an incident of the effort to secure free white manhood suffrage, Alexander 

Campbell, of Brooke, and Charles Morgan, of Monongalia, tried in vain to secure a provision for a system of public education.28 

CONVENTION OF 1850-51 REFUSED TO PROVIDE FOR PUBLIC EDUCATION 

When the convention of 1850-51 met to write a new constitution, the leading questions before the convention were, first, whether property qualifications for suffrage should be abolished; and, second, whether the convention would establish in the constitution a system of public education. The first question was answered in the affirmative; the second, in the negative.

John R. Thompson, editor of the Southern Literary Messenger, in an address before the literary societies of Washington College, June 18, 1850, used the following language: 29 A convention is soon to assemble to retouch and modify the Constitution under which we live. Without presuming to inquire into the counsels by which the deliberations of that body shall be guided, it is not too much to assume that great changes will be made in the right of suffrage; that this inestimable privilege of a freeman, indeed, will be universally extended without regard to property qualifications. Now if a change of such magnitude as this is to be made, should we not look well to its consequences, and see to it that those upon whom this privilege is to be bestowed are well qualified to use it to advantage? If we cast pearls to swine, we may expect that they will turn again and rend us. * *

If it require all our energies in all time to come, to teach the people the rudiments of knowledge, and progress in the arts be incompatible with universal education, then I say, be it so. As indicated above, while the convention abolished property qualifications as a prerequisite for suffrage, no provision was made for a system of public education in the constitution of 1850-51.

His messages to the legislature and the support of his aims by other prominent Virginians resulted in a convention in Richmond, on August 28, 1845. This convention urged the adoption of a plan for public education which was evidently the basis of the act passed by the legislature in 1845.30

The requirement that, before a public free-school system could be adopted in any county, it must receive the assent of two-thirds of the voters, and that, after the system had been adopted it might be discontinued, by the vote of a majority, damned the system from the very beginning; and the statute might be denominated another gesture in favor of public education rather than an effort to bring about such a system. Thus in 1845, as in Jefferson's time, the legislature threw a sop to the liberal forces striving for public education, but effectively postponed the accomplishment of this reform to the time when a war and social revolution brought about this result. 

Every effort for a system of public education in Virginia before the War between the States failed because the people of Virginia were unwilling to tax themselves for that purpose, and were willing to appropriate for public education only those funds exacted from malefactors or derived from lost and escheated property. 

CONSTITUTION OF 1869-70 

By the constitution of 1869-70, the general assembly was obligated to establish a uniform system of public free schools and to provide for its gradual, equal, and full introduction into all of the counties of the State by the year 1876, or as much earlier as practicable; and, for the establishment and maintenance of public free schools, the general assembly was also required to apply the annual interest on the literary fund, the capitation tax, and an annual tax upon property of not less. than 1 mill or more than 5 mills on the dollar for the equal benefit of all the people in the State, the number of children between the ages 

31 Code of 1860, ch. LXXXII, p. 424 et seq.

of 5 and 21 years in each public free school district being the basis of such division. And each county and public free school district was authorized to raise additional sums by a tax on property for the support of public free schools.32

Provision was made by the legislature 3 to carry out the terms of the constitution, and providing for a board of education, the superintendent of public instruction and county superintendents of schools, and district school trustees. The board of education consisted of the 

Governor, the superintendent of public instruction, and the attorney general.

The first superintendent chosen was Dr. William H. Ruffner. He devoted his life and all of the powers of his mind and heart to the establishment of the system. The activities of the board of education and the growth of the system established by it were slow and painful. 

The people were impoverished by war and saddled with a large public debt. It is not surprising, therefore, that Dr. Ruffner and his assistants met with almost insurmountable obstructions in establishing a system for both the white and the colored children. 

A study of the meager appropriations made by the legislature in the early life of the system will show clearly the difficulties. 

In the first year 72 counties voted a local tax and 25 counties did not vote at all.34 From 1870 to 1879 the legislature diverted to other objects a large portion of the fund which arose from taxation for public education; and in 1877 there was found to be a deficit of $550,000 due to the diversion of school funds.35 Dr. Ruffner's estimate of the diversion was $1,113,052.56, but finally the State acknowledged a debt of $382,732.26. The contest was a severe one between the friends of public education and its foes. Finally this was settled in 1880, and since that time, in spite of strong sentiment against the system, it has gradually increased in power and the confidence of the people.

APPROPRIATIONS FOR 1944 

In the Acts of Assembly for 1944, the appropriations for the department of education for the first year of the biennium for the maintenance of free public schools was $12,287,000; and for the second year, $12,455,000. In addition, for the first year for the expenses of the State board of education, the appropriation is $296,421, and for the second year, $297,600.37

The department of education also has given the following information:

There was appropriated for general administration, supervision, and special educational activities, including Federal funds, carried on by the State Department of Education, an additional amount so that the total State and Federal funds administered by the State Department of Education will amount to $16,206,941 for the year which began July 1, 1944. 

In addition The localities will spend during the current fiscal year at least $22,000,000, probably more, making a grand total of $38,206,941. 

NEEDS OF THE DEPARTMENT OF EDUCATION 

The superintendent of public instruction, in speaking of the needs of his department for the future, has made this statement: 

In planning for the period that will follow the termination of the war, we must, first of all, take into consideration the building needs of the schools. According to a survey made recently by the Division of School Buildings of the State Department, it appears from reports made by thirty-five or forty school divisions, that there will be an expenditure of from $15,000,000 to $20,000,000 very soon after the cessation of hostilities. If this is the case, it seems likely that there will be large demands made upon the Literary Fund for loans, particularly as the interest rate has been reduced to two percent. This brings us to the question of the advisability of the proposed Constitutional amendment, which will freeze the Literary Fund at $10,000,000. The corpus of the fund is now approximately $11,500,000, and it is increasing at the rate of approximately $800,000 a year. 

It seems probable that with the oustanding loans and the demands that will come, we could quite readily use a Literary Fund, amounting to $15,000,000 or $20,000,000. The proposed amendment, as I understand it, permits the legislature either to leave all amounts going into the Literary Fund in the corpus of the fund, or to appropriate any amounts above $10,000,000 to the schools for operation and maintenance, or for the support of teacher retirement. This is the question that has to be answered by a vote of the people in November, since it has already passed two sessions of the General Assembly.

PUBLIC WORKS IN VIRGINIA BEFORE 1860 

About 1816 the Legislature of Virginia began to enact a series of statutes relating to public works which provided for the construction of internal improvements by contributions in the proportion of threefifths by private capital or subscription and two-fifths by public money or subscription.38 These public works included canals, turnpikes, and railroads. The canals thus constructed included the James River, Dismal Swamp, Appomattox, and Potomac Canals. Indeed, the legislature generously subscribed to the Chesapeake & Ohio Canal which was constructed in the Potomac River Valley and entirely contained in the State of Maryland. The railroads included the roads which were the beginnings of the present Chesapeake & Ohio; the Norfolk & Western; Atlantic Coast Railway; the Southern Railway; and the

Richmond, Fredericksburg & Potomac Railway. The turnpikes included the Valley Turnpike, the Little River Turnpike from Alexandria to Middleburg, and the turnpike from Fairfax to Warrenton. 

Indeed, the public debt of Virginia, existing at the end of the Civil War, consisted of obligations incurred for the construction of these public works or internal improvements.39

PUBLIC WORKS ADMINISTRATION COMPARED WITH VIRGINIA'S INTERNAL IMPROVEMENTS 

It is interesting to compare the history of public works instituted by the Federal Government after the debacle of 1929-33, with the public works instituted by Virginia, beginning in 1816, and paralleling the efforts made for public education. When the Civil War ended, the canals were dry. The railroads had been denuded of their rails and the turnpikes had no surface and were not in use. All of the railroads except the Richmond, Fredericksburg & Potomac were put, by a Federal court, through receivership proceedings, and at the end thereof the private subscribers and the Ŝtate were deprived of all their interest in these railroads; so that, at present, of all the assets which were created by the public works in Virginia between 1816 and 1860, the State has lost all of its interest except a small interest in the Richmond, Fredericksburg & Potomac Railroad from Richmond to Quantico.

The institution of public works by the Federal Government after 1933 was for two distinct purposes, first, to give work to the people who were idle; and, second, to enable the capital which had been invested in building and allied industries to be profitably employed in work that was beneficial to the community. Another purpose was to assist the schools and colleges, cities and towns, and other public institutions in furnishing facilities for the improvement of the health, morals, and intelligence of the community. 

I am not familiar with this whole program in Virginia but I do know that the Public Works Administration made grants to the University of Virginia for its library, engineering building, arts museum, hospital, Naval Reserve Officers Training Corps building and for rebuilding the rotunda, amounting to $813,000; and the construction and the reconstruction work done on these buildings amounts to $2,114,000. 

I am sure that the other State institutions received grants in equal proportion; and one has only to traverse the highways of Virginia to see in nearly every county, and certainly in every town and city, magnificent public school buildings which were constructed as a result, in part, of the grants by the Public Works Administration. 

These are internal improvements and public works whose effects will live and grow with the ages.

CONCLUSION 

In my view, the time is not far distant when "grim visaged war will smooth his wrinkled front," and universal peace will- 

Lie like a shaft of light across the land,

And like a lane of beams athwart the sea, Thro' all the circle of the golden year.

The thoughts of men will turn from plans for destruction to plans of reconstruction, regeneration, and rehabilitation.

To aid in this work, the legislature, at its last session, provided for the appointment of a commission to make a thorough and complete study of the system of public free schools, and that body, under the chairmanship of Dr. George H. Denny, formerly president, now chancellor of the University of Alabama, is already at work, and will doubtless discover many ways in which our school system can be improved and strengthened. This will call for additional expenditures. 

Many of the boys who entered the Army when they were 18 had not finished a primary course, and hardly any of them had entered college. When they return, provision must be made to enable them to make up this deficiency. 

Certainly the Commonwealth of Virginia, proud of her glorious past, will be willing now to make whatever sacrifice may be necessary to bring her educational system up to the highest standard, in order that her returning soldiers and her growing sons and daughters may have the best method of educating and training their minds and bodies.
