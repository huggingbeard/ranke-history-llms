1. 

Introduction 

The Committee on Court Delay in New York City was formed at the invitation of the State Administrator in 

November, 1971, to explore the capacity of the courts in New York City to meet the speedy trial standards adopted by the Administrative Board in 1971, and to make specific recommendations as to additional resources and new procedures which may be required.

All of the agencies and individuals listed on the cover page as members of the Committee have contributed in some manner to the preparation of this report. In addition, members of working staff teams have been in touch with and derived benefits from the suggestions of the New York City Bureau of the Budget, the Chairman of the Committee on the Administration of Criminal 

Justice of the New York State Bar Association, the Assistant

Chairman of the Special Committee on Criminal Justice of the 

Association of the Bar of the City of New York, and Dr. 

Michael Wong, space consultant. 

Finally, the Committee's efforts have been guided from time to time by the advice of Counsel to the Governor, Counsel to the Temporary 

President of the Senate, and Counsel to the Speaker of the 

Assembly.

The Committee's principal concern throughout has been the processing of felony cases which are carried into the Supreme Court, since problems in meeting the Administrative Board rules will occur almost exclusively with respect subcommittees were formed to these cases. Two working to formulate resource requirements and to draft uniform rules, respectively.

The mandate of the resources subcommittee was to "recommend in specific terms what resources will be needed by the Supreme Court and all ancillary services in each of the four principal boroughs, taking into account the resources added by the Narcotics Court grant in that borough, [and to] determine how much these resources will cost and where they will be housed." 

The mandate of the subcommittee on court rules and sanctions was to "devise and draft rules for proceedings on the criminal side in the Supreme Court within New York City from the filing of an indictment to the sentencing of a defendant, with special regard for the speedy disposition of cases, [and to] devise fair, workable sanctions for attorneys (whether prosecution or defense) who violate those rules."

3. Resource Needs for 1972-1973 

In this section, estimates are made of the resources needed in the Supreme Court in each of the four principal counties within New York City. These estimates are divided into resources required prior to the filing of an indictment and resources required thereafter. They are based on data relating to current backlogs, projected felony charge intake during 1972, and Grand Jury and Supreme Court disposition rates.

Resource recommendations are keyed to at least maintaining current time to disposition rates for felony charge cases entering the courts while significantly decreasing existing Grand Jury and Supreme Court backlogs. The and cannot analysis does not guarantee that all cases will be processed within the speedy trial standard after new resources have been added. However, the Committee is of the opinion that the addition of the resources recommended, coupled with the innovations and the administrative reforms discussed in Section 4, infra, will result in substantially faster disposition of felony charge cases. 4. Proposals for Expediting the Processing of Felony Cases 

In discussing the complexities of reducing backlogs and delays in the major urban courts, the 1972 New York 

State Comprehensive Crime Control Plan recognizes that the infusion of new resources is not a total answer:

"To some extent, delay can be attributed to a lack of resources. An infusion into the criminal justice system of more judges and clerks, more courtrooms, more assistant district attorneys, more defense attorneys and more probation officers might make it possible at least in the short run to process more cases and thus cut down on backlog and delay. 

Clearly, however, a massive investment in additional resources cannot be justified in the absence of sound evidence that existing resources are being effectively utilized and that practical assessments have been made about what new resources are truly needed and how they might best be allocated". 

1972 Comprehensive Crime Control Plan, pp. D-2 and 3.

The Division of Criminal Justice (for the State) 

and the Criminal Justice Coordinating Council (for the City) have been working with all agencies and persons concerned with the criminal courts and related agencies, and a number of innovative programs have been funded by the Crime Control Planning Board out of Federal "Safe 

Streets" funds. The Division and CJCC will continue to work closely with the courts and others to develop a number of significant programs aimed at enabling the criminal courts in New York City to meet the speedy trial standards as soon as possible. 

The following subsections describe in general terms a number of ideas which should be explored by criminal justice planners in 1972.

(a) Improved Case Screening 

The district attorneys in New York City have established complaint screening operations in the Criminal Court which have conserved prosecution and court resources by culling out prior to arraignment those arrests in which conviction is impossible or highly unlikely. Similarly, Criminal Court bureaus have identified large numbers of felony complaint cases in which a misdemeanor plea is acceptable, thus reducing the burdens of grand juries and the Supreme Court.

However, the concept of case screening, or "early case assessment", as it is called in the 1972 Comprehensive 

Crime Control Plan, goes far beyond complaint desk operations by a district attorney and may be an appropriate basis for a series of far-reaching, innovative programs in the City. 

Several ideas are involved: (1) "Case assessment" involves not only the notion of appropriate charging, but also the thought that all parties necessary to an appropriate disposition of a case should be brought together as soon after arrest as possible to discuss the issues and possibly dispose of the case on the basis of full information about it. 

Both notions presuppose that experienced assistants and experienced defense counsel should be assigned to early case screening, preparation and assessment roles.

sta of i-

(2) "Case assessment" also involves clearly stated policies by the district attorney as to the kinds of cases which merit indictment and vigorous prosecution in the Supreme Court and which do not. If experienced assistants are in decision-making roles, a greater proportion of the cases ultimately sent to the grand jury can be presented sooner by way of direct presentment. 

And if major, violent felonies which are not now handled by a specialized unit are processed by a "major offenses bureau", as will soon be the case in Kings County under a Federal grant, the result may be prompter and sounder dispositions of these cases. (3) "Case assessment" may be appropriate at later stages in the process. (b) Early Notification of Grand Jury Witnesses 

Closely related to the concept of improved case screening is the idea of early notification of Grand Jury witnesses. The early case assessment concept involves the interviewing of prosecution witnesses by experienced assistants as soon after an arrest as possible. In cases where the assistant determines that the case is an appropriate one for Grand Jury action, it makes sense to set a date certain for presentation to the Grand Jury and to notify witnesses at the time of the interview. 

In the usual felony case, there are only two times when it is relatively easy to interview prosecution witnesses and arrange for them to appear before the Grand Jury: (1) immediately after an arrest is made, when prosecution witnesses first appear at the courthouse; and (2) at the preliminary hearing, if one is held. If witnesses are not notified of a date for Grand Jury appearance on one of these occasions, notification must be made subsequently by telephone, mail, or service of a subpoena by a process server. Inevitably, some witnesses are never notified and others are notified only after considerable delay.

If witnesses can be notified of the date for their Grand Jury appearance at the time of their initial appearance at the courthouse, or at the time of the preliminary hearing,

much time can be saved in the processing of felony cases. 

In some instances, it might even be possible to have 

Grand Jury presentations the same day 

particularly if a Grand Jury were to hold its sessions during the late afternoon or early evening hours. In any event, it certainly should be possible, except in highly unusual circumstances, to arrange for witnesses to appear before the Grand Jury within a period of one to three weeks after notification. Bronx County, which according to the time lapse data is the most efficient in processing cases at the pre-indictment stage, has already made considerable use of early notification procedures. In recent months, Kings County has also experimented with such procecedures and preliminary evidence indicates that pre-indictment delays have been considerably reduced as a result. 

If speedy trial rule standards are to be met, the time lapses between arrest and the filing of an indictment must be reduced to a minimum. Toward this end, the Committee recommends a concerted effort to schedule Grand Jury presentations as soon as possible after the initial arrest. Court Management 

Elsewhere in this report, the Committee recommends that substantial new resources be made available to the courts and court-related agencies in New York City.

If the changes this Committee recommends are to be successfully implemented, there must be clear lines of administrative authority and responsibility within the unified court system and competent staff work at all levels. 

While the primary focus of the Committee has been the courts within New York City, the following discussion applies in part to court administration for New York State as a whole, for progress in achieving speedy trials in New York City depends in large measure on effective judicial policy making and implementation at the 

Administrative Board- State Administrator level. 

Under the State's Constitution, as amended in 1961, the Administrative Board is responsible for the administrative supervision of the unified court system.

In fulfilling this responsibility, the Board must establish "standards and administrative policies for general application throughout the State". 

Consistent with these

standards and policies, "the appellate divisions shall supervise the administration and operation of the courts in their respective departments". 

Article VI, Section 28. 

Despite this clear, hierarchical structure, few standards have been devised thus far by the Board, and there is considerable doubt among judicial and nonjudicial administrators as to who has authority to make decisions of various kinds. This uncertainty is not new and was summed up succinctly in a management survey done for the Judicial Conference in 1965: "There is an urgent need for greater clarity and consistency in functional assignments and relationships within the state system of judicial management. Widespread uncertainty exists regarding the roles and responsibilities of the judicial conference, administrative board, state administrator, appellate divisions, presiding justices, directors of administration, and administrative judges." Survey of

Functional Organization and Administrative 

Practices of the Court System of the State of 

New York. Booz, Allen & Hamilton, Inc., March 15, 1965, p. II-10 (hereinafter referred to as "Booz, Allen Survey").

In New York City, the management consultant found, decision-making is particularly cumbersome, due to the presence of two appellate divisions: "Although close cooperation has marked the administrative relationships of the appellate divisions of the first and second departments, the existing structure poses some formidable obstacles to effective management. Excessive coordination is called for, making the decisionmaking process protracted and cumbersome.

The formulation of decisions customarily requires the concurrence of the directors of administration of both departments, both presiding justices (acting pursuant to policies of their appellate divisions) and frequently the state administrator. This dispersion of responsibility inhibits the development of progressive and dynamic management policies geared toward integrated administration of the metropolitan court system." Booz, Allen Survey, p. II-5.

To achieve a clarification of authority, the Administrative Board should set goals, review and adopt a plan for achieving these goals and delegate to the State Administrator the authority to see that the plan is properly implemented. The delegation to the State Administrator should include express authority to assign duties to the departmental administrators in implementing the plan, as well as the authority to supervise the departmental administrators' efforts to carry out their assigned duties.

Appendix A Uniform Rules for Criminal Terms of the Supreme Court in the City of New York 1. 

Criminal Parts 

In the Supreme Court in each of the five counties within the City of New York, a criminal term part structure shall be established, consisting of the following: arraignment part; pre-trial conference part; and hearing-trial part. In New York, Kings, Queens and Bronx Counties, there shall be at least one arraignment, one pre-trial conference and one hearing-trial part in operation during each month of the year in each county and as many additional parts as the appropriate Presiding Justice may direct in a particular county. In Richmond 

County, the Presiding Justice of the Second Judicial 

Department may designate such number of separate arraignment, pre-trial conference and hearing-trial parts, or parts combining the functions of one or more of such parts, as he deems necessary to process the current criminal caseload of said county in an expeditious manner. 

The arraignment part serves three functions: (a) to hear and dispose of all matters associated with the arraignment of a defendant on an indictment; 1-a

2-a (b) (c) to hear and dispose of pre-trial motions by either party in which the taking of testimony is not called for, or in which there is an insufficient basis in the factual allegations of the moving party for a hearing requiring testimony; and to hear and dispose of other matters and applications which are not required to be heard in a pre-trial conference part or a hearing-trial part. 

The primary purpose of a pre-trial conference part is the prompt resolution of those cases in which a disposition can be agreed to by the defendant, the prosecutor and the Court. Insofar as possible, therefore, a pretrial conference justice shall be relieved of calendar management work, and, except for matters which must be returned to him for consideration, he shall be available for matters other than the pre-trial conferencing of cases only to the extent that no such cases are or can promptly be made ready for discussion.

Hearings on all motions requiring the taking of testimony and all trials shall be conducted in a hearing-trial part. 

Other matters and applications shall be heard in a hearingtrial part only to the extent that the hearing-trial justice is the only judge who can deal with the matter, or there are no cases which are or can promptly be made ready for trial.

3-a 2. 

Assignment of Justices to Criminal Terms 

The Presiding Justice of the appropriate appellate division, or such justice or justices with administrative duties relating to criminal parts as he shall designate, shall make all assignments of justices to criminal term parts. In making such assignments, the aptitude, interest and experience of judges in criminal work will be important considerations, and no assignment to a criminal term part in any county shall be made until the Presiding Justice or his designee has conferred with and received the recommendations of a council for that county consisting of the district attorney, the president of the Legal Aid Society, or their designees, and a representative of the private bar.

Insofar as possible, any assignment of a justice to a pre-trial conference part shall be for a minimum of two months, and any assignment of a justice to a hearingtrial part shall be for a minimum of six months. 

Inter-county transfers of justices with criminal term responsibilities will be avoided whenever possible. 3. 

Assignment of Cases to a Pre-Trial Conference Part 

Any case which is not disposed of at arraignment by dismissal or a plea of guilty shall be referred to a pre-trial conference part for a date and time certain; provided, that where an issue or matter, such as the competency of the defendant or the obtaining or assignment

4-a of counsel must be resolved before a case can be discussed productively in a pre-trial conference part, a case shall not be so referred until such issue or matter is resolved. 

In any county in which there are two or more pretrial conference parts, the arraigning justice shall assign indictments among such parts so that caseloads are approximately even; provided, that all indictments arising out of the same occurrence or transaction shall be assigned to the same pre-trial conference part, and that cases assigned to one of the specialized units of the district attorney's office shall be assigned to the same pre-trial conference part. 4. 

Proceedings in the Pre-trial Conference Part 

(a) Initial Appearance

A case must be scheduled for its initial appearance in a pre-trial conference part within seven calendar days after arraignment unless later scheduled for good cause.

At the initial appearance, a conference shall be held among the pre-trial conference justice, an assistant district attorney and counsel for the defendant. The conference may, in the discretion of the pre-trial conference justice, be held in Chambers. The defendant or defendants shall be present in court and be available for consultation. No witnesses will be required to be present.

5-a The parties shall discuss the issues in the case to determine whether an agreed disposition is possible. 

The assistant shall make an offer to the defendant through his counsel for the disposition of the case. 

Such offer shall be specific as to Penal Law section or sections, the position of the prosecutor, if any, with respect to sentence, and charges or counts to be covered or dismissed. If requested by defendant's counsel, the pre-trial conference justice must indicate whether the prosecutor's offer, if agreed to by the defendant, will be acceptable to the Court and, as specifically as possible, what sentence he would impose, subject to the contents of the presentence report or any other new information. It is appropriate for the pretrial conference justice to grant plea and sentence concessions when the interest of the public in the prompt and effective administration of criminal justice will be served thereby. (b) 

Second Appearance 

Any case which is not disposed of at the initial appearance may, if the defendant requests, be adjourned for a second appearance in the pre-trial conference part. 

The second conference shall be scheduled within seven calendar days after the initial conference unless later scheduled for good cause. If a case is not disposed of after two appearances in a pre-trial conference part,

6-a it shall forthwith be referred to a hearing-trial part. 

(c) Proceedings at the Conclusion of a Pre- Trial Conference. 

At the conclusion of any pre-trial conference, after defense counsel has had a reasonable opportunity to convey to the defendant or defendants the offer made by the prosecutor and explain matters discussed during the conference, the pre-trial conference justice shall apprise the defendant or defendants of the offer made by the prosecutor and shall note each defendant's response on the court papers. 5. 

Pre-Trial Motions 

All pre-trial motions shall be made within seven calendar days after the final appearance of a case in a pre-trial conference part. All such motions, with all necessary affidavits, affirmations, exhibits and memoranda of law, shall be included within the same set of motion papers and shall be made returnable on the same date in the arraignment part. Motions

7-a shall be made returnable within ten calendar days after they are made.

Any pre-trial motion which is not made within seven calendar days after the final appearance of a case in a pre-trial conference part shall be deemed waived by the defendant; provided, that the Court shall entertain and decide on its merits, at any time prior to entry of a plea of guilty or the end of trial, any pre-trial motion based upon grounds which the defendant could not, with due diligence, have been previously aware, or which, for other good cause, could not reasonably have been raised within seven days after the final appearance of a case in the pre-trial conference part. Any application to make a motion beyond the time limit set forth hereinabove shall be made before the justice in the hearing-trial part to which the case is assigned. 

6.Assignment of Cases to a Hearing-trial Part 

All cases which are not disposed of in a pre-trial conference part shall be referred to a hearing-trial part and scheduled for an initial appearance therein within ten calendar days after the last pre-trial conference appearance unless later scheduled for good cause.

-a Cases shall be assigned among hearing-trial parts so that workloads are approximately even; provided, that all indictments arising out of the same occurrence or transaction shall be assigned to the same hearingtrial part. Nothing herein stated shall prevent a county from operating specialized hearing-trial parts for particular categories of criminal charges. 7. Adjournments

Except for the adjournment referred to in Paragraph 4(b), supra, no case may be adjourned except for good cause.

Whenever a justice grants an adjournment, the reasons therefor and the party chargeable under Rule 29 shall be stated for the record and noted over the justice's initials on the court papers. 8.
