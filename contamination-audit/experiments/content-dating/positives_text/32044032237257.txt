THE JOINT CENTER FOR URBAN STUDIES 

The growing importance of research programs that cut across the traditional boundaries of disciplines is one of the most promising developments in university life. Such programs, by employing a wide range of contemporary research strategies, offer fresh approaches to persistent and complex problems. The stimulation they afford scholars and students enables universities to extend their leadership in teaching and in advancing knowledge. 

Among these research programs, the Joint Center for 

Urban Studies is an ambitious experiment. Uniting two great institutions with their varying but complementary goals and research styles is no easy task. Nevertheless, many scholars and administrators at both Harvard and M.I.T. are convinced that by combining forces they can vastly increase the effectiveness of their activities in urban and regional affairs.

The Joint Center aims to overcome the neglect of basic research on the city and the region. Through research, it aims to help solve some of the practical problems and deal with some of the emerging policy issues facing cities and regions in this country and abroad. Both objectives require that the Joint Center attract the ablest scholars, advanced students, and professional and civic leaders. They also require the collaboration of many disciplines, including technology, architecture and city planning, business, law, the social sciences, and the humanities. Toward these ends, the 

Joint Center for Urban Studies is creating a unique, interuniversity program.

ORGANIZATION AND POLICIES 

The purposes outlined above were laid down in a memorandum of agreement of January 1959, establishing 999522 3

the Joint Center. The memorandum is appended to this report. Formal operations of the Joint Center started on 

April 1, 1959. To establish the basic organization, the Presidents of the two institutions appointed a Director and set up a Faculty Committee consisting of six faculty members from each institution. The Faculty Committee, which includes the Director, formulates the general policies of the Joint Center. It reports through the Director and its Chairman to an Administrative Committee representing the governing bodies of both institutions. The two institutions also established a Visiting Committee of national leaders in business, professional, and civic affairs to review the activities of the Joint Center and advise on how it can best implement its program and contribute to the improvement of the urban environment. The members of these committees are listed in the back of this report, along with the members of the Joint Center's research staff. 

At the start, the criteria for allocation of funds had to be set. The main general support for the Joint Center had come from the Ford Foundation. All the Center's funds could be absorbed by a single study requiring a large staff and large amounts of data. But the Faculty Committee stressed the importance of working in a variety of disciplines. In the interest of providing balance to the program of research as a whole, it seemed advisable to concentrate the general funds of the Joint Center largely in four fields. These fields are: 1) comparative analysis, structure and growth of cities, 2) urban transportation and technology, 3) urban design, 4) urban and regional problems of developing countries. 

They are fields in which application of the resources of the two institutions appeared most likely to produce promising results.

In order to preserve diversity in the program as it developed, the Faculty Committee felt that no more than 20 percent of the annual general funds of the Joint Center should be devoted to research on any one project. There

was general agreement that, especially in the beginning, some resources should be deliberately allocated for exploratory efforts over a wide range of topics. The object was to take soundings to determine which kinds of research would prove to be rewarding for future investigation in greater detail. For larger projects, it was decided that special financing rather than the general funds of the Joint Center should be used. In addition, if the study was an applied one, resulting in policy recommendations, it was to have scholarly and practical significance beyond the particular case. Furthermore, such practical undertakings should be of a kind which universities, not private consultants, are especially equipped to handle, and should generally be projects in which the Joint Center and only the Joint Center is requested to submit a proposal.

RESEARCH ACTIVITIES 

To date, about 80 percent of the general funds of the Joint Center have gone into the four main subject fields mentioned. The individual studies initiated within that broad framework are those which the Director and the Faculty Committee considered important and which at the same time reflected the interests of the faculty and staff available to the Joint Center during this period. 

A. Comparative Analysis, Structure and Growth of Cities 

Some of the greatest gaps in fundamental knowledge are in this field. Many of the difficulties metropolitan areas face today, in this country and abroad, result from rapid growth. This growth is changing or outmoding the pattern of land uses. It influences the scale and the location of industrial and commercial activities. It strains the adequacy of existing housing, utilities, and municipal services. And it is creating enormous challenges for the private firms and

the federal, state, and local agencies which must mobilize new personnel, resources, and techniques of control. 

In the analysis of urban growth and the changing structure of urban land and activities, and in the analysis of other political, economic and social changes in urban life, the literature is based on few or even single cases. Comparative investigations are indispensable to achieve a more adequate understanding of various cities and regions within a country, among different countries, and in different historical periods. 

A variety of studies are under way in this field at the Joint 

Center. Edward C. Banfield, Professor of Government at

Harvard, is concentrating on a comparative study of political party structure, power and leadership patterns, the ethos of the politician and the voter and the political relations between central cities and suburbs, and between central cities and states. These matters are studied within the context of such problems of urban growth and change as housing and renewal, metropolitan organization, race relations, law, city planning, and the development of mass transportation.

Sam B. Warner, Jr., Instructor in History at Harvard, has completed an analysis of the development decisions in early suburbs of the Boston metropolitan area. The study concentrates on the growth of Roxbury, West Roxbury, and 

Dorchester between 1870 and 1900. Warner located the principal house-builders from an examination of 28,000 building permits and attempted to account for the uniformities of the suburbs by relating the findings of economic and social history to the background of these decision makers. 

He is now engaged in a general historical survey of American middle-class life in relation to urban policy in three different time periods: 1760-1780, 1830-1850, and 1900-1920. 

Lloyd Rodwin of M.I.T., Chairman of the Faculty Com mittee, received editing assistance from the Joint Center in the completion of his book, Housing and Economic Progress, an historical case study of the housing problems of middle-

income families during the last century of Boston's growth. 

This study has been published in the Joint Center series. 

Grady Clay, a member of the Joint Center for 1960-1961, is engaged in a study of urban renewal. He is real estate editor of the Louisville Courier-Journal and an editor of Landscape Architecture. During his year's association with the Joint Center, Clay is making an analysis of the changes in the urban landscape, and the political, economic, and other decisions leading to these changes, with particular reference to urban renewal programs in selected cities. 

Charles Abrams, Visiting Professor of Land Economics at 

M.I.T., later in 1961, will work on a related study evaluating governmental policies for urban renewal. 

Martin Anderson, of the School of Industrial Management at M.I.T., holds a Research Fellowship. He will undertake a study of the role of private investment in urban renewal. 

The sources and uses of private funds in renewal programs will be investigated in several large cities, together with an analysis of the financial incentives necessary to induce private developers to undertake such projects. Bernard Frieden, one of the Research Fellows for 1960-1961, is dealing with the problems of renewal in the middle-aged or gray areas of cities, from the point of view of the developer or entrepreneur. 

Charles R. Cherington, Professor of Government at Harvard, has begun an analysis of Boston's Metropolitan District Commission. It is an administrative agency with operating functions crossing corporate boundary lines which has served as a prototype for advocates of metropolitan integration. Although the agency and its antecedents have a history of more than sixty years, the agency has never been carefully studied. 

Charles M. Haar, Professor at the Harvard Law School, is editing a volume comparing British and American law governing the control of land use and development. The 

British material, prepared by several specialists, puts partic-

ular emphasis on the Town and Country Planning Act introduced by the Conservative Party in 1959. The laws are being examined for their city planning implications, with special attention to eminent-domain procedures, in an attempt to illuminate problems of concern to both countries. 

Daniel Lerner, Professor of Sociology at M.I.T., is engaged in an evaluation of differences among the development patterns of French cities. This study will try to explain the reasons for the differences in urban and regional development as between France "A" (La France Dynamique) and 

France "B" (La France Statique) - a classification hypoth esized during the past few years by leading French thinkers such as Maurice Duverger and Francois Goguel. Most characterizations of the two Frances have centered on a comparison between the principal cities representing the two types. For example, Lille is the dynamic type of city which supposedly characterizes France "A"; Bordeaux is the model of the retrograde city which characterizes France "B." Professor Lerner believes there are fruitful issues worth more detailed investigation growing out of this distinction between the two Frances. Such problems as constitutional reform, taxation, investment, and industrial location are being studied in this context. 

Several studies of zoning and urban land markets have also been undertaken. William Doebele, Assistant Research

Professor of Urban Studies at Harvard, has completed a manuscript entitled "Discretion and Certainty in Land-Use 

Controls: The Problem of Non-Euclidean Zoning of Large 

Parcels." William Alonso, Assistant Professor of Regional

B. Urban Transportation and Technology 

Technological change is a strategic aspect of urban development. The technology and analysis of urban trans-

portation have been particularly neglected. Many attempts have been made to deal with traffic circulation, and to minimize the friction and inefficiency of the movement of passengers and goods. These efforts have often failed through a lack of adequate information about sources of traffic generation and about traffic flows in relation to land use and to the social, economic, and political characteristics of urban areas. There is no effective framework for analyzing prospective technological changes in transportation. The same can be said of innovations in housing, waste disposal, public health, education, and other elements of urban and suburban life.

The study in this field closest to policy is being conducted by Robert C. Wood, Associate Professor of Political Science at M.I.T. It was prompted by a request that the Joint 

Center assist the Municipal Manpower Commission, a private, non-profit organization established by the Ford Foundation, in evaluating the manpower problems of municipalities. Wood prepared a reconnaissance report on these needs, taking into account the future size and characteristics of metropolitan communities, with special emphasis on the technological innovations which they must be prepared to reckon with in the future. These requirements are studied in relation to the choices in public policy which the municipal enterprises will have to make. 

Richard L. Meier, of the University of Michigan, who was a member of the Joint Center during 1959-1960, is concerned with new information technologies and their influence on urban life. While at the Joint Center, Professor 

Meier prepared a monograph, entitled "The Theory of 

Urban Growth in a Communications Perspective."

Aaron Fleisher, Lecturer in City and Regional Planning at M.I.T., is engaged in an inquiry into how the form of the city affects its transportation; particularly whether it is possible to prescribe optimum systems of transportation for different patterns of land use and how those optimum sys-

tems compare. Parameters for the city are density and land use, and the transportation system consists of nodes, channels, and capacities. Possible relations are tested by analysis of variance and regression models. The relevance of other analytical procedures such as waiting-line theory and linear programming are also being explored. Fleisher has prepared two papers as incidental aspects of these studies, one dealing with the effect of technology on urban form, and a second, entitled "Transportation and Traffic Prediction."

Roland B. Greeley, Associate Professor of Regional Plan ning at M.I.T., and William W. Nash, Assistant Professor of

City Planning at Harvard, are collaborating to study the interdependence of transportation planning and urban planning. They are attempting to develop a unified set of techniques and principles which can help to integrate these two planning processes. The investigation is supported by the 

U.S. Bureau of Public Roads. 

A. Scheffer Lang, Assistant Professor of Civil Engineering at M.I.T., is investigating the comparative physical characteristics of urban passenger transportation systems. His purpose is to provide transportation planners with information helpful in evaluating rail and other transit proposals on the basis of both technological and economic feasibility. Lang also plans to develop a set of simple models to permit analysis of the relative capacities of different means of transportation, given specific demand situations.

C. Urban Design 

A large proportion of the basic policy decisions affecting cities and regions are ultimately expressed in three dimensions. The Joint Center is especially interested in encouraging a better understanding of the forces shaping the physical environment of cities and the ways in which the component elements of this environment can be designed to serve public purposes. Four studies are now under way 10

which approach problems of design from different points of view.

An analysis undertaken by Kevin Lynch, Associate Professor of City Planning at M.I.T., attempts to analyze citizens' images of the metropolitan region and to determine how environments at the metropolitan scale can be given visual shape and form. The metropolitan image is studied in terms of its nature, its function, and how it may be clarified and strengthened. Earlier, Professor Lynch's book, The 

Image of the City, published by the Technology Press and the Harvard University Press, was the first of the Joint Center series to be published by the two presses. His current work on the visual form of the metropolis and on the aesthetics of the highway is an outgrowth of the research 

Professor Lynch completed for the book. 

Serge Chermayeff, Professor of Architecture at Harvard, has been engaged in a study aimed at designing new kinds of individual urban family houses and new kinds of groupings of these houses which will combine high-density population with amenity and privacy. The title of his manuscript is "Community and Privacy.' 

Eduard F. Sekler, Associate Professor of Architecture at

Harvard, is exploring the extent to which principles of art history may be employed in urban design criticism. Up to this point, urban design criticism has not made use of the methods of morphological analysis in art criticism. 

Finally, John W. Whiting, Bigelow Professor of Education at Harvard, is exploring the Yale University Human Relations Area Files for ethnological materials dealing with community spatial patterns of various preliterate societies. 

These materials have never been brought together and analyzed.

D. Urban and Regional Problems in Developing Countries 

Rapid urban and regional growth in developing coun- 11

tries has caused serious bottlenecks impeding programs of economic and social development. In countries such as 

India, a considerable proportion of national development budgets is being allocated for overhead capital investment which will help to minimize the toll of disease, social disorganization, political disorder, and sheer inefficiency in their sprawling cities. The problems take on added significance because of the importance of these countries in the contemporary world. Such investigations are also likely to shed light on some of the problems of urban growth and development in Western countries. 

Charles Abrams is studying housing problems that result from rapid urbanization in several developing countries. 

The Director of the Joint Center and the Chairman of the Faculty Committee have also worked and written in this field.

The Joint Center expects to start a comprehensive study of the Orinoco-Caroni area in southeastern Venezuela. The confluence of the Orinoco and Caroni Rivers is the center of the undeveloped Guayana region, which has vast resources in high-grade ores, rich gas and oil fields, and a large hydroelectric potential. The Venezuelan government has established a new agency, the Guayana Corporation, and charged it with the task of developing this region. A hydroelectric plant has been built and a steel plant will be completed in 1961. In cooperation with the Guayana Corporation and other government agencies of Venezuela, the Joint Center was asked to analyze the economic structure of this emerging industrial complex. A development plan will be prepared in order to provide an integrated pattern for urban and regional growth. A new industrial city of 200,000 people or more near the junction of the two rivers will be included in the plan. 

For this new city, recommendations will be made for the location of transportation; for the allocation of land to residential, industrial, and other uses; and for schools, public health, cultural facilities and other public services. This 12

study will extend over three years and will begin when a Project Director is selected. 

E. Other Studies 

As already noted, the Joint Center also conducts research which does not fall neatly under the above headings. 

For example, Professor Morton G. White, Professor of

Philosophy at Harvard, is writing a history of attitudes toward the American city of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, as reflected in the writings of major American thinkers ranging from Thomas Jefferson to John Dewey. Mrs.

White is collaborating with him on this study. 

Current legal systems of title registration and title insurance are being evaluated by Ted J. Fiflis. His study will make recommendations for more efficient assurance of title, leading to a reduction in the cost of conveyancing for housing.

Nathan Glazer, a member of the Joint Center for 19601961, is preparing a manuscript on ethnic groups in New York City. This study, supported in part by The Post Foundation, concentrates on the changing social, economic, and political characteristics of several ethnic groups in the setting of other urban changes.

Mrs. Helen Kistin prepared a report on data aids for urban economic research. This preliminary analysis was supported by Resources For the Future, Inc. 

PARTICIPATION OF STUDENTS 

Though the Joint Center is a research organization and does not provide formal instruction or confer degrees, it does have a strategic interest in providing research opportunities for advanced students in urban studies. 

In order to contribute directly to this end, the Joint Center provides each year for several doctoral research fellowships for graduate students at both Harvard and M.I.T. The 13

Fellows spend a year at the Center completing research for their theses on some aspect of the Joint Center's interests. 

The fellowships for 1961-1962 are named the Samuel Andrew Stouffer Fellowships in memory of the late Professor of Sociology and Director of the Laboratory of Social Relations at Harvard, who was a member of the Faculty Committee of the Joint Center. R. Stephen Rodd, a Research 

Fellow, studied the economic implications of city size; 

George Sternlieb, another Research Fellow, is analyzing the present and future status of department stores and downtown areas; Leon H. Mayhew is examining the impact of anti-discrimination legislation on the employment, education, and housing of Negroes, particularly in Greater Boston; and Frank C. Colcord is studying the politics of metropolitan transportation planning, and using Boston as a particular case. Stephan Thernstrom is analyzing the social and economic position of the manual laborer in the nineteenth century mill town of Newburyport, Massachusetts. 

He expects the study will result in a reevaluation of Lloyd Warner's analysis of Newburyport, one of the most influential studies in American urban sociology. The work of 

Martin Anderson and Bernard Frieden, who are also Research Fellows, was described in A, above. 

In addition to the fellowships, the Joint Center provides research assistantships in connection with research projects under way. The policy of encouraging the assistance of students from M.I.T. and Harvard has been an important aim of the Joint Center from the beginning. Many students from different disciplines have benefited from these research opportunities.

OTHER ACTIVITIES 

The Joint Center engages in many activities besides research. These include publications, weekly lunch meetings, colloquia, staff seminars, and conferences. 

Through arrangements with both the Technology Press 14

of M.I.T. and the Harvard University Press, the Joint Center has begun publication of a series of books growing out of its activities. As noted above, two books have already been published and several other books are near completion. 

In addition to books, the Joint Center issues a series of working papers and reports in mimeographed and multilithed form. It has also started a reprint series, bringing together relevant journal articles prepared by members of the Joint Center.

Besides its publications, the Joint Center employs various other means of encouraging the interchange of ideas. The weekly lunch meetings held in the conference room throughout the academic year have been a successful part of the Joint Center's activities since its inception. At each meeting a speaker discusses his work on some aspect of urban affairs of interest to members and their guests. A period of discussion follows. Sometimes the speaker is a member of the 

Joint Center, sometimes a visiting scholar or practitioner.

The senior members of the Joint Center are in most cases teaching at M.I.T. or Harvard, and by this means their work 16

at the Joint Center makes itself felt among a widening group of students, many of whom will become research scholars and professionals in urban affairs. The participation of staff of the Joint Center in outside activities is another means by which the research conducted here is put to broad use. The Director, the Chairman of the Faculty Committee, and other members are closely involved in the activities of the Boston and Cambridge communities. One of the most important tasks for the immediate future is to explore the means for establishing the most productive continuing relationship between the Joint Center and the Boston metropolitan area. Joint Center members also perform services for other cities and for the Federal Government and have acted as consultants to foreign governments and to the United Nations.

The Joint Center serves as a base for visiting scholars and practitioners who work for a period of from several months to a year. Known as Visiting Associates, their names appear at the end of this report.

THE FUTURE 

In pursuing its initial policies, the Joint Center has evolved a distinctive pattern. It has undertaken many small studies. It has also been using its general funds predominantly for existing faculty or for persons who could be brought in as faculty. In some cases the Joint Center recruited personnel who otherwise would not have worked on urban studies or would not have been in Cambridge. 

However, because the funds available are short-term, the Joint Center has not often been in a position to help finance tenure appointments in teaching departments. 

Despite the risk of scatter in the early stages of its program, the Joint Center deliberately chose to be exploratory during the first two years. Exploratory studies permitted the Joint Center to bring within its orbit a variety of highly 17

qualified scholars from different disciplines and to provide a basis for obtaining outside financing for those investigations which appear to warrant more extended efforts. 

Perhaps the most important current policy issue now is whether the Joint Center has developed the most effective allocation of its resources for the next stage. There can be no easy resolution of this question. If the Joint Center develops great depth in investigations, it may be obliged to turn down many capable individuals in favor of predetermined subjects. If it tries to encourage or respond to all of the demands upon it, its program may become diffused and without focus.

For the present, the Joint Center concentrates almost all of its general funds within the four major fields described earlier in this report. Of the funds for these four fields, 40 percent are reserved for the comparative analysis, growth and structure of cities, and the remaining 60 percent to the other three areas. In addition, some funds will be set aside to support promising leads in fields such as social values and the community, methods of public and private control over urban change, and decision-making and the planning process in metropolitan areas. In the future, a greater emphasis will be placed on the policy implications of Joint Center studies with special consideration for communities in the Boston and New England regions. Efforts will also be made to strengthen the linkages between the major research fields as well as within them. Finally, in accordance with the original program outlined for the Joint Center, the policy of encouraging the participation of advanced students in the research programs of the senior members will be continued and expanded.

Beyond the problems surrounding the Joint Center's research policy and administration, there is also another side of its activities which cannot easily be summarized in such a review. How can one convey the interest in serious study of the city and region which has been stimulated at both 18

institutions by the existence and the activities of the Joint Center? Or the increasing readiness of many departments to appoint specialists to teach and work on the urban community; or the interest at other American and foreign institutions which the Joint Center has stimulated; or the way in which many officials turn to the Joint Center in hopes that in the period ahead it will be a source of knowledge, ideas for action, and capable personnel?

Just as it is very hard to express the quality of this interest, so it is premature to conclude that the Joint Center will necessarily fulfill the promise it has for so many. It will do so only if its present modest successes are expanded; as more long-term or tenure appointments of scholars are encouraged; as more advanced students and other capable young people are attracted; as more contributions are made to scholarly literature; as a greater fund of knowledge is provided for solving such problems as those which arise in housing, transportation, public services, political reorganization, and the aesthetic improvement of cities. 19

MEMORANDUM OF AGREEMENT 

ON 

 OF THE MASSACHUSETTS INSTITUTE OF TECHNOLOGY 

AND

HARVARD UNIVERSITY 

In the last few years the problems and potentialities of urban areas and regions have aroused great public and scholarly interest, ranging from concern with practical problems to consideration of the very nature of urban culture. 

Harvard and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology have discussed at length how they might increase their effectiveness in the field of urban and regional studies by joint research efforts. To advance their common interest and to further their educational purposes, the two Institutions hereby agree to establish a Joint Center for Urban Studies. 

The two Institutions have deep commitments in fields relating to urban affairs such as architecture, public administration, government, law, economics, social relations, public health, science, technology, civil engineering, international studies, business and industrial management. Urban planning and design programs at the two Institutions have long operated with a broad concern for the environmental problems of cities and regions. The library collections and the computation facilities in the Cambridge community are excellent.

The purpose of the new Joint Center for Urban Studies will be to focus research on the physical environment of cities and regions, the social, economic, governmental, legal, technical and aesthetic forces that shape them, and the 24

interrelations between urbanization and society. Although urgent urban problems have led to demands for immediate effort, successful programs of action depend ultimately on the adequacy of knowledge of cities and regions. This knowledge at present is thin. Therefore, the principal responsibility of the Joint Center will be in basic research. An essential, but secondary, objective is to build a bridge between fundamental research and policy application at national and international as well as local levels. The findings of fundamental research must be made available through reports, books, seminars and other means, valuable to the public official, the citizen leader, the professional and the private investor, here and abroad.

The research activities of the Joint Center should strengthen the related educational programs at Harvard and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in a number of ways. Research findings will be available for teaching purposes in many disciplines concerned with urbanism and regionalism. New research possibilities will be opened to the teaching faculty and advanced graduate students in any interested department at both Institutions. The Joint Center will attempt to provide an interdisciplinary focus and stimulus for the varied urban and regional research interests in the Cambridge community.

The two Institutions hereby agree that the following specific provisions shall govern the organization and operation of the Joint Center: 

1. The academic activities of the Center will be based on the equal participation of the two Institutions. 

2. The Center will conduct research activities and related programs in urban studies. It will also aim to strengthen the interest in urban and regional studies in relevant disciplines at the two Institutions and to encourage communication among these disciplines, between the two Institutions, and among scholars and practitioners here and abroad. 25

Among the activities it may support, when funds are available, are research studies on all aspects of urban and regional problems, inter-faculty seminars, individual research by members of the two Institutions, of visiting scholars and practitioners, and publication of results of any and all such activities. 

3. Funds to support the Joint Center will be solicited on the express understanding that they will be held, invested, administered and disbursed by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology for the purposes of the Joint Center, subject to the conditions stated in paragraph 9 below. Similarly, the business affairs of the Joint Center will be handled by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. 

4. General supervision of the Joint Center will be the responsibility of an Administrative Committee constituted as the two Presidents may from time to time decide. 

5. The academic policies of the Joint Center including recommendations for academic and research appointments will be the responsibility of a Faculty Committee appointed in equal numbers from each of the two Institutions by the Presidents after consultation with the Administrative Committee; the Presidents will appoint a Chairman from among the members of the Faculty Committee in the same fashion. 

6. Direction of the program of the Joint Center will be the responsibility of a Director who may be a member of the Faculty of either Institution but will also hold administrative appointment from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. Recommendation for this appointment will be made jointly by the Presidents of the two Institutions after consultation with the Administrative Committee. 

7. Except with the consent of both Institutions, the

Director and the Chairman of the Faculty Committee will not be members of the Faculty of the same Institution. 26

1. 

8. The Joint Center will have a Visiting Committee of persons with an interest in urban problems, who will be appointed by the Presidents of the two Institutions. The Visiting Committee will have functions of advice and assistance similar to those which are customary in the two Institutions. 

9. In the event that the governing bodies of the two institutions, or of either of them, shall at any time desire to terminate this agreement, it shall be terminated, subject only to the fulfillment of any obligations that have been accepted in the receipt of funds. Grants and payments in support of the Center which carry commitments in time beyond two years shall be reported before definite acceptance to the appropriate officers of both Institutions. In the event of termination of this agreement, all funds and facilities shall be divided as nearly equally as may be, between the two Institutions, and then applied to such work in the area of urban studies as each may decide.
