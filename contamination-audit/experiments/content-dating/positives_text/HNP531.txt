HOW TO PROMOTE AND PROLONG UNEMPLOYMENT, or 

THE BEST WAY TO WRECK DEMOCRACIES, or 

THE IMPORTANT RESULTS OF COLLECTIVE BARGAINING -AS USUALLY PRACTISED- Chapter I Preliminary I 

I am not a labor hater, as some persons apparently think. They apparently think this merely because I call attention to the efficient way in which labor is now busily cutting its own throat. Quite the opposite! I am earnestly trying to help labor. I am a truly sympathetic friend of labor. I am earnestly trying to promote labor's greater welfare and prosperity by criticizing fairly the present labor policies and laws of the Visionaries, and by pointing out the necessary results thereof. I am doing my small best to keep labor from wrecking itself. Those who cannot understand the reasoning of this pamphlet, and who therefore cannot see what my intentions are, may, so far as I am concerned, say what they want to about me. Such persons, according to my notion, cannot be reached by rational argument. Perhaps they are too prejudiced in their views.

Preliminary II 

Economic matters cannot be discussed in highly entertaining language. Books on economics do not intrigue like detective stories and romances. The reader of economics must do a little thinking for himself. He must cultivate some rather rocky and uninspiring ground. It seems to me that the message of this pamphlet is most important. I have done my best to cover the ground simply and clearly, and I hope you will be willing to contribute the relatively small amount of mental effort needed to make you understand my reasoning. If you are willing to do this you 3

will perhaps attain a new point of view on perhaps the most important issue before the American people today.

Preliminary III 

If you have had the nerve and patience to read and understand this pamphlet, and if you think well of it, you might go a little further with your good will and order extra copies, and distribute them where you think they will do the most good. 

AS THINGS NOW ARE 

First, an assumption of conditions: $90.00 is being paid daily for automobile radiators. The radiators are made by members of "group A" who are typical industrial workers in a typical community. The buyer who puts up the $90.00 supplies all the raw materials-everything but the labor. We assume also that there is no profit to be deducted from the $90.00. Starting on this assumption, what follows?

Group A has 30 members. Hence, in the average, each man must receive $3.00, for thirty times $3.00 equals $90.00-neither more nor less.

In the average, the members of group A (if they do not save) spend daily $90.00 for what they live on-food, clothes, amusements, etc. This money is paid to other workers who make the things the members of group A prefer to buy. Then, shortly, these "other workers" spend the $90.00 daily for things (produced by some third group) that they want-and so on. 

In this way the $90.00 keeps on circulating. Finally, it (or its equivalent) comes back again to the members of group A. By such continuing circulating of all the money that there is, group A is enabled to receive constantly its daily $90.00. 

These are universal conditions. A definite quantity of money does circulate each day in each community, and this quantity of circulation-whatever it may be, and it alone-pays the wages of the workers in the various communities. Why the average quantity of daily circulation reaching group A is exactly $90.00 at the present time, is another matter. There is a good reason, of course, why today there is not $120.00, and not $60.00, but precisely $90.00, buying radiators from group A. This involves other problems, and we shall merely ask you to accept the statement that, as things now are, the members of group A are re-

ceiving $90.00 each day as their fair share of the total quantity of circulation of the whole country. This is the fact which is assumed to be true. (Note: With your permission we do earnestly advise you of group A to accept the $90.00 each day for one thing seems to be certain: you cannot get more than this, for, the way things are (the facts that we have assumed) there is now no more money for you to get. That is to say, no more circulation than this actually exists for you to receive if other workers are to receive their fair share also. This is the fact. Take it or leave it! You can get more only by robbing someone else, and you can rob someone else only if you have the power-legal or illegal to do so. As to what industry gets-what it pays in dividends-this is set forth at length in Chapter 3. (The part of the circulation received by industry as profits, and then distributed as dividends, is extremely small, as you will see, and it cannot be further reduced without harming labor itself.)

A HEALTHY CONDITION 

We assume that the thirty workers of group A, at this time, are wisely sharing the $90.00 a day. They do not happen to be organized into labor unions, hence they are not demanding the impossible. They are not refusing to work merely because they are not being paid more money than there is for them to receive. 

Nor are they trying to get more money for themselves at the expense of other equally intelligent and diligent workers somewhere else. Fortunately for the members of group A, they are not being led astray by uninformed or unscrupulous leaders. For this, they, and the rest of society, may be thankful. 

The 30 workers of group A, as we have said, have wisely decided to accept the $90.00 per day. They also fairly divide it among themselves. This gives the average member of the group a daily wage of $3.00. 

By accepting $3.00 a day, and by not demanding (or striking for) a wage of $3.25 a day, all of the 30 persons can continue to work. There will then be no possibility of any member of the group being unemployed who wants to work. That is to say, as long as the $90.00 keeps circulating each day as this particular group's share of all the buying power in circulation in the country, and as long as the group accepts the average wage-rate thus

provided, every one of its members can remain employed. Moreover, it is impossible for these men to receive a higher average wage while all of them work, because the fact is that there is no more money circulating for this group to get unless, at the same time, some other group takes less than it is entitled to receive. (Query: What, under such circumstances, would a modern collective bargaining group do? Who doesn't know? What is usually done depends upon the nature of the strangle-hold that the particular union has upon the throat of society in general. If the strangle-hold is sufficiently strong, if the output must be had by society regardless of cost, there is scarcely any limit to the wage-rate demanded and obtained under compulsion. Normally, the strong union calls for a "closed" shop in order that the strangle-hold-monopoly may be retained for the exclusive benefits of the members of the particular organization. No outsiders will be allowed to intrude. The penalty will be dire happeningseven death. (How many times has this happened in the past few years?)

INFLATION NOW STARTS 

Let us assume, now, that, for one reason or another, the quantity of money in circulation in all communities suddenly increases. Perhaps Uncle Sam uses its Federal Reserve printing presses to print money to loan people (the banks having run out of deposits to loan), and a large quantity of new paper money goes into use, and starts circulating and buying, just as if it were gold money. (Note: After the World War, President Coolidge permitted more than $2,000,000,000.00 of Federal Reserve paper money to be printed and go into circulation. No wonder he made the Coolidge boom!) If enough new printing-press money comes into use, $120.00 will be offered to group A each day for the radiators, for this will be its share of the increased circulation. 

What happens now?

THE START OF INFLATION 

The larger number of dollars being offered for the radiators quickly raises the average daily wage of the members of group A from $3.00 to 4.00, for $120.00 will pay 30 men an average of $4.00 each-neither more nor less. At this higher wage-rate, the 

30 workers will have their full share of the extra money in cir-

culation. A wage-rate of $4.00 can now be paid without unemployment, for there is now this much money to go around. There is now this amount of money for each average worker to have. 

When the average wage-rate is $4.00, the quantity of money circulating each day in the market places, and the total wages of all the workers, will again equal each other. Indeed, now that the circulation has increased, there is no way of stopping average wages from being $4.00, for the greater money-demand will quickly have the effect of raising wages this much. Only if average wages of $4.25 per day are demanded will there be any unemployment. Only if people insist on getting $4.25 a day will there now be insufficient money to go around. At one time the workers could get only $3.00 a day-not $3.25. Now they can get $4.00, but not $4.25. To demand $4.25 now is to create unemployment. 

Wages have now been raised to $4.00 a day for the 30 makers of radiators, and proportionately for the growers of food, the makers of clothes, etc. Why? Because there has been a general inflation-a general increase in the number of paper dollars circulating in the market places. Use of the printing presses and credit-expansion have caused the general advance in wage-rates. 

Nothing else! No law can make average wages advance. Believe it or not. The money must circulate in a big way for big wages to be paid. But, to circulate, it must exist. During inflation, much fiat paper money exists. Real wages have not advanced because each dollar is now worth proportionately less.

Any increased circulation of paper dollars, while it raises dollar wages, does not in the least increase the standard of living, because no more goods are produced or consumed than when the wage-rate is lower. No one buys any more goods. Hence, the standard of average living does not increase. Merely the average price of things goes up. Prices advance in proportion to the extra wages being paid. How, then, can wages buy any more things? The same number of radiators now sell for $120.00 which before sold for $90.00.

This is also true of food, clothes, etc. It has to be this way. 

Anyone who thinks about it can see that when there is more money in circulation to buy things (more "demand," as we say), and when there are no more things being produced for the money to buy, the price of the average thing produced must go up to establish the equilibrium between the money being offered for

the production of things and the average quantity of things being produced. If group A makes 90 radiators a day and receives $90.00 a day in wages, the radiators will cost $1.00 each.

When the wages of group A are advanced to $120.00 a day the same 90 radiators will cost $120.00, or $1.33 each.

SUPPLY AND DEMAND 

People used to say that: "Supply must equal demand." Most people do not clearly realize, however, what the words supply and demand mean.

The "supply" is really the quantity of things offered for sale-and sold—each average day, and the "demand" is the quantity of money that happens to be in circulation and buys the things each day. At the time that the quantity of money wanting to buy things (the "demand") increases, the "supply" of things being produced may or may not increase. Unless production-efficiency has happened to increase at the time that the money-demand increases which is not likely to happen-the supply of things to be bought will not be increased. (Note: For years the labor unions have fought more efficient methods of production, thus they have held down the quantity of things produced, and have held up the price-level of each thing correspondingly, so that a certain money-demand would buy fewer things than it otherwise would. This mistaken policy, of course, has reduced the average standard of living. It has injured labor much, and capital very little.)

Usually the supply of things is reduced in times of big moneydemand, for during these boom periods the workers like to loaf more. They are deceived by the false prosperity of higher money-wages.

The important thing to note is this: When the "demand" increases, the "supply" must in the average equal it, however large the demand for things may be. The equality is established normally, not by increasing the quantity of the things supplied, but by advancing the average selling price of each thing, so that the same old quantity (or perhaps even a smaller quantity) of things will sell for the larger amount of money. If money-demand increases by a third when there has been no change in the quantity of things produced, unit prices will increase by a third, and wages will soon be increased proportionately.

As we have just said when $120.00 is offered for the 90 radiators produced daily by group A, instead of the $90.00 formerly offered, the wage-rate jumps from $3.00 to $4.00 a day and the price of radiators rises from $1.00 to $1.33 each. If the men loaf more when money-wages are higher, and produce only 80 radiators a day, the price of each will be $1.50, and fewer can be bought and used with the dollar buying-demand that there is. 

If this happens generally there will be less consumption, and a lower standard of living.

WAGES VERSUS PROFITS 

Suppose now, that average wages are advanced from $3.00 to $4.00 a day without production being increased, and suppose prices do not go up proportionately. What happens then? Why, capital-profits will be reduced proportionately, for capital will pay more wages and will sell the production of the men for proportionately less money. But, can the profits of capital suffer such reducing? Surely not if people want enough capital to stay in business to give all labor a job. The fact is that much capital is always just "breaking even" anyhow, and grimly hanging on in hopes for a turn for the better. If this capital is forced to advance wages out it will go unless prices may be advanced at least proportionately. So, when money-demand increases, and wage-rates go up proportionately, it is best for everyone that prices should also go up proportionately in order to give the same amount of capital the same average margin of profit that it received before the wage-advance, and thus keep enough of it operating to give everyone a job. Only thus can employment be full.

The fact is, also, that when the wages of group A are advanced from $3.00 to $4.00 a day these workers are no better off after this general advance than they were before, because everything which the men now buy has advanced proportionately. 

The men just imagine they are better off when they get the higher wage. Each man receives more dollars each day, to be sure, but each dollar is proportionately less valuable. 

As we have said, each dollar will buy proportionately less. The prosperity is a fake, and the fact that a fake prosperity is believed to be a true prosperity is one cause of our troubles. This makes people fight for higher wages regardless. The truth is that the

members of group A can benefit from a wage-advance only if the prices of things which they buy do not advance proportionately. But this can happen only if the wage-advance is not general. 

It can happen, that is to say, only if the wages of those persons who produce what group A buys have not advanced proportionately.

For example, when automobile radiators go up in selling price, thus permitting an advance in the wages coming to the members of group A, if, at the same time, the price of food, clothes, etc., goes up proportionately, the members of group A are not a bit better off.

THE DIVISION OF WAGES 

Everyone should agree then that an advance in wages to one group does not mean a thing to that group unless other workers fail to get the same proportional advance in wages. It also means that if one particular group can succeed in getting an artificial (unearned) wage-advance while other groups do not, the group that gets the unearned wage will benefit at the expense of other groups. (Note: As we have said, the benefit cannot come at the expense of capital for average profits must be kept at a figure which just keeps enough capital alive to give all labor a jobprovided labor unions will permit this to happen.) For instance, if the wages of clothing makers are forced up while the wages of farmers stay down, it is nice for the clothing makers, but it is proportionately hard on the farmers. And, when the wage-advance is general, no one is benefited. Indeed, many people will be hurt if the wage-advance has not resulted from a natural increase in the money-demand, but has been created by artificial means, for this always spells either unemployment or artificially low wages for some persons.

If the coal miners' union forces wages artificially high (more than can be relatively earned in a free competitive market with other workers), the miners will benefit, but provided only that they can buy their food and clothes at the old prices. When the coal miners strike for higher wages, and shut down railroads and factories, hospitals, etc., and cause general inconveniences to society and succeed in getting by the hold-up route more pay for producing the same quantity of coal, the wage-advance may benefit them, but, what the miners gain, comes from the pockets 10

of other workers who, directly or indirectly, pay for the increased cost of coal. Who else but society in general can pay for it? 

Certainly the coal companies cannot pay the wage-advance out of profits. Most coal companies have been on the ragged edge for years.

Here is another illustration: The railroad labor unions, by forcing their wages artificially high by strikes, and by the usual collective bargaining methods (having had a strangle-hold on the transportation facilities of the country even tighter than that of the coal miners), have hurt the farming class a lot, because the farmer has been forced to pay more freight on everything he buys and sells. The farmer gets less net. The railroad worker gets his relatively high wage-and a wage not earned competitively by seizing it. He uses the collective bargaining hold-up methods with which all persons are now familiar, and which represent anything but natural competition. The rest of the people pay the railroad men their unearned wages. Let them not kid themselves.

In many cases the labor unions have used the methods of 

Hitler. Might makes right with them. Recently the plumbers in

New York City held up the World Fair and delayed its opening. 

The T. V. A. exhibit could not be opened on arrival because the boxes did not bear a union label. It's the old story, but one that is getting steadily worse. Those who can squeeze hardest the most vital organs of society usually get the most pay. 

As we have said, in most cases the high wages are paid, not for relatively efficient production, but because force has been employed, and force has been used because its use has been condoned-even approved. Yet, the other workers who applaud pay these high wages. Pennsylvania even pays its coal diggers doles from state funds when they are on a hold-up strike (a “hold-up” of most of the people of that very state) for higher wages for producing coal. The government arbitrator, Mr. Steelman, said significantly enough for all to understand, that the public will not stand for anyone starving-even though they refuse the work offered and are holding up the public at the same time. But that's nothing. For years the state of Pennsylvania has condoned the stealing of anthracite coal from the properties of the companies. 

Thieves have been protected in their operations even though they have sold their stolen coal in direct competition with the 11

Companies in Baltimore, Philadelphia, New York, etc. Can things come to a much worse pass than this? Perhaps the worst phase of it is that the public approved this open stealing and also approved protecting the thieves. In the coal strike of March, April and May, 1939, it is no secret that Madame Perkins, our feminine Secretary of Labor, approves of the "closed - shop" (euphemistically referred to as the "union-shop") for coal miners. 

Government was obviously on the side of the U. M. W. Only 

Governor Chandler of Kentucky was willing to provide troops to protect men against being killed who wanted to work in the mines on the old basis. What a horrible state of affairs this is! Is society going to stand such punishment from organized labor indefinitely? Striking miners were allowed to go on public relief rolls and be paid money by the masses they were attempting to "hold-up."

Let no one be fooled, we repeat, into thinking that these relatively unearned wages come from the pockets of capital. 

With only so much money-demand existing each day to pay wages, if one group gets more, another group must get less. 

That's all there is to it. The fact is that today too much capital is shut down merely because it cannot get the small amount of profit necessary to keep it alive. It would be far better for the workers today if more capital could make a profit. Too much capital cannot now afford to operate. There is not enough money in circulation today to permit a lot of capital of this country to pay the wage-rates demanded while selling output for the amount of money now being offered. Hence a lot of potential capital is now shut down, and it will stay shut down until average profits are advanced either by decreasing wages or by increasing the quantity of money-demand so that prices can be advanced while wagerates remain as they are.

EFFECT OF REDUCING PROFITS BY COLLECTIVE BARGAINING 

The fact is that today any further reduction in the profits of capital that might come from paying still higher average wages will increase largely the unemployment and suffering. One more step in the wrong direction. Hence, if the wages of one group are forced higher by collective bargaining, it is imperative today that the wages of some other group should be correspond- 12

ingly lowered-or else there will surely be more unemployment than there now is. Labor unions have already choked too much capital to death. Too many willing workers are now idle on this account.

Labor has foolishly killed many of the geese that lay their golden eggs. High wage-rates, low selling-prices, full employment, and poor labor-efficiency do not go together happily. The inevitable result of this miserable combination is low production and consumption, a lower average standard of living than might easily obtain, much unemployment, much starvation (which is reduced only by federal relief that unbalances the national budget), and excessive taxation of those at work who must be taxed to keep the idle from starving.

When will we learn these simple truths about the effects of artificially high wage rates? How unfair it is when some persons get their high wages by force! Not only are the wages of others thereby made unduly low, but the cost of everything that the poorly paid must buy is increased unreasonably. Indeed, many persons can get no wages when some are getting more than their share. There is no money left to pay wages with. These persons are idle from no fault of their own. Millions are now idle because some wages have already been forced too high for this very reason. What other reason can there be? Money-demand being what it is, how can many persons get more than they earn without some other persons getting less than they earn-or perhaps nothing at all? Democracies beware!

Isn't it strange-even pitifully absurd-that the farmers applaud the high wages of the union workers, when a large part of these high collective bargaining wages comes out of their own pockets and when they are not earned relatively? No doubt the farmers (and the masses also) do not realize what is being done to them, because, surely, if they did realize it, they would not applaud acts which unjustly reduce their own standards of living. 

The farmers evidently do not realize that they themselves are paying a large part of these high wages, wages which, as we have said, are not being earned competitively, but which are being seized forcefully. If unorganized workers, and the millions of unemployed, realized today what organized labor and its collective bargaining had done to them, and what it is now more than ever doing to them, they wouldn't stand for it a moment. They 13

would vote collective bargaining out of existence, and they would quickly cancel the National Labor Relations Board which promotes this bargaining. But they have been coddled and deceived —and they still are. Nor do they understand. So they applaud high wages, however obtained, and they raise no voice against the many strikes for higher wages for exclusive groups. Nor do they object to the impositions upon industry of the most unfortunate aims of the Wagner Labor act. 

How unfortunate all this is! How unfortunate for the idle, the unorganized, and all the victims. How unfortunate for the country at large! How unfortunate even for those who are now working, but who are producing relatively little in their short working weeks, and who are producing things that must be divided among the idle so they do not starve. How gloomy a picture this procedure is for the future of Democracies! How long can 

Democracies endure this sort of thing? How unfortunate that our viewpoint is so distorted. 

We are not off the subject when we talk about the devastating effect of artificial wages obtained by the hold-up methods, for, after all, the wage-rates obtained by collective bargaining have been the cause of most of the unemployment. With labor in liquid competition, and with the workers accepting the possible wages (the wages that may be paid to all who want to work) there could be no unemployment. Capital never shuts down if there is any profit left after paying wages, and there is always some profit left for enough capital to give all labor a job if average wages are not out of balance with money-demand. Who can deny this? Capital is never shut down, therefore, except when impossibly high wages are being demanded. Hence, whenever capital is shut down to the extent that there is forced unemployment, this fact alone proves that average wages are pegged so high in comparison to the money actually in circulation that not enough capital (not enough for labor's highest good) can get the profit it must have to stay in business. There is not enough money circulating to buy the output at the prices that must be charged if the capital is to break even and pay the wages demanded.

Unemployment means that average wages and prices are pegged too high. Unemployment means that average wages are too high for the money-demand to support. 14

The activities of labor unions, made effective by their often illegal collective bargaining methods, prevent the normal equilibrium between demand and supply, between the money in circulation and the wages demanded. This is the situation that today smothers the people of the United States and England. 

Can't we see how and why this is true? We must understand the cause of our troubles if we are ever to eliminate them. We must understand the true cause of unemployment if the Democracies are to continue!

INFLATION ADVANCES WAGES IN GENERAL 

A wave of artificial inflation, caused by the artificial increase of the paper-money circulation of Federal Reserve notes has sent the average wages of group A soaring from $3.00 to $4.00 a day. 

The quantity of paper-tickets to be used in exchanging goods has increased, and every average worker now receives 4 instead of 3 tickets a day which he uses to exchange with somebody else for the things he wants to buy. (Note: Some workers may get 

6 tickets a day and others 2, but the average must be 4 if there is to be no unemployment. If one man gets more than another some other man must get less if there are to be enough tickets to go around.) So, every one feels prosperous. It's foolish to let this make you feel prosperous, isn't it? Since the average person can buy no more for his 4 tickets than he could formerly with 3 tickets, why should he feel prosperous merely because he has more tickets? There is no logical answer. It is merely part of human nature to feel this way. 

DEFLATION NOW FOLLOWS 

Now, for reasons that we shall not go into, rising waves in the sea of circulation and wages are always followed by falling waves. Moreover, the waves fall approximately as far below the average level of the sea as they have risen about it. 

In 1930, the Federal Reserve money inflation-wave (such waves having been fostered by Democrats and Republicans alike) came to an end. The high wave started falling down, down, down. The number of tickets of exchange had been abnormally great back in 1928 and 1929. The printing presses had been over-worked, while every cue smiled cheerfully. President 

Coolidge was popular because he had helped promote a fake 15

prosperity. Now, in 1930, fewer and fewer exchange-tickets were to be found in circulation. Loans were being paid off. Interest rates were high. People were frightened about the future. 

Federal Reserve paper money was being retired by the bushel. 

What was to be done about this? What is still to be done about this?

We have assumed that, at the normal level of circulation of tickets, the average worker in group A can receive 3 tickets a day and still stay employed, for there are 90 tickets a day to be distributed among these 30 men. At the height of the printingpress orgy there were 120 dollar-exchange-paper tickets a day coming to each average group A. So, as we have said, every one got more tickets and felt happy over this fake prosperity.

But, alas, when the inflation-wave went down, down, there were only 60 tickets being offered daily for the production of the 30 members of group A. This was the new fact. Take it or leave it. Adapt yourself to it, men, or fight it as you please. Here's the chance, citizens, to butt your brains out against a wall-or do the thing best for your own interests. What are you going to do? This is a most important issue. Whatever you may do about it, the fact will remain just what it is-namely, that, in 

1930, only 60 exchange tickets existed to be paid to each average group A each day. Will you of the average group A face this fact, or will you not?

The question might be put another way: Will part of you of the average group A stop receiving any tickets, and let some of the workers have all the tickets of exchange? Will part of you go on relief now that the inflation-wave is subsiding, or will you not? The point is: Will you take what exchange-tickets there are, or will you insist on receiving more tickets than there are if everyone is to have his fair share? Will you act so foolishly that many of you will stay idle, unemployed, supported by others still working, or will you adjust yourselves wisely to the new situation so that all can work? What will you do, members of average group A? Will you sanely adjust yourselves to the situation, or will you foolishly refuse to do so?

When the deflation in the number of exchange-tickets in use came about, it affected almost everyone directly or indirectly. 

The number of tickets of exchange available to the farmers, to group A, to the clothing makers, etc., all went down in about 16

the same general proportion. Deflations, similar to inflations,

What was to be done about this deflation in reach everyone. 

1930 following the boom of the 1920's?

WHAT TO DO WHEN DEFLATION COMES 

Here, then, is the crucial, critical problem. How we act, facing it, may make or break us. So, we must think hard. We must be sure to do the right thing now that another deflation has come. We know what to do in times of inflation. We accept the wage-advance cheerfully, with no grumbling, believing foolishly that we are better off. But this is the reverse process. What shall we do about it? Is it really necessary that many people should be idle and on relief? When the deflation in the number of exchange tickets takes place is it really necessary that anyone should suffer, or starve? What about it? Let us think things over for a moment.

First, let us look at the past for its lesson. Before the inflation in exchange-tickets took place by the printing press route, the average number of group A received 3 tickets a day. After the printing presses had done their job of creating a few billion extra paper-tickets of exchange, the average member of group A received not 3 but 4 tickets a day. But, as we have already discovered, the worker was no better off with 4 than he had been with 3 tickets a day, because 4 tickets were needed now to buy what could formerly be bought with 3. The prosperity was fake. 

That is something well worth thinking about. It may give us a good lead. How about taking fewer tickets a day now that deflation in exchange-tickets has come, and when there are not as many tickets to be divided among the people? Will we be any worse off when taking 2 tickets a day than we were when we had 

3 tickets? If we were no better off really when the number of exchange tickets increased from 3 to 4, why should we be worse off when the number of tickets decreases, and when each person has fewer? Why doesn't the rule work in both directions-up and down? Aren't we kidding ourselves about being hurt by a declining wage-rate, just as we kidded ourselves into thinking we were benefited really from an increased wage-rate? May not the members of group A be just as well off when receiving only 

2 tickets a day? This is the important consideration. 17

Now that deflation in the number of exchange-tickets has actually taken place, isn't it possible to make the necessary adjustments to this new situation without being hurt? Why isn't it possible? Haven't we enough sense to do what seems to be necessary to do? It appears to be simple enough. It appears to be completely logical-that which can be done. Who can advance a conclusive argument against it?

Alas! Apparently, we haven't this much sense-and this is the real cause of our social troubles. If Democracies crumble this will be the real reason. Lack of average intelligence will be the reason. We insist on valuing the quantity of paper-tickets of exchange, and not the quantity of things which each ticket will buy. Alas and alack, how ridiculous all this is! How foolish all this is. Yet, pitifully enough, this represents us. Poor, miserable, inane, human animals, only partly evolved-barely selfconscious-mostly animals still. Even so, it is not too much to say that the fate of Democracies depends upon our ability to change this average view-point of ours-and upon nothing else. 

THE PRESENT ATTITUDE TOWARDS DEFLATION 

In this case, our prospects are dismal, indeed, for each year our attitude towards this matter of wage-rates seems to be getting worse. Each year more impractical idealists and Visionaries (who should be gagged) talk about the number of dollars the average person must receive as wages to be prosperous, to have a "living wage" (the preposterous phrase), and each year more of these Visionaries insist that the dollar-incomes shall be increased this way or that. They even applaud the reduced workweek as a means of maintaining high wages, and they acclaim every other harmful method of supporting high wage-rates at the expense of production and consumption. Look at them in 

Washington preaching their worse than nonsense. "High wages increase employment and prosperity," they say. Bah! 

But let us continue.

WHAT FORMERLY HAPPENED 

In the days of old, when people didn't think themselves so wise, when we didn't have so many labor unions, or capitalistic organizations trying to do the impossible thing of maintaining profitable price-levels for everyone, and when we did not have 18

Federal laws calling for minimum wages per hour, and a small maximum number of hours for people to work each week, when we had no destructive Wagner Act, with its direct encouragement of collective bargaining, and when we did not have unemployment insurance, etc., etc., and when the idealistic, visionary women (particularly) stayed at home and kept quiet, what happened? In those days when deflation came (as it did come periodically because, even then, we had the printing presses tampering foolishly with the number of exchange-tickets on the foolish argument that as production increased more tickets were needed. Bah!), everyone accepted fairly cheerfully the smaller number of exchange-tickets to be had. Workers then accepted the possible wage. They did not then refuse to work unless they received the impossible wage. For this reason the deflation periods were soon over, and there was relatively little unemployment or suffering.

We mean by this that in the old days, when only 60 exchange-tickets were available for the 30 members of group A, the average member of the group finally accepted (grudgingly, perhaps) 2 tickets a day for his wage. Moreover, everyone else in the country did the same. At this lowered wage-rate everyone could soon have a job even in the period of deflation, for there were enough exchange-tickets circulating, even then, to give everyone a job at this low wage-and-price-rate. After this adjustment had been made and it could be made quickly under fluid and strictly competitive labor-conditions, the unemployment situation was at an end. The quicker the wage-rate was lowered and accepted the quicker everyone was back at work. When prices hit the bottom, the hitherto frightened savers again commenced to spend, and the number of exchange-tickets in the market places rapidly increased again. (People spend more readily when they think they are getting bargains. Putting wages and prices up has the reverse effect.) In those old days, in fact, the workers did the opposite of what our Idealists now recommend.

The Visionaries forever talk about the necessity of maintaining certain minimum "living" wages as the sole means of making people prosperous. What bosh! What criminal stupidity! As though anyone could be made prosperous by exchange-tickets- 19

even though bushels of them were paid each man for only one hour's work.

When, in the old days, everybody was back at work after the deflation, receiving 2 exchange tickets a day in the average instead of 3 or 4, it was found, of course, that buying prices had declined in proportion. Hence the 2 tickets bought as much as the larger quantity did before. The material standard of living was not reduced by the lowered payments of exchange-tickets, for each ticket would buy proportionately more. However much the number of tickets might have been reduced, the result would be precisely the same.

In these good old days everyone went back to work rather quickly, whatever the possible wage was for all to receive. Receiving this possible wage, the production of goods was as much as it had ever been, and all the goods could be purchased and consumed, and no one suffered because a job could be had. 

Let us repeat also that production and consumption per capita increased 400% between 1850 and 1920. This increase in the average standards of living was due to the improvements in method introduced by capital-many of the new methods being bitterly resisted by the organized collective bargainists. It is fortunate that there were so few unions in those days, else average living standards could not have improved so rapidly in this United States. We shall see later that owners of industry receive as dividends and interest less than 8% of the national income before paying income taxes. 

Even so, people may have felt poor at the time wages went down because, some way or another, people refuse to feel prosperous when dollar wage-rates are lowered, whatever the standard of real living may be.

WHAT COULD HAVE BEEN DONE IN 1930 

There was therefore no real reason why, in 1930, everyone should not have gone quickly back to work receiving 2 instead of 4 exchange-tickets as the average daily wage. All the workers could have been just as prosperous really, with the lowered total wage, and they could have had just as high an average standard of living as in 1929. The maximum production of all the people working as long as they would work with the most modern machines could have been purchased and consumed. 20

There is no valid argument against this, no good argument in the world, dear people. Whatever you may insist upon thinking about it to the contrary, I firmly believe, this is the truth! 

Alas, however, the unfortunate fact is that few of you agree. You still love the larger quantity of exchange-tickets above everything else. You still think that your prosperity is measurable solely in terms of the number of tickets daily received. This, alas, is the cruel burden of this country. This crude fallacy, this childish way of regarding the collective good, is the cause of most of our woes.

In- This attitude and its effects now threaten Democracies! deed, if the people who believe theoretically in Democracies refuse to change their thinking in this respect, their Democracies will fold up. They will not continue to exist, we predict. The break-down of Democracies will prove that the average person has not enough sense to run his own life, that his mental level does not entitle him to have the advantages of a modern system of industrial production. If the masses are to do what is for their own good, either they must understand what is for their own good, and make laws that will call for the good being done, or else they must obey an autocratic government that will forcefully do for them what must be done. An autocracy can make the masses accept the prosperity that is possible for them. How can Democracies exist with millions of persons unemployed year after year while Facisms and Communisms give everyone a job?

DEFLATION WITHOUT SUFFERING 

To return to our subject more definitely: In the old days, deflations ended quickly, and with little unemployment or suffering. 

Even then they lasted far too long. Indeed, they should never have been permitted to start, for the printing press inflations of the old days should never have been permitted. Our fallacious thinking about the need of certain quantities of exchange-tickets to support certain volumes of production and consumption is still grievously wrong. With one-tenth the present quantity of money, I claim, ten times the present quantity of production could be bought and consumed provided only that wages and prices were adjusted to the situation. In these old days, however, at any rate, there were no minimum wage-laws that prevented 21

the necessary lowering of wage-rates at times of deflation. In these old days every person could accept half the number of exchange-tickets a day (each one buying twice as much) if he wanted to, and thus maintain his old standard of living. In these days no one tried to pay the workers 4 tickets of exchange for a smaller out-put. No one then in power was insane enough to ask people to work only 40 hours a week instead of 54 as a way of attaining a real prosperity-namely, a prosperity measured in terms of a high material standard of living. No one was guilty of trying to "spread employment" this way. Everybody seemed to realize then (what no one in power seems to realize today) that in the 40-hour week less could be produced than in a 54hour week, and hence that less could be consumed, and that the real problem was to produce as much as possible and to regulate the cost of producing it so that the circulation actually existing would buy it all. Only in this way can the biggest possible average quantity of everything be consumed and enjoyed.

No one then tried to subsidize one group at the expense of another. No one believed, for instance, that the farmers should have the prices of their crops subsidized at the expense of the taxpayers (the masses). Most people believed then that if 

Government would leave these things alone they would adjust themselves fairly, and that there was no other fair way of obtaining an adjustment. How far we have wandered!

Anyhow, why should we now reduce production as a means of increasing consumption? What's the use? Isn't it a silly, cruel, and devastating, scheme? What harm can possibly be done to any worker if he accepts the 2 tickets of exchange instead of the 4 tickets, if it be true-as it will be-that he can buy as much with the 2 tickets as he could formerly buy with 4 tickets?

By doing this simple thing, full production can still be had, and the maximum output can be bought and consumed. Oh, for the happy days again when people will act with some sanity! They used to act with some sanity, not, perhaps, because they were really more sensible than they are now, but because they were forced to do the sane thing or starve. In those blessed times there were no laws and no doles. No public relief-schemes had then been designed to prevent the workers from accepting the possible wage-rate as a means of stopping unemployment-that which they never really wanted to do. Now the workers, alas, 22

are protected from doing what is for their own good. They are even urged not to do what is for their own good. 

HOW ABOUT UNEMPLOYMENT INSURANCE? 

Is unemployment insurance a good thing? Does it not keep men from accepting the number of exchange-tickets a day that will keep them at work? If all can go to work at the lower wagerate without being hurt really, and if we can still consume as much as we ever did (providing that we still produce as much by doing a decent week's work), who wants idleness and unemployment-insurance as a means of trying to hold up wage-rates to heights that cannot possibly be maintained for everyone—the facts of money-circulation being what they are? And who wants production and consumption cut down while people refuse to accept the only possible wage-rate that will put them all to work and make them truly prosperous again? Who wants to keep on trying to accomplish the impossible-trying to make 4 exchange tickets exist where there are actually only 2-which, even if it could be accomplished, would benefit no one? Who wants everlasting doles?

WHAT ABOUT MINIMUM-WAGE LAWS? 

Let us continue with our questioning. Who wants a minimum-wage law that will prevent wages from being adjusted downward when only a downward adjustment will give full employment and full buying of full production? The inflation of tickets of exchange was over in 1930. Fewer tickets of exchange exist today. Who, on this account, wants to cut down the number of working hours per week to 40 or less? Who would not prefer to work at least 48 hours a week-and enjoy the greater production thereof? Who wants to loaf more of each week than this? 

Why not say, then, good people, "Damn the number of tickets of exchange!"? Who cares about them? 

Let us cheerfully our share of those

Let this, then, be our new point of view. accept the number of tickets that we can get that are being offered in the market places. Let us not cut down production as if this were a way of getting a quantity of exchange-tickets that does not exist for each one of us to have. Let us produce as much as we can at the smallest wage-cost so that 23

unit-prices may be as low as possible, and so that our wages will purchase as many things as possible. (Note: And let competition decide how many tickets each of us shall receive relatively to the other. This is the only fair way!)

WHAT REALLY COUNTS! 

After all, it's the things that count. If we of group A put our wages down when only 60 exchange-tickets are coming to our particular community as our average share of the tickets being exchanged in the whole country, all of our output, however large it may may be, and whether we work 48 or 54 hours a week, can and will be bought. Moreover, what is true here will be true of every community. 

Then, for heaven's sake, why should we not put our wages down to the average of 2 tickets a day per worker, even though each worker in some inflation period did get an average of 4 less valuable tickets a day? Why shouldn't we do this thing if, by so doing, we can buy as much and be as prosperous in real things as we ever were, and when, if we do not do these things, many will be idle as they now are? If we increase our efficiency and produce more things per hour, and if we work as many hours as we did, we can be more prosperous than we ever were, because there will be more things produced and more to consume. 

Why, then, don't we all go to work in the way open to us? 

Why don't we accept the number of exchange-tickets that there are the number per average worker that will give every person his share? Why? Oh, why, why? 

Why don't we? For one thing, we are taught not to. We still believe that if each worker receives more tickets of exchange each day he will be proportionately better off. We still believe we are more prosperous in certain times merely because the ticket-wages are temporarily higher then.

Question-1 Does Labor Need To Be Protected Against Capital? 

Answer "No".

Why? Because economic laws hold the profits of capital down to a very small percentage of the national income-a percentage of profit that is limited to the percentage of progress that capital makes when introducing more efficient methods or more desirable articles. 

There is another reason: Only that amount of capital operates which can obtain operatives. If profits become unusually large, more money will be saved and invested in industry, more capital will seek to go to work, more demand for labor will appear, and the competition for both labor and markets will reduce prices until the profit-margin is sufficient to keep only that amount of capital alive which is needed to give all workers a job. 

On the other hand, if labor does something to reduce the average profit to a point less than what is needed to keep sufficient capital in operation to give everyone a job, then labor will suffer unemployment. Some labor needs protection, to be sure, but not against capitalistic profits taking more than a proper share of the national income, and only against the labor unions who constantly and uniformly obtain more than their share of the national buying-power at the expense of the unorganized workers, the farmers, the family servants, etc. Yet, strangely enough, unorganized labor abuses the capitalistic system at times, and scarcely ever protests against the labor union activities and results. 68

How Long Does A New Capital Investment Make Money? 

Answer: New and old capital investments make money only so long as they are especially efficient.

When enough efficient units of capital come into being, the profits of all concerns having equal efficiency quickly reduce to the vanishing point. Competition has this effect. But, when still more efficient capital starts up, the older capital-that capital which at one time was the most efficient-now suffers from a relative lack of efficiency, and, with its higher costs of production, is soon crowded out. The old capital then goes broke. 

The percentage of profit is obviously limited to the percentage of progress made by the more efficient capital in competition with the less efficient capital-the latter capital being that which at the time is being forced gradually out of business. The new capital receives this profit only so long as it retains the relatively higher margin of efficiency competitively. The profit, while received, is equal to the margin of greater efficiency. 

The capital Profits of Progress do not come from the pockets of labor. Rather, these increases in production are soon completely turned over to labor in the form of higher wages paid, or in the form of lower prices for things produced. The profits of the extra efficiency of the units of new capital are turned over 100% to labor at the time that still more progressive and more efficient capital comes into being to supplant the old capital and reduce its profits to zero. 74

Here we find the explanation of the fact that the standards of real living of labor have steadily and rapidly advanced in the United States for the last one hundred years. The steps upward resulting in the Profits of Progress have steadily been turned over one by one to labor. Labor has persistently received 100% of the increased production due to the introduction of more efficient methods of production. The standards of average living have improved, therefore, almost solely because thrifty investors, in search of profits, have taken the risk of introducing the more efficient methods of production which inventors (themselves, or others) have devised. Labor, other than inventive labor, has not been in any way responsible for its own improved standards of living. On the contrary, the activities of organized labor have prevented the standards of living of the masses from being nearly as high as they might otherwise have been. The combination-policy of Government and labor unions is slowing down more and more this advance in living standards. The prospects for the future are therefore rather dismal. The Labor Leaders and the Visionaries are co-operating vigorously in promoting high money-wages combined with small production, much idleness, and a lower average standard of real living. How long, we wonder, will this unfortunate state of affairs continue? Question-6

What Effect, If Any, Will A Stationary Population 

Have On Industry? 

Answer: A very slight effect indeed.

Alarmists knowing little about economics have been writing articles recently to the effect that when the growth of population of the United States ceases, we shall be in for hard times, and that industry will suffer, and that the standards of living will be lowered. 

They say this on the grounds that there will not be so many new houses to build, not so many new automobiles to be sold, etc., etc. 

This is another notion based upon economics misunderstanding. 

The people at work in a country receive an average wagescale which, as stated, is proportional to the daily quantity of money actively in circulation in the market-places-to this and to this only. No change in population changes this daily quantity of money in active circulation, and hence no change in population can possibly affect adversely the average national income or the average wage-scale. Moreover, if a certain amount of circulation is in the market places buying goods, there is no reason to suppose that there will not be a demand for labor (at the proper wage-scale) to make all the things that labor can make which the people wish to purchase with their dollar incomes. 77

It is impossible to imagine, therefore, that there can be any serious let-up in the use of money for buying things until the wants of all the people are fully satisfied. Hence, if the population ceases to grow, there is no reason to suppose that either the quantity of money in circulation or the speed of circulation will reduce, or that there will be any lack of purchasing of the things that men can produce. Therefore, there is no reason to suppose that the country will suffer in any way (in the average) from a stationary population.

It is admitted that there will be fewer new homes required, that there will be fewer new school-houses needed and that there will perhaps not be so many automobiles. Nevertheless, the same quantity of money will continue to circulate and it will support and pay the same quantity of wages. Therefore, so long as people want anything at all, or can find any place to invest money, or can find anyone to borrow savings, the buying-power that there is will be expressed in the purchase of consumable goods in such fashion as to give everyone employment who wants it (provided the wagescale is properly adjusted with the quantity of money in circulation). The average standard of living will then, as now, depend solely upon the quantity of goods that can be produced by these people at work.

When the population ceases to grow, it will be as easy for full production to be produced and purchased and consumed as in periods when the population is increasing steadily. The standard of living can just as easily become higher and higher in a country having a stationary population. The improvement in the standard of living comes from increased productivity per capita per annum, and from nothing else, and there is no reason to suppose that efficient methods will not be introduced just as much by stationary population as by a growing population. 

The fact is that, as a population grows, more and more exchange-tickets are required to maintain the same wage-scale per capita. More labor is used (wasted, in one sense) in producing unconsumable gold. When, however, population ceases to grow, a uniform quantity of money will support the regular wage-scale persistently. But, as we have been trying to say, there is no particular benefit in the level of a wage-scale, per se, so it makes little difference whether the wage-scale is high or low. The main thing is that it should be properly adjusted to the necessities, and fairly 78

distributed among the workers according to their relative skill and efficiency. Hence, the growth of population, or the failure of population to increase, will have no economic effect upon the real standard of living.

In one respect, however, the change from a growing to a stationary population may have an effect. The effect to which we refer may be felt in the profits of industry. As we have said in the preceding section, old capital is constantly being destroyed because new and more efficient capital takes away its labor by paying wages that the older and less efficient capital cannot afford to pay.

When population is growing, some of the old capital stays alive facing this strenuous competition merely because more potential labor exists each year than in the preceding year. For this reason, in countries with a growing population the profits of capital have been maintained at a somewhat higher level that would perhaps be possible (with the same progress and efficiency) in countries with a stationary population. Therefore, the only effect of the change from the growing population to the stationary population would be to increase slightly the share of the masses in the national income. Wages would get a slightly larger percentage of the national income than they now receive. 

But, in the long run, even this might not be true, because, if the profits of capital are abnormally reduced there will be less incentive for people to save money and invest. Hence, there will be less new capital going into competition with the old capital each year. This might tend to hold profits up, and average profits, therefore, might be about the same when population ceases to grow as they now are. 

In this event, the progress of methods would slow down, to be sure, but perhaps the same percentage of the national income would come to capitalists and workers respectively. Question-8 

Are There "Deterrent Taxes"? 

Answer: Yes and no.

Mr. Roosevelt has been quoted as remarking that there are no "deterrent taxes"-namely, taxes that effect the economic situation adversely. This, at least, is what we suppose he means, and there is some justification for this point of view. 

Unquestionably, everyone in business is working as hard as he can today to get along, and the fact that there are high taxes 79

on profits is not adversely affecting his efforts. Indeed, it may be said that the less the profits remaining to business after taxes, the harder the management will strive to make profits. Unless someone can show that business men are loafing on the job merely because they are being forced to pay such a large part of their earnings to the Government in taxes, it cannot well be argued that the present taxation is "deterrent" to business from this standpoint. 

I, for one, therefore, agree that the slump in business has not been due to the taxation. The capital "net-gains-tax" has been in effect during the most prosperous years of the last decade. The "undistributed profits tax" was put into effect prior to the prosperity of 1937. 1937 was a relatively good year, even though business paid maximum taxes of the various varieties. Moreover, the cancellation of a large part of the undistributed profits tax had no effect in improving business. There is no statistical evidence to show that any of these things had anything to do with business efforts, business earnings, or business prosperity. The business depression has not been due to any failure on the part of business men to do everything they could to make things go. Rather, it has been due, first, to the unwillingness of the average person to spend all his income; secondly, to the forcing of wages up to such a point that few people could be employed by the circulation actually in use; thirdly to the high prices that have come from the artificially high wages, these high prices having dampened the incentive of people to buy and spend their income; fourthly, by the labor union activities that have been making things constantly worse, etc., etc.

It is possible, of course, that a reduction in income taxes might have had some psychological effect on the public mind. Hence, taxreduction might possibly cause the average person to spend more of his income rather than save so much of it. This, however is doubtful. Futhermore, even so, and regardless of the taxes in vogue, if wages and prices had been lowered sufficiently everyone would have gone to work, and everyone would have been prosperous and business as well.

The idea that high taxes "deter" business is based upon the mistaken notion that a country cannot be prosperous unless a great deal of new money is being invested currently in new capitalistic enterprise. A school of modern economists has advanced the false theory, first, that a country cannot be prosperous and secondly, 80

that its people cannot be fully employed, unless a large quantity of "capital goods" is being purchased currently. This school tries to make a distinction between the buying of "capital goods" and "consumer goods".

The whole theory, to my mind, is based upon a misunderstanding of economic principles. This school has never attempted to prove that the quantity or speed of the circulation of exchangetickets in the market places would be reduced at a period when capital goods were not being purchased to a normal degree. If such a proof cannot be made, there is no ground whatsoever for the view that people will be idle and that business will be depressed whenever capital goods are not being purchased normally. 

These economists have evidently taken an effect for a cause. 

During optimistic periods of advance in wages and prices there always has been a large buying of capital goods. People have been encouraged to invest. In booms, people are optimistic; they see prospects of price-advances. But before the boom starts there has been an opportunity of making investments when things were cheap. Many a new industry has started up at such favorable times, and has reaped the benefit of the advance that came later. 

Now, the boom did not cause the unusual investment in capital goods. Nor did the unusual investment in capital goods create the good times. But the anticipation of the good times that always follow depressions caused the buying of capital goods to take place at the time when prosperity was at hand. 

For these reasons there has always been a large investment in capital goods at the end of each depression period, and during the first stages of the inflation period. This fact, however, does not signify at all that the inflation period was produced by the purchase of capital goods. Nor does it signify that there cannot be a boom-period unless there is at least a normal buying of capital goods. There is no sound reason to suppose, indeed, that all of the people of the United States could not be fully employed, and that the standards of living could not be maintained at the current maximum, if there was not a single item of capital goods being purchased-except, perhaps, repairs for buildings and machines.

In fact, there will always be full employment and full production and consumption at any time when the wage-scale is adjusted to the quantity of exchange-tickets actually in circulation at that time. 81

Hence, regardless of whether or not capital goods are being normally purchased, there can readily be at all times full employment and a high standard of living. The very fact that no one eats or wears or consumes personally any capital goods tends to prove, rather, that the standard of real living, the standard of "consumable living," would be higher if fewer capital goods were being purchased and if more of the buying of production was addressed to articles of personal consumption. 

When these modern pseudo-economists properly understand these principles, they will not go off on their various tangents. 

Haven't we just pointed out that the profits of old capital are destroyed by the competition of new capital? How, then, is it possible for any business man to feel that the average profits of the operation of his company will be increased by large investments in capital goods which will compete with his own company for labor and for markets?

It is a strange situation, indeed, that today business men should be pessimistic because so little money is being invested in new plants, machines, and methods to go into competition with their own companies, now struggling along trying to exist. Why any business man should desire new capital investment to compete with himself today is more than my mind can conceive. The greatest item of hope to the business man today rests on the fact that he is not suffering the normal amount of competition from new and improved methods, that new factories are not being built, that many new machines are not now being purchased, and that the old companies already in business will, on this account, have a better chance of obtaining the trade, the workers, and the profits that may be had.

It is, of course, true that when fewer capital goods are being purchased, the makers of capital goods will suffer. The situation is much the same as when the price of wheat goes down, and when farmers suffer on this account. When, however, the demand for capital goods is lower than normal, the demand for consumer goods must be higher than normal provided that the same quantity of money still circulates.

As we have seen, if a man pays 50c for a bushel of wheat instead of $1.00, he will have 50c to spend for something else, and the party who makes this something else will get this extra 50c of buying-demand-provided the money stays in circulation. 82

Exactly the same principle applies to the manufacture of consumer goods. When there is a certain amount of circulation in the market places, and when this circulation is not used to the average extent in buying capital goods, it naturally means that it is being used in buying consumer goods to an abnormal extent. 

Hence, the manufacturer of consumer goods will then employ more labor than normal at the time that the manufacturer of capital goods employs less. The total amount of employment and wagepayment will remain the same provided that the circulation stays the same. Only one thing can possibly change this picture, and that will be that the total circulation would, for some strange, unknown, and unproved reason, be reduced by the fact that capital goods were not in as much demand as normal. 

This result is improbable. No demonstration of the validity of this point of view has ever been given, but, even if it had some portion of truth in it, even so, as stated, full employment and production and consumption of consumer goods could still go on provided that the wage-scale of workers was adjusted to that point that does correspond with the quantity of exchange tickets actually in circulation-whether this quantity be normal or below the former normal.

Some economists argue that if capital goods are not bought in normal quantity money will never circulate normally, that more and more money will stagnate in unused bank deposits, and that unemployment will last forever. No statistics or theory has been given in support of this notion. 

In the first place, as we have already stated several times, whatever quantity of money does circulate (whether it be the quantity which at present is actually circulating, or whether it is a quantity only one-half as large), full employment may always be had by proper adjustment of wages in proportion to the quantity of money which actually circulates. Having made the necessary adjustment along these lines, employment might be full in the United States even if the circulation in the market places were much smaller than it now is, and even if it were being used solely to buy things for personal consumption.

The fact of the matter is that income which is spent to buy unneeded plants and machinery (and new plants are certainly not needed today when so many good plants are idle partly or wholly) represents pure waste. Plants and machinery, the same as money, 83

can neither be eaten nor enjoyed. New plants and machinery are not needed unless they serve a useful end. That is to say, unless the new plant is better than the old one which it will destroy competitively, the time and energy put into building the new plant are utterly wasted. We might as well use labor to pile up sea-sand for the ocean waves to level down at the next high tide. Such use of labor does not add to the average standard of living in the least. Quite the contrary. What good does such spending do, then? The standard of living derives only from consumable goods. Wasting labor to produce unneeded capital goods cannot possibly improve living standards. Indeed, it will tend to lower such standards. 

Why will people not understand this simple principle? Why would anybody want money and labor to be spent today to build plants which are not needed and which will remain idle-or cause others to stay idle? New investments in capital goods are worth while only if they introduce methods which increase the supply of consumable goods per capita. For this reason, the investment itself never benefits society directly, and it normally destroys an old investment.

In conclusion, therefore, it seems logical that, regardless of how un-loaned deposits may increase in banks, we might easily have extraordinary prosperity if the money now actually circulating were used solely to purchase consumer goods. To accomplish this result we require only proper adjustments of the wage-andprice-scales. We should realize the real necessity, and we should abandon these foolish notions. Even so, however unwise it may be, I predict that when prosperity does return again, for whatever reason, there will be the normal rush to build more factoriesprobably more than the country needs or can use. We shall then indulge in another waste of savings-a repetition of the same struggle to accomplish the impossible. Every man in business, therefore, if he were completely selfish, should discourage others in every way possible from buying capital goods. To encourage such buying represents either a complete misunderstanding of economic principles, or else represents an amazing willingness of business men to sacrifice themselves for the possible, but not probable, general good of society. 

From another point of view, however, as we have already suggested, soaking the rich with taxes is a "deterrent." As stated, it "deters" the introduction of new methods, because the people 84

who normally make the new investments in more efficient machines of production do not have the possibility of accumulating the funds necessary for this purpose to the normal extent. Therefore, there may be "deterrent taxes”-deterrent of the progress of the masses in the long run. Such taxes, if they deter the progress of the masses must also be regarded as deterring the improvement in the standard of living of the masses. From the standpoint of society, therefore, there is a cause of just complaint against our present system of taxation. But, the average business man and banker is far off the track when he says that business cannot go forward full steam with everybody employed merely because the present system of taxation deters new investments in capital goods. Our present system of taxation has really been changed very little for the last fifteen or twenty years, and during this period we have gone through the biggest inflation boom in our history. Question-9

Is Present Government Lending And Spending Advisable? 

Answer: This is hard to say, but at least it is an abnormal and probably ill-advised way of trying to get money into circulation. 

It is an artificial way of trying to stimulate confidence in the use of income. It is a round-about way of stopping hoarding, with its consequent stagnation of buying-power. The normal, natural way (the way that has always been used successfully in past depressions) of getting enough exchange-tickets into the market places to give everyone a job, consists in cutting down wages and prices together (this hurting no one) until fewer exchange-tickets -those actually in circulation-will give everyone a job. At some wage-and-price-scale, the smallest circulation will give everyone work and a standard of living which is directly proportional to the amount of things produced by the average worker. Let me repeat that at some wage-and-price-scale the maximum possible production can be purchased and consumed by the masses. The sole problem is to get wages and prices lowered to this point. When this is done, the depression will be over and prosperity will be on the way back. When this is done, surely Government spending and lending will be regarded as both unnecessary and inadvisable, Government spending on the present scale being far the worse of the two possible ways of starting people spending normally. 85

In the old days unemployment always came to an end by means of a lowering of the wage-and-price-rates, but today the Visionaries propose to do things in their own peculiar new and better (?) ways. 

Yet, it should be admitted that today the situation is somewhat unusual. There is perhaps a little more justification than normal for the recent Government pump-priming. The Wilson- 

Coolidge-Harding-Hoover boom went higher and stayed up longer than most. A wild inflation took place during the War, fostered and not discouraged by national policies. Gold came in from Europe by the ton. On top of this, our pseudo-statesmen permitted the printing of billions of dollars of paper money-buying-power. 

These billions of paper money went into use, creating this tremendous inflation. The deflation which would normally follow such an inflation would go to disastrously low levels unless something quite artificial was done to prevent it. 

President Hoover did just the wrong thing. He made matters worse by trying to hold up wages after the deflation was on. Thus he delayed the necessary readjustments, and thus he increased unemployment. Nor did he do anything which was constructively very valuable.

When Mr. Roosevelt became President, the people had suffered depression for more than two years. The savings of the people were mostly exhausted; unemployment was wide-spread; there was no relief in sight. Mr. Roosevelt now put through the devaluation of the gold value of the dollar. This was a most constructive and essential policy under the circumstances-one for which the New Deal has never been given sufficient credit. Devaluation probably saved the banks and life insurance companies. 

Devaluation gave the debtors a chance to pay their creditors. This program as carried through was most constructive and helpful, but its accomplishment received nothing but loud protests from the bankers and the business men. 

Probably the average banker knows less about economic theory than any other class. At least, he knows more things backwards. 

He never seems to comprehend even that which is for his own interest.

For example, a president of one of our largest banks recently said that business would not be profitable again until more people saved their money and invested in industry. Isn't it absurd? 86

Well, anyhow, this is the sort of thing that bankers think. 

They were sure that the World War could not be financed for six months. They were sure that cotton would sell for 5c a pound throughout the War. They were sure of everything that did not happen. They fostered the Federal Reserve Bank Act that permitted and encouraged the wild inflation for the War period. 

On account of this inflation-encouraged as it was by the bankers the banks did not receive their normal share of the national income during the War years. Moreover, the after effects of this inflation which the bankers fostered and approved caused thousands of banks to go into receivership. The bankers really brought this trouble down on their own heads, or, at least, they could have easily prevented it.

Mr. Roosevelt did a most valuable thing in devaluating the dollar. He did this regardless of the criticism of the bankers. 

He thus probably saved the country from a complete wash-out of the debtor-creditor-relation. He probably saved the country from a revolution. For this he was much abused. 

The Government, by its lowering of the intrinsic value of each paper dollar, paved the way for the return of a larger national income and for higher average wages-higher than would have been normal or possible had deflation taken place until prices reached the world gold values in terms of the old dollar. Except for the devaluation the debtors would never have received again a sufficient number of dollars as income to pay the principal and interest of the debts which had been established in terms of the inflated dollars. For this reason it was almost imperative that the dollars should be devaluated. This devaluation represented a lowering of real wages (gold wages) by approximately 40%. Gold prices were lowered approximately the same percentage. Moreover, labor was none the wiser and even the labor unions did not object. 

They understood too little about what was being done, this time, to resist that which was for the ultimate salvation of labor itself, but which doubtless would have been resisted had labor realized that the program virtually reduced the gold-value of wages by this amount.

By this program of devaluation, about 40% of the deflation in wages which otherwise would have been necessary was avoided. 

On this account, the New Deal was somewhat justified in believing that wages could, after the devaluation, be held fairly high in 87

terms of dollars, and that a severe deflation in wages could be avoided. The Government concluded that it might be possible, without further deflation, to bring back normal spending and normal wages and prices (in terms of devaluated dollars) if the Government started things going by borrowing and spending a certain amount of money itself until the confidence of the general public had been restored. In fact, it was difficult to carry out any other program after the Republicans had been preaching for three years the old fallacy that the policy of paying wages artificially high during the depression would increase employment. The country was not in the shape to stand open deflation of wages and prices when the New Deal took charge, and after the depression had been prolonged to such an extent by the irrational policies of the Republicans.

We therefore conclude that the policy adopted by the New Deal to try to restore confidence by public spending was not a bad one. Unfortunately, however, the program was tied up with so many red-tape regulations, and was burdened with so many reform-cures, that the people became unduly pessimistic. On this account, the expected return of buying-power by the people did not take place, and the money of the average man stayed in hiding instead of coming back into the market places.

Even today the buying-power is being saved rather than spent. 

Moreover, when, in these times, some additional buying-power does start to function, the collective bargaining activities of the labor unions force wages up for their preferred members. Today, whenever more circulation of exchange-tickets takes place, the labor union force wages and prices up, and buying then gets scared off. 

The worst of it is that the Government, which has done some wise things, sits by and encourages and approves these destructive policies. No wonder, then, that general confidence is still lacking. No wonder, then, that Government spending is still necessary for relief, etc.

The worst part of the New Deal policy has been that of supporting collective bargaining. This country will never get on its feet permanently until this policy is changed. Until those who have the power to do things realize that certain groups must not be allowed to use hold-up methods (or any methods) by which they can receive more than their competitively earned share of the 88

national income, this country cannot get back on its feet permanently.

Therefore, while Mr. Roosevelt argued in his address of May 

22, 1939, that the Government must keep on spending-to take the place of the thousands of people who are still afraid to spend, and who are therefore still piling up savings in banks-he does not seem to realize that this necessity is due to the attitude of the Government towards some rather fundamental economic problems. 

If Washington had understood its economics better, this depression might have been over several years ago. Unless Washington changes its present attitude in reference to wages, etc., probably the best chance of return to normal conditions will come with a Republican success in 1940. If the Republicans take office, the national psychology may be changed-whether the change is warranted or not.

WHAT ABOUT THE REPUBLICANS? 

Why should people be optimistic over the prospects of a Republican administration? What constructive suggestions have the Republicans made in the last ten years? What is the present

Republican attitude toward collective bargaining? Isn't this attitude about the same as that of the Democrats? What will Republicans do about relief? Will not they do the same as the Democrats until buying confidence is restored? What will the Republicans do about the farmers? Will they not cater to the farm vote by promising all sorts of impossible help and plans that involve wasting the taxpayers' money? Haven't we the example of what 

Mr. Hoover did along these lines? How can the Republicans ever be elected unless they promise the impossible to the farmers and others? How can the Republicans be elected unless they get the labor vote by promising much to organized labor? What, then, may we expect from a Republican administration? Probably nothing at all except what may irrationally come from the very fact that the administration has been changed. The people may become more confident, however little this additional confidence may be justified. And, when confidence has returned, and when the normal circulation of exchange-tickets is taking place, we shall doubtless have good times again, unless, meanwhile, the collective bargainists are obtaining such a large share of the exchange-tick- 89

ets that it is impossible for the other workers to get along comfortably on the balance of the available buying-power. 

A SUGGESTION 

New Jersey, I understand, has recently eliminated all payments of interest on bank deposits. This seems to me to be a most excellent way of putting idle deposits into use so as to increase employment at the present wage scale. The trouble is that the plan has not been universally adopted. If every bank in the United 

States would stop paying interest on deposits this policy would probably be most helpful in getting more money into the market places and into use.

The fact is that today the banks are paying about as much interest on savings deposits as the Government pays on its bonds. 

In Knoxville the rate is probably 14%. This is paid on deposits which the banks cannot loan. Isn't this foolish? Why do the banks do it? Chiefly because each banker wants his deposit-statement to be as large as possible, and is afraid that if he stops paying interest on deposits his competitor will get his accounts. Some day the banker hopes to have a demand for money. The banks compete jealously like other forms of capital. They often bankrupt one another by competition, and when they fail, the depositor loses. Now they are losing money by competing in this matter of paying interest on unloaned deposits. This not only hurts the banks, but if all such interest payments were stopped, deposits would doubtless get back to work much sooner, because people soon get sick and tired of idle funds in banks that bring no returns whatsoever. 

Can someone not do something about this? Is there no way to induce the bankers to cut out all interest on bank deposits? The results might be startling! I can think of no better way of restoring prosperity to the banks, to the depositors in the banks (the people at large), and to industry (which employs the people at large).

In Conclusion 

There are almost innumerable other fallacies of public opinion that could be mentioned in this pamphlet if there were room. 

There is, for example, the fallacy of unemployment insurance. The 90

fallacy of old age pensions. The fallacy that America cannot be prosperous unless Europe is prosperous also. The fallacy that installment buying has any effect on total buying, or in any way accounts for periods of inflation or deflation. 

But what is the use of going over any more ground? If the reader of this pamphlet accepts the logic of it and agrees with the conclusions he will be interested in going further with the subject probably, and the argument may do some good. If he does not agree with the general economic argument of the preceding pages there is not much point in going over more ground. Hence it appears to be time to close.
