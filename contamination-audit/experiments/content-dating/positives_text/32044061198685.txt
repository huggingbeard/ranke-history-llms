Report on International Trade Charter SUMMARY PURPOSES 

March 19, 1947 Chapter I, which was not agreed upon at London, outlines the general purposes of the Organization as being to promote the solution of problems of international commercial policy through consultation and collaboration; to expand production, exchange, and consumption of goods; to reduce tariffs and other trade barriers; to eliminate discriminatory trade practices; to assist in the economic and industrial development of Member countries (particularly those in the early stages of development) and in maintaining high levels of employment and real income in all countries; and to create economic conditions conducive to world peace. 

MEMBERSHIP Chapter II regarding membership in the International Trade Organiza tion (hereinafter abbreviated as ITO) provides that the original Members are to be those countries represented at the United Nations Conference on Trade and Employment (scheduled to be held after the close of the second meeting of the Preparatory Committee of the Conference, which convenes in April 1947) and which accept the Organization Charter by a date yet to be thereafter membership will be open to other countries which accept the obligations of the Charter, subject to the approval of the 

Organization. 

determined;

1.

EMPLOYMENT 

Chapter III (Articles 3 to 9) obligates Members to take action within their respective jurisdictions to achieve and maintain high levels of employment and demand and to raise standards of productivity, through measures which are appropriate to their own political and economic institutions; also to take whatever measures are appropriate and feasible to eliminate substandard labor conditions in export industries. Members do not guarantee the success of their efforts and no penalty is provided for failure to take action. They, however, recognize that any measure taken should be consistent with the other purposes and provisions of the Charter and should not be such as will create balance-of-payments difficulties for They promise to make their full contribution toward correcting any fundamental disequilibrium in their own balance of payments which handicaps other countries in maintaining employment. other countries. 

The ITO, for its part, is required to take into account, in the exercise of its functions under other provisions of the Charter, the need of Members to safeguard their economies against deflationary pressure arising from an abrupt decline in the foreign demand for their products. 

ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT 

In Chapter IV (Articles 10 to 13), which is intended primarily for the benefit of underdeveloped countries, Members undertake to promote their own economic and industrial development and to cooperate through the Economic and Social Council of the United Nations and other appropriate agencies to

promote such development generally. 

The ITO is required to assist Members Members agree to cooperate in in their plans for economic development. providing capital funds, material, and techniques necessary for economic development in other Member countries. The Members which receive such assistance reciprocally agree not to take any unreasonable action injurious to the interests of Members, business entities, or persons supplying it. 

Members are permitted to impose protective measures otherwise forbidden under the Charter for the purpose of promoting the establishment or reconstruction of particular domestic industries, but only after the ITO has considered the views of all Members concerned and made its recommendations. 

Measures reducing the benefit of any tariff concession made in trade agreements pursuant to Chapter V may be put into effect only upon recommendation of the ITO and with the consent of the other Members affected. 

Thus, a concession received by the United States in a trade agreement could not under this chapter be withdrawn or modified without our consent. The ITO is authorized, however, in its discretion, to release the applicant Member from any of the other obligations assumed under the Charter.

GENERAL COMMERCIAL PROVISIONS Under Chapter V Members of the International Trade Organization assume definite obligations looking toward the reduction or elimination of most of the principal governmental trade-regulation devices which have been employed by various nations to limit imports and exports of merchandise. The chapter is, in effect, a code of governmental conduct with respect to (1) tariff duties and internal taxes on imports; (2) quotas; (3) subsidies; (4) state-trading operations; and (5) miscellaneous nontariff trade controls. No such code is provided by the Charter in the case of exchange controls, which are now so widely used. Such controls are, however, regulated by the International Monetary Fund Agreement, to which the United States has already subscribed. There is, moreover, a provision in Article 28 of the Charter which may serve to limit discrimination between countries in use of exchange controls. 

Nondiscriminatory treatment

Article 14 requires the Members to extend to each other unconditional most-favored-nation treatment with respect to import and export duties and with respect to internal taxes; this conforms to the general policy of the United States. Excepted from this commitment, however, are most of the important existing preferences in import tariffs, including preferences within the British, French and other Empires, and the preferences between the United States and Cuba and between the United States and the Philippines; but these excepted preferences are to be the subject of negotiation for reduction or elimination. No new preferences are to be established. 

Preferential export tariffs and preferential internal taxes are unqualifiedly prohibited.

Internal taxes 

Article 15, which was not agreed upon at London, provides that imported products shall not be subject to internal taxes higher than on like domestic products. Internal taxes imposed on imported commodities to protect domestic production of unlike but competitive commodities is recognized as contrary to the spirit of this article, and it is provided that all possible measures shall be taken to prevent the adoption in the future of new or higher taxes of this kind. The present United States taxes on the processing of coconut, palm, and palm kernel oils, imposed primarily to protect domestic producers of other oils, need not be removed, but they may not be increased; and new internal taxes on products supplied almost exclusively by imports could not be imposed if the products so imported compete with related domestic products, unless such domestic products should be similarly taxed. 

Negotiations regarding tariffs and tariff preferences Whenever requested by another Member, each Member is required by Article 24 to negotiate for the reduction of tariffs on imports and exports and for the reduction or elimination of those preferences in import tariffs which are exempt from the prohibition of Article 14. With respect to such preferences, it is stipulated that margins of preference will automatically be reduced or eliminated by any reductions agreed upon in nonpreferential rates; preferential rates may not be reduced except to meet a reduction of the general rate below the level of the previous preferential rate. The binding of low tariffs or duty-free treatment is, in principle, recognized as equivalent to the substantial reduction of high tariffs or the elimination of tariff preferences. 1

If a Member refuses to negotiate, the ITO, if it finds that there is not "sufficient justification" for the refusal, may authorize the complaining Member (or in exceptional cases Members generally) to withhold from the refusing Member the tariff concessions made in trade agreements with other countries.

Under Article 24, the United States would be required to continue in effect a tariff bargaining program as long as it remains a Member. The program need not be the one in force under the Trade Agreements Act, but it must be one which provides in some way for the bona fide consideration of tariff reductions.

The requirement to negotiate for the reduction or elimination of preferences in import duties involves largely preferences, granted by Members of the ITO to associated or dependent countries, such as between countries of the British and French Empires. Compared with countries associated with those Empires, the United States has relatively few tariff preferences, although it has preferential arrangements with Cube, the Philippines, and certain minor insular possessions. The United States has already undertaken to negotiate regarding the reduction or elimination of the tariff preferences accorded to Cuba, and if requested to do so, it would need to negotiate similarly with respect to its other preferences, especially those granted the Philippines. Should negotiations result in agreement to reduce the preferences accorded Cuba or the Philippines and should those countries refuse to accede thereto, the United States would have to terminate the existing agreements granting the preferences. 

Escape clause and nonnullification provisions Article 34 authorizes emergency action for the protection of domestic industries on a basis similar to that in Article XI of our trade agreement

with Mexico and to that which is now required for all trade agreements by 

Executive Order No. 9832 of February 25, 1947. It provides that if, as a result of unforeseen developments and of a concession granted pursuant to the Charter, a product is being imported into a Member country in such increased quantities and under such conditions as to cause or threaten serious injury to domestic producers, the Member may withdraw the concession or modify it by increasing the duty or imposing a quota. If other Members do not agree with the action, they may withdraw equivalent concessions from the trade of the Member taking such action, unless the ITO opposes their doing so.

The Executive Order mentioned above establishes the procedure under Action by the President is to be after an investigation, this provision. report, and recommendations have been made by the Tariff Commission, which is charged with the duty of receiving and passing upon complaints of injury to domestic industries.

Article 35 provides that if the ITO finds, after complaint, that a Member has taken action which violates the spirit of the Charter (even though there is no breach of its terms), the withdrawal of concessions by other countries from the trade of such Member may be authorized. This article is a recognition that the Charter cannot succeed unless the Members cooperate fully toward achieving its objectives; that devices are readily available to restrict trade without conflicting with the exact letter of the Charter; and that, in the final analysis, avoidance of nullification of concessions is dependent more on the vigor with which the interest of each country is defended in its contacts with other countries than on the exact phraseology of the Charter.

Miscellaneous commercial provisions Although these articles were discussed, no agreement on the precise language of Articles 16 to 23 was reached at London. The provisions, in general, are patterned after United States laws and policies, although some important changes in existing laws would be required if the Charter is adopted by the United States in its present form. 

Transit traffic.-Article 16 provides for freedom of movement in transit through each Member country of goods destined to or coming from any other Member, without unreasonable charges and without discrimination against any Member country; also for nondiscrimination between goods coming directly from the exporting country and those coming through a third Member country. This article is in accord with United States practices. Practices of the kind prohibited by this article are, however, used by certain other countries contrary to the interests of the United States. 

Antidumping and countervailing duties.--Article 17 requires that antidumping duties shall not be imposed on the product of any Member country except to the extent that the goods are sold for export at a lower price than for domestic consumption, and that countervailing duties shall be imposed only to the extent necessary to offset subsidies given by the exporting Member country.

These provisions correspond to United States practice. In both cases, there must be injury to industries in the importing country; this provision would necessitate no change in the United States antidumping law but would require a change in the United States countervailing-duty law (section 303 of the Tariff Act of 1930).

Dutiable value.-The most important provisions of Article 18 are that value of imports for duty purposes shall be based on actual not arbitrary values and not on the value of domestic goods; that internal taxes of the country of export, if not imposed on exports, shall not be included in

dutiable value; and that in determining such value commercial exchange values should be used for converting foreign currencies. The first of these provisions would necessitate changes in United States, laws to eliminate the assessing of duties, in certain instances, on the American selling price of the domestic counterpart of the imported article; in some other countries more drastic changes in methods would be required. The provision excluding from dutiable value internal taxes in the exporting country not applied to exports would also require a change in United States laws. 

Subsidiary customs charges.--Article 19 recognizes that subsidiary. charges and fees relating to customs formalities (such as issuance or certification of documents, analysis, and inspection service) should not be used for protective or fiscal purposes and that customs formalities should be simplified. Members agree to review their laws and to make appropriate modifications in them.

Marks of origin.--Article 20 relates to provisions requiring the marking of imported goods to show the country of origin and seeks to prevent provisions of this kind being burdensome to trade or discriminatory as be- United States practice in general conforms with tween Member countries. the article, but there would have to be a change in the law applying to certain articles to permit them to be marked after instead of before importation.

Publication of laws and decisions.--Article 21 requires that laws, regulations, and decisions regarding customs duties and requirements must be published, and that each Member shall maintain independent tribunals for the review and correction of administrative action. Goods in transit at the time of the adoption of laws or decisions resulting in higher duties shall "as a general rule" be exempted from their effect if they are put into effect less than 30 days after publication. This would necessitate

1 a departure from the United States practice of putting new tariff acts of Congress into effect upon their enactment, unless the phrase "as a general rule" should be interpreted as permitting that practice. In other respects 

United States laws and practices conform to the requirements of Article 21. 

Information, statistics, and trade terminology. --Article 22 relates to trade and economic statistics which Members agree to supply the ITO. It also authorizes the Organization to adopt standards of nomenclature, terms, and forms of documents and statistics upon vote of two-thirds of the Members of the Organization present and voting (Article 66, par. 8); any Member isfree, however, within a stated period to notify the Organization of rejection or reservations.

Boycotts.--Article 23 is intended to discourage boycotts, sponsored by national, state, or local governments, designed to curtail the consumption of products of other Member countries on grounds of origin or the sale of products for consumption within other Member countries on grounds of destination. 

Quotas and exchange control !

Article 25 contains a pledge to eliminate quota restrictions on imports and exports in trade with Member countries, subject to exceptions stated in Articles 25, 26, and 28. Certain of the exceptions are of short duration only and are designed to aid in the correction of abnormal surplus and scarcity situations resulting from the war. 

Permanent exceptions are provided for (1) import quotas imposed on agricultural or fishery products in conjunction with domestic programs operating to restrict domestic production or marketing or to remove surpluses by disposel at less than current prices; (2) export or import quotas imposed in connection with international commodity agreements under Chapter VII; and (3) export or

import prohibitions or restrictions on private trade for the purpose of establishing or maintaining a monopoly by a government-trading organization.

These exceptions would make it possible to impose import quotas in order to maintain production-subsidy arrangements, including price-support programs such as those now in effect under the Steagall amendment, provided that domestic production or marketing controls or surplus disposal programs are in effect. 

Otherwise, quotas could not be imposed for this purpose except, up to July 1, 1949, in cases where wartime price-support programs have resulted in government-held surplus stocks. 

Article 26 authorizes quotas when necessary either to forestall or stop a decline in monetary reserves, or if the Member has, very low monetary reserves to achieve a reasonable increase in them. 

Quotas permitted under this article must be removed when balance-of-payments conditions would no longer justify the imposition of new quotas. Various provisions in article 26 for consultation are designed to discourage the misuse of quotas for balance-of-payments reasons. Quotas found by the ITO to be unnecessarily damaging, or to discriminate inconsistently with Articles 27. and 28, must be withdrawn or modified; otherwise the Members adversely affected are authorized to take such retaliatory action as the Organization may specify. However, quotas approved by the Organization before imposition may not be challenged except as to discriminations forbidden by 

Articles 27 and 28.

Article 28, in addition to provisions regarding exceptions to the rule of nondiscrimination in quotas, provides that the products of a Member whose currency the International Monetary Fund has declared "scarce" may be subjected by other Members to quotas to whatever extent is necessary to

bring the demand for the scarce currency into balance with the available supply. For example, should the Monetary Fund declare United States dollars "scarce," any country, whether or not experiencing overall balanceof-payments difficulties, would then be free by quotas to restrict imports from the United States to the amount which could be paid for by the dollars available to it.

Article 27 provides that quotas must be administered so as not to discriminate against Member countries and sets up rules and procedures for accomplishing that purpose. Article 28 permits discriminatory quotes in certain defined cases. 

Discrimination may be practiced until December 1, 1951, by countries whose economies have been disrupted by war, provided that the discrimination does not involve a "substantial departure" from Article Discrimination may also be used pursuant to the scarce currency pro- 27. vision of the Monetary Fund.

Discriminatory quotas may be used when necessary to provide a Member with larger imports than could be obtained by quotas conforming to the provisions of Article 27, provided these quotas have an effect equivalent to exchange-restrictions permitted under the International Monetary Fund. The ITO may grant advance approval for a given discrimination in quotas, after which it may not be challenged. ITO.

On this point it should be noted that paragraph 2 of Article 28 provides for the removal of any exchange-control measure affecting imports (as well as any quota on imports) which the ITO, after consultation with the Fund, finds to be unnecessarily discriminatory or to discriminate in violation of

Subsidies

Article 30 imposes upon Members obligations regarding both production · and export subsidies. Effective 3 years after the Charter comes into force, export subsidies and subsidies of any kind which result in lower prices for export than for domestic consumption are prohibited, excert in cases where it is determined by the ITO that an extension requested by any Member should be granted. Export subsidies may, however, be granted, after this 3-year period, on primary products with respect to which burdensome world surpluses exist, or threaten to exist, and with respect to which it is determined that the commodity-agreement procedure provided in Chapter VII has not succeeded, and is unlikely to succeed within a reasonable time in remedying the situation. This determination is to be made "through the Organization by consultation among the Members having an important interest in the trade in the product concerned." Under this provision the problem of various United States export products, such as cotton, wheat, and tobacco (possibly also such articles as walnuts and prunes) could be handled by commodity agreement, or, if that fails, by export. 

In no case, however, may export subsidies be used to obtain subsidies. a larger share of world trade than the subsidizing Member had in a previous representative period.)

Production subsidies are permitted if they do not result in lower prices for export than for domestic consumption, but if any Member grants a production subsidy which, although it does not have that result on prices, operates to increase exports or decrease imports, it must report to the ITO regarding the extent, nature, and effects of the subsidy and is obligated to discuss with other Members or with the ITO the possibility of limiting any such subsidy which is determined to be seriously prejudicial to the interests of any Member.

Article 30 would not interfere with price support prógrams under the Steagall amendment through December 31, 1948, the present date of the termination of its provisions, even though the programs should result in prices higher for domestic consumption than for export. Moreover, it would not prevent the same or similar programs being continued in force up to 3 years from the date the Charter enters into effect. Thereafter, however, export, subsidies or other programs resulting in lower prices for export than for domestic consumption could be maintained only on primary products of which burdensome world surpluses exist, and only under the procedures already set forth in a preceding paragraph. For a statement of the possible effects of the quota provisions of Article 25 upon programs under the Steagall amendment, see a preceding section of this summary dealing with quotas and exchange controls. 

State trading

Article 31 provides that state-trading enterprises will accord the commerce of each Member country treatment at least as favorable as the treatment accorded the products of any country other than that in which the enterprise is located. External commercial purchases and sales are. to be based solely on commercial considerations. In making purchases for government use, the commerce of other Members must be afforded "fair and equitable treatment." These provisions are intended as an approximate quid pro quo for the nondiscriminatory rules applicable to privatetrading countries under other articles of the Charter. 

Article 32 requires that in the case of individual-product monopolies there must (if requested by any Member) be negotiations, similar to tariff negotiations under Article 24, for the reduction of the margin by which the price of the imported product sold in the home market may exceed the landed

cost. A Member newly establishing such a monopoly may not create such a margin greater than the previously negotiated tariff rate. Similar pro- visions are made regarding the margin by which the price for monopolized products when exported may exceed the price of such products when sold in the domestic market.

Article 33, which was not agreed to at London, provides that a Member which maintains a complete monopoly of its import trade shall promote trade expansion by undertaking periodically to agree to the importation of products of other Members, as a group, valued at a stipulated aggregate amount. 

This is intended as a quid pro quo for extension to the state-trading country of the benefit of tariff reductions made by private-trading Member countries in agreements with one another. 

Relations with non-Members

Under Article 36, which was not agreed to at London, countries which, although eligible to membership, do not join the ITO are to be denied, beginning one year 1/ after the Charter comes into effect, the trade benefits under the Charter, including the benefit of trade agreements made pursuant thereto. This means that Members would have to terminate their unconditional most-favored-nation agreements with such non-Members. This would require that the United States modify its policy of extending reduced tradeagreement rates to all foreign countries not found to be discriminating against United States trade. This could probably be done, so far as tradeagreement concessions are concerned, under the provision of the Trade Agreements Act permitting the President to withhold concessions from countries 1/ The Organization may extend this time for periods not to exceed 

6 months each.

found by him to be pursuing policies which tend to defeat the purposes of that act.

General exceptions to Chapter V 

Article 37 reserves to the Members complete freedom of action to impose whatever restrictions they wish to safeguard public morals or health (including animal and plant quarantines). Exceptions are also made for measures relating. to fissionable materials and traffic in arms and military supplies; 'in time of war any measures relating to essential security interests may be enforced unilaterally. There are other exceptions of types customary in commercial treaties (e.g. as to gold and silver, prison-made goods, and conservation of natural resources) and finally there is a general exception under which the ITO may relieve a Member of any of its obligations under Chapter V. 

Territorial application

Article 38 contains an appropriate definition of "customs territory" based on section 338 (i) of the United States Tariff Act of 1930; this is designed to insure that Empire tariff preferences are subject to negotiation and elimination. 

By the same token, the United States must, if requested, negotiate for the elimination of the preferences it grants to those of its possessions which have separate tariffs, all of which are areas of very minor importance in our trade. 

Customs Unions between Members or between a Member and a non-Member are permitted as an exception to the provisions requiring nondiscriminatory treatment, but there must be practically free trade within the Union and practically the same tariffs in force in all parts of the Union. Finally, the tariffs of any new Union when formed may not "on the whole" be higher than the average in the constituent territories prior to formation of the Union

RESTRICTIVE BUSINESS PRACTICES Chapter VI (Articles 39-45) relates primarily to international cartel arrangements among private producing interests for the restraint of competition by such practices as fixing prices and terms of sale, allocating markets among the cartel members, limiting their production or sales, and suppressing technology or invention. Restrictive trade practices in international trade are not prohibited as such, but Members agree to take appropriate measures, individually and through the ITO, to prevent them whenever they have "harmful effects on the expansion of production and trade and the maintenance in all countries of high levels of real income, or on any of the purposes of the Organization...." The ITO is authorized to investigate complaints that any business practice has such effects and to make and publish its findings thereon. If the Organization finds that the practice does have the effects in question, it may in its discretion recommend remedial measures. Members agree to take the fullest account of the findings and recommendations of the ITO in considering remedial action within their own systems of law and economic organization, and to inform the Organization of what action they have taken pursuant thereto. 

Chapter VI is consistent with the Sherman Antitrust Act; it does not appear to conflict with existing United States laws, unless the ITO should hold that agreements under the Webb-Pomerene Act among United States concerns governing their export sales have the harmful effects specified in

CHAPTER III EMPLOYMENT 

Chapter III, which includes Articles 3 to 9, deals with the subject of employment and with several other subjects regarded as closely related to the maintenance of a high level of employment. 

Provisions Relating Specifically to Employment 

(Articles 3, 4, 8, and 9)

Charter Provisions 

In Article 3 Member countries recognize that the avoidance of unemployment or under-employment is not of domestic concern alone, but "is a necessary condition for the expansion of international trade," and that, although measures to maintain employment must be primarily domestic measures, in the choice of such measures Members should seek to avoid creating balanceof-payment difficulties for other countries. There follows in Article 4 The the most definite commitment in the Charter regarding employment. 

United States and other Member countries obligate themselves to "take action designed to achieve and maintain full and productive employment and high and stable levels of effective demand within their own jurisdiction." 

Article 8 recognizes the need of Member countries to safeguard their economies against deflationary pressures arising from "serious or abrupt decline in the effective demand of other countries" for their products. 

It, therefore, provides that the International Trade Organization shall take account of that need in exercising its functions as defined in other articles of the Charter.

In Article 9 there is the further commitment that Member countries shall participate in arrangements, sponsored by the Economic and Social Council of the United Nations, (1) "for the regular collection, analysis, and exchange of information" bearing upon "domestic employment problems, trends, and policies," and (2) "for consultations with a view to concerted action on the part of governments and intergovernmental organizations in the field of employment policies." Comment

It will be noted that the commitment in Article 4 to "take action designed to achieve and maintain full and productive employment and high and stable levels of effective demand" involves no obligation to take any particular line of action. It is specifically provided that action shall be "through measures appropriate" to each country's political and economic institutions. Moreover, Members are not called upon to guarantee the success of their efforts, end no penalty is provided for failure to take The principal effect of the commitment is to place upon each country the obligation to adopt measures of some kind to maintain employment, and to direct special attention to the interrelation of programs affecting employment and those affecting international trade. action.

Article 8 was inserted principally because of the fear of a number of countries that a recurrence of a severe and prolonged depression in the United States and in other industrialized creditor countries would have repercussions on their economies as unfortunate as those which occurred after 1929.

Provisions in other articles of the Charter also recognize the right of the countries to take measures to minimize the effects upon them of

economic disturbances originating in other countries. Reference is made particularly to the discussion of the provisions regarding quotas and exchange controls (Section C of Chapter V including Articles 25 through 

29), particularly Article 26 regarding restrictions to safeguard the balance of payments and Article 28 setting forth exceptions to the rule of nondiscrimination in the administration of quotas and exchange controls. 

In this connection it should also be pointed out that the United States has already adopted legislation looking toward maintenance of high levels of domestic employment. The "Employment Act of 1946" in general terms outlines national objectives with respect to employment, production, and purchasing power. Under it a Council of Economic Advisers has been appointed to assist in carrying out the purpose of the act. 

Other Related Provisions 

(Articles 5, 6, and 7)

Charter Provisions 

The provisions of Articles 5, 6, and 7 of Chapter III are designed to take into account the fact that the maintenance of high levels of employment is closely related to other factors. Member countries obligate themselves (1) in Article 5 to take action "designed progressively to develop economic resources and to raise standards of productivity within their jurisdiction through measures compatible with the purposes of the Organization"; (2) in Article 6 to take "whatever action may be appropriate and feasible to eliminate substandard conditions of labor," particularly in production for export; and (3) in Article 7 to make their "full contribution to action" designed to correct any "fundamental disequilibrium in their balance of payments involving other countries in persistent balance of payment difficulties which handicap them in maintaining employment."

Comment 

All three of the provisions outlined above merely obligate Member countries to take some kind of action designed to accomplish the specified objectives, but they are not required to guarantee their achievement and the obligation is not accompanied by any sanction. 

The obligation to develop natural resources and raise standards of productivity is evidently included here because of the relation to employment. It is repeated substantially in Chapter IV, particularly Article 11. 

See the discussion of that chapter.

The obligation to take action looking toward the elimination of substandard conditions of labor is in recognition of the fact that the existence of such conditions in the export industries of certain countries is a deterrent to reduction in trade barriers by other countries and to the expansion of world trade. The volume of trade depends not only upon the height of trade barriers but upon popular purchasing power in the various countries. 

The provision in Article 7 that Member countries shall make their contribution to correct maladjustments in their balances of payment which create persistent balance of payment difficulties for other countries is an important one. It was inserted largely because of the fear of certain countries that they will not have the dollar exchange to pay for the volume of goods needed by them from the United States. This fear relates particularly to the postwar period of rehabilitation and reconstruction and to the possibility of a depression in the United States which would curtail United States demand for their goods and cause a drastic reduction in their export prices.

Exceptions to Charter Provisions When Necessary to Assist in Economic Development (Article 13) Charter Provision 

Article 13 makes special provision for exceptions from the Charter prohibitions of restrictive trade practices, and for release from tariff obligations under trade agreements. It does not state that these provisions are intended only or chiefly for underdeveloped countries, or countries particularly injured by the war, but from the context it appears in fact that it was intended mainly for their benefit. 

The article states that Members recognize that governmental assistance may be required to promote the establishment or reconstruction of particular industries, and may take the form of protective measures against foreign competition, but that the "unwise" use of such measures by any country would impose "undue" burdens on its own economy and "unwarranted" restrictions on international trade and might unnecessarily increase the difficulties of adjustment for other countries.

Paragraph 3 of Article 13 provides that if a Member, in the interests of its developmental program, proposes to employ protective measures which would conflict with its obligations under the Charter (including trade agreements made pursuant to Article 24), it shall so advise the Organization giving the reasons for the proposed action. The Organization shall then give those Members whose trade would be "substantially affected" by the proposed program an opportunity to present their views. After that the Organization shall examine the merits of the proposal, táking into account the stage of economic development or reconstruction of the applicant country.

If the Organization finds that any measure is desirable which would conflict with an obligation of the Member under a trade agreement (for example, an obligation fixing a rate of duty), it shall sponsor and assist in negotiations between the applicant Member and other Members substantially affected, with a view to obtaining agreement among them (if agreement is not reached apparently the obligation remains in effect). 

If, on the other hand, the Organization favors any action not in conflict with a trade agreement but in conflict with provisions of the Charter (for example, the general prohibition against quantitative restrictions on imports), it may, in its discretion, release the applicant Member from the obligation, subject to such limitations as the Organization may impose. Comment

The provision, under certain conditions, for exceptions to Charter prohibitions of restrictive trade practices such as quotas, and for release from trade-agreement obligations with respect to tariffs, will presumably make it easier for underindustrialized countries to accept the obligations of the Charter and to enter into trade agreements reducing tariffs as required by the Charter.

This is true because there is a sharp tendency in such countries toward increased protection, particularly to "infant" industries. Under Article 13 of the Charter, however, protection cannot be given to an "infant" industry by withdrawing a tariff concession made in a trade agreement or by impairing the value of such concession by imposing an import quote unless the country to which the concession is made agrees to it.

CHAPTER V. GENERAL COMMERCIAL POLICY SECTION A. GENERAL COMMERCIAL PROVISIONS SECTION B. TARIFFS AND TARIFF PREFERENCES 

This group of Articles has to do with customs duties and internal taxes applied by Members which influence their foreign trade. In general, the purposes of these articles are (1) to promote equal tariff treatment by Member countries of their imports from and exports to all other Member countries of like commodities; (2) to concentrate protection of domestic industries in customs duties, that is to say, to eliminate the practice of affording domestic industries the equivalent of tariff protection in the guise of internal revenue taxes; (3) to provide for reductions in tariff duties and tariff preferences by negotiation among Member countries and, except under certain conditions described in these Articles and in Chapter IV, to assure each subscribing country that the others will not increase their tariff protection on articles included in the agreements.

Negotiation for the Reduction of Tariffs and Tariff Preferences 

(Article 24) Charter provisions 

Each Member undertakes, in Article 24, to conduct negotiations with each other Member "directed to the substantial reduction of tariffs ***" and, as stated above, to "the elimination of import tariff preferences" (i.e., of those preferences which, by Art. 14, par. 2, are excepted from outright prohibition).

The commitment to negotiate with respect to preferences is binding notwithstanding that the preferences had been granted pursuant to agreements with third countries; the preferences need not be eliminated in violation of such agreements, but if the third countries do not agree to the modifications negotiated between Members of the International Trade Organization, the agreements granting preferences must be terminated in accordance with their terms. Negotiated reductions in nonpreferential tariffs are to operate automatically to reduce or eliminate margins of preference; i. e., preferential rates may not be reduced as such.

The binding of low triffs or duty-free treatment is, in principle, recognized as equivalent in value to the substantial reduction of high tariffs or elimination of tariff preferences.

It may also suspend any obligation in Chapter V of the Charter, insofar as that product is concerned; the obligation which is most pertinent is that contained in Article 25 not to maintain quota limitations on imports. 

This "escape clause" also authorizes similar protection to countries the tariff preferences received by which have been removed or lessened by concessions of the preference-granting country under the Charter. 

The other interested Members must be consulted about proposed emergency action under Article 34, and if they do not agree with the action taken, they may suspend the application of equivalent concessions to the trade of the Member taking the action, unless the International Trade Organization opposes their doing so. 

Non-nullification provision

Article. 35, in addition to a provision in paragraph 1 for consultation between Members regarding all matters affecting the operation of the Charter, contains in paragraph 2 an anti-nullification provision designed to protect against action contrary to the spirit of the Charter. It requires the 

Members to give sympathetic consideration to complaints of other Members against measures which, even though they do not violate the letter of the Charter, are alleged to violate its object. If the matter is not satisfactorily adjusted, it may be referred to the Organization, which, upon finding the complaint justified, may authorize the withdrawal, to an appropriate extent, of concessions applicable to the trade of the member found to have violated an object of the Charter. A Member from whom concessions are so withdrawn may resign from the Organization on 60 days' written notice.

The "escape clause."-The over-all objective of the International 

Trade Organization is the reduction of barriers to international trade. The Charter, however, recognizes the propriety of using both import and export tariffs for the protection of domestic industries. 

Quite apart from the provisions in Chapter IV of the Charter devoted to economic development, Article 34 provides for the protection of established industries from injury resulting from trade agreement concessions. 

Article 

34 is an adaptation of the general escape clause first used by the United States in the trade agreement with Mexico. It provides for the modification (by imposing a quota limitation on imports or otherwise) or withdrawal of concessions when the concessions have operated in conjunction with unforeseen developments to cause or threaten serious injury to domestic producers. 

The determination that modification is appropriate is vested in the first instance with the Member granting the concessions. 

However, that Member undertakes also to give notice of modifications or withdrawals to the International Trade Organization and to discuss with other interested Member countries the action proposed to be taken. Even though agreement may not be reached between the interested countries as to the propriety of the escape clause action, the Member proposing to take it is free to do so but the other Member or Members affected thereby may withdraw equivalent concessions applicable to that Member's trade even though such withdrawal is not necessary for the protection of their own industries. Article 34 does not contemplate any modification in the existing laws or policies of the United States.

ANTIDUMPING AND COUNTERVAILING DUTIES Charter provisions 

(Article 17--not red upon at 

London Conference) .

Article 17 provides limitations on the conditions under which antidumping and countervailing duties may be imposed and on the amount of such duties. Antidumping duties are not to be imposed unless the goods are sold for export at a price below the price at which they are sold for 1/ domestic consumption, and the antidumping duty may not exceed the amount by which the domestic price exceeds the export price. 

Countervailing duties are to be imposed only to offset bounties or subsidies on production or export and are limited in amount to the bounty or subsidy ascertained to have been granted directly or indirectly. 

The exemption of exported goods from internal taxes or import duties is not to be the basis for assessment of either antidumping or countervailing duties. Neither type of duty is to be imposed except where the dumping or subsidization, as the case may be, causes or threatens injury to domestic industries in the importing country, or prevents the establishment of a domestic industry.

Comment on the antidumping provisions This article requires that if any of the Members of the International Trade Organization desire to apply antidumping duties, they must proceed on the basis which is most closely in accordance with the generally accepted economic definition of dumping; that is to say, that dumping duties are assessable when goods are sold for export at less than the prevailing price 1 There are some special provisions for cases where there is no domestic market price.

V in the domestic market of the exporting country and that the antidumping duty should be the amount of such disparity. 

Further, if the lower export price results solely from the remission of internal taxes or import duties on the goods exported, that there is no occasion for assessing antidumping duties, and in no event should antidumping duties be assessed unless the dumping threatens or causes injury to domestic industry.

Effect on United States policies.-The antidumping definition in in substance,

Article 17 is, that contained in the antidumping law of the United States and the provisions of the Charter respecting antidumping duties would not require any change in United States law or policy. 

Effect on the policies of foreign countries.--On the other hand, the antidumping provisions of Article 17 would require change in the laws of some foreign countries. For example, in Canada there is considerable administrative discretion for determining the dutiable value of specified fruits and vegetables during the period in which such products are produced Arbitrary valuations are employed, and if the actual selling price of such fruits and vegetables in the United States for export to in Canada.

Canada is less than such arbitrary valuations antidumping duties are imposed in the amount of such disparity.

The duties are imposed in such cases even though the export price of the products in the United States is identical with the current market price for domestic consumption in the United States. 

Canadian law and practice would need to be substantially modified if the Charter is adopted in its present form.. 

Comment on the countervailing duty provision

The provision that countervailing duties should not exceed the amount of the subsidy granted is in accordance with the existing law and practice

V of the United States, and, so far as is known, of such foreign countries as have countervailing duty laws. It is also the general practice to regard the remission of duties and taxes on exports as not being an appropriate basis for assessing countervailing duties. Of course, if there is an excessive remission, that is, if the so-called tax rebate on exportation is more than the taxes actually paid, the excess is a proper basis for countervailing duties and the Charter would not preclude the assessment of countervailing duties equal to the amount of such excess payments. The proposal that countervailing duties are not to be imposed unless the subsidization of the foreign product causes injury to domestic industries in the importing country would require a change in the United States law (section 303 of the Tariff Act of 1930). The present law requires the assessment of the countervailing duty upon a finding that a subsidy has in fact been paid by a foreign country on the imported article, without any limitacion respecting injury to domestic industries. In this respect our present countervailing duty law differs from our antidumping act, where injury must be shown.

BOYCOTTS 

(Article 23-not agreed upon at London Conference) 

Charter Provisions 

Article 23 is designed to discourage governmentally sponsored boycotts and other campaigns designed to curtail the consumption of products of other Member countries on grounds of origin or the sale of products for consumption within other Member countries on grounds of destination. 

Comment 

This article applies to boycotts encouraged, financed, or sponsored by Governments, whether Federal, State, or Municipal. It does not undertake to restrict private boycotts, to engage in which is regarded in the United States as being an inherent privilege of citizenship. 

Adoption of the Charter would apparently commit the Government of the United States not only to refrain from itself encouraging boycotts against the products of other Members on the grounds of origin but also to impose or threaten sanctions or take some other steps designed to discourage State and Municipal officers from promoting such boycotts, Approval of the Charter by the Congress would itself be a strong discouragement to local officers against promoting boycotts. The Charter does not seem to require so drastic an action as a Federal criminal law providing that any governmental officer encouraging boycotts shall be subjected to criminal penalties. 

Rather, it seems to contemplate that if a situation arises requiring the exercise of Federal authority to support the commitment of Article 23, appropriate action to discourage such boycotts will be taken at that time. 

The decision could then be made as to whether the action should be admonitory,

V advisory, civil, or penal. The important thing would be the forestalling of a threatened boycott, or the prompt ending of an actual boycott, rather than punishment of an officer for promoting a boycott.

Nondiscriminatory Administration of Quotas and Exceptions Thereto (Articles 27 and 28)

Charter Provisions as to nondiscrimination Article 27 provides that quotas (including tariff quotas) shall not be administered in a discriminatory manner, i.e., quotas may not be applied on products of a Member unless they are also applied to the like products of all other foreign countries. Nondiscriminatory practice is to be achieved by the use of global quotas, whether or not allocated among supplying countries, wherever practicable. 

If global quotas are not practicable import licenses may be used, but in general such licenses should not require that the product be imported from a particular country, unless the quota is allocated among countries. If these methods of licensing are found impracticable, quotas "should in principle" be allocated among particular countries, in accordance with commercial considerations, e.g., price, quality, and customary sources of supply.

In appraising such considerations, the Member may seek an agreement with other interested Members, or, if this is not practicable, it shall make an allocation on the basis of the sources of imports in a previous representative period, taking account of special factors affecting the trade.

V Members imposing import restrictions on any given article undertake to furnish other Members all relevant information about their licensing or quota system applicable to the article, including the amount or value of the product permitted entry and the allocation, if any, by supplying countries.

The Member enforcing the quota makes the initial selection of the representative period for quota purposes, and the initial appraisal of special factors, under both Articles 25 and 27 but agrees, on request, to consult with other interested Members, or with the Organization, regarding adjustments.

A further qualification might result from the fact that the imposition of quotas on imports to assist in maintaining a production subsidy arrangement is prohibited (by Chapter V, section C) unless there is restriction on the amount of the commodity marketed or produced domestically, or unless there is with respect thereto a governmental disposal program at less than current market prices. (See discussion of Quotas and Exchange Controls). At the end of the 3 years following the coming into effect of this Charter, still another qualification might result from the provisions restricting the use of export subsidies discussed in the next paragraph. In order to continue exports of commodities which owing to price guarantees are raised above world price levels, export subsidies in some form or another are frequently required. 

The prohibition of export subsidies, as has been stated, does not become effective until after 3 years from the date the Charter enters into force. 

Under certain circumstances, moreover, such subsidies may be continued thereafter. Of special importance is the provision regarding articles which are, world or threaten to be, in burdensome/surplus. If international commodity

V agreements with respect to such articles do not succeed, within a reasonable time, in remedying the surplus situation, subsidies on exports may be continued. Under this provision such United States export products as cotton, wheat, tobacco, walnuts, and prunes, if they should be in burdensome world surplus after the end of the 3-year period, could still, if not made the subject of effective international commodity agreements, be given export subsidies, notwithstanding the general prohibition of such subsidies. 

The United States has in the past used export subsidies as a means of alleviating domestic surpluses even in the absence of world surpluses. 

The existing commitment of the United States Government to maintain prices of a considerable number of agricultural products at 90 or 92 percent of parity in the period ending December 31, 1948, would not be affected by adoption of the Charter. Even though the program should be extended for an additional period, it is not likely to be affected by the Charter, at least until the Charter has been in effect for 3 years. Even after 3 years the domestic price support measures could be maintained to whatever extent the Government desired, but export subsidies could not be used as a support for the domestic program except in the case of primary commodities of which there may be a burdensome world surplus.

The long-term exception to the prohibition of subsidies in Article 30 relates only to primary commodities. If the definition of "primary commodity" in the chapter (VII) relating to Commodity Agreements (there is no definition in Article 30) is applied to Article 30, that article would not permit the granting of export subsidies on a manufactured product such as cotton cloth, even though an export subsidy on raw cotton may be permissible.

Va This would mean that the country granting a subsidy on raw cotton probably would not be able to export any appreciable amount of cotton textiles during the period of subsidization. 

It is not clear under the Charter whether export subsidies may be. granted in conjunction with the operation of a commodity agreement under Chapter VII. While the Charter clearly permits such subsidies to be granted on primary commodities of which there is a burdensome world surplus in the absence of or failure of such an agreement, it contains no express exception authorizing a subsidy during the agreement. It would seem that if it is intended to tolerate export subsidies as a means of carrying out commodity agreements, express provision should be incorporated to that effect, especially since an express exception in the case of such agreements is made in Article 25 (d) to the provisions prohibiting quotas on imports.

CHAPTER VII. INTERGOVERNMENTAL COMMODITY AGREEMENTS Scope, General Purpose, and Objectives 

Scope and general purpose Chapter VII, which is divided into 4 sections and 15 articles (46-60). relates to the principles and procedures which must be observed in making and operating intergovernmental commodity agreements. The chapter would confine such agreements principally to primary products defined in Article 60 as any agricultural product or any mineral which enters into international trade in substantial volume in a form customarily called primary. This is based upon the recognition (Art. 46) that some primary products present special difficulties, which differ in character from those generally presented by manufactured goods. These difficulties, the article states, arise out of such conditions as the disequilibrium between production and consumption, the accumulation of burdensome stocks, and pronounced fluctuations in prices. 

Because of special difficulties of this kind, the Charter would permit, in the case of primary products, the adoption of intergovernmental commodity. arrangements, including so-called regulatory agreements also under which control of production, exports, imports, and prices would be permissible. It would permit such agreements with respect to nonprimary products, if the Organization finds that exceptional circumstances justify such action. 

Export and import quotas, imposed under the authority of intergovernmental commodity agreements are excepted from the general provisions of Article 25 (par. 2, d) of the Charter which requires the elimination of quantitative trade restrictions,

VII The provisions of Chapter VII are not to apply to intergovernmental commodity arrangements which relate solely to the equitable distribution of commodities in short supply; to arrangements designed to protect public morals, or human, animal, or plant life or health; to arrangements relating to fissionable materials or to traffic in arms, munitions, and other materials for military establishments; or, in time of war or other emergency in international relations, to arrangements designed to protect the essential security interests of a Member. Objectives

Article 47 formulates six general objectives of intergovernmental commodity arrangements. The first is to enable countries to solve the special difficulties connected with primary commodities (referred to in Article 46) without resorting to action inconsistent with the general purposes of the Charter (such, for example, as the unilateral imposition of export or import quotas). Other stated objectives are to facilitate necessary production adjustments which cannot be effected swiftly enough by the free play of market forces; to provide a temporary framework for working out long-run adjustments designed to promote the expansion of consumption, or the shifting of resources and manpower from over-expanded industries into new and more productive fields; to moderate pronounced fluctuations in the price of a primary commodity "above and below the level which expresses the long-term '1 However, when quarantine provisions involve the regulation of production, trade, or prices, Members agree to participate only if such regulation is authorized either by a multilateral convention subscribed to by a majority of the countries affected, or if it is operated under the auspices of the organization itself.

Conditions under which commodity agreements are permitted Chapter VII (Article 60, par. 4) defines a regulatory agreement as one which involves the "regulation of production, exports, imports, or prices." 

Apparently, agreements not coming within the definition of regulatory and not excluded entirely from the purview of Chapter VII may be made when necessary to accomplish any of the objectives described above and may relate to nonprimary as well as primary products, although the last three of the six stated objectives relate exclusively to primary products. Article 52 provides special rules for resort to regulatory agreements. 

Such agreements may be made when there has developed, or when there is expected to develop (1) a burdensome surplus of a primary commodity which a substantial reduction in price would not readily, and with sufficient promptness, remedy by causing a significant increase in consumption or decrease in production; or (2) widespread unemployment which, in the absence of specific government action, would not be corrected by normal market forces in time to prevent videspread and undue hardship to workers. It is provided, moreover, that when the International Trade Organization finds "exceptional circumstances justify such action" (Article 52, paragraph 3), commodity agreements may be extended to other than primary products. Charter Provisions 

Article 51 sets forth the general principles governing participation in all intergovernmental commodity arrangements irrespective of character. 

Initially, the arrangements shall be open to participation by any Members of the International Trade Organization on terms equally favorable with those accorded to any other participating country; and thereafter, on terms approved by the International Trade Organization. Article 63 provides that the Conference shall consist of the representatives of the Members of the International Trade Organization and that each Member shall have one representative with the right to appoint alternates and advisers. There was considerable discussion as to what provision, if any, should be made in the Charter for giving individual Members a weight in the Organization somewhat in accordance with their importance in international trade.
