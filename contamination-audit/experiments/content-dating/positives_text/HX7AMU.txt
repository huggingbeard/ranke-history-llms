THE MEANING OF RECONSTRUCTIONISM

THE MEANING OF RECONSTRUCTIONISM I. REMOVING SOME MISCONCEPTIONS 

To expect that an idea or a movement which calls itself by the name of "Reconstructionism" should be grasped in its entirety by everybody is to doubt the truth of the well known story about the elephant and the blind man. Although 

Reconstructionism is only a few years old, it already shares the fate of that famous elephant, in being frequently mistaken for something which it remotely resembles. 

The fact, for example, that Reconstructionism arose in answer to the call voiced in the book Judaism as a Civilization at once started the rumor that it was a secularist movement. The main point in the concept of Judaism as a 

Civilization is that we fail to grasp the meaning of Judaism, in all its implications, unless we see it as the dynamic life-pattern of a people with a long history and with the aspiration for a creative future. That concept provides the basic principle of Reconstructionism. Indeed, without a concept of that kind, it is impossible to approach any problem in Jewish life intelligently or constructively. If Judaism, or that which differentiates the Jewish people from the rest of the world, is to us merely a fixed, or even a changing, system of distinctive religious beliefs and practices, or the product of unique hereditary racial traits, or as Kaufmann Kohler suggests, a combination of both, we are bound to misunderstand every fact in the Jewish past and present, to 

Page Five

misconceive every Jewish issue, and to misinterpret every 

Jewish hope.

Many of the modern reinterpreters of Judaism have come under the sway of the German, denatured approach to all human problems. They fail, therefore, to see that Judaism is a complex living reality and not an abstract idea. Merely capitalizing the "I" in idea does not make Judaism more alive. Living Judaism comprises three elements,—nationhood, religion and culture-which are so organically interrelated that, if one is eliminated, the other two are bound to decay. As a civilization, Judaism consists of such categories as a land, either attained or promised, a language more or less living, a literature more or less growing, a religion more or less evolving, a code of laws more or less operative, and a social organization more or less centralized. The notion of Judaism as a Civilization was definitely intended to offset and negate the Reformist assumption which has prevailed till recently, and with which Reformists of the old school still operate, that Judaism was a religion only, or a religion plus race. But, according to the perverted logic of some people, the negative of "not a religion only" is "not even a religion." Ergo, to say that Judaism is a civilization is to reduce it to a purely secular affair. This is how Reconstructionism came to be misrepresented as a secular movement.

If Others again misrepresent Reconstructionism as a belated Reform movement. To be sure, the very term "Reconstructionism" implies change, as does the term "Reform." its purpose is to find a way for the Jewish people to survive in a changing world, how could it possibly fail to advocate change? Life is not the identity of a stone, but the continuity of a changing organism. Reconstructionism has at all times duly credited Reform with having been the first movement in Judaism to recognize the principle of change 

Page Six

as integral to the destiny of the Jewish people, and as capable of being impressed, under the aegis of progress, into the service of the higher life. But it is one thing to accept the principle of change, and quite another to agree on the nature of the changes to be effected. Reform until recently advo- || cated so radical a change in the very pattern of Jewish life, that all that remained of it looked more like a vague Theism than like Judaism. The Pittsburgh Platform of 1885, which was recently revoked by the Central Conference of American Rabbis, and which had served as the basis of Reform ideology, tried to transform the Jewish people into a religious denomination. It vehemently sought to expunge from the 

Jewish conciousness all yearning for the opportunity to function once more as a living creative people, the yearning implied in the prayer that God return to Zion. That kind of violent change has nothing in common with the reconstruction which takes place in all living tissue when it undergoes the process of natural growth. It is more like the mythical metempsychosis in Indian lore, which never worked in reality.

In 1937, the Central Conference of American Rabbis took a very significant step when it substituted a new set of guiding principles for its Pittsburgh Platform. One cannot help sensing in those principles an attempt to reckon conscientiously with the Judaism of history as well as with the realities of the present. It would be fanciful to represent this action of the Central Conference as due to a change of heart on the part of its veteran leaders. Something quite different has happened. The consistency of the Reform synagogue had itself undergone a marked change. The Pittsburgh Platform did much to eliminate from Jewish life the children of those to whom that Platform constituted the definition of Judaism. The empty places in the Temples have been filled largely by those who were brought up in 

Page Seven

Orthodox or Conservative homes. Among the spiritual leaders, likewise, one begins to note an increase of former students of the yeshibot. Circumstances such as these have led to the formulation of the "Guiding Principles" on the basis of which one might characterize the most recent developments of 

Reform as faintly echoing Reconstructionism. But that is a far cry from misrepresenting Reconstructionism as belated Reform.

The secularists, on the other hand, noting, in particular, the Reconstructionist emphasis on religion as the soul of the entire complex of elements which constitute the Jewish civilization, interpret Reconstructionism as nothing but another attempt to have the synagogue monopolize Jewish life. They are disappointed in the movement which they had hoped would bring them allies. They thought that within the synagogue itself there had arisen at last a movement that would shift the center of gravity from religion to social activities, art, Palestine, and social reform. Does not Reconstructionism, as a matter of fact, advocate the conversion of the synagogue into a Bet Am, or communal center? Does it not contend that the syngagogue as now constituted is little more than a social club to which only those who have a certain competence or degree of security can belong? Does it not insist that the frame of organization ought to be the community, in which non-religious groups shall also be represented? Does it not stress the importance of correlating the struggle for Jewish survival with the struggle for democracy and social justice? What the secularists cannot understand is that the entire character of Jewish civilization, as well as the particular character of American democracy, is bound to make any cultural minority, which is not religiously motivated, into a superfluous nuisance. A Jewish cultural life that would lack the central purpose of achieving salvation of the individual Jew, a purpose analogous to that which 

Page Eight

religious bodies claim they achieve for their adherents, is something that a Jewish child, who is brought up in the American tradition, could never fully grasp. That fact alone is enough to doom the attempt to foster secular Jewish life in America. The centrality of religion in Jewish civilization is not due merely to its being indispensable to Jewish survival. Religion is an indispensable directive and corrective element in any civilization. Certainly Jewish civilization outside its homeland can least afford to dispense with it. 

We have thus permitted the various misinterpretations of Reconstructionism to cancel one another. It is now a simple matter to understand its real meaning. That meaning is best conveyed through the purpose to which it is dedicated, namely: The advancement of Judaism as an evolving religious civilization. To attain that purpoes, Reconstructionism stresses the need of our engaging conscientiously and unremittingly in the following six categories of action: 

1. Active participation in the upbuilding of Palestine. 

2. The reinterpretation of our tradition in the light of growing knowledge. 

3. The reorganization of all our communal endeavors with a view to their functioning as a means to Jewish survival. 

4. The democratization of the synagogue and the vitalization of its religious, ethical and educational functions. 

5. The fostering of Hebraic cultural creativity. 

6. Active participation in social reform.

In the various writings published by the group identified with the Reconstructionist, ample explanation is given of each of the six points in the foregoing program. In what is to follow, my purpose is to point out the prerequisite which is essential to the adoption and implementation of those categories of action. That prerequisite, I believe, is to approach 

Page Nine

the problem of Judaism in the spirit of one of our great seers, whom I venture to designate as the first great reconstructionist, the priest-prophet Ezekiel. If we Jews were in the habit of adopting patron saints, I would have suggested Ezekiel as the patron saint for the Reconstructionist movement. 

There are many striking points of resemblance between the situation he confronted and the one we confront. He had to cope with unwarranted complacency, with inward despair, with cringing imitation of foreign idolatries, with outworn orthodox doctrines that needed change and reinterpretation, with religious practices that were in an anarchic condition, with the waning hopes of a restored Palestine that had to be kept alive, and with the need of drafting plans for a reorganized people.

The prophet Ezekiel can teach us not merely to be cognizant of similar problems in the Judaism of today, but to avail ourselves of that distinctive contribution of prophetic religion which can best be described as moral realism. No less important than the unique conception of God, which the Prophets taught mankind, is the particular temper or disposition of which they were the living incarnation, the moral realism which views with open-eyed sincerity all that is, and which greets with warm-hearted fervor all that ought to be. It represents a combination of courage, faith and wisdom: the courage to face the facts however disconcerting or upsetting, the faith that those facts do not represent the irrevocable ultimatum of inexorable fate, and the wisdom to discern where true salvation lies.

This is the moral realism we must cultivate. It will enable us to grasp the full magnitude of the crisis in present-day Judaism. It will prevent us from falling into hopeless despair. 

Most important of all, it will indicate the specific course of action we have to take in order to give Judaism a new lease on life.

II. MORAL REALISM and Courage 

If moral realism is to function in our generation, it must help us face with unflinching courage the tragic realities of present-day Judaism.

Any Jew whose heart is set upon the survival of American Jewry needs all the moral courage of an Ezekiel to admit to himself, and to make known to others, that Judaism in America is rapidly disintegrating, and that unless some heroic measures be taken forthwith to stop that disintegration, it is doomed. It is natural to conceal from ourselves any unpleasant knowledge. If we cannot help being aware of the unpleasant truth, we do not want to figure as bringers of evil tidings. But this is no excuse, when we know that withholding the truth might lead to fatal negligence. Ezekiel denounces with bitter invective the misleaders of his day who kept on saying, "All's well," when all was far from well. He describes them as daubing flimsy walls with white-wash instead of building strong walls to keep out the danger. Anyone who is inclined to resent the disclosure of the critical state of affairs in Jewish life should remember that he is apt to put himself in the same class with those of whom the prophet said in the name of God: "They shall have no place in the council of my people, nor be enrolled in the register of the Household of Israel." For the good of Israel, it is better to be right and distressed with Ezekiel, than wrong and complacent with the false prophets.

The outside observer of the surface of Jewish life is undoubtedly impressed by the multiplicity and the scope of Jewish public activities. We have numerous federations, welfare funds, community chests, the Joint Distribution Committee and United Palestine Appeal, philanthropic institutions and fund raising agencies for all kinds of purposes, local, national and world-wide in scope. No one can gainsay Page Eleven

the vast energy that goes into these endeavors, the humanitarian spirit that pulsates in them, and the actual saving of lives in which they result. It would be the height of ingratitude to belittle in any way the inherent moral value of all these efforts. But all that does not preclude the need or even the right of asking the question: Are these activities part of the advance or of the retreat of Jewish life? An army that is beating a hasty retreat presents a picture of the most intensive activity, the transportation of the wounded, the seeking of safety from pursuit, the saving of as much of the provisions as possible; but it is all part of the defeat the army has suffered. If we are interested in the future of the Jewish people we cannot avoid asking: To what extent do all these undertakings contribute to the desire on the part of our young to carry on as Jews, to be loyal to our people's ideals and to seek to realize its hopes?

Some may say that such a question is irrelevant, if not unfair. unfair. These philanthropic and social activities justify themselves by the concrete good they accomplish. We must remember, however, that, in calling for large sacrifices in energy, time and resources, they constitute an additional burden which the Jew has to carry alone, because he happens to belong to the Jewish people. Every people has a right to exact sacrifices of those who belong to it. But it may do so only provided it has a future. A share in that future is the necessary and legitimate reward to which the individual must be able to look forward. Otherwise the sacrifice he is called upon to make becomes an unrelieved, meaningless burden, which the young will not be persuaded to assume. 

Here and there, out of an extraordinary sense of filial piety, a young man will assume such a burden; but as a rule the sense of responsibility for the stricken of one's people dies out as soon as the hope for the people's future is abandoned or the desire for it is lost. It will take at least three or four 

Page Twelve

generations of American Jewry to rebuild the ruins of shattered European Jewry and to render Palestine secure as a Jewish home. But if there is lack of faith in the Jewish future, or if the very yearning for such a future is dead, the effort to salvage the ruined half of our people is all too liable to be halted in its initial stages.

The ominous truth about present-day Jewish life, a truth which is seldom mentioned in good Jewish company, is that the desire to escape Jewish life is deepening and spreading. 

That desire is taking possession of the entire conscious and sub-conscious life of the individual Jew, and it is reaching out into all strata of Jewish society. We dare not be finicky in disclosing the flimsiness of the whitewashed wall, because if we insist on hiding the truth from ourselves, nothing will be done about it. If we permit the tendency to escapism to continue unchecked much longer, it will develop into a stampede. Let us not allow ourselves to be squelched by those who demand the suppression of the truth about Jewish life, on the ground that it does more harm than good to know facts which are discouraging. We are confronted with the old issue of what is known as "constructive falsehood" vs. "destructive truth." On that issue no wiser words have been spoken than those of Goethe (quoted by Max Eastman in "Socialism Revalued") to the effect that destructive truth is in the long run constructive because the harm it does is only temporary, but in leading to other truths it becomes constructive, whereas constructive falsehood is, in the long run, destructive because the good it does is only temporary, but in leading to other untruths it becomes progressively more destructive.

One would expect that the two main institutions which are dedicated to the perpetuation of Jewish life, the school and the synagogue, would reckon frankly and fearlessly with the growing tendency to escapism. Instead, they pursue the 

Page Thirteen

old policy of daubing the flimsy walls with whitewash. They avert their faces from the basic cause of the difficulties they are encountering, and even resent its exposure. They enjoy the clatter of organization and machinery and membership drives, because it gives them the illusion that all is well. They insist upon squashing all attempts to open up the problem of escapism. As, in former times, people exorcised spooks and dybbuks with some magic formula, they too have a magic formula of their own. That formula is: "We must avoid all controversial issues; we must have shalom, shalom." 

It is evident that Jewish survival is unthinkable without Jewish education. Judaism is not transmitted through the blood stream, but through the process of education, both formal and informal. When, therefore, we see that process rapidly being reduced in intensity, and making very little progress, if any, in the numbers it reaches, we should realize that we are confronted with a serious danger which calls for a most thoroughgoing diagnosis. Occasionally, in large cities where Jews move into new neighborhoods, a new congregation springs up, and under the leadership of an energetic young rabbi, it manages to build up a successful religious school. But such instances are far too few in number to counteract the decided decline in the desire of parents to give their children a Jewish education. Far more symptomatic of the actuality is the fact that the vigorous attempt made a few years ago by a national educational bureau of Jewish education to induce congregations to add a day or two of schooling to their Sunday school program was a complete failure.

The truth is that, in spite of an occasional local flare-up of educational interest, every year adds to the list of parents who want to give their children less and less Jewish education. Here and there a rabbi tries to resist the growing tide of apathy to the Jewish upbringing of the child, but most 

Page Fourteen

rabbis find themselves compelled to yield to the standards which the parents keep on lowering. More than one large 

Temple has taken over the function of the old itinerant barmitzvah melammed, and for a stipulated price it gives the youngster a concentrated five-or-six weeks' drill in the performance of the bar-mitzvah stunt. This is the beginning and the end of the Jewish education of thousands of Jewish boys who a generation ago would have spent at least four or five years in a Hebrew school.

There has been of late an increase in the number of congregational schools and a decrease in the number of Talmud Torahs. Some rabbis interpret that fact as cause for congratulation, in that it indicates a swing over from secularism to religion. What it actually means is that, instead of children giving to Jewish education at least ten to twelve hours a week, they now give at most two or four. The most constructive educational effort thus far in American Jewish life is the establishment of courses for adults. But so far they have shown no sign of conquering the general aversion of the overwhelming majority of our young people to Jewish subject matter or to Jewish cultural interests. 

The synagogue functioned throughout the centuries as a means of Jewish survival through its worship and its instruction. It fostered the unity of Israel through the use of 

Hebrew and the uniformity of the prayers, and it fortified the Jewish spirit by interpreting the current needs and problems in the light of Jewish tradition. In pre-modern days the synagogue was able to carry out these functions successfully, because it was aided by the prevailing universal attitude. That attitude toward worship and prayers may be described as theurgic, that is, it was based upon the belief that the very act of worship exercised a kind of magic potency. The attitude towards the authority of the past was one of unquestioning and implicit faith. These attitudes 

Page Fifteen

are still retained in the Orthodox synagogues. This is why the Orthodox synagogue still contributes to Jewish survival. 

But the atmosphere in which Judaism survives there is so intellectually asphyxiating and so spiritually confusing that it becomes an added reason why so many Jews want to escape 

Judaism. 

On the other hand, the non-Orthodox synagogue, instead of wrestling with the problem of what should replace the theurgic attitude and the unquestioning acceptance of the authority of the past, is preoccupied with all kinds of schemes to render the service attractive. It has practically forgotten its principal function. The gradual elimination of Hebrew from the prayers is an index of the progressive dejudaization of the synagogue. Some years ago a questionnaire was sent around among the Reform Congregations to find out whether they wanted more or less Hebrew in prayers, and the overwhelming majority replied that it was in favor of less. 

The Conservative synagogues, too, are gradually finding Hebrew an obstacle in the conduct of the services. The growing ignorance of the Bible and Rabbinics makes preaching less and less Jewish. People come to synagogue not to worship but to hear the sermon or lecture. After a while they discover that Town Hall or University extension lectures offer a greater variety of subjects and speakers, or that they can spend the time to better advantage by staying at home and reading a book. A few outstanding orators whose great gifts afford esthetic pleasure will always draw large, and usually transient, audiences. In the smaller towns where the range of entertainment is limited, religious services are occasionally well attended, especially if there is an out-of-town speaker. 

But to maintain that the worship and the instruction in the synagogue are functioning effectively at present as a means of Jewish survival is to be extremely unrealistic. 

The truth is that the synagogue itself is beginning to be 

Page Sixteen

apprehensive of its future. The danger to the synagogue is no longer merely from the secularism which is opposed to religion in general. It is rather from those Jewish organizations whose basic appeal is the promise of security from anti- 

Semitism. They convince you that you fulfill your function as a good American Jew by fighting anti-Semitism. Incidentally, the less you do that offers the anti-Semites a conspicuous mark of attack the better. You are left to draw your own conclusions as to the virtues of camouflage. These virtues the synagogue is not very helpful in promoting, if it is really a synagogue. No wonder the synagogue today has become jittery and is worried by the numerous agencies which compete with it for the Jews' affiliation. 

But we have not plumbed the bottom of the inner Jewish problem, if we stop with the consideration of the losses through ignorance and religious indifference. To determine the chances of Jewish survival we must take account of what goes on in the innermost recesses of the Jewish heart. There we find all too often a sense of inferiority, self-contempt and self-hate, and a hopeless yearning after a life where one can forget one is a Jew and where one can be forgotten by Jews. We can well understand that finding oneself hampered at every step because one is a Jew, when making friends or seeking a career, when traveling, working or playing, is bound to destroy one's moral stamina. 

But the environment of medieval Europe was no less hostile or vicious toward the Jew, yet it did not evoke from him such bitter feelings of self-pity and self-contempt. In the long struggle of the Jewish people for existence, the Jewish consciousness of the individual Jew was the decisive factor. That consciousness was not ruffled by the slightest doubt or questioning as to the high privilege of being a Jew, however great a price in suffering and persecution it incurred. The sharp contrast between the attitude of the pre-modern Jew 

Page Seventeen

toward his people and its heritage and that of the modern Jew makes the problem of Jewish survival an extremely difficult one. We have no large empires and no great navies to depend on. We cannot afford the luxury of "muddling through." We must think through the present crisis, and the prime requisite to thinking through it is to adopt the moral realism of the great Prophets and not to flinch at the gruesome task of becoming fully aware of what's wrong with our people. 

III. MORAL REALISM AND FAITH 

The second function of moral realism is to instil in us a faith in the power of Israel to reconstruct its life and assure a future for itself—a faith which is not blind, but one that is based upon an intellectually acceptable interpretation of our history.

To know what is wrong with us is not moral realism, if it leads to defeatism. The Prophets' moral realism did not lead to defeatism. While they exposed the corruption which was undermining Jewish existence, they confidently foresaw the ultimate repentance and regeneration of the Jewish people. 

In the familiar vision of the valley, God tells Ezekiel, “These bones are the Household of Israel. Behold they keep on saying, 'Our bones are dried up, and our hope is lost; we are clean cut off." God tells him to prophesy and to say to the Household of Israel, "Behold I am opening your graves and will raise you out of your graves, O my people, and I will bring you into the land of Israel." It is all too easy to conclude from the decadence of the inner life of our people in the diaspora that Israel is spiritually as dead as the dry bones which Ezekiel beheld. Outstanding Jewish thinkers in our day, like Jacob Klatzkin and Ezekiel Kaufman, have long pronounced the doom of diaspora Judaism. By means of what seems irresistible logic, which is borne out by the 

Page Eighteen

centrifugal tendencies in Jewish life, they dismiss as sheer folly the belief that it is possible for the Jewish people to survive anywhere except in Jewish territory, whether it be in Palestine or in any other country. 

No doubt the acquisition of a territory or territories where Jewish life can be lived unhampered by the need of accommodating itself to other civilizations has become more necessary to Jewish survival than it was before the turning point in human history marked by the French Revolution. Jewish life has since demonstrated its inherent capacity for survival by actually responding to the urgent need of territory. Jews have acquired a foothold in Palestine and are growing more insistent that their historic and moral claim be honored by the nations of the world in deed as it has been in word. But that claim itself and the energy to follow it up presuppose a type of national vitality that is not subject to the ordinary laws of nationhood, and that demands other outlets for selfexpression besides autonomous statehood. 

If the Jewish people were entirely subject to the social laws which have operated in the case of other peoples, it would have disappeared long ago. Other peoples have always assumed that territorial deracination justified national suicide. The Jews proved an exception to that rule. They have exhibited an unparalleled capacity to live in dispersion, united by a common historic consciousness and by common aims and ideals. They have thus shown the ability to circumvent the sociological law of national suicide. The negators of diaspora Judaism base their reasoning upon the mistaken assumption that the Jewish nationality is in all respects subject to the working of well-known sociological forces. would indeed be foolhardy to deny that, to a large extent, the Jewish people is like all other peoples, and that it could benefit mightily from reckoning with the interplay of economic and social forces with moral and spiritual forces. But It

Page Nineteen

it would, on the other hand, be unrealistic to deny that the Jews have displayed an original and supernormal power in reconstructing their social organization to fit the requirements imposed by new surroundings and in reinterpreting their ideals to fit the needs accentuated by new contacts and new problems.

This power of our people is apparent when we look at it at close range, but it stands out even more conspicuously when our people is viewed in the large perspective of history. Thrice in its career did our people undergo complete reconstruction in its social set-up and thought-pattern without suffering a break in the continuity of its life. As we read the Biblical account of Israel's beginnings, the transition from the nomadic stage to the agricultural seems easy and natural. Yet a careful study of what was involved in that transition would reveal the unique power of reconstruction that marked our people from its very beginning. That nomadic tribes should take possession of an inhabited territory and become a nation was nothing unusual in ancient times. But for a nation not only to maintain a sense of continuity with its early nomadic stage, but also to derive from that nomadic stage the initial impulse to its highest strivings is contrary to all sociological precedent.

The Jewish people defied sociological law a second time, when its first commonwealth was destroyed, its Temple burned down and its leading families carried off to strange lands. While some Jews obeyed the natural urge to national suicide, both Jeremiah and Ezekiel taught their people in Babylon to maintain the continuity of Jewish life. They reinterpreted some deeply rooted ideas about national sin and responsibility; they indicated new opportunities and new tasks as a spur to national continuity. Ezekiel even drew up new plans for a reconstructed Jewish commonwealth. And when the second commonwealth did finally emerge, its 

Page Twenty

changed social structure and its reorganized inner life around the Torah of Moses did not prevent it from knowing itself as the same people whose ancestors had been slaves in Egypt. 

The Jewish people defied sociological law a third time when the second commonwealth was destroyed and all seemed lost. R. Johannan ben Zakkai and the Sages of 

Yabneh prevented the Jewish people from meeting the usual fate of dissolution through national suicide by evincing once again the native capacity for national reconstruction. From being a nation with a central state, the Jews became a nationality with a common code of law and a common religiouscultural life. When the general world-outlook was transformed into one which placed life's center of gravity in the immortality of the hereafter, the entire religious outlook of the Jewish people took on the same hue. And with all that, there was never a sense of break with the past. Jews in 

Persia, Spain, Italy, Germany and France were linked together by a common consciousness. Every Jew regarded himself as though he personally had gone forth from Egypt with Israel in the Exodus. Thus the thrice-repeated achievement of converting the urge to national suicide into selftransformation and reconstruction so as to be able to go on living proves how moral realism can help a people to negotiate the dangerous whirlpool of sudden and rapid change in habitat and mode of life.

There is, of course, one important distinction between the task of reconstruction in days of old and that same task in our day. Then it was carried out under the impact of a will that was identified as divine. It was not merely because 

Jeremiah, or Ezekiel, or R. Johannan ben Zakkai wanted the 

Jewish people to survive, that it was able to effect the needed reconstruction. Those great leaders and teachers were certain that God, who guides the destinies of nations, was using them as His instruments to rescue Israel from impending 

Page Twenty-One

doom. This confidence gave them the power to effect the miracle of national resurrection. Nowadays, however, it would be presumptuous for any one person to treat as a divine imperative the call to arouse our people from its fatal lethargy, and to invoke the spirit to enter once again its lifeless body. Taken individually, we are too sophisticated and too self-critical to lay claim to experiencing any divine imperative. But a number of Jews laboring under the same compelling urge to find for our people a way out of its present crisis might well interpret that urge as a call from God. 

Indeed, the only kind of reconstruction which will set Israel again on the path of salvation will be that which will be felt as a high consecration and as a profound religious experience. 

It is essential, however, that those Jews who cannot make peace with the decadence of Judaism should not sigh in solitariness. They should find one another and achieve that community of spirit which will beget the confidence and wisdom necessary to effect the needed reconstruction of Jewish life.

IV. MORAL REALISM AND WISDOM 

The third function of moral realism is to endow us with the wisdom to understand the catacylsmic character of world events which have helped to create our problem, and to formulate that conception of individual and group salvation which will reflect the true needs of our generation. 

Why the reconstruction which is a prerequisite to Jewish survival must come with all the zeal of a divine imperative becomes clear only when we fully realize the shattering impact of the modern world upon the Judaism of tradition. 

If the Reconstructionist movement were to do nothing more than to call attention to the magnitude of the psychic and moral upheaval which has taken place in the world during 

Page Twenty-Two

the last century-and-a-half, and to the effect of that upheaval upon the Jewish people, it would justify its existence. 

This it attempts to do by the very term "reconstruction," a term associated in people's minds with the efforts of populations to resume life after some great cataclysmic changes like earthquakes, wars, revolutions, famines and pestilences. It is necessary to impress upon the mind of the Jew that the changes in the world which are responsible for his present crisis have been nothing less than cataclysmic. The realization of this fact in its full import would help the Jew understand what has caused the breakdown of his moral stamina, what has transformed his self-confidence and defiance of a hostile world into self-questioning and self-contempt. Such understanding is essential, if the Jew is to master the forces which threaten to destroy him, and if he is to command them to rebuild his life.

So long as all the world lived by a philosophy which placed life's center of gravity in a supernatural hereafter, the Jew had every reason to believe that being a Jew was an enviable privilege. Affiliation with the Jewish people was a guaranty of life eternal. He had no difficulty in meeting the challenge of either Christendom or Islam, which tried in vain to prove that the Jews had forfeited the means to salvation, in stubbornly clinging to the Torah and in refusing to recognize the subsequent revelation of God's will. He could not help feeling that both the Church and Islam practically justified his loyalty to Judaism by the very fact of their persecuting his people.

But all this became irrelevant when men began to identify salvation with this-worldly self-fulfillment or realization, and to seek it in the opportunities for power, achievement, enjoyment or creativity, which the natural life of this world offered. The familiar slogan about the inalienable right to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness is the political form 

Page Twenty-Three

of the new conception of salvation, and the modern state arose with the avowed purpose of helping the citizen attain it. Such was the development which took place during the century following the French Revolution. During the first stages of that development, the Jews were led to believe that the millenium was at hand. There were new worlds to explore and to exploit, and those in control of the economic situation saw no special need for keeping out anyone who possessed the spirit of enterprise. This was sufficient to raise the hopes of the Jews in central and west European countries, that they would at last be integrated into the body politic. 

When Napoleon, however, made it clear to the Jews that, to become part of the body politic, they would have to surrender their nationhood and become merely a religious sect, the representative Jews of the time who composed the French Sanhedrin showed that they, too, had become thoroughly secularized. They allowed the considerations of this-worldly salvation to sway them, and they submitted to Napeolon's wish. Their example set the standard for the Jewish Reform movement. If that process of integration had been permitted to go on unhampered, it would have led to complete assimilation of western Jewry. But about 1870 a new trend in 

European political life set in. The general hunger for thisworldly salvation was whetted. But the national states, which had led men to believe that they would find in them all that they needed to achieve salvation, were unable to keep their promise. The rich were growing richer, but the poor were growing poorer. The masses were becoming restive. It was necessary to divert their attention from the true cause of their misery. It was then that the new type of Jew-hatred known as anti-Semitism arose. It tried to prevent the Jew from becoming like the non-Jew, thereby becoming an economic rival. 

It applied itself to the task of pushing the Jews back into the Ghetto and reducing them once more to the status of 

Page Twenty-Four

pariahs. Ever since then Jews have been placed more and more in the unfortunate position of having lost all taste for other-worldly salvation, and having become avid of thisworldly salvation, with less chance of securing the latter than have their fellow citizens.

The outcome of the present crisis in Judaism depends, in the last analysis, upon the way the Jew will interpret thisworldly salvation. If this-worldly salvation will mean to him personal success in achieving the means to power and pleasure, he will see in anti-Semitism merely an obstacle to his becoming so completely identified with the non-Jewish majority as to be able to join them on even terms in the scramble for success. He will try to elude the scrutiny of the anti-Semites by changing his name and appearance, and to pass off as a Gentile. He may for a time think he is on the road to his kind of salvation, only to be disillusioned sooner or later. But if he cannot very well camouflage his Jewish descent, he will become a fanatic for Jewish self-immolation. 

He is bound to look upon whatever Jews do to perpetuate their tradition and distinctiveness as additional fuel for the flames of anti-Semitism. He cannot have much love for his fellow-Jews, however strongly he may commiserate with them in their plight; for Jews as such mean to him merely a handicap in his striving for personal success. This accounts for the well-known fact that Jews in the upper brackets of wealth, influence and intellectual achievement are seldom associated with the upbuilding of Palestine, or with fostering Jewish education, Jewish culture and religion. Whenever they do take part in communal Jewish effort which is of a philanthropic character, or which is intended to combat anti- Semitism, they insist upon the policy of dejudaizing everything Jewish. With men of this type at the helm of large 

Jewish affairs, the future of American Judaism is very precarious, and the counter-efforts for Jewish survival will have 

Page Twenty-Five

to be of an extraordinarily strenuous and consecrated character to make any headway.

Reconstructionism would plead with the Jew to give careful thought to all that is involved in the cataclysmic change which has taken place in man's outlook on life. If the traditional notion of this world as an antechamber to the worldto-come is no longer tenable, the Jew should not hastily conclude that the only alternative is to eat, drink and make merry, or to join the reckless race for success and let the devil take the hindmost. There is a permanent truth in the other-worldly conception of salvation which mankind in general, and the Jew in particular, cannot afford to discard. 

That truth is that we must not regard anything as a means to our self-fulfillment, unless it enables us to add our mite toward rendering this-worldly life more livable for the generations to come. This principle must color all our strivings for thisworldly salvation, if such salvation is not to degenerate into a sybarite dream or into a Napoleonic complex, but to signify the attainment of virtue and well-being in community. This principle calls upon the Jew to treat the individual predicament, in which he finds himself because of anti-Semitism, not merely as his personal misfortune, but as part of the collective struggle of the Jewish people for survival. 

Among the nations which have come to regard man's salvation as being natural and of this world, the Jewish people stands a chance of survival only on one proviso: that those nations recognize the primacy of justice and reason in the relation of the state to the individual and of states to one another. Thus has Jewish survival become more dependent than ever upon the triumph of right over might. That triumph has become an essential element in the Jew's conception of this-worldly salvation, and its promulgation and advancement his inevitable mission. If we want to know 

Page Twenty-Six

what basically has gone wrong with the Jew, we must resort to a realistic understanding of the revolution which has taken place in the entire world-outlook of mankind. But if we want to know what is to set the Jew right again, we must resort to the moral realism of the Prophets. They taught us to put faith in God and in the ultimate triumph of His will. 

The Jew can do nothing better than to adopt that faith as the basis of what must for him constitute this-worldly salvation. This means that there is no other moral choice for the modern Jew than to strive with all his might for the establishment of a just social order, which would render democratic nationalism safe for the creative survival of the 

Jewish people.

In order, however, that the Jew shall adopt this moral choice, he must be fully convinced of the intrinsic value of Jewish survival. He must see in Jewish life high creative possibilities. He must find Judaism capable of eliciting the best that is in him. To meet this vital requirement, Judaism must come to possess not only social structure, but the kind of social structure which will be organically related to the high purposes of a religious civilization. Moreover, it must have its entire ideology transposed into the key of thought which is relevant to the problems that agitate us today. All this effort at reconstruction and reinterpretation must come entirely from the urge of an inward creative life. The sloughing off of the old must be only incidental to the triumphant creation of the new.

Reconstructionism does admittedly introduce a new factor into Jewish life, the factor of deliberate social and spiritual planning. It does that because all of human living has reached a point of complexity where unplanned action is bound to lead to disaster. There was a time, we are told, when American architecture consisted mainly in a new kind of facade; it left the design of the structure unchanged, Page Twenty-Seven

thus producing a kind of hybrid style. Then came the famous architect, Frank Lloyd Wright, and placed the emphasis on structural truth and organic engineering and design. We should cease tinkering with the facade of Jewish life, and concentrate on evolving those forms of teaching and practice, of organization and institutionalism which would express structural truth, forms that would answer to the vital needs of the Jew in his struggle for existence and his striving for salvation.

It goes without saying that all attempts to halt the proggressive decay of Jewish life, which must lead to its extinction, are without the least avail so long as our communal, social and religious undertakings are controlled by men who are at heart eager for the Jews to be absorbed by the general population. All public Jewish endavors must be entrusted to Jews who have faith in the value of a Jewish future and are eager to place it beyond doubt or danger. But the ultimate hope of American Jewry rests with those whose being is rooted in Jewish life, and who cannot contemplate without dread the passing of Judaism. If they want to avert so tragic a denouement, they must strive for something far more organic and vital than improved machinery of organization. 

They must take themselves in hand and together constitute a community of mind and heart, animated by the one purpose of creative Jewish survival. They need not, nor should they, form another "party" in Jewish life. On the contrary, they should carry on their work of reconstruction through the very organizations and institutions already existing. But they must be a ferment and a challenge, the restless and creative element within the complacent body of Israel. They must bring forth-or help to bring forth-books, pamphlets, brochures, texts, reiterating the ideal of Reconstructionism and helping to achieve it. They should organize occasions for the public airing of our problems: debates, symposia, Page Twenty-Eight

forums. They should endow the creative aritst, the poet, the dramatist, the musician, the novelist, the author of prayer and psalmody, so that the creative spirit of our people will stir again. Theirs is the obligation to become the faithful remnant, the body of Israel's survivors. Upon them rests the responsibility of preventing American Jewry from deteriorating into a meaningless human detritus, superfluous to itself and to the rest of the world. They have the opportunity to make of Judaism a beneficent influence in American civilization, by becoming a potent factor for the advancement of genuine democracy. They alone can help to bring about the much needed psychic and moral reconstruction in our day, which the prophet Ezekiel urged upon his contemporaries, when he pleaded: "Make you a new heart and a new spirit; for why will ye die, O House of Israel?"
