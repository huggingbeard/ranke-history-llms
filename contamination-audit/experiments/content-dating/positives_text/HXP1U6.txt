The wagon trail that stretched away north and south until its sides seemed to meet in the distance, toward the Columbia River, brought the man with a long beard to a log cabin. It had a well-worn look, old and shabby but comfortable to live in. The thought of coming there to live pleased him. Surely there would be peace; long evenings in which to read, quiet nights in which to sleep and forget the only woman he had loved. It was the impression of home, that really gave him relief. He did not care particularly for the looks, as he had been wandering about a long time. Not a long time in years, for he was less than thirty, but what to him seemed a very long time; ever since he was twenty.

The log cabin no one had seemed to think, nor care about. The old man who had lived there for years, an old bachelor who ran a ferry boat, was dead, and his sister had sold it to the man with a long beard who was so glad to have possession of it. 

There was no neighbor to bother him; however there was a house across and a little way down the river, with a sign that read: "General Merchandise.

Buys and sells everything; Meals served any time at twenty-five cents." That sign brought him wandering up and down the river looking for the way to get there. He saw a man who looked like a mill worker, hurrying away; evidently the midday meal was over; and just inside the house, he could hear a voice, a woman's voice, singing a popular song, "I'm a dreamer." That song made his heart beat faster,

and he was more determined to cross the river. It was getting dark, but he hated to give up. 

He consulted his watch; it was after eight o'clock. Back to his log cabin by moonlight, he went with a disconsolate droop, his eyes on the house across, watching the young man of his age in the far distance waiting in the boat for the girl. When she came out he appeared miraculously happy, at which Arnold stared with envy. 

The young man took her in the boat and sat beside her. Across the river was Arnold's log cabin. The two had been wondering who the newcomer was. In some way it aroused their curiosity. 

It was a matter of respect perhaps, if not curiosity, that lead the two lovers to stop and visit him. 

They pretended to ask for a drink and the man with the long beard invited them to come into the house and got a drink for them. The girl first noticed the tidiness inside the house; it was not the same house as that in which the old man used to live. Then it was never clean, but now it was as clean as if there were a woman keeping house for him; everything was nicely arranged. Marjorie's. After she had satisfied her curiosity she thanked Arnold for his hospitality and stepped out into a world of soft lights and wavering shadows, fragrant of apple blossoms. A year ago her half promise to George had gratified her romance; she was loved, and she had thrilled to it.

She had learned the law of compensation, that for every joy one pays in suffering; women who married went down into the valley of death for children. One must love and be loved very tenderly to pay for that. The scale must balance, and there were other things; women grew old, and age was not always lovely. This very maternity was fatal to beauty.
