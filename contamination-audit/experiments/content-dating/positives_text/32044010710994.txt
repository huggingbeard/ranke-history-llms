Introduction WHO, AFTER ALL, IS MY NEIGHBOR? 

Currently race relations issues are a widespread subject for head-wagging and discussion. Afrikaaners dominate the Bantu;

Portuguese fight bloodily to preserve the status quo in their colonies; Southerners are faced with a dilemna which they have yet to conquer. But we New Englanders are beginning to see overtones of some of these issues here, where we live. We can understand them, if we will, for many of our forebears suffered some of the same problems upon coming here which Negroes still face. And since we all share this immigrant heritage we can see this problem as one of understanding, and of giving the new man a chance. 

This is a Christian issue, for Christians do not commonly push some of their fellow men under the rug and forget them. Unlike the 

Pharisee we are not supposed to go by on the other side. If this be true, if knowing our neighbor, and giving him a fair chance to realize himself is the Christian thing to do, then we are the logical body to deal with the problem. 

To deal with a problem you have to know something about it, so we have tried to do precisely this, in the hope that, with the information we have been able to get, we shall be in a better position to help our neighbor help himself. 

During the week of November 26th, 1961 fifty volunteers interviewed one hundred and eight Negro family heads in Holyoke. Since the 1960 Census of Housing for Holyoke lists only 148 nonwhite households, a number of which are not Negro, it appears that our interviewers covered the population pretty thoroughly. 

This report will present factual material on the characteristics of the population and on housing, and data on attitudes and opinions. 

First let us take a quick look at some of the salient characteristics of the population. This group as a whole is young, predominately from outside Massachusetts, highly mobile, given to marriage and children, church oriented, not well educated or paid, employed at relatively menial tasks, overcrowded in their housing, which is twice as likely to be below average condition as not, and remarkably clean

In many of these respects they resemble members of immigrant groups which Holyoke has had in the past, and from which some of our best citizens have come.

Now to look at some of these characteristics in more detail; as to age these people are in their prime, which ends early in a working class group such as this. Seventeen percent of the household heads are under 25 years of age; one-third are under 30, and almost two-thirds are under 40 years old. (Table 1)

Predominately, as one would expect from their age pattern, they live in families and their families usually include children. 

Fifteen percent are currently not married; fifteen percent are married and have no children; most of the group have three or fewer children, though 23 percent uphold the rural tradition of large families, which they share with many of our white citizens. (Table 2) 

Their education is rather poor in quantity. This lack of education cannot be blamed primarily on Holyoke, however, since less than 10 percent of our Negroes were brought up here, the bulk of our sample coming from outside of Massachusetts, largely from the south. (Table 3) 

Table 3, while statistically dangerous because of small cells, does point out some significant things about Holyoke's Negro population and its related educational problems. Two-thirds of our adult males come from the south, and three-fourths of these have less than three years of high school, nearly half of them having left school before the 7th grade. Our northern minority, however, have all completed 9th grade and more than half of them at least finished high school.

Perhaps a valid question to ask here is how is the younger generation doing in getting an education? Of the 53 children in our study who are between 13 and 21 and living at home, 2 are in college, one is in nursing school, 16 more are attending or graduating from secondary school, and only 2 boys and 5 girls are not in school. This suggests that our Negro citizens, regardless of

their own educational shortcomings, are making some attempt to see that their children get a decent educational start in life. (Tables 

4 and 5)

As we can see from Tables and these people are not only relative strangers but are also relative newcomers. More than onefifth have been two years or less in Holyoke. More than half have been here ten years or less, and only 12 percent have more than 20 years residence here. This brings out a question in which the town might be interested.

Since the number of nonwhite households has nearly doubled in each of the last two Federal Censuses (1940-39 nonwhite dwelling units, 1950-77 nonwhite dwelling units, 1960-148 nonwhite dwelling units) this would suggest that Holyoke was operating as a staging area, losing its older Negro residents, and more than replacing them with others from less advantaged areas. 

It also suggests that Holyoke is and has been educating Negroes which it then loses and replaces with less educated ones. 

Once in Holyoke these people continue to be highly mobile. 

Nearly one-third have lived less than one year at their present addresses. Seventy percent have lived five years or less at the same place. Only two families were found who still lived where they had 20 years ago. (Table 7) This high mobility is probably explained by three factors: namely, low income, uncertain and poor jobs, and poor housing.

The first of these factors which we shall deal with is housing. 

About one-tenth of the group own their own homes, and only one of these was classified as dilapidated by our interviewers. (Table 8) 

The owner frankly stated that he was sick of the whole thing and hoped to be "redeveloped" out of his house. The remaining ninety percent who rent their housing were no happier. The group as a whole showed an average density of occupancy of more than one person per room (including the kitchen as a room) which is considered by some housing people to be overcrowded. (Table 9) This average was helped by one man with seven rooms to himself and hurt by such cases as that of a man, wife, and eleven children, getting on in a clean but somewhat cramped four room tenement.

4Aside from space, housing conditions left much to be desired. 

When asked what they thought about their housing, seventy-five of our 107 families had something uncomplimentary to say. Heating problems, lack of repairs, poor sanitation, inadequate plumbing, and overcrowding led a long and gloomy list. (Table 10) This view was borne out by what our interviewers saw during their visits. 

Sixty-six of the dwellings were classed as dilapidated or run down; only 12 were listed by the investigators as being in good condition. 

At the same time, however, 63 homes were judged to be spotless, or generally clean, and only 13 were classified as generally unclean or downright dirty. (Table 11 and 12) This is cleanliness under 

Herculean difficulties and is a better index of character than many sociologists will admit.

The rent for the 83 households who provided figures average $40.73 per month exclusive of heat, with a range in costs from $22-$68 per month. The average rent for heated apartments is $55 per month, but only 10 tenants had heat included in their housing, and many of these were in public housing. The average tenant heated with oil stoves, electric or gas ovens methods which combine the maximum of inconvenience with the maximum of danger. The cost of such heat was reported as varying between 12 and 60 dollars per month. 

Rental costs of equivalent housing in the white real estate market are not known to the committee. There is no evidence available to indicate the amount of rent gouging which Negroes suffer. 

We do know of some specific cases, however, such as the woman who rents a four room apartment with no heat, no running water, and in generally poor condition, for which she pays $52 per month. 

Despite the relatively high cost of housing more than threequarters of the respondents would be willing to pay more for housing if they could find any, and many have looked. (Table 13) For example, one respondent kept a record of her househunting and found that at the time of the interview she had tried 75 places. Ten of these tries resulted in her being told that they couldn't accept colored people, but most of the rest simply said that there was no vacancy. 

Few whites have to face such a speciously active real estate market in Holyoke.

Moreover, an interview check of the Holyoke Council's Rental Survey now in process shows that of 44 telephone calls to people with housing to rent only five would rent to Negroes. Two of the yesses were qualified (e.g. "I will accept applications from anyone"), and do not necessarily mean that the landlord would actually rent to Negroes. Twenty-three people answered the question with a flat "no", and 16 used various dodges which added up to the same thing. 

It seems odd that in a slowly growing city like Holyoke, with its flight of population to the suburbs and the west of town, that better housing should be so hard to find. 

The group is predictably deficient in racial pioneers, and in supporters of the Negro ghetto. Only one informant would prefer to live in a purely white neighborhood, and less than one-tenth were interested in a Negro neighborhood. Forty-seven percent preferred the mixed neighborhood as a place to settle, and nearly an equal number were undoctrinaire realists who were not interested in the racial composition of their neighborhood so long as it was a decent neighborhood with decent housing. (Table 14) However, when the question was asked differently, and the informants were invited to mention specific places where they would like to move if they could do so without prejudice, a somewhat different pattern appeared. Table 15 shows a great diversity of neighborhoods, many of them showing no relationship to the areas of current Negro distribution. Many of those who expressed no particular preference obviously hadn't thought about it before, or couldn't conceive of a situation where they would have free choice. For many the immediate problem is better housing, and they tend not to think in neighborhood terms. When asked about their current neighborhoods 

48 made no complaint, 18 did not complain but clearly were being discreet, as in the case of the respondent who said his neighborhood was "all right, as long as I can stand it". Of the 35 who took an openly poor view of their surroundings, nearly half made general complaints such as "look about you" or "what do you think?", and the rest had specific complaints which appear in Table 16. 

Their opinions of their neighborhoods as places to bring up children were more forcibly expressed. As one said, "I'm grown up, so I can take it, but...". The most frequent objection was to

lack of play space followed by dangerous traffic. Table 17 gives the picture simply and clearly. In The employment picture of the group is not encouraging. the main, their occupations are menial, their seniority low, and their tenure uncertain. Similarly there is little positive evidence of upward mobility in employment.

The committee found less evidence of unemployment than expected. 

The picture is more one of under-employment. As Table 18 shows, a high proportion of employed families hold more than one job. Actually, this proportion is undoubtedly higher, as interviewers frequently did not ask about additional jobs, and we have data only on those volunteered by the subjects. Many of these jobs are part-time in nature, and many of the full-time jobs are subject to seasonal fluctuation.

The kinds of jobs held by our respondents are typical of any depressed group. In general jobs of high status and high pay are conspicuously absent. Although we know of three Negro professionals in Holyoke, they were not in our study. The class of proprietors, managers, and officials contains no Negroes. Clerks and kindred workers are rare, and when found, generally in the case of males, turn out to be blue-collar rather than white collar clerks. Similarly our class of craftsmen and foremen include an overwhelming proportion of Westover Airmen who usually claim that they are in the service mainly because only there can they practice their skills. 

This leaves operatives, service workers and unskilled labor, all relatively low status positions, and even the operatives usually tend to have jobs at the lower end of the classification. This general situation is partly due to the fact that Holyoke tends to attract relatively ill-educated and untrained Negroes, but only partly so. Twenty-nine of our respondents showed evidence of being equipped to hold jobs of higher status than they now had. Some others were training or had been partially trained for higher jobs. (Table 20) Of those who appeared to be qualified for higher jobs, some had looked or tried to be promoted where they were, clearly without success, while others preferred the jobs which they now held. 

All this should be borne in mind when one considers the following data. Of 82 families reporting income on a total of 120 jobs, the

groups total monthly take home pay was $30,668, or an average of $374 per family per month, but this amount is only taken home when everyone is working, which is frequently not the case. 

Negroes' expressed reactions to Holyoke as a place to work in are as diverse as their background and personalities, ranging from "heaven compared to Georgia", to "worse discrimination that Alabama,

Tennessee, and Ohio". The truth probably lies at neither extreme, While less than half of the respondents made favorable judgments of Holyoke as a work place, only 39 made unfavorable ones. However, these comments become of interest when we discover that over threequarters of them refer directly to discrimination and prejudice, (Table 21) and eight of these provide specific instances of discrimination in hiring. It is possible that some of these responses could be results of misunderstanding, but the overwhelming nature of the data suggests that this is an area which might well be noted. 

Holyoke as a place to live in has greater appeal for its Negro citizens than as a town to work in. A bare majority, though hardly a healthy one, see the town as a good place to live in; a few were noncommital or refused to answer, and 29 made unfavorable comments. 

About half of these again referred directly or indirectly to problems of prejudice and discrimination. (Table 22) 

Conclusions 

All Holyoke is gaining in Negro population, while losing its leavening of relatively sophisticated colored citizens. evidence suggests that the local Negro population will continue to increase regardless of what happens. The problem Holyoke faces is two-fold.

First: to keep and attract back its older residents. These are people who want and can afford housing above the slum level. A number have told a member of our committee that if conditions were suitable they would return. A better chance at upgraded jobs, if possible, would help the situation.

The second part of the problem is to help our recent in-migrants to improve their status. Currently about the only area where they can do this is in the field of education, where all children are supposed to attend school until age 16, and where public schools are

provided. As far as housing and jobs are concerned they are struggling along as best they can in most cases, but with little in the way of temporal glory to support them. 

It is legitimate to ask here why this two-fold fact should be considered a problem, and furthermore, why Holyoke should be interested in affecting it.

The answer, while perhaps not obvious, is a compelling one. 

Given the current demographic picture of the United States in general and of New England in particular, we can assume that Holyoke is going to possess an increasing population of Negroes, one which can be expected to grow much more rapidly than her white population.

If this population is leavened with trained, prosperous, and consequently responsible citizens, and if the less sophisticated in-migrants can be assimilated to northern urban ways rapidly, the group can make a real contribution to the life of the city. 

But, if the present trend continues, and able men continue to leave the city only to be replaced by relatively unskilled and uneducated in-migrants who are allowed to stew in their own juice, as it were, then, instead of a well integrated group of useful and potentially useful persons there will be an unstable and growing group of relative strangers with no particular respect for or motivation to conform to the customs and order of our city. 

Until now Holyoke has been lucky with her Negroes. They have remained a small, cooperative, and relatively troublefree group, but with increasing numbers, and increasing Negro awareness of their state (whether real or imagined) Holyoke will have to move in the direction of raising their status eventually. It will be infinitely easier to do it now. Recommendations

The problem, either as an ethical one, or one of enlightened self-interest, appears to be twofold: first to upgrade our Negroes, particularly in regard to jobs and housing; second to retain and, and perhaps even attract Negroes who are already equipped to provide leadership, both for Negroes and for whites in this area. Other-

wise, as we have suggested, the situation is highly likely to deteriorate.

To achieve these ends we could use several methods, preferably in combination.

First: A clearing house where jobs and housing available to 

Negroes can be connected up with Negroes who need and are qualified for them.

Second: A committee for reconciliation which would have two functions. a. Use carefully selected men to approach key employers and property owners to get grist for the clearing house. b. Operate as a complaint department and mediator in cases where Negroes, employers, or landlords feel that all is not going well. 

Third: Get the facts and let them be known. a. Many Holyokers know very little about their Negro neighbors. This survey, for instance, shows them to be a hard-working, thoroughly decent lot of ordinary people who are starting at the bottom, just as most of our forebears did. Their only significant difference seems to be that they're not always allowed to rise on their own merits. 1 Publicize the kinds of jobs Negroes hold elsewhere, including any Holyoke Negroes who have gone on to better things. 

Let the facts be known about property values and Со race. A lot of housing discrimination is based on fear of monetary loss and there's considerable weight of evidence to show this fear to be an illusion. d. In cases where reason gets you nowhere, do not hesitate to publicize violations of the laws of this Commonwealth in regard to discrimination. It is not radical to render unto Caesar, particularly when you are also rendering unto God. 

Fourth: Do not do this for the Negroes, do it with them. 

Each needs the other.

Fifth: 

Remember: we can control events now. If we do not, events will control us.
