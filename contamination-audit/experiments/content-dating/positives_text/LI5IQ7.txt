To appreciate the relevance of the setting in which legislation transpires, it is well to reflect initially upon the political atmosphere of state government in Oklahoma as the Twenty-ninth legislative session convened in January, 1963. has been the case since statehood, the legislative assembly was predominantly Democratic; only six of the 44 members of the Senate and 25 of the 120 members of the House of Representatives were Republican.* However, most unique in the legislative process was the presence of the state's first Republican Governor. This new emphasis on two-party political responsibility for the affairs of state led to the development of two independent programs, one of Governor Henry Bellmon and another under the guidance of Senate 

President Pro Tempore Roy Boecher and Speaker of the House J.D. 

McCarty. As will be made evident, these programs, particularly as regard fiscal matters, were resolved through the art of compromise.

Against this background of cooperation the session concluded its business in five months and one week, the shortest session over the past six years. The brevity of the session was all the more commendable in view of the fact that its predecessor (the Twenty-eighth session) was the longest in state history, having necessitated an additional month and one-half to write a program under a Democratic Governor and Legislature. 

All told, the Twenty-ninth session considered approximately 

1,300 proposals, of which 689 acts or resolutions were enrolled.

Dozens of the new acts and resolutions required but one page, whereas the Public Health Code, covering 177 pages, was by far the longest. And indicative of the usual "last-minute" compromises reached, the lawmakers passed upon many of the key proposals, particularly as regard agency appropriations, during the final week of the session. As a result, they left more than one-fourth of their enactments on the desk of the Governor to certify or veto in the 15 days after the session adjourned. The membership of both legislative chambers, including the county or district from which they were elected and their political affiliation, is provided in the Appendix.

CHAPTER II STATE GOVERNMENT 

As distinguished from legislation that affects fiscal policy or the local subdivisions, subjects set aside for separate consideration, the focus here is on the more prominent of approved acts that relate to each of the three branches of state government. This sequence is supplemented in a concluding section on pertinent enactments that pervade all of the branches. 

A. Legislative

Legislative apportionment proved to be one of the crucial problems of the past session. Although this subject has long been a matter of deep concern to many, its new status was prompted by a decision of the U.S. Supreme Court in the spring of 1962.1 In essence, the court held that lower courts may take jurisdiction where a complaint contained allegations that a state statute or constitutional provision effected an apportionment that deprived a plaintiff of the equal protection of the laws in violation of the 14th Amendment. Therefore, upon being presented with a justiciable constitutional cause of action, the court ruled that lower tribunals can make available a judicial remedy. It did not state, however, what that remedy need be.

As a result, an Oklahoma reapportionment suit, instituted more than a year earlier but held in abeyance by a special three judge tribunal pending the above decision, was again docketed.2 Although much of significance transpired in subsequent hearings and opinions of the court, it is sufficient here to note that it culminated in a decree issued in the summer of 1962.3 Five momentous decisions were rendered:

1) that the laws under which the present legislature is apportioned are prospectively null and void for all future elections; 

2) that the court would defer action to the 1963 Legislature and at the same time give it both guidelines for reapportionment as well as a deadline to accomplish the duty; 

3) established the principle of substantial numerical equality to the end that each voter would have approximately the same power and influence in the election of members to the two chambers, and proceeded to specify a formula that would be acceptable to the court; 

4) left the matter of forming legislative districts to the discretion of the Legislature; and 

5) set March 8, 1963 as the deadline for legislative action and continuance of the case.

In response to this challenge the Legislature, after considering a dozen proposals, passed reapportionment measures for both houses during February, 1963, and each gained the endorsement of the Governor. Each act failed, however, to meet the letter of the guidelines set forth by the court.

Early in the month the House of Representatives took the initiative and passed a plan in general compliance with the Oklahoma Constitution.4 Retaining the limit of seven members that any county may elect, instead of increasing that number to 19 and 15, respectively, for the state's two most populous counties (which the court believed each is entitled), the House plan would be but a slight improvement over the present apportionment. If carried out, the majority of this chamber will be responsible to 31 per cent of the people, as opposed to the current 29 per cent and the 33 per cent if a reapportionment were conducted in strict accordance with the Oklahoma Constitution. It would, in addition, gradually reduce 

House membership from the present 120 to 91 during the next four sessions of the Legislature. The court guideline, on the other hand, would have the majority of an average 108-member lower chamber responsible to 42 per cent of the people.

Later in the month the Senate followed with a plan to reapportion its chamber premised upon its constitutional prerogative to use population "as ascertained by the next preceding Federal census, or in such manner as the Legislature may direct."5 Believing that the census does not depict a true picture of population, to the extent that it includes the migratory, transient and temporary residents, the upper chamber supported a reapportionment based 

House Bill 586. 

Senate Joint Resolution 8.

upon "permanent population" that placed emphasis upon registered voters and other undefined factors. As a result, the state's two most populous counties would be allotted four and three senators, respectively, as opposed to the eight and seven, respectively, which the court maintained they are entitled. The Senate, under this arrangement, would be composed of 47 members, as contrasted with the present 44, and they would be elected from 47 districts as compared with the 36 at present. Moreover, the majority of the upper chamber is now responsible to 24 per cent of the people and, should this plan prove acceptable to the court, it will provide some relief to urban residents since 33 per cent of the people would elect the majority of members, but be very far from the court guideline which would have the majority of the Senate responsible to 49 per cent of the people.

To conclude this brief discussion of apportionment, it is well to note that contrary to the plea of the Legislature, there are those who contend that population, or representation of people alone, should be the only criteria in determining the distribution of legislative seats. To them, the fundamental issue is one most important in a representative democracy, namely, whether we shall have a government of, by and for the people, or shall "other factors" be considered. As of mid-July, 1963, the U.S. Federal 

District Court for the Western District of Oklahoma, then four months since the continuance of the litigation on this issue was concluded, had neither made judgment on the apportionment acts of the Legislature nor issued a final decree.*

C. Judicial 

Although much effort and money was expended, both private and public, to study and bring about reforms in the judicial system, little was accomplished in this realm other than to popularize alternatives embodied in the "modified Missouri plan." Nevertheless, worthy of note are the following five acts designed to make more effective the judicial processes. 

In order to alleviate congested dockets in the seventh judicial district, the Legislature authorized an additional district judge to be nominated from Oklahoma County and elected at-large within the district.25

For the same reason, the Court of Common Pleas jurisdictional amount was increased to $10,000, and authorized one additional judge and court reporter.26 And in an effort to stem an alarming increase in the divorce rate within the larger counties, there was provided the establishment of marriage counseling and conciliation procedures through a Conciliation Department of the District Court in those counties in the 200,000 to 400,000 population bracket.27 Commonly referred to as a family court, this department can presently become operative only in Oklahoma and 

Tulsa counties.

D. Election and Succession 

Related in part to two preceding sections but more appropriate for consideration at this point is an act approved during the Twentyninth session which repeals the authority of the Governor to fill vacancies by appointment in the offices of U.S. Senator, District and Superior Court Judge and County Commissioner, directing that vacancies in each are to be filled by a special election called by the Governor within 30 days after the vacancy occurs.30 This proposal incurred one of the 17 vetoes issued by the Governor during the course of the session, and has claim to fame in that it was the only veto overridden by the Legislature. However, because the emergency clause was not added to this act, it is by law subject to a referendum petition within the 90 days from sine die adjournment before becoming law. Consequently, leaders in the Republican Party have announced that they will file such a petition against the act, and should said petition secure, before September 12, 1963 approximately 37,000 valid signatures, the measure will be suspended pending a vote of the people on the question. It should be noted that although this proposal calls for a special election to fill vacancies caused by death, resignation or removal in the office of County Commissioner, this has been so provided since 1961.31 

With regard to succession in office, two emergency interim acts dealing with the legislative, executive and judicial branches are relevant. To provide for continuity of legislative functions in case of attack or unavailability, one act authorized each legislator to designate not fewer than three nor more than seven emergency interim successors to his powers and duties, and to specify their order of succession.32 Should a legislator fail to register the minimum number of successors within a designated period of time, provision is made for the floor leader of the same political party in the same legislative chamber to designate, by and with the consent of the Speaker of the House or President Pro Tempore of the Senate, the additional interim successors. Similarly, another emergency interim act governs continuity of state executive officials, officers of the political subdivisions and judges.33 The prescribed successors of the Governor, in the order named and until a new Governor is elected, are for this purpose the Lieutenant Governor, President Pro Tempore of the Senate, Speaker of the House of Representatives, Secretary of State, State Auditor, Attorney General,

State Treasurer, State Examiner and Inspector, and members of the State Highway Commission in the order of their numerical districts.

CHAPTER III LOCAL GOVERNMENT 

Indicative of the attention given the state's subdivisions during the past session, approximately one-eleventh of the total number of legislative proposals dealt with this subject. A proportional number of these proposals were passed, among which the following are most significant.

A. Cities and Towns 

Hailed by many as foremost among approved measures in this category is an act authorizing incorporated cities and towns to levy taxes, on subjects other than ad valorem, sales and insurance premiums, upon a favorable vote of the people. It is estimated that this will broaden the tax base of cities and towns to as many as 15 prospective sources of revenue. Admittedly, this act is important for heretofore, with two exceptions, the people of cities and towns in Oklahoma could not tax themselves even if they so desired. However, a delegation of legislative authority is only as useful as it can be made effective, and it remains to be seen whether the citizens of the cities and towns with financial problems will respond favorably to its use. 

Equally important in the short and long run are a series of water laws passed, the more prominent of which provides that beneficial use will be the basis of the right to the use of water, unless such water is used for domestic purposes.2 Heretofore, water rights have been solely that of the owner who could use the same as he saw fit. In the interest of conservation, however, that doctrine has been changed to one of appropriation for beneficial use. Also in the interest of appropriate water utilization, the Legislature authorized cities and towns to enter into contracts or leases for the purchase, sale and distribution of water within or outside the municipal limits.3 1

CHAPTER IV STATE FINANCE 

The challenging game of making revenues equal expenditures did not escape the attention of the Governor and Legislature during the past session. In fact, it went on constantly in a situation where it presumably mattered not who won or lost, but rather how well the game was played.

To avoid a misunderstanding it need be repeated that the above figures do not necessarily represent the total funds made available to the respective functions of government, for federal grants-in-aid and earmarked revenue serve to supplement these appropriations. An outstanding example of this practice is the effect it has upon the operating fund of the Highway Department, for in addition to the above noted figure this department can expect approximately $68.5 million in gasoline taxes plus an estimated $5 million in surplus gasoline taxes, thus making available to it approximately $92 million of state revenue alone for the construction and improvement of thoroughfares. 

Finally, as it may appear difficult to reconcile in one's mind a fiscal program premised on no tax increases with a record budget, an explanation of this development is in order. First of all, the previously discussed utilization of $11.5 million in earmarked sales tax revenue is a factor in point. By using this amount to support institutions earlier financed by general fund appropriations, the general fund was in effect supplemented by a similar amount. Secondly, authority for additional appropriations was provided during the last week of the session when the State Board of Equalization increased its estimate of anticipated revenue ($3.8 million above that estimated earlier in the year) over the biennium. This permitted more to be expended from the general fund because the state could expect a larger income. 

And third, as has been the practice now for two decades, the general fund budget was made possible by open end or deficit financing. In simple terms, this means that although the state has sufficient funds to meet expenses during the present fiscal year, it in fact has overextended itself slightly more than $10 million for the second year (this figure was in excess of $13 million for 1961-63), thereby making it incumbent upon the Thirtieth session of the Oklahoma Legislature to begin its fiscal program by first meeting this obligation of its predecessor.

CHAPTER V CONSTITUTIONAL AMENDMENTS 

Perhaps among the least recognized of measures enrolled during a legislative session are those proposals to submit to a vote of the people amendments to the state constitution. Ordinarily, the average citizen will pay little attention to referendum items until such time that a vote on the issue approaches. This chapter serves in this sense as a preview of such questions, and is supplemented by legislative decisions on other proposals to amend both the U.S. and Oklahoma Constitutions.

A. Oklahoma

During the course of Oklahoma's 55 year history, 183 initiatives and referendums have been brought to a vote of the people. 

In the period 1907-62, 104 of these were submitted as legislative referendums and 44 adopted; 17 by referendum petitions and 8 adopted; and 62 by initiative petitions, of which 18 were adopted. 

Moreover, of the total, 138 were proposed as constitutional amendments and 53 approved, whereas 16 of the remaining 45 proposed acts and resolutions proved acceptable to the people.

CHAPTER VI EVALUATIONS AND CONCLUSIONS 

To assess any legislative session in an objective manner is a difficult task. Ideally, it would best be undertaken by one throughly unbiased who has observed carefully all of the sessions since statehood. This study, not having been prepared by persons so free of conviction or sufficiently aged, can hardly supply the model by which the Twenty-ninth session might be judged. It can conclude, however, with a review of that which did not come to pass, some thoughts on what was and what was not accomplished, and those efforts presently underway for future legislation.

C. Commendation and Criticism 

As stated, it is difficult to assess in an objective manner the work of the Twenty-ninth session. This has not prevented, however, an evaluation made public by the Chief Executive, nor should it preclude reflections from other quarters.

No better commendation of the efforts of the predominantly Democratic Legislature has been provided than that by our Republican Governor. In the same spirit of cooperation that necessarily transpired during the course of the session, he addressed both chambers on the day of sine die adjournment and expressed great satisfaction for all that was accomplished. He dubbed the assembly "the trail-blazing 29th" because of its outstanding record of accomplishments, and went on to cite the following: the largest budget in state history without a tax increase; b) provision for $7 million more than was made available by the last session; c) a reduction in the amount of "open-end" financing by $3.2 million; a) the biggest educational and best financed public safety programs in state history; e) authorization for increases in assistance payments and expanded pension benefits for teachers, police and firemen; f) provision for a 40-hour work week for mental health employees for the first time in state history; establishment of a state employees retirement system; h) met the March 8 deadline on reapportionment; i) authorized additional taxing power for cities and towns; j) reduced taxes in one industry by raising the oil and gas depletion allowance 7 per cent; k) utilized $11.5 million of sales tax revenue to better accommodate the needs of other state agencies; and 

1) established a number of new Boards and Commissions.

The sum total of these accomplishments, the Governor noted, added dignity to the Legislature and helped to restore the confidence of the people in state government. 

That the Legislature accomplished the foregoing, with the cooperation of the Governor, is not questioned. And how far each of these efforts will go to better serve the interests of all of the people of the state can be left to individual judgment. 

In contrast, although all appreciate that a given legislative session cannot be expected to solve all of the problems of state government, it is nonetheless desirable to comment briefly upon a few of the fundamental issues by no means resolved by the Twentyninth Legislature. 1 1

Perhaps foremost in this category is the subject of Legislative apportionment. Indicative of legislative reaction to this problem, none can deny that the Senate has not been apportioned in accord with the Oklahoma Constitution since statehood, and the House of Representative has ignored the related provisions of the fundamental law since 1921. In light of this custom and usage, coupled with a federal court guideline to afford equitable representation and no clear remedy prescribed by the U.S. Supreme 

Court, the session found itself in a most difficult position. 

Rather than elaborate upon their experiences with a dozen possible solutions, suffice it to say that the Senate again adopted a plan contrary to the Oklahoma Constitution, whereas the House enrolled a measure very nearly in accord with the state's fundamental law. 

Neither chamber, however, came near the guideline of the special three-judge federal district court. Moreover, each of these measures was endorsed by a Governor whose political party has long been an advocate of constitutional reapportionment. 

Similarly a problem of long standing for states throughout the country is that of developing fiscal programs that meet the needs for goods and services of state government. If one were to evaluate the recent financial requests of the various departments and agencies in view of the actual appropriations, it is evident that demand by far exceeds supply and the desired level of performance is not to be met. However, if one were to judge official reactions to the allocation of anticipated revenue of the general fund, one is led to believe that such appropriations are deemed adequate. Therefore, citizens of the state can expect most agencies of their state government to function well, or at least at the level of the preceding biennium. Reference to most agencies is quite intentional for, as the Alcoholic Beverage Control Board can best attest, some appropriations must necessarily result in a reduction-in-force.21

As for finance as it relates to state employment practice, a few figures recently made available by the research staff in the Governor's office are worthy of note. In Oklahoma today approximately 32,000 persons are drawing annually a sum of $104 million for services rendered. The national average of state employees to population is 8 per 1,000; in Oklahoma it is 10 per 1,000. Further, the national average per capita cost of state government is $159 per year; in Oklahoma it approximates $200. The implication here, and evidently the fact of the matter, is that state government in Oklahoma costs more to operate than in most other states (on a per capita basis), and the reason can be attributed to an abnormal number of state employees. As a follow-up to the Governor's avowed 

21 Senate Bill 81.

interest to reduce the state payroll by attrition, the Legislature passed a resolution encouraging a reduction of 1,500 employees. 

Meanwhile, both the Governor and Legislature teamed up to create 

18 additional boards, commissions and committees of state government, thereby supplementing the bureaucratic machinery and demands upon the state payroll. Evidently, each chamber and the chief executive has seen much need for such additions, and such is not the point in issue. At issue is the obvious contradiction between the goal and the practice. Moreover, few will argue that economy and efficiency in government are not desireable, but it is too often overlooked that effectiveness is an equally important criteria. 

Finally, as noted throughout the preceding chapters, the Twenty-ninth session made much headway toward alleviating a great variety of problems that beset state and local governments in Oklahoma. On the other hand, a number of objectives long advocated in many quarters toward improving the public administration of the state have been left for a more daring assembly. At the head of such a list of basic reforms would undoubtedly appear the consolidation of boards, commissions and committees under appropriate administrative departments whose heads would be appointed by the Governor, a unified judicial system which would serve to eliminate duplicate jurisdictions and provide for the appointment of many if not all judges, and provision for a short ballot that would have the effect of making appointive such officers as mine inspectors and county assessors. Viewed realistically, these reforms, among other unmentionables, are simply not deemed politically desirable at this time and otherwise require in large part constitutional amendments. The necessity for the latter raises, in turn, the question of a constitutional convention as the foundation upon which to build a framework within which the state can function more effectively. In this regard, we can learn much from the 

Michigan experience with its 1908 Constitution. Financially, politically and legally, Michigan was in a straitjacket which its citizens promptly removed at the conclusion of seven and one-half months in convention. As a result, the state is no longer beset with problems of the past, no longer restrained by extreme partisan bickering or an archaic set of codes called the fundamental law. 

It has moved, in fact, from a condition of virtual bankruptcy in 1959 to one of economic prosperity in 1963. This example, however, is by no means intended to imply that Oklahoma government has reached a comparable stage of crisis, but rather to submit that the time for re-evaluation of the state's fundamental law is long overdue.

C. MOSS V. BURKHART: THE FINAL JUDGMENT 

On July 17, 1963, approximately two and one-half years after this case was filed with a special three-judge U.S. District Court, the tribunal issued a unanimous decree ordering immediately the reapportionment of the Oklahoma Legislature. This action came in response to a failure of the Legislature to meet by March 8, 1963 the guidelines of the court adopted August 3, 1962. 

These guidelines, popularly referred to as Model "C, "* pre- scribe a Senate of 44 members to be elected from 44 districts, and a House of Representatives of 109 members for the ensuing two sessions and 107 members for the remaining two sessions of the decennial period. Presently, the 44 members of the Senate are elected from 36 districts, and 120 members comprise the House of Representatives. As a result of this decision, the majority of the 

Senate will be responsible to 49 per cent of the people, as compared to the present 24.5 per cent, and the majority of the House of 

Representatives will be responsible to 42 per cent of the people, as compared to the present 29.5 per cent.

Moreover, the court reiterated its position that population, or substantial numerical equality, forms the basis of equal protection of the laws. And of all the plans made a part of the record, it held that Model "C" best met its construction of that standard of the 14th Amendment to the U.S. Constitution. 

In addition, the court decided that all legislative candidates must run at-large for seats and terms distinguished numerically within the districts designated in the model until the next Legislature defines boundaries within districts. Accordingly, all legislative offices will expire 15 days after the general election in November, 1964. The court did not, however, rule on the apportionment acts of the Twenty-ninth Legislature (1963), because these enactments were adopted without the emergency clause, thereby not to become law until 90 days after sine die adjournment. 

Further, the court ordered all county election boards to accept filings of candidates for legislative seats as provided by its decree.

With the foregoing paragraphs serving to form the substance and effect of the final judgment, it is in order to comment briefly upon the implications of this far reaching decision. * This alternative for the reapportionment of the Oklahoma Legislature was one of three models offered in The New Perspective of 

Legislative Apportionment in Oklahoma, by Joseph C. Pray and 

George J. Mauer, Bureau of Government Research, University of 

Oklahoma, Norman, July, 1962.

1. Basis: 

Strictly Population or Otherwise? 

Although it can be said that the prescribed reapportionment of the Senate is strictly on the basis of population, this is not true of the House of Representatives. Indicative of this fact is the significant difference in the per cent of population to which a majority of each chamber will be responsible. The reason for this development is that part of the Oklahoma Constitution (Article V, Section 10 d) which permits a county with one-half ratio of representation to be entitled to one full-time representative, and to a county with such ratio of population and three-fourths over, two representatives, and so on to infinity. Therefore, it should be made clear that although Model "C" is predominantly based upon population, it is not totally the case in the apportionment of the House of Representatives. Clearly, the formula for the latter chamber is slightly weighted in favor of the smaller counties or rural representative districts. 11 

2. Relationship to Oklahoma Constitution?

This decision has the effect of eliminating or overriding two clauses in the state's fundamental law. On the one hand, it necessarily strikes that portion of Article V, Section 10 d, prescribing that no county may ever take part in the election of more than seven representatives. On the other hand, it necessarily strikes that portion of Article V, Section 9 (a), known as the 'except" clause. It follows the provision for 44 senators to be elected from 44 districts, "except that in event any county shall be entitled to three or more senators at the time of any apportionment such additional senator or senators shall be given such county in addition to the forty-four senators and the whole number to that extent." It is of interest to note that the court did not explain in its final judgment the reason for the elimination of the latter of these clauses, but it need be recalled that in its August 3, 1962 decree it instructed the Legislature to disregard the upper limit on county representation in the House of Representatives, and alluded to the "except" clause as an "irreconcilable incongruity. In any event, with the exception of the temporary emergency provision for elections to be held at-large within districts, the court has in fact left intact all other provisions on legislative apportionment in the Oklahoma Constitution. 11 11 

3. Prospects for the Future?

As for the significance of the court's final judgment, it is undoubtedly the most far reaching to date and will have an indirect, if not direct, effect upon similar litigation across the country. 

Meanwhile, the big question yet to be answered is whether the decree will affect the 1964 elections or be delayed until 1966.

And obviously more basic than this is whether the highest court in the land will ultimately uphold the substance of the decree. Should

the expected appeal be filed in the 60 days following issuance of the judgment or 60 days following a ruling on a new trial, only 

Associate Justice Byron White can presently issue a "stay" to affect the former question; the latter will be resolved one day by the U.S. Supreme Court. Further, if these inquiries offer no particular challenge, perhaps one can then entertain the proper disposition of the 1963 apportionment acts of the Legislature due to become law in mid-September, coupled with the status of the legislative referendum to establish a new constitutional formula on apportionment that is scheduled for a vote of the people at the next runoff primary election. In summary, it has become clear that Oklahoma has forged its way through the political thicket only to encounter what may prove to be an equally obstructive legal thicket.
