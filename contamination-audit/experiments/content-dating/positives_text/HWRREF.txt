GENERAL SURVEY OF THE SOUTHERN HIGHLANDS 

Even before the American Revolution colonists who feared wild animals and hostile Indians less than conditions in Europe or along the Atlantic coast in America, had pushed back into the mountains of Pennsylvania, Virginia, and North Carolina. 

Included among them were Englishmen who sought religious freedom, a chance to earn a better living than England offered, relief from political oppression, or who perhaps had been sent from England because of political offences or minor crimes; 

Scotch-Irish who had suffered religious, economic, and political oppression; Germans driven from their homes by religious persecution, ruined by religious wars and political oppression; and a few French Huguenots, also driven from their homeland because of their religious beliefs. The greater part, therefore, found the highest value of their life in their religion, shared a hatred of the Roman Catholic Church as the source of their troubles, and displayed a persistence and hardihood above the average. They were men of intelligence, but tended to a strong individualism and distrust of others. 

After the Revolution still different groups joined the movement westward. Small farmers from the eastern parts of Virginia and North Carolina found it impossible to compete with the large plantations using slave labor; Baptists left Virginia in search of religious freedom; ex-soldiers, tradesmen and artisans, as well as a few educated men such as doctors and preachers -all sought new opportunities in the sparsely settled land in or beyond the mountains. While many went on to the Mississippi valley and the Northwest Territory, others remained to make their homes in the mountain valleys of what are now West Virginia, North Carolina, Tennessee, and Kentucky, which

seemed quite as inviting as the forest land beyond. Here there was fertile land to be had and the opportunity to establish homes far from the conditions they had fled. Secluded hollows among the hills promised the isolation their individualism demanded. 

During the first part of the nineteenth century westward migration continued to pass through this mountain region. But when travel shifted to the river and the railroad along the northern routes, these southern highlands lost touch with developments in the rest of the continent. Furthermore, as large family followed large family, successive generations were pushed farther back into the hill country, to find ever steeper slopes on which to make their precarious living. For them life continued much as it had for their pioneer ancestors, while other parts of the country were experiencing an amazing material progress.

So it was that the twentieth century considered the region a real discovery when a few writers began to tell of certain aspects of it, often greatly exaggerated, and tourists began to pass along the new automobile highways which were built across it. Hundreds of thousands of visitors have been attract-

ed to the Great Smoky Mountains National Park, and no doubt. many have been surprised to see towns and cities which do not differ greatly from those nearer their home. For it is in the rural regions where the roads are still unpaved, in the "coves" reached by trails that must follow the bed of the stream, in the "flats" which the rainy season may cut off from the nearest neighbors, that time has stood still and women still weave at looms or wash by the side of the stream. 

Life is hard in such regions, and the needs are many. Hillsides that were poor farms in the beginning have grown less fertile and have washed. Cash crops are few and not much use where poor roads make transportation to market impossible. 

Workers in some sections estimate the cash income of their farms at even less than a hundred dollars a year. Under such conditions equipment is naturally poor, and aids to good crops like fertilizer are out of the question. During the drought of the early 1930's even these poor crops failed, and suffering was intense. At the same time the depression drove back to the mountain farms thousands of young people who had found work in the industrial centers of the North. Much of the land is too hilly for farming, while some that is suitable is owned by large mining and lumbering corporations. County agriculture agents have been working in a number of the counties in recent years, trying to introduce new crops and new methods, but the lack of money for tools and seed is a handicap. In addition, the people of these hidden valleys are reluctant to accept theoretical advice; they must be shown by actual example that the new methods produce better results.

There is not much opportunity to earn a living away from the farm. The best forests have been cut, and many of the coal mines which are found in parts of the mountain region have stopped operations. While new factories are moving in, attracted by the cheap labor, legislation in the states is not yet strong enough to protect the workers from industrialists of the exploiting type. their workers at heart, others have become involved in bitter disputes over their treatment of their employees. 

The homes of the people reflect their degree of financial success. The more advanced farmer (there are a few in almost every district) has a good, comfortable home, while the farmer with nothing but steep fields lives in a house of rough planks or of logs. The few shiftless families to be found in any community are probably living in tumbledown shacks, but the greater part have found ways to make their small cabins as comfortable as possible. They love these homes deeply, as well as the overshadowing hills; many who have left the mountains return because they cannot be happy away from them. 

A Mountain Cabin 

The clear air of the highlands promises health to all, yet disease is widespread. Primitive sanitary arrangements mean the prevalence of some diseases which more progressive sections of the country have been able to stamp out. Crowded living conditions make it difficult to control the spread of disease; when nine children are living with their parents in one or two

small rooms, it is easy to imagine the results when disease strikes. Infant and child mortality is high. Pellagra, rickets, and other diseases which rise from malnutrition are also common. 

While there are physicians in most of the county seats and larger towns, the fees for a trip into the hills make their attentions impossible for most of the Highlanders. 

Schools. The same poor roads and lack of communications have made difficult the problem of schools. Although school terms are being lengthened from four or five months to eight months, often the teachers are poorly trained and poorly paid. In many sections where some church organization outside has maintained a school, the public schools have raised their own standards to meet the challenge. Others have new, consolidated schools which are fairly well equipped. But the more remote districts still have the one-room school, with one teacher for all grades and perhaps forty pupils. There is probably no equipment such as maps or large dictionaries, and the building may be badly in need of repair. Attendance is irregular, for the older people do not see much value in education. In the past, comfort has depended on a man's ingenuity and manual skill, and there. seemed nothing to be gained from study in books. 

Much has been written of lawlessness in the mountains, of murders and moonshine stills. Conditions of life such as those prevailing in the hills naturally give rise to unstable temperaments and encourage violence. In many sections local government is corrupt and the officers are in league with the violators of law. But most workers feel that the illicit liquor-making is turned to as the only way of getting a cash income; corn is much more easily transported after it has been turned into whisky. They find even more disturbing the general use of the whisky among the mountaineers, including boys and even children. A large part of the population recognizes the evil. 

In such communities there is little organized social life or amusement. The day's work often begins at four in the morning, and by evening few would want to travel the poor roads

even if there were any gatherings to attend. Ordinarily neighborhood visiting and aid in times of trouble are the only diversions. But there are special events which may call together the whole community, such as a decoration at the cemetery or dinner on the grounds at a singing or a revival, or in these days at a farm meeting or demonstration. Love of music is general and a singing school one of the greatest treats. 

Oak Grove Meetinghouse

Religion. The people of the mountains have kept that love of religion which drew so many of their ancestors to build homes in the hills. The church has been called the one universal institution of the Highlands. But it too retains many of its pioneer customs and in fact clings to them even when no longer necessary, feeling they are hallowed by tradition. One preaching service a month may be considered best because it is more appreciated, and one preacher divides his time between several congregations. 

A salary for the preacher will be condemned because the gospel should be free. An educated preacher is thought less able to receive the inspiration of God than one who may not even have known how to read when he felt his call to serve. So the average preacher is one who has received little education, who works

during the week as a farmer or perhaps at some handicraft, and who travels many miles to serve various congregations on Sundays. Nevertheless, the most of them are men of noble character, feeling a genuine conviction that they have been called to serve. 

Some of the younger ones among them feel that their lack of education is a handicap now that more of the young people are attending high school and they are called upon to meet more people from outside.

The people retained their belief in the Bible as the source of all truth even after continued frontier hardships had reduced. a large percent of the population to illiteracy and they could no longer read it for themselves. Religion for them is a religion of the next world, and they resent any effort on the part of the church to regulate their customs here. Man is prepared for the future life by hardships and sorrows; the church is not expected to make any effort to alleviate these. The climax of their religious experience is their conversion, which in some denominations is a violent emotional experience, the goal of the yearly revival or protracted meetings. Unfortunately, many believe that after once they have experienced this conversion, their subsequent behavior is of no importance and they are free to continue in their old habits. Other denominations strive for a definite conversion without emotional stress and emphasize the Christian life and character as its natural consequence. 

An unfortunate result of the individualistic tendencies of the Highlanders has been an extreme development of denominationalism. Among the 14,359 churches found within 170 counties lying in the region, ninety-eight denominations were represented. There were eleven divisions of Baptists, nine of 

Methodists, and seven of Presbyterians. There is little cooperation between the denominations or even between the different branches of one denomination. Each believes that it alone holds the key to salvation and the future life and often attacks the others bitterly. Sometimes when one church sends workers. into a neglected district, others rush in also; and soon there are

so many small groups that none can survive, and the district is again left without a church. In recent years the so-called perfectionist denominations such as the Holy Rollers have been making great advances among the people. 

FRIENDS HISTORY IN TENNESSEE 

Friends who penetrated the mountains of western North Carolina soon pushed over into Tennessee. Although a history of North Carolina Yearly Meeting which appeared in Friends Review in 1861 states that Lost Creek Quarterly Meeting in Tennessee was established in 1802, Stephen Grellet writes in his Memoirs that the quarterly meeting was already established there when he visited the meetings in 1800. On his trip through the Middle West and the South to testify against slavery he again visited eastern Tennessee in 1825. Another prominent 

Friend who visited Tennessee in the first half of the nineteenth century was the Englishman, William Forster, who died in Friendsville in 1854 and was buried there. He too was traveling in protest against slavery, presenting messages from English Friends to the President of the United States and the governors of various states. 

Friends seem to have prospered in eastern Tennessee throughout the first half of the century. Of the schools they established only one survives, Friendsville Academy, established in 1857. But the Civil War brought great hardship to the region. Although the greater part of the region favored the 

Union cause, Friends remained true to their pacifist principles. Unlike the Friends in North Carolina, most of the men in Tennessee escaped to the western states. Only twenty men of conscription age were left at New Hope Monthly Meeting and five at Lost Creek. Part of these paid a high exemption fee to the Confederate enlistment officers, while others refused even this compromise. They were subject to arrest by both 

Confederate and Union forces, and the adventures of such men as James F. Beals, John Beals, William J. Hackney, William

Morgan, and Riley Lee offer material to a novelist. Some fifteen. men were hidden for a time in a cave which later was described by Trowbridge in his Cudjo's Cave. James Beals. returning. from imprisonment by the Union army, found the Friends of Friendsville almost destitute, without seed to plant new crops. 

He made his way north through the lines to ask aid from other Friends and was immediately entrusted with a check for $1500, with which he bought wagonloads of supplies which he was able to get back to his people.

For several years there was almost continual fighting through the district, and the material loss to Friends was great. 

Friendsville Monthly Meeting alone lost $165,000 in gold value, without counting their loss in horses and grain. The meeting at Lost Creek was sacked by the Confederate forces. 

Allen Jay, who visited the meeting in 1867, comments in his Autobiography on the suffering which resulted and states that the once large Friendsville Academy was then a small school. 

The monthly meeting at Hickory Valley was established following services which he held on this trip.

The quarterly meeting in Tennessee was later transferred from North Carolina Yearly Meeting to Wilmington Yearly Meeting, to which it now belongs. It includes five monthly meetings, with some nine hundred members.

Following the Civil War Friends in Tennessee interested themselves in the problems of the education of Negroes, mountaineers, and Cherokee Indians. In 1880 it was reported that graduates of the Maryville Coloured Institute held their own in competition with graduates of Fisk University. At the same place a normal school was established to prepare teachers for service in the mountain districts. In 1880 Friends of Maryville became concerned for the education of the Indians in the eastern part of the State and requested that Indiana and Western Yearly Meetings investigate conditions. Barnabas C. Hobbs was sent and arranged to open schools. The following year five day

schools were opened under the superintendency of Thomas C. 

Brown, while two Indian girls were sent to the normal school at Maryville. In 1883 a higher school was opened with government aid at Cherokee, over the North Carolina line. This remained under the control of Friends for about ten years, receiving as high as 89 scholars a year. This school is located near the boundaries of the present Great Smoky Mountains National Park and may be seen by Friends who visit that region; it has been under Government control since 1892. 

FRIENDS IN MONROE COUNTY 

Not long after the Civil War Friends in Tennessee began. work to alleviate the physical, mental, and spiritual poverty of the dwellers in some of the hidden valleys of the eastern part of the state. One of the leaders in this was Dr. Jephtha Garner, physician of Maryville. Under his supervision eight meeting- and schoolhouses were built, Bibles, school and library books were distributed, and several thousand were taught to read. He induced Friends from other states to come to Tennessee to aid in the work, among them Benjamin C. Coppock and James B. 

Bruff of Ohio, and John N. Parker and William P. Trueblood of Indiana. With these there worked several from the Tennessee meetings, including John Calvin Jones and his wife Rachel, and Rachel Binford. Local leaders also gave splendid service as evangelists; among these should be mentioned John Farr and 

David Sanders.

In 1920 the Board of Home Missions of the Five Years Meeting began to contribute towards the support of a pastor who could give full-time services to the meetings of Monroe County. J. Edward and Daisy Ransome were secured and set up their home at Oak Grove in September of that year. A short time previously Pearl Redding, a Friend and student at Earlham

College, had arrived to teach in the public school at Farr's Chapel. Other teachers who have served for varying periods

have been Sophia Pribbenow of Oklahoma, Gladys Morris of 

Iowa, Earl Clark of Kansas, Helen Hester of Illinois and Carrie

Miles of Vermont. In 1928 another full-time worker was sent by the Board, Fred McMillin, who served until ill health compelled his retirement in 1938.

In 1939 Franklin and Lillian Chant were secured to take the place of Fred McMillin, bringing to their work the experience gained from a number of years of service in Indiana Yearly Meeting.

Highland ways are rooted in long tradition and are therefore very hard to change. The mountaineer is very independent, very individualistic: he does not readily recognize that new ways are desirable, especially new ways involving cooperation. To change these attitudes, personal and friendly contact with wise friends exemplifying new ways in the home neighborhood, over a long period of time, has so far proved the most effective means. has been and is still a time-consuming function of the teacher in the. . . . school, of the worker at the community center, and of the . . . . pastor and his family.¹ 

Probably the Ransomes, because of their longer service, have indentified themselves most completely with all that is best in the life of the communities. It is as friends who have the genuine good of their neighbors at heart that they have made their place in Tennessee.

FRIENDS FACE THE PROBLEMS 

Making a Living. Friends recognized early that poverty was the cause of much of the suffering and disorder among the mountaineers and have sought means to enable the inhabitants of Monroe County to earn a better living. Dr. Garner had so vivid a realization of what an industrial school could do for the young people that he purchased a tract of 440 acres at White Oak Flats as the site for his proposed school. To his great disappointment, he was unable to raise the necessary money to develop his plan. One of the chief concerns of Edward Ransome has been to introduce better crops and better farming methods. among the farmers. Soon after his arrival he rented three acres of ground and raised a crop of corn to demonstrate to the farmers the value of fertilizer. Within a few years he had increased the yield from twelve bushels per acre to thirty-five. Mountain 

Friends were so interested that they raised almost the entire sum needed to purchase a tract of several acres near the Ransome home. Edward Ransome has continued this work through the years, experimenting with new crops such as Lespedesa Sericea, strawberry plants, orchards, and vineyards. Strawberries are shipped in carload lots from Madisonville, the county seat, and offer a cash income to the region. He himself believes that the land where he works is not suited to cultivation and that the

It is average land which offers the same problems that most of the farmers of the district face and is so situated that the work could be readily observed from the road. But so far funds have not been available for its purchase. 

Oak Grove Parsonage

Home Making. The two homes provided for Friends pastors in Monroe County are not only revelations to the region of the comforts of modern living where plumbing and lighting are possible. Because Edward Ransome and Fred McMillin did so much of the work themselves, they also showed their neighbors what could be accomplished by a man's own efforts. The two

men worked side by side with the men of the community in the building of the new meetinghouses at Oak Grove and at 

Three Points. For a number of years Edward Ransome has conducted a manual training class for boys, while Daisy Ransome teaches a Saturday class for girls in home making. 

Physical Well-being. Dr. Garner first became interested in the physical needs of the mountaineers through his practice as a physician and from that became concerned for their mental and spiritual needs as well. Edward Ransome is not a physician, but he has learned to give some simple treatments and can often advise better remedies than the ones used by his neighbors. He cooperates with the Red Cross and relief agencies in providing for cases of extreme need. In emergencies he has often been able to secure medical care, transporting the patient to Madisonville. 

Not of least importance is the teaching of healthful habits of living. 

Educational Opportunities. Wherever Friends have located they have established schools and interested themselves in the problems of education. Tennessee has been no exception. 

Friendsville Academy has for years been the only hope of many of the mountain boys and girls for a higher education. Dr. 

Garner, working in the more secluded valleys, estimated that in the first eight years of his travels three thousand had been taught to read. The service of the many Friends teachers in the mountain schools has extended beyond the class room; they have organized Sunday Schools, conducted community classes, and given practical testimony against lawless living in general. 

Perhaps most encouraging of all has been the development of teachers among young people of the district who have been given the opportunity to study at Friendsville Academy, Guilford College, etc. Between 1920 and 1930 the percentage of illiteracy in the county among those over ten years of age was lowered from 18% to 12.9%.

No single person has done more for the progress of educa-

tion in Monroe County in recent years than Edward Ransome. 

He has urged the lengthening of the school term in the outlying schools and the choice of well prepared teachers. Permission has been given for the use of one or two meetinghouses as schools. As trustee of Friendsville Academy he himself has assumed the responsibility for the expenses of a number of Monroe County young people to attend the school, trusting to the generosity of Friends in other parts of the country to enable him to meet the obligation. While in the first years his difficulty was to obtain the consent of the parents to the child's going so far from home, of recent years it has been to provide the opportunity for the many who wish to avail themselves of it. His dream is to bring the possibility of higher education within the reach of more young people of his communities by the establishment of a consolidated school at Oak Grove. 

Roger's Chapel Parsonage 

Temperance. Friends from the first have taken a firm stand against the liquor business, at the same time that they have recognized its economic basis. In 1879 it was reported that "a reward of $5.00, offered by a hostile individual, to any one who would get a member of the Friends' Church to break the

pledge, has never yet been claimed." Edward Ransome has always been open in his opposition: both he and Fred McMillin report it as one of the chief problems faced by the worker in the territory. Friends' teachers have found their lives in danger through their opposition to the moonshiners.

Wholesome Social Life. Daisy and Edward Ransome, on their first arrival in Tennessee, saw the lack of a wholesome social life among the people, and it has been their aim to make both the homes of the Friends' workers and the meetinghouses centers of community social life. The Ransome home offers the same generous hospitality which is typical of the smallest mountain cabin. The big social event at all five meetings is the annual Christmas tree. Friends from all parts of the country share in this expression of the Christmas spirit by the gifts they send for the trees, and the memory of this spirit of Christian fellowship is kept alive throughout the year in the mountains as the gifts fill a need or provide a spot of beauty in the little cabins.

Spiritual Ministry. All of these activities have been but secondary to the ministry to the spiritual life of the community. 

Dr. Garner established several stations in Monroe County early in his service in the 1870's; John Parker opened church and school work in Hopewell Springs in 1876, where there is now no Friends' work carried on. Rachel Jones and Rachel Binford of

North Carolina Yearly Meeting established a meeting at Talassie, which was later taken over by Seventh Day Adventists. 

The land around the church at White Oak Flats, established sometime before 1903, has been taken over by the government for a game preserve and the inhabitants have been moved to other places. But the other groups established by Friends have been enduring. They represent the devotion and persistence of many local leaders, aided by the sympathy and contributions of 

Friends throughout the country.

Farr's Chapel is named for John Farr, who is listed among 

Friends evangelists in Tennessee in a report of 1879. Originally

a Methodist, he is said to have been attracted to Friends by John Parker. At first his meetings in this region were held in the home of Bayless Y. Kitchen, next in a Methodist church, and later in a school house nearby. When a jealous faction in the neighborhood attempted to close this school to him, Bayless Kitchen had a key made and opened the building to the meetings again. In 1899 it was felt that the work needed a regular meetinghouse of its own. In the month between regular visits of John Farr, the men of the community built, without his knowledge, a house from logs furnished by Bayless Kitchen on land donated by his father, Columbus, a Methodist, and with the help of oxen furnished by Pete Hampton, a Baptist. No road led to the new meetinghouse, and Bayless Kitchen and John Cormany carried the stove on their shoulders over three miles of rought trail. The original log house was later replaced by a boxed house, and in 1928 an addition was built to this meetinghouse. It was in the school there that Pearl Redding began her teaching in the mountains in 1920. Preaching services are held once a month. 

Farr's Chapel Meetinghouse 

Chestnut Valley, where Edward Ransome began preaching services and Sunday School in 1921, conducting the work under

an apple tree, was known in 1903 to Dr. Garner as the meeting at Otta May. In 1922 special contributions made it possible to build a meetinghouse; in it the following year the first day school in the community was started. There Sophia Pribbenow taught from 1923 to 1927 and was followed by Pearl Redding. 

Preaching services are held once a month.

The Oak Grove Meeting, which gives its name to the monthly meeting, is also one of the older meetings. With its meetinghouse, manual training shop, and the home of the Ransomes, it forms an impressive center for Friends' work in Monroe County. The new meetinghouse there was completed in 

1927 through contributions of money and service from many interested Friends. Stanfordville Meeting of New York contributed $500.00 received from the sale of its meetinghouse, while Marlboro Monthly Meeting of New York contributed the Delco plant and a Wilmington, Ohio, Friend, the fixtures; an electrician of Friendsville donated his services to install these, and the men of the community aided in the erection of the building. Fred McMillin said of it when he first preached there that he could hardly believe he was not in some city church. 

Preaching services are held weekly. 

Three Points Meetinghouse

The work at Maynard's Chapel and at Roger's Chapel was organized during the ministry of David Sanders, who came. into Monroe County in 1893, and served for twenty-seven years as a spiritual leader among the widely scattered groups. 

In the fall of 1920 the meeting at Maynard's Chapel was reopened by Edward Ransome. In 1929 a new meetinghouse was completed at Three Points, one-half mile from the old. 

Maynard's Chapel. Land for this was contributed by a Baptist neighbor, and the men of the community contributed much of the labor to build it. An interested Friend gave the $1700.00 cash expended, and Edward Ransome and Fred McMillin took active part in the carpentry and masonry involved. 

Although Dr. Garner conducted a school at Roger's Chapel in 1903, the work was rather weak until a new home was built there in 1930 for Fred McMillin. Sunday night prayer meeting is held weekly, but the preaching service is only part time. 

The leaders in the community feel that the work would be much strengthened if the meetinghouse could be moved to a more central location. The school at White Oak Flats was moved to the Roger's Chapel Meetinghouse when the government took over the land where it was first located. 

In 1928 Fred McMillin began preaching services in the Lovin Schoolhouse. These six congregations are united in the 

Oak Grove Monthly Meeting, organized in 1906 by Friendsville Quarterly Meeting. Membership has increased gradually; in 1930 there were three hundred and twenty-two members and in 1938 four hundred and forty-seven. They contribute to the United Budget, also pay their proportion of Yearly Meeting stock.

Summer time in Monroe County is the season of the yearly revival meetings, usually held for a period of two weeks in each of the congregations. Such meetings are an accepted and expected service of the church in the southern mountains and offer a concentrated period of religious meetings in many commonthly or at best fortnightly. They lead many to accept the

munities where services during the winter have been only Christian life, while others are strengthened in their beliefs. 

Edward Ransome and Fred McMillin have conducted many of these meetings themselves, while in others they have been aided by evangelists from other meetings of Friendsville Quarterly

Meeting, of Wilmington Yearly Meeting, or elsewhere. 

The twentieth century has not been without progress in the Friends communities of Monroe County. Mountain trails are slowly being replaced by graded roads, school terms have been lengthened and teachers are somewhat better prepared; log cabins have been made more comfortable, and meetinghouses have been improved. But work is still left in which sympathetic 

Friends elsewhere may aid: a high school education should be brought within reach of the many boys and girls who desire it; experimentation should be continued (and the farm purchased) until some means is found of relieving the poverty of most of the mountaineers. And parallel with this and running beyond, the message of a changed life following the acceptance of Christ must be carried, as it is now, by consecrated and devoted workers who have become a part of the communities to which they have been called.
