INTRODUCTION 

IT is three thousand miles, more or less, from the quiet lanes of Devonshire to the roaring avenues of New York. Between the two there is much more difference than the matter of noise or of five hours in time. 

Customs are different, speech is different, faces are different. New York is the fastest city in the world: Devonshire is one of the slowest of country-sides. The Devon farmer uses his tools and his cart for a lifetime, and he will, more likely than not, die in the same house in which he was born. In New York the majority of the inhabitants change their residence every year and their interests every day. The farmer's cart bumps and rattles, year after year, through the lanes at its steady pace of two or three miles an hour. In the short time which has elapsed since the events of this story took place the tri-motored transport plane has given place to new modelshuge, bullet-like machines capable of nearly two hundred miles per hour, spanning America in twenty hours from coast to coast.

But whether the airplane in which Walter Schlaf flew to his death was travelling at one hundred and twenty or one hundred and eighty miles per hour, and whether he died. in the clear air above the hanging gardens of this modern Babylon or under a fragrant hedge in Devonshire, makes, in the final analysis, very little difference. For human beings remain the same in both countries, and have the same hates, loves and impulses to violence. the guardians of law and order use much the same technique in dealing with them. Inspector 

Haskell of Scotland Yard and Inspector Grady of the Homicide Squad, New York Police, are brothers (although they will never admit it) under the skin. And

Since my earlier account of Haskell's work on a case in the West Country* was so favourably received, I have ventured in this book to present a case from the private files of my other good friend and excellent detective, Inspector Grady.

I have endeavoured, moreover, by the use of a somewhat unusual structural design, to help the reader see the people concerned in it as they really were, rather than as the conventional, puppet-like characters of works of Behind The Head-lines, published in 1933 by J. W.

fiction. Inspector Grady, who has as little liking for publicity as Haskell has, urged me particularly to do this. Like myself, he is intensely interested in people as people. The mental processes of human beings are too complex, and their actions too unpredictable, he maintains, to be very convincing in readymade heroes and villains.

It is not that the Inspector believes himself any genius at psycho-analysis. On the contrary, he admits that the solution of this case came through ordinary police work, plus a rather lucky accident.

The fact that these events took place during the final days of Prohibition may perhaps add some special interest, as well as throw some more light on the conditions which that unfortunate law produced in America. But it remains essentially, as Grady points out, a story of people rather than events.

In their bright glare the little plane left the ground. It climbed a hundred-a hundred and fifty feet. 

Then, sweeping back from the engine, a flash of fire appeared. It grew swiftly. In a few seconds the whole fuselage was smothered in flames. The plane wavered in its steep climb. For a moment it seemed to hang suspended in the air. Then, with engine still roaring and the wires whistling in the wind, it fell off to one side and crashed into the ground. 

Instantly there was a terrific explosion as the petrol tank blew up. 

Inspector Grady lowered his pistol. "Last shot must have got the engine!" he said to himself.

From the other side of the field a siren began to cut the air and a fire engine dashed out toward the flaming wreckage, the firemen unlimbering chemical extinguishers and struggling into white asbestos suits.
