The Study of Law 

Blackstone, the great English Commentator, writing on the laws of England, from which country the United States derives its main source of law, declared: "Three classes of men should read law, the lawyer for his profession, the business man for business reasons, and every man for increased efficiency and his own protection." 

Thomas Jefferson, author of the Declaration of Independence and apostle of modern democracy, gave as his opinion that, "The study of the law qualifies a man to be useful to himself, to his neighbors, and to the public." 

In no country so much as in ours should these statements hold true, for the people of the United States and of the several States make the laws under which they are governed, and therefore some knowledge of the law is essential to good citizenship. Yet as we shall later learn there is a tendency to make the law and the practice of it a closed profession and a sealed book to the general public. 

The Law as a Profession 

As a profession, the law is an ancient and honorable one, going back into the early days of English history. 

Its members have been pioneers in the formation of constitutions and in the framing of laws under which the people of England and of the United States have been governed for centuries. 

Originally in England, as elsewhere, parties appeared in person to plead or defend their causes in court. This right remains to-day. Later, on application to the court, a party was permitted to be represented by another, called an "attorney", one who acts in the "turn" or place of another. "Attorneys" became numerous; some were unfitted for their work. As a consequence their number was restricted to those who by reason of special study and attendance upon the sessions of court had made themselves familiar with legal principles and procedure. In time, the study of law and admission to the Bar came to be controlled by the members of the legal profession, organized into what were called "Inns of Court". The profession itself was divided into two classes, known as "solicitors" and "barristers". The latter alone could appear for a client before the bar of any court. Solicitors correspond largely to our office lawyers: they try cases in the municipal and divorce courts, and when necessary, 5

engage a barrister for a client. The barrister depends on the solicitor for the facts in the case and for his retainer and fee, and does not deal with the client directly. 

In view of this origin of attorneys or representatives, it is historically incorrect to say that lawyers are "officers of the courts," when, in fact, they are representatives of clients appearing for them by permission of the court in individual cases.

The Legal Profession in England 

In the English system anyone of character, with a high school education, can become a solicitor by entering upon an apprenticeship of five years in the office of a solicitor, and later proving his fitness to enter that rank. 

The office of barrister, however, is a closed profession to a poor man in England. That great English statesman, former Chancellor of the Exchequer, Philip Snowden, was unable to achieve his ambition to become a barrister because of the poverty of his father who found the expense too great to overcome. For to be a barrister, one must belong to the leisure class; in other words, he must be a "gentleman". He must have a university education, become a member of one of the Inns of Court, and submit to examinations conducted by the members of his Inn, for all of which he pays large fees, the expense running into thousands of dollars. Meantime, he must abstain from any gainful occupation, literature alone excepted. 

This is the ideal of a Bar entertained by some members of the profession in the United States whose lines have fallen into pleasant places!

The Legal Profession in the United States 

In our colonial days, there being no Inns of Court, permission to practice law was granted in most colonies by the courts. In Massachusetts and New York, lawyers had what today might be called "unions", which restricted the number of applicants to those who could afford to pay to enter their offices and study law and thus secure sponsors for their admission. Many colonial lawyers derived a large part of their income from students in their offices. 

After the colonies became States, admission to the Bar in most of them was entrusted by the constitution to the legislatures, which in many cases delegated this authority to the Supreme Court of the State. In some States, the constitution was silent on the subject, and the courts claimed they had inherent power to admit to practice on the

ground that lawyers were "officers of the court" and that the court was the best judge of the qualifications needed for the practice of law. There are fallacies in this theory as well as practical objections to it based on the American ideal of government. That ideal contemplates a threefold division of power and authority, each constituting a check on or a balance against each of the other two.* 

Admission to the Bar in the United States In every State having laws on the subjects the question of who shall practice medicine, dentistry, accounting, and other professions, is determined by the legislature, a majority of whose members are generally lawyers, not particularly conversant with these professions. Nevertheless, this system has worked; and if an injustice is done to any class of citizens, the judicial department stands ready to correct any constitutional mistake on the part of the legislature. 

But when the judicial department assumes authority unconstitutionally, there is no power of government to overrule it. Therefore, where the constitution of a State does not confer upon the courts authority to regulate admission to the Bar, wisdom and the genius of our system of government would seem to demand that the power should be exercised by the legislative branch of the government, which is always subject to correction for abuse of its power by the Supreme Court of the State. 

As a matter of fact courts themselves do not any longer exercise their power of admission directly, but delegate to Boards of Law Examiners the function of examining candidates for the bar. Where such examinations are open to all citizens of the State, little serious objection can be made thereto.

But when a court overrides its constitution and usurps such power, or having it constitutionally, practically abdicates the power and instead of prescribing its own rules for Bar Examinations and admissions to the Bar, in effect delegates to outside agencies the determination of who shall and who shall not practice law, then it is time to denounce and to oppose, even politically, if need be, the promoters and the upholders of this arbitrary and un- American method. Study of Law in Early Days 

Before the Civil War the majority of those admitted to the Bar studied law in law offices and learned practice in watching the trial of cases. Blackstone's Commentaries, published in England just previous to the Revolutionary War, was the lawyer's Bible and was the basis and frequently the whole course of legal instruction. The late 

Judge Cooley of Michigan, a great judge, law school professor and legal author, recommended to the lawyers of his day the reading and re-reading of Blackstone yearly.

Rise of Law Schools 

Law was first taught systematically in the College of William and Mary in Virginia before the Revolutionary War, by the scholarly lawyer, Wythe, one of the framers of the Constitution. Later a law school was established in Connecticut, the Litchfield Law School, famous for many years because of its two illustrious teachers, Reeve and Gould, the latter the author of a work on Common Law Pleading still used in law schools today. Its distinguished *"Unjust Standards for Law Practice." 11

roll of students, among them. Aaron Burr, John C. Calhoun, Horace Mann, great pioneer educator, Judge Metcalf, noted legal author; George Mason, Amasa Parker, founder of the Albany Law School, and Edward King, founder of the Cincinnati Law School, has been surpassed by only one other school since, the Cumberland Law School, at Lebanon, Tennessee. The alumni list of this humble school includes more Justices of the Supreme Court of the United States, judges of State Courts, Senators and Representatives in Congress, Cabinet Officers, Governors, members of State Legislatures and lawyers of celebrity than any other law school in the country. 

Most of the law schools in the early history of the United States were "proprietary" schools. established by lawyers and judges with a desire to disseminate legal knowledge and to supply or augment their income. Sometimes they maintained a nominal connection with a college. 

Later, for various reasons, including financial, colleges like Harvard, Yale and Columbia "grafted" on to their institutions schools of law, as the law school and its students had little more than a nominal connection with the college, as continues true to-day.

College Law Schools Prior to 1890 

Speaking of college law schools prior to 1890, Dr. Reed in his historic volume on "Training For the Public Profession of the Law"* says: "The colleges did not command sufficient financial resources to warrant them in offering instruction that few or no students would take. 

They were obliged, accordingly, to so organize their law schools as to appeal to the greatest possible number. In the first place they had to meet the wishes of those who would not come to the school if required to do more than the irreducible minimum of work demanded by the State. 

No vexatious obstacles must be thrown in the path of possible students. Entrance requirements were accordingly scrupulously avoided."

Yet, he adds, they granted law degrees at the end of a one or two years course!

Day Schools Oppose Evening Schools 

These schools outnumbered the day law schools in attendance, and the latter formed an association known as the "Association of American Law Schools," composed of endowed and University State Law Schools, which started a crusade for the extermination of evening law schools. 

The successive steps may be briefly traced in its annual meetings, as reported in its "Handbooks" for 1915-1920. 

In 1915 William Draper Lewis, former Dean of the University of Pennsylvania Law School, in discussing the annual address of the president of the Association, declared: "The suggestion of the president that the time. has now come to substitute for the Legal Section of The American Bar Association this Association and to identify ourselves as an integral part of the 

American Bar Association, was a most excellent one."

In 1916 in a discussion of a resolution hostile to evening law schools, the secretary of the Association, Eugene A. 

Gilmore, then a University of Wisconsin Law School professor, now president of the University of Iowa (both institutions supported by public taxation) used this cold blooded language: "The resolution, fairly interpreted, strikes at night law school instruction, or instruction in law at night. Not that a man cannot learn things after the sun goes down, but the night school as an educational institution is patronized by the man whose time is substantially occupied with other things. 

He is during the day earning a living. For that reason we struck at a type of institution, or a school, which does supply the needs of the man 13

who, while doing other things and spending the major part of his energy in other fields, incidentally and collaterally fits himself to the bar. We have plenty of lawyers, and we do not need to sit up nights devising ways for poor and worthy individuals to get to the bar."

In 1919 the Association passed a resolution that no law school should be admitted to it if it conducted classes for evening law students preparing for the bar. Dean Vance, of the University of Minnesota Law School, at that meeting declared: "We have got to connect up and connect up closely with the American Bar Association. We have got a job to do, if we want to accomplish the purpose set for ourselves; we have got to go to the 

American Bar Association meetings.****** 

We have got to recognize facts that we were beaten on the last contest, and we have got to do better the next time." 

And Dean Alberts, of Boston University Law School, declared at the same meeting: "If half of you had been present [the preceding year] at the Section of Legal Education you could have passed any vote you wanted to. I urge you to go, gentlemen." 99 And go they did-to a special summer meeting of their Association held in 1920-fifty-seven of them, for the purpose of packing the meeting of the Section of Legal Education of the American Bar Association to be held there at the same time. The present writer attended the meeting, the proceedings of which were never published. 

This purpose was admitted by Dean Lewis of the University of Pennsylvania Law School, when he explained to the delegates of the Law School Association that it was necessary to have this summer meeting (the regular meeting was always held in December in Chicago) in order that they might be there to attend the meeting of the Section of Legal Education, and have their expenses paid by their schools. And when the Section of Legal Education met, these paid delegates captured the meeting, elected their own men as officers and created a special committee on legal education, of which committee Dean Lewis was the working member. 14

In December, 1920, at the annual meeting of the Association of American Law Schools, in Chicago, Professor Lewis said: "That we succeeded in affecting the organization of the Section of Legal Education of the American 

Bar Association through our general influence and representation is understood."

In 1921 at Cincinnati, this committee reported a set of recommendations on legal education to the Section of Legal Education, still controlled by the same element, and the report was adopted. The following day it was presented to the annual meeting of the American Bar Association, held in a hotel dining room which contained less than eight hundred members out of the fifteen thousand members at that time composing the American Bar Association. After some discussion it was adopted by a viva voce vote.

This report recommended that every applicant for the Bar should have a high school education and two years of college work in a college, and be a graduate of a law school requiring for admission two years of work in college, thus preventing any young man or woman from making up the equivalent work by outside study, no matter how thorough and excellent it might be.

These requirements would have barred from admission in the past many of our present great lawyers, judges, and law teachers, including Dean Pound of Harvard, who had only one year of law school education. 

The object of the promoters of the recommendations was to give the Association of American Law Schools, as far as possible, a monopoly of legal education and to close the legal profession to the rank and file of youth. No referendum of the fifteen thousand members of the American Bar Association not present at the meeting was ever taken on these recommendations.

That this was the object became clear in 1927 when the Section of Legal Education undertook to classify the law schools of the country into approved and unapproved schools. It selected as its "adviser" and investigator the secretary (later president) of the American Law School Association at a salary of $10,000 a year (paid not by the "Association of American Law Schools," whose work he was doing but by the American Bar Association) to lobby with courts and legislatures for the adoption in all the States of these so-called recommendations of the American 15

Bar Association, which were passed in a meeting of less than eight hundred members. Since that time there has been practically an interlocking_directorate between the 

"Association of American Law Schools" and the Section of Legal Education of the American Bar Association. 

No official denial of these facts has even been made, although fourteen years after their happening, a president of the Association of American Law Schools in his annual address has attempted to extenuate them.* 

They are printed here not as illustrations of unethical conduct, but for the information of prospective law students who are likely to be confused and misled by published statements carried by law school catalogs that such schools have been approved by the American Bar Association instead of by a meeting of less than eight hundred casual members.

How much more American and democratic is the attitude displayed by Mr. Justice Ethridge of the Supreme Court of 

Mississippi when he says:** "Every condition and class of people should have as far as it is possible to do so, a lawyer who has that class sympathy. In no other way can the very truth in all cases be made to appear. If we limit lawyers to classes who have the wealth to attend the full course in college, and who alone can become lawyers, we will lose this class understanding and sympathy. It is important that we maintain it." 

And again: "Another very important reason for not limiting lawyers to the wealthy class is that the judges who preside over the courts and interpret the law in the courts must be selected from the lawyers, and if only one class of society is represented on the bench we will have a one sided view and an unjust set of rules for the trying of issues both of law and of fact. * 

Let the fields of opportunity stay open and let those who can succeed."

Illinois Rejects a Legal Hierarchy 

Illinois, with other states, has stood like adamant against monopoly in legal education and against control of legal requirements for admission to the Bar by outside, irresponsible agencies. How could it consistently have done otherwise, recalling such names and their lack of collegiate training, in the past, as Lincoln, Douglas, David Davis, and 

Lyman Trumbull, and in our day, Frank J. Loesch, George 

T. Buckingham, Henry I. Green, and Silas H. Strawn, former President of the American Bar Association, leaders at the Bar and in public service. In the absence of any constitutional provision on the subject, the Supreme Court of Illinois prescribes the rules governing admission. These rules provide that every applicant for an examination must produce evidence that he has had: (1) A four-year high school education or its equivalent; (2) years of college work or its equivalent; (3) a law course covering three school years in a day law school or four years in an evening school, accredited by the State Board of Law Examiners, or four years of legal study in a law office. By "equivalent" is meant that the pre-legal work, both high school and college, may be made up outside of any school or college, and credit obtained by passing special examinations therein conducted by the University of Illinois. 

Criticism of the Legal Profession 

Late attempts to restrict and dominate legal education have come at a time when it is unfortunately true that there is much just criticism of lawyers on the charge that they have deserted the Temple of Justice for that of Mammon. Anciently the law, like medicine and religion, was uncommercialized, and the ministration of the lawyer, like that of the clergyman and of the physician, was distinctly personal. He was the guardian and champion of the rights of other men; their adviser, counsellor, and before the Bar of Justice, their advocate and defender. In his breast were locked secrets, business and domestic, to be shared with none. The idea of a partnership with another lawyer would have been repellent, implying incompetency or inability 18

to stand alone. It would mean to commercialize the law and to lower him from the high plane of a member of a profession to the level of a trader. Service not only private, but public, was the badge of the American lawyer in the past. Today the practice of law, especially in the large cities, has become a business. We find Hydra-headed firms, some employing on permanent salaries forty or fifty of their less fortunate brethren and a small army of investigators, stenographers, typists, secretaries, bookkeepers, collectors, switchboard operators and office boys, with a code name, and broadcasting connections. Some of these firms are interurban, New York, Chicago and Washington being a favorite combination.*

There is danger that such lawyers cease to be students of the law and custodians of its ethical standards and become legal engineers, financial promoters, lobbyists, exploiters of their brethren in their employ and ruthless competitors of their brethren outside their employ; that they are forced, in order to meet their overhead, to solicit business by the year at wholesale rates, to monopolize the practice of the law, and by their very size and seeming importance to sway courts and judges and juries. In many cases they are mere satellites of great corporations, indifferent to the public interests as recent Congressional investigations have revealed. What this system has brought about is dramatically described by Professor Karl N. 

Llewellyn, of Columbia University Law School, in an article entitled "The Bar Specializes-With What Re sults?"** 

The rank and file of the profession, however, are still found practicing law alone or in such small partnerships as insure personal attention to clients' business. And it can be said, without fear of successful contradiction, that no profession maintains a higher standard of ethics and proves more trustworthy to the interests committed to it by clients than does the legal profession. 

Much of current criticism can be traced back to the propaganda begun in 1920 and 1921 by the proponents of undemocratic standards of legal education who in order to camouflage their drive to secure control of legal education did not hesitate to besmirch their own profession as a whole. To them the end seemed to justify the means.

Adequate Preparation for Study of Law 

Every intelligent person realizes that special preparation is needed for the successful study of the law, that certain subjects form a better introduction than others, and that what might have been adequate preparation years ago is inadequate today when, "Modern commercial conditions have resulted in almost a complete change in our lives, habits, and modes of thought. The political, economical, social, and legal conditions which confront us are of immeasurable importance, and profoundly difficult to solve. To comprehend them, they at least require a knowledge of the structural elements of civil society, and of the fundamental principles of the different kinds of government. * * * The tui tion of the lawyer should therefore keep pace with the progress of the age.' ""* An inadequate preparation is often a permanent handicap to a successful legal career. This fact should never be lost sight of by the student whose ambition is to become an all-around good lawyer. If the prospective law student can secure a college training or the equivalent, he should. do so, and he should pay special attention to studies bearing upon the origin and development of human society, such as anthropology, sociology, political, financial and industrial history, and mental and moral sciences, like logic, psychology and ethics. If unable to attend college, he should endeavor to pursue this line of study wherever possible. He should not be satisfied with a high school course, which is too eclectic to be a preparation for any particular profession, and is ordinarily completed when a student is sixteen or seventeen years of age.

The Illinois Supreme Court has recognized this fact in its rules and has also recognized the further fact that there is no royal road to learning. The law is not so easy a subject that it can be mastered by an immature, undisciplined, and uninformed mind. "Reading law," to use the old expression, is not studying law, in the modern sense. 

The fact that the law is printed in the English language is not an assurance that mere knowledge of the language is sufficient to comprehend and apply it today.

The Law as a Public Profession 

In all ages and in all countries the law as a profession has ever attracted the youth of intelligence, ability and public ambition. This has been particularly true in our own country from the days of the American Revolution to the present.

As was once said by John N. Jewett, a former Dean of 

The John Marshall Law School and one of the great lawyers of his day: "In its all-pervading influence, the law is like the air we breathe. It is as necessary to the continuous enjoyment of our social and civil life as the atmospheric air is to our physical well being."

This is particularly true in the United States where the people are responsible for their laws. 

Hence the leaders of our political life, our legislators and statesmen have come chiefly from the ranks of lawyers. 

Of 56 signers of the Declaration of Independence 25 were lawyers; of the 55 men who drafted the Constitution of the United States 30 were lawyers. Of 32 Presidents of the United States, 25 have been lawyers. And of cabinet officers, members of the Congress, Governors of States and members of legislatures, the larger percentage has been lawyers. So with the judges, Federal, State and municipal -they have been, with very rare exceptions, lawyers. 

Indeed, the profession of law is a necessity to modern society. With the development and extension of constitutional government, the lawyer must remain the chief political factor in our Nation and States. And statistics prove this to be so to-day. 21

The Lawyer and Business Frederick J. Allen, of the Bureau of Vocational Guidance of Harvard University, in his excellent treatise on "The 

Law as a Vocation," observes: "With the gradual evolution of society, and the development of the complex system of law in our modern civilization, the profession of the law has broadened into various and clearly defined fields of service. Thus the lawyer of today may choose fields of activity that were not open to his predecessors. Court practice, while still a conspicuous part of his duty, has given way in large degree to many other kinds of service, ranging from the writing of legal papers to acting as counselor for corporations.' 

And he enumerates twenty-seven different activities of a legal nature that may occupy the time of a lawyer today. 

No longer is the lawyer to be found solely in his office and in the court room, but as counselor and legal guide he may head vast business interests or public service enterprises. 

New fields and opportunities are steadily opening to him. 

With this enlargement of activities, specialization in many branches of the law has become common in great cities. 

So that while the general practitioner is still to be found there, specialists abound in criminal, real estate, patent, corporation and income tax law. As judges, masters-inchancery, referees in bankruptcy, public prosecutors, public administrators, justices of the peace, and clerks of courts, a greater variety of choice is offered in the legal profession than in any other of the learned professions.

Place of Legal Training 

Where and when then should one study law? Naturally in the best school within his reach and means. In the daytime if he can, devoting all his time to it. If he cannot attend day school, there is no intrinsic reason why he should not pursue successfully a course of study in an evening school. Greater ambition, pluck and perseverence may be demanded in this case, but the thing can be done, and well done if the right school be selected. Obviously there are differences in evening schools as well as in day schools, and the same care must be taken to make the right choice. 

Faculty, method of instruction, standard and volume of work required, success in meeting bar examinations, morale of student body, library facilities are all to be considered. 

The late Charles F. Carusi, Dean of the National Uni versity Law School of Washington and President of the 

Board of Education of that city, said of evening schools and their student body: "The essential characteristic of the night school, that which primarily distinguishes such a one from the typical day school, is the night school student body. This student body is uniform in but two particulars, one is that the men devote the business hours of the day to gainful employment, the other that they seem uniformly inspired by an ambition to gain a knowledge of the law in spite of every handicap and difficulty.*

That evening law schools and evening law school students compare most favorably with the day schools and their students is the deliberate judgment of those in a position to know. 

Professor William F. Walsh, author of "Outlines of the 

History of English and American Law," states: "Since the beginning of my work as instructor in the New York University Law School in 1902,

I have been meeting the classes of the evening division at least two evenings every week. For

Such schools have a distinct function to perform in teaching the law as others have made it, in place of making over the law themselves."

Obviously, however, the large city is best for the student who has to earn his own living, and the evening school furnishes him the opportunity of learning. The law is both a science and an art, and given a place of study, the next important thing is to go where the art flourishes. A large city affords this field, with its numerous civil and criminal courts, and with an ever changing variety of cases, conducted by able and distinguished lawyers, before learned and distinguished judges.

That a course of legal study may be successfully pursued in a large city in the evening by youth of more than ordinary energy and determination is proved by the number of prominent men in the profession and on the bench and in the legislative halls of the Nation and of the States who pursued their studies in evening law schools while following some gainful occupation by day. 

Public Service of Evening Law Schools 

The service to the profession and to the public rendered by evening schools is fully acknowledged by Dr. Reed, of the Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching. In a comparison of the growth in numbers and of attendance in day and evening law schools, to the advantage of the latter, he says: "Now, let there be no misunderstanding as to the significance of this development. Broadly considered, with reference to what can ultimately be made out of these part-time schools, the phenomenon is a healthy and a desirable one. Humanitarian and political consideration unite in leading us to approve of efforts to widen the circle of those who are able to study law. The organization of educational machinery especially designed to abolish economic handicaps-intended to place the poor boy, so far as possible, on an equal footing with the rich-constitutes one of America's fundamental ideals. It is particularly important that the opportunity to exercise an essentially governmental function should be open to the mass of our citizens. Undoubtedly, there are many ways of attempting to realize this ideal, and some of these ways are bad ways, that defeat their own end. "Inherently, however, the night school movement in legal education is sound. It provides a 26

necessary corrective to the monopolistic tendencies that are likely to appear in every professional class -tendencies that in some professions may be ignored, but that in a profession connected with politics constitute a genuine element of danger. 

A decidedly intolerant attitude toward any sort of night law school training is sometimes displayed by those who have received their education in other ways. When this attitude does not reflect merely a failure to grasp the necessary implications of a democratic form of government, it is in itself an indication of how badly these schools are needed."* 

Method of Instruction 

The method of instruction is a very important matter for a prospective law student to consider. In the main two methods of instruction have been used in law school teaching the lecture, text-book and illustrative case system, and, second, the exclusive leading case system, sometimes called the Harvard or the Langdell system from the name of the man who introduced it at Harvard.

The Leading Case or Harvard System 

The so-called case system of studying law was introduced in the Harvard Law School, in 1870, by Christopher Columbus Langdell, its original protagonist, an old-time lawyer called from his New York office to Cambridge by that pioneer in modern education, the late President Eliot. 

The system has now been in vogue in Harvard and its principal imitators for more than a half century.

Many generations of lawyers, counting a law school generation as three years, have been trained under that system. 

Who will maintain that this system has produced as great lawyers and as profound jurists as were developed in this country under the old text-book and illustrative case system carried on in offices or in such remarkable law schools, among others, as William and Mary, the Litchfield Law

School in Connecticut, and the Cumberland University Law 

School in Lebanon, Tennessee?

But the case system now taught is no longer the simonpure Langdell or Harvard system at all. It is a counterfeit presentment or dilution thereof. Langdell believed that the proper way to acquire a knowledge of law was to study the evolution of the law in each of its branches, beginning with the earliest case that could be found on a subject. It was a logical and historical system and is certainly one way, if a long way, to reach a knowledge of the law. 

But his books, even at Harvard, have long since been thrown into the discard. His pupils who became teachers deserted the old master and, with other pupils who have taken possession of the day law schools of the country as professional teachers of law, have published their own case books, now running into hundreds. These new case books, 28

are not constructed on the Langdell principle-leading cases in the historical development of a subject. For obviously there cannot be two sets of leading cases on the same subject; consequently the case books of the pupils are little more than collections of illustrative cases. 

The latest casebooks compiled by teachers eminent in the casebook world, even Harvard Law School professors, illustrate the new departure, the favorite title of these new books being Cases and Materials on the subject of the volume, the materials being copious introductions, comments, citations, and notes illustrative of the cases given in the volume. This is really an approach to a text and case system of instruction and is a complete abandonment and burial of the Langdell case system.

Moreover, the case system as the sole road to the law has been attacked in recent years by eminent lawyers, law teachers, and legal scholars. Jerome N. Frank, educated in a case law school, Research Associate Yale Law School, and former chief counsel of the Agricultural Adjustment Administration, now chairman of the Securities Exchange Commission in an address on "What Constitutes a Good 

Legal Education?" to the Section of Legal Education in 

1933, said: "The law student should learn, while in school, the art of legal practice. And to that end, the law schools should bodily, not shyly and evasively, repudiate the false dogmas of Langdell. They must say 'Yes' to his 'No'. They must decide not to exclude, as did Langdell, but to include the 'methods of learning law by work in the lawyer's office and attendance upon the proceedings of courts and justice.' They must right-about-face, and say, 'What qualifies a person to teach law is experience in the work of a lawyer's office, in dealing with men, in the trial and argument of cases.' 

They must repudiate the absurd notions that the heart of a law school is its library, that what distinguishes one law school from another is the number and kind of books on its library shelves, that the library is the lawyer's laboratory, that the 'living founts' for the lawyers are to be found in inert paper covered with printer's ink. They must learn to see that libraries and books are on the outer edge of matters lawyer-like, and that at the center is the conduct of human beings-clients, witnesses, judges, juries, legislative committees, 29

stock market manipulators, labor leaders, presidents of corporations, farmers, and other lawyers. 

Who Should Aspire to Be Lawyers 

Who should study law to become lawyers? Not the mercenary young man nor the young man who likes to talk and mistake a spirit of contentiousness for argumentative skill, nor he whose parents are at a loss what else he can study after leaving college, and prefer for him a professional to a business career. Positive qualifications are needed in the law. "Our common law is a glorious inheritance. Its principles and history give a renewing power of adaptation to changing circumstances. The task of the nineteenth century was the definition and the spread of political liberty; that of the twentieth is the definition and the spread of political justice."** 

The late Joseph H. Choate, a leader of the American 

Bar, a man of international fame, declared the seat of a lawyer's success to be the stomach. Next to health there must be resolute ambition, ready and eager to comply with all conditions that promise success; hard work, concentration, and enthusiasm; above all, integrity,-not meaning thereby mere absence of the criminal mind, but intellectual honesty, hospitality to truth, even when opposed to one's immediate interest. Justice being the goal of law, right thinking alone can reach it. Moreover, the student who intends to enter the profession must never forget that the law is a life investment, and, like any other investment, calls for the fullest investigation, prudence and judgment,. if a heavy loss of time and effort and years of experimentation is to be avoided. To those who approach the bar with mercenary motives, without fit preparation, the law, like the ancient sybil, commands: "Be at a distance, profane ones." Properly entered upon, however, the law will prove an investment of the highest and richest returns-if not financial, certainly always intellectual, social and moral.

The Law and the Public 

The importance of the law and of lawyers to organized society today is the same as it was in the time of the great preacher and writer of Colonial days, Cotton Mather, whose address to lawyers, delivered in 1710, is as applicable now as it was when delivered: "Gentlemen. Your opportunities to do good are such and so liberal and gentlemanly is your education *** that proposals of what you may do cannot but promise themselves an obliging reception with you. 'Tis not come to so sad a pass that an honest lawyer may, as of old the honest publican, require a statue merely on the score of rarity. "A lawyer should be a scholar, but, sirs, when you are called upon to be wise, the main intention is that you may be wise to do good. * * * A lawyer that is a knave deserves death, more than a band of robbers; for he profanes the sanctuary of the distressed and betrays the liberties of the people. To ward off such a censure, a lawyer must shun all those indirect ways of making haste to be rich, in which a man cannot be innocent; such ways as provoked the father of Sir Matthew Hale to give over the practice of the law because of the extreme difficulty to preserve a good conscience in it. "Sirs, be prevailed withal to keep constantly a court of Chancery in your own breast. * * * You will abhor, sirs, to appear in a dirty cause. If you discern that your client has an unjust cause, you 31

will faithfully advise him of it. You will be sincerely desirous that truth and justice may take place. You will speak nothing which shall be to the prejudice of either. You will abominate the use of all unfair arts to confound evidence, to browbeat testimonies, to suppress what may give light in the case. *** "There has been an old complaint that a good lawyer seldom is a good neighbor. You know how to confute it, gentlemen, by making your skill in the law a blessing to your neighborhood. You may, gentlemen, if you please, be a vast accession to the felicity of your country. *** Perhaps you may discover many things yet wanting in the law; mischiefs in the execution and application of the laws which ought to be better provided against; mischiefs annoying of mankind against which no laws are yet provided. the reformation of the law, and more law for the reformation of the world is what is mightily called for."

Old Time Advice to a Student of Law Good To-day 

John Adams, second President of the United States, says that when as an applicant for admission to the Bar he asked advice of a noted lawyer of the day, Attorney-General 

Gridley, he was told: "I have a few pieces of advice to give you, Mr. Adams, said Gridley. One is, to pursue the study of the law, rather than the gain of it; pursue the gain of it enough to keep out of it, enough to keep out of the briers, but give your main attention to the study of it. The next is, not to marry early; for an early marriage will obstruct your improvement; and, in the next place, it will involve you in expense. 

Another thing is, not to keep much company, for the application of a man who aims to be a lawyer must be incessant; his attention to his books must be constant, which is inconsistent with keeping much company. In the study of the law, the Common Law, be sure, deserves your first and last attention."

The Ideal Lawyer 

"The old-fashioned American lawyer, much like the family doctor, was provided by the nature of his work with. tolerable roundness. He worked in the court more than in the office. He engaged in criminal trials and understood them. His cases brought him, on the civil side, rich and 32

poor alike. His interests rounded out. In the measure that his experience was balanced, his influence tended toward balance. In the measure that his own work was varied, he knew and noted varied phases of the work of his profession. In the measure that he met occasion-toknow, he met also occasion-to-do."*

Such a lawyer was Leonard Swett, an associate of Abraham Lincoln, a towering figure at the Bar of his day, who wrote to a young man contemplating the study of law, the following letter: 

Dear Sir: 

Chicago, Illinois, September 13, 1886. 

Your letter of August 29th did not reach me until today owing to my absence in the East. I find, therefore, I am too late to reply before you will probably leave for your college, but I send this in care of your father, with the request that he forward it to you.

I regard the practice of law as the very best profession there is, and I naturally incline to advise everybody to enter it. So much for my prejudices. Any young man entering upon a course of education, should think for himself, what his inclinations lead him to. If he trusts to and follows his own instincts on the subject, he will be likely to decide the question of his occupation rightly. I would not decide this question, if I were you, absolutely, if you are going through college, until you get through, for I know no reason why the college course does not fit a young man for one profession as well as another. 

There is room enough in the law profession at the top. 

There it is not crowded. I count on the fingers of both hands, the names of all the lawyers out of twelve hundred in this city, who are thoroughly capable of taking a difficult case and managing it to the end, cautiously, prudently, and successfully. If, in looking at the legal profession, it suits your tastes and inclinations, you had better adopt it. 

I have made and turned out a good many fine lawyers in my life, and I should be happy to take you into my office to see if you cannot begin at the bottom and work up to the top. 

There is nothing in the profession requiring any especial talent, but it does require industry, careful observance of the details of business, accuracy, and success. If you should come to my office, I should set you down at a desk
