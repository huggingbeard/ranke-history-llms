PREFACE 

These books have been designed to present in a systematized fashion the basic and essential principles on which the law is founded. In a work of this nature it is not possible to carry these principles into great detail, but it must be noted in this connection that detail often tends toward confusion rather than to clarification. It is believed that the material is sufficiently adequate to provide the graduate with a review of the fundamental rules he has previously studied and to supply the student with the means of gaining a clearer conception of the law in conjunction with his casebook study. 

The author has throughout relied upon authority, supporting his statements by citations to case, leading textbooks, encyclopedias, and dictionaries. In particular, this work cites William M. Fletcher's 

Cyclopedia of the Law of Private Corporations, (Rev. and Perm. Ed., 

1931), Ballantine's California Corporation Laws (1932), Clark and Marshall, Treatise on the Law of Private Corporations (1901), Bal lantine's Manual of Corporation Law and Practice (1930), and Corpus 

Juris. These references serve not only as a justification for the principles enunciated, but, in addition, point the way to a more detailed study.

The second part of each volume is a compilation of bar examination questions selected from the several jurisdictions throughout the United States. The questions were thoroughly culled and only the best have been included. As the valuable practice of applying law to facts receives little attention in law schools, it is believed that both students and lawyers alike will receive much benefit from solving these problems.

The thanks of the author are tendered to his many friends in the legal profession who have given valuable help and suggestions. In particular he desires to express his appreciation to Mr. Louis Naiditch of the California Bar and to Mr. Ralph J. Eastgate of the North Dakota Bar.

I. 

DEFINITION 

A CORPORATION may consist of a single person, but it usually consists of an association of individuals formed under the sanction of the state for a distinct and definite purpose. It is a "body, consisting of one or more natural persons, established by law, usually for some specific purpose, and continued by a succession of members."1 Or, "a corporation is an intellectual body, created by law, composed of individuals united under a common name, the members of which succeed each other, so that the body continues always the same, notwithstanding the change of the individuals who compose it, and which, for certain purposes, is considered a natural person.'

THE FIRST REAL CORPORATIONS were municipal. When families congregated in towns, then in cities, and when countries and states were formed, the idea of an everlasting body was applied to them. The town, the city or the state, was looked upon as having an existence of its own. The citizens made up the entity, but a distinct legal person existed. No matter how its members changed, by death or otherwise, the entity remained virtually the same.³ 

THE FIRST ENGLISH CORPORATIONS were guilds. A guild is defined as being "a voluntary association of persons pursuing the same trade, art, profession, or business, such as printers, goldsmiths, wool merchants, etc., united under a distinct organization of their own, analogous to that of a corporation, regulating the affairs of their trade or business by their own laws and rules, and aiming, by co-operation and organization, to protect and promote the interests of their common vocation."4 

The guild, itself, did not carry on business, but, through bylaws, governed the members which composed it. 

THE EAST INDIA COMPANY, founded in 1600, was the first association to use joint stock. The need for large sums of money to finance the business was the reason for the joint stock scheme. Individuals subscribed stock to be used for certain adventures, the profits to be distributed in accordance with the amount subscribed. A few years later stock was subscribed for a term of years. 

During the Latter Part of the SeveNTEENTH CENTURY and the beginning of the eighteenth century, joint stock companies became frequent, many of them incorporated and others unincorporated, but the right of the unincorporated to do business was challenged, and many of them were terminated. The panic of the early eighteenth century put an end to many more.

corporation is a matter of public concern, and the state is an important party in its formation. The requisite authorization by the state is based in part upon the following reasons: 

1. Guilds, early corporations, governed their members by bylaws, and naturally, any form of government would be of concern to the chief governing body, the king. 

2. Corporations had given considerable trouble to the country of 

France, and England decided that supervision over them was desirable, to the extent of preventing their formation without the consent of the crown. 

3. Another source of revenue to the crown was seized upon. The consent of the king had to be purchased and paid for.

IN AMERICA, THe Consent of the States is shown either by special act or by general law. CORPORATION COMPARED WITH OTHER BUSINESS ASSOCIATIONS A. COMPARISON OF A CORPORATION WITH A PARTNERSHIP will show the following distinctions:20 

1. A corporation is an entity-a partnership is not. 

2. A corporation requires the sanction of the state-a partnership does not. 

3. A corporation is not affected by the death of a member-a partnership is dissolved. 

4. A corporation may sue a member-a partner must sue for an accounting in equity. 

5. A shareholder has no ownership of the corporate property which is vested in the corporation-a partner is a tenant in the partnership property. 

6. A shareholder has no direct control-a partner has control. 

7. A shareholder is not a manager-a partner may manage. 

8. A shareholder is not an agent-a partner is an agent. 

9. A shareholder is not liable for the debts-a partner is.

I. 

POWERS OF A CORPORATION THE CHARTER of a corporation controls its powers. The term 

"charter" under modern law includes the incorporation papers and the laws under which the corporation was created. Whatever limits exist in the charter, or in statutes or constitutions are binding on the corporation because it exists only by permission of the state.29 It is important to keep in mind the fact that where the word "charter" is used, in books, or in decisions of courts, it really means the articles or certificate of incorporation, and the general laws under which the corporation was created.

CERTAIN INHERENT POWERS attach at the time of creation of the corporation (which is usually upon the filing of the articles) as incidental to it. In the absence of express law or charter restrictions, these powers include the power of perpetual succession; to sue or be sued or to act in its corporate name; to purchase and hold real property; to have a common seal; to make bylaws consistent with its charter for its government, and, in proper cases, to disfranchise or members.80 remove AT COMMON LAW a corporation had the capacity to hold title to real property unless contrary to the purposes for which it was created. RIGHT TO DIVIDENDS THE PROMISE TO PAY DIVIDENDS may be by express contract or it may be implied from the fact that the purpose of the corporation is to make a profit and distribute it to the shareholders.¹ An example of an express contractual promise is the promise which is generally made to holders of preferred stock that their stock will entitle them to a certain per cent per annum on their investment, or at least before any payments are made to holders of common stock who are not guaranteed any particular return.

THE RIGHT TO DIVIDENDS does not vest until the corporation, through its directors, has declared the dividends.2 The declaration is usually required to be some formal action by the directors entered on the corporate records, or an actual setting apart of funds for the purpose. Once made, the declaration is generally held to be irrevocable. But if the directors merely agree among themselves that there will be a dividend declared, without any formal action, it may be rescinded. When the declaration is made a new chose in action does not arise, but it is merely the performance of the promise which the corporation made when the shareholder became a member. This is the basis for holding that the dividend does not pass to a transferee of the stock unless expressly assigned.

PART II. 

CREDITORS 

THE CREDITOR AND THE CORPORATION 

GENERALLY SPEAKING, the relationship between the creditor and the corporation is no different than the relationship between the ordinary creditor and debtor. The creditors of a corporation may, as against a natural person, obtain judgment and proceed in attachment against the corporate assets. At common law an exception is made, the franchises of a public service corporation, and property that is necessary to enable it to exercise its franchises, are not subject to execution. The creditor may attack fraudulent conveyances, and any other acts which are clearly injurious or a fraud on the creditors. But ordinarily, where the corporation remains a solvent going concern, the acts of the corporation are of no concern to the creditor.18 

THE TRUST FUND DOCTRINE, is the theory that all of the capital' stock, or assets representing the capital stock, of a corporation, imme. diately upon its becoming insolvent, are treated as a trust fund for the benefit, first of the creditors and then of the stockholders.14 The direc tors very often are the trustees in this situation but more often the receiver, or trustee in bankruptcy gets the assets for equitable distribution to innocent corporate creditors. By a suit in equity the creditors may compel the stockholders to answer for all unpaid subscriptions, and especially is this true in the case of "watered" stock. Where assets have been wrongfully distributed, or dividends wrongfully paid, they may be recovered by a creditors' suit. One of the main results of this doctrine is to forestall preferences of one creditor over another. 

THE THEORY was originated by Mr. Justice Story in the case of

The individual stockholders are not liable for the debts of the bank in their private capacities. The charter relieves them from personal responsibility and substitutes the capital stock in its stead. 

Credit is universally given to this fund by the public, as the only means of repayment. During the existence of the corporation it is the sole property of the corporation, and can be applied only according to its charter, that is, as a fund for the payment of its debts, upon the security of which it may discount and circulate notes. Why, otherwise, is any capital stock required by our charters? If the stock may, the next day after it is paid in, be withdrawn by the stockholders without payment of the debts of the corporation, why is its amount so studiously provided for, and its payment by the stockholders so diligently required? To me this point appears so plain upon principles of law, as well as common sense, that I cannot be brought into any doubt, that the charters of our banks make the capital stock a trust fund for the payment of all the debts of the corporation. The bill holders and other creditors have the first claims upon it, and the stockholders have no rights, until all the other creditors are satisfied. The doctrine of following trust funds into the hands of any persons, who are not innocent purchasers, or do not otherwise possess superior equities, has long been established."

SUCCESSION OF CORPORATIONS 

DIRECT PURCHASE AND TRANSFER 

SUCCESSION, in general, refers to all situations in which one corporation succeeds to the business and property of another corporation by a direct purchase and transfer, or by purchase of the same at a sale under execution, or under foreclosure of a mortgage, or other judicial sale, or by consolidation or merger." 1 

WHERE SUCCESSION IS BY DIRECT PURCHASE AND TRANSFER, neither the succeeding corporation nor its property is liable for the debts or other liabilities of the old corporation, except: 

1. Where there is an express agreement to pay or assume the liabilities, or it is imposed by statute; 

2. Where there are facts showing an implied agreement to pay them; 

3. Where the transfer would be a fraud on the creditors of the old corporation; 

4. Where the corporation is a mere continuance of the old corporation.

AS A GENERAL RULE, the creditors of the old corporation may fol. low its assets by bringing suit in equity, though they may be prohibited from suing at law.2

CONSOLIDATION 

A CONSOLIDATION, in its true sense, occurs when two or more corporations, by agreement, unite to form a new corporation, transferring all of their property and assets to it. 

THERE MUST BE CONSENT by the holders of a majority, usually two. thirds of the issued and outstanding shares of stock. Where the required number have consented to the proceedings, but there are still some who do not wish to take part, they are generally allowed compensation for their holdings, at the fair market value. If an unanimous consent is required, a dissenting stockholder may have compensation* or sue to enjoin the consolidation. Fraud, or lack of good faith may also be grounds for injunction by dissenting stockholders. 

THE CREDITORS of the old corporations are protected. In most cases a statute gives them the same rights against the new corporation as they had against the old. Where not so protected, the creditors may follow the assets of the old corporation and recover their debt by a suit in equity.

THE STOCKHOLDERS of the old corporations are generally the stockholders of the new, although they need not be."

FOREIGN CORPORATIONS I. 

IN GENERAL 

A FOREIGN CORPORATION is a corporation created by or under the laws of another state, government, or country, as distinct from a domestic corporation.¹

STATUTES generally require foreign corporations to deposit with the secretary of state (or other official) a copy of their articles of incorporation, and statements as to: location and address of its principal home office and its principal office within the state, the name and address of an agent for service of process with its irrevocable consent to such service, and to service on the state official in his stead.2 This procedure is essential to qualify the corporation to do business in the state on a par with similar domestic corporations. 

THE POWER OF THE STATE Over a foreign corporation is based upon the fact that the corporation is "doing business" within its boundaries. This becomes important in three connections. 

1. Service of process; 

2. Taxation; 

3. Qualification, as to registration, filing of articles, license fees, etc.

ADMISSION BY PERMISSION 

PERMISSION to act in another state must be secured by a foreign corporation. This permission may be express or implied, or based upon the doctrine of comity. It may be denied either expressly, by statute, or by implication, where such corporations are contrary to public policy, as betting syndicates, etc.

CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS 

A CORPORATION IS NOT A CITIZEN within the "equal privileges and immunities" clause of the Constitution, except in cases of federal jurisdiction between citizens of the different states. Therefore, a foreign corporation may be excluded or admitted on terms. 

A CORPORATION IS A PERSON within the "equal protection of the laws" clause, after it is admitted to do business.10 It then becomes subject to and is entitled to the protection of the laws of that state as applied to corporations of like character.

UNIFORM STOCK TRANSFER ACT 

[As approved by the Commissioners on Uniform State Laws in 1909] 

Section 1. [How Title to Certificates and Shares may be Transferred.] 

Title to a certificate and to the shares represented thereby can be transferred only, (a) By delivery of the certificate indorsed either in blank or to a specified person by the person appearing by the certificate to be the owner of the shares represented thereby, or (b) By delivery of the certificate and a separate document containing a written assignment of the certificate or a power of attorney to sell, assign, or transfer the same or the shares represented thereby, signed by the person appearing by the certificate to be the owner of the shares represented thereby. Such assignment or power of attorney may be either in blank or to a specified person.

The provisions of this section shall be applicable although the charter or articles of incorporation or code of regulations or by-laws of the corporation issuing the certificate and the certificate itself, provide that the shares represented thereby shall be transferable only on the books of the corporation or shall be registered by a registrar or transferred by a transfer agent. 

Sec. 2. [Powers of Those Lacking Full Legal Capacity and of Fiduciaries not Enlarged.] Nothing in this act shall be construed as enlarging the powers of an infant or other person lacking full legal capacity, or of a trustee, executor or administrator, or other fiduciary, to make a valid indorsement, assignment or power of attorney. 

Sec. 3. [Corporation not Forbidden to Treat Registered Holder as 

Owner.] Nothing in this act shall be construed as forbidding a corporation, (a) To recognize the exclusive right of a person registered on its books as the owner of shares to receive dividends, and to vote as such owner, or (b) To hold liable for calls and assessments a person registered on its books as the owner of shares. 

Sec. 4. [Title Derived from Certificate Extinguishes Title Derived from a 

Separate Document.] The title of a transferee of a certificate under a power of attorney or assignment not written upon the certificate, and the title of any person claiming under such transferee, shall cease and determine if, at any time prior to the surrender of the certificate to the corporation issuing it, another person, for value in good faith, and without notice of the prior transfer, shall purchase and obtain delivery of such certificate with the indorsement of the person appearing by the certificate to be the owner thereof, or shall purchase and obtain delivery of such certificate and the written assignment or power of attorney of such person, though contained in a separate document.

Sec. 5. [Who May Deliver a Certificate.] The delivery of a certificate to transfer title in accordance with the provisions of section 1, is effectual, except as provided in section 7, though made by one having no right of possession and having no authority from the owner of the certificate or from the person purporting to transfer the title. 102

Sec. 6. [Indorsement Effectual in Spite of Fraud, Duress, Mistake, Revocation, Death, Incapacity or Lack of Consideration or Authority.] The indorsement of a certificate by the person appearing by the certificate to be the owner of the shares represented thereby is effectual, except as provided in section 7, though the indorser or transferor. (a) was induced by fraud, duress or mistake, to make the indorsement or delivery, or (b) has revoked the delivery of the certificate, or the authority givin by the indorsement or delivery of the certificate, or (c) has died or become legally incapacitated after the indorsement, whether before or after the delivery of the certificate, or (d) has received no consideration.

Sec. 7. [Rescission of Transfer.] If the indorsement or delivery of a certificate, (a) was procured by fraud or duress, or (b) was made under such mistake as to make the indorsement or delivery inequitable; or

If the delivery of a certificate was made (c) without authority from the owner, or (d) after the owner's death or legal incapacity, the possession of the certificate may be reclaimed and the transfer thereof rescinded, unless: (1) The certificate has been transferred to a purchaser for value in good faith without notice of any facts making the transfer wrongful, or, (2) The injured person has elected to waive the injury, or has been guilty of laches in endeavoring to enforce his rights. 

Any court of appropriate jurisdiction may enforce specifically such right to reclaim the possession of the certificate or to rescind the transfer thereof and, pending litigation, may enjoin the further transfer of the certificate or impound it.

Sec. 8. [Rescission of Transfer of Certificate Does Not Invalidate Sub sequent Transfer by Transferee in Possession.] Although the transfer of a certificate or of shares represented thereby has been rescinded or set aside, nevertheless, if the transferee has possession of the certificate or of a new certificate representing part or the whole of the same shares of stock, a subsequent transfer of such certificate by the transferee, mediately or immediately, to a purchaser for value in good faith, without notice of any facts making the transfer wrongful, shall give such purchaser an indefeasible right to the certificate and the shares represented thereby. 

Sec. 9. [Delivery of Unindorsed Certificate Imposes Obligation to Indorse.] The delivery of a certificate by the person appearing by the certificate to be the owner thereof without the indorsement requisite for the transfer of the certificate and the shares represented thereby, but with intent to transfer such certificate or shares, shall impose an obligation, in the absence of an agreement to the contrary, upon the person so delivering, to complete the transfer by making the necessary indorsement. The transfer shall take effect as of the time when the indorsement is actually made. This obligation may be specifically enforced. 

Sec. 10. [Ineffectual Attempt to Transfer Amounts to a Promise to 

Transfer.] An attempted transfer of title to a certificate or to the shares represented thereby without delivery of the certificate shall have the effect

of a promise to transfer and the obligation, if any, imposed by such promise shall be determined by the law governing the formation and performance of contracts.

Sec. 11. [Warranties on Sale of Certificate.] A person who for value transfers a certificate, including one who assigns for value a claim secured by a certificate, unless a contrary intention appears, warrants- (a) That the certificate is genuine, (b) That he has a legal right to transfer it, and (c) That he has no knowledge of any fact which would impair the validity of the certificate.

In the case of an assignment of a claim secured by a certificate, the liability of the assignor upon such warranty shall not exceed the amount of the claim.

Sec. 12. [No Warranty Implied from Accepting Payment of a Debt.] 

A mortgagee, pledgee, or other holder for security of a certificate who in good faith demands or receives payment of the debt for which such certificate is security, whether from a party to a draft drawn for such debt, or from any other person, shall not by so doing be deemed to represent or to warrant the genuineness of such certificate, or the value of the shares represented thereby.

Sec. 13. [No Attachment or Levy Upon Shares Unless Certificate Sur rendered or Transfer Enjoined.] No attachment or levy upon shares of stock for which a certificate is outstanding shall be valid until such certificate be actually seized by the officer making the attachment or levy, or be surrendered to the corporation which issued it, or its transfer by the holder be enjoined. Except where a certificate is lost or destroyed, such corporation shall not be compelled to issue a new certificate for the stock until the old certificate is surrendered to it. 

Sec. 14. [Creditor's Remedies to Reach Certificate.] A creditor whose debtor is the owner of a certificate shall be entitled to such aid from courts of appropriate jurisdiction, by injunction and otherwise, in attaching such certificate or in satisfying the claim by means thereof as is allowed at law or in equity, in regard to property which cannot readily be attached or levied upon by ordinary legal process. 

Sec. 15. [There Shall Be No Lien or Restriction Unless Indicated on 

Certificate.] There shall be no lien in favor of a corporation upon the shares represented by a certificate issued by such corporation and there shall be no restriction upon the transfer of shares so represented by virtue of any by-laws of such corporation, or otherwise, unless the right of the corporation to such lien or the restriction is stated upon the certificate. 

Sec. 16. [Alteration of Certificate Does Not Divest Title to Shares.] 

The alteration of a certificate, whether fraudulent or not and by whomsoever made, shall not deprive the owner of his title to the certificate and the shares originally represented thereby, and the transfer of such a certificate shall convey to the transferee a good title to such certificate and to the shares originally represented thereby. 

Sec. 17. [Lost or Destroyed Certificate.] Where a certificate has been lost or destroyed, a court of competent jurisdiction may order the issue of a new certificate therefor on service of process upon the corporation and

The court may also in its discretion order the payment of the corporation's reasonable costs and counsel fees. 

The issue of a new certificate under an order of the court as provided in this section, shall not relieve the corporation from liability in damages to a person to whom the original certificate has been or shall be transferred for value without notice of the proceedings or of the issuance of the new certificate.

Sec. 18. [Rule for Cases Not Provided for by This Act.] In any case not provided for by this act, the rules of law and equity, including the law merchant, and in particular the rules relating to the law of principal and agent, executors, administrators and trustees, and to the effect of fraud, misrepresentation, duress or coercion, mistake, bankruptcy, or other invalidating cause, shall govern.

Sec. 19. [Interpretation Shall Give Effect to Purpose of Uniformity.] 

This act shall be so interpreted and construed as to effectuate its general purpose to make uniform the law of those States which enact it. 

Sec. 20. [Definition of Indorsement] A certificate is indorsed when an assignment or a power of attorney to sell, assign, or transfer the certificate or the shares represented thereby is written on the certificate and signed by the person appearing by the certificate to be the owner of the shares represented thereby, or when the signature of such person is written without more upon the back of the certificate. In any of such cases a certificate is indorsed though it has not been delivered. 

Sec. 21. [Definition of Person Appearing to Be the Owner of Certifi cate.] The person to whom a certificate was originally issued is the person appearing by the certificate to be the owner thereof, and of the shares represented thereby, until and unless he indorses the certificate to another specified person, and thereupon such other specified person is the person appearing by the certificate to be the owner thereof until and unless he also indorses the certificate to another specified person. Subsequent special indorsements may be made with like effect.
