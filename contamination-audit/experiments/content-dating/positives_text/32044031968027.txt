Dear Dr. Compton: 

You have asked for an expression of opinion on the legal aspects of a corporate gift in support of higher education. At the outset, it may be pertinent to mention that any questions concerning the legality of such a contribution should, in each instance, be passed upon by counsel for the corporation proposing to make such a gift, taking into account the facts and circumstances pertaining to the particular sit uation. While the Council for Financial Aid to Education, Inc. performs an important pub. lic service in stimulating and encouraging increased financial support of higher education, particularly by corporations, the Council is not empowered by law, or competent, to prac tice law or to provide legal advice to anyone, and, I should say, must carefully refrain from doing so at all times. 

It may be that the following general review of the subject will be helpful to you and your associates at the Council, and perhaps useful in your discussions of corporate aid with representatives of various colleges and corporations. 

Mr. Olds is Chairman of the Board of Directors of the 

Council for Financial Aid to Education, Inc., and a member of White & Case. He is former Chairman of the Board of Directors of the United States Steel Corporation. 3 838144

Each corporation is created for the accom plishment of certain objectives, ordinarily re ferred to as the "corporate purposes." I should suppose that the first question to be determined by the board of directors of a corporation be fore authorizing any corporate action, whether it be the building of a new plant, or the mar keting of a new product, or a gift in suppor of an educational institution, or something else, is whether the proposed action will con tribute in some way toward the advancemen or attainment of the "corporate purposes.'

That determination may involve nothing more than the exercise of sound business judgmen as to what is desirable and in the best interest of the particular corporation.

In recent years, corporate officers and direc tors in increasing numbers have come to recog nize that a relationship of real significance exists between higher education and the busi ness enterprises under their direction. The existence of such a relationship is an importan factor in determining whether a corporate con tribution in aid of higher education concern the advancement or attainment of the "cor porate purposes."

One may properly inquire as to the nature and substance of this relationship. Nothing is of greater importance to American business, in my opinion, than the continuation and main tenance of a system of competitive, free private enterprise in this country. Upon reflection, the conclusion seems clear that free enterprise in higher education is a necessary corollary of free enterprise in industry. Our independent pri vately supported colleges and universities many of which today are in serious financial plights, are and long have been an essential par of American higher education. In view of the

great contribution made by these institutions to the public welfare, a financial way must be Found to assure their continuation and healthy Future growth. This is a situation which, in my judgment, American business in general cannot refuse or fail to heed in the protection of its own interests.

In other words, I have not changed in any way the views which I expressed at Yale's two hundred and fiftieth anniversary in New

Haven in October 1951, when I said that every American business has a direct obligation to support the free, independent, privately-endowed colleges and universities of this country to the limit of its financial ability and legal authority; and that unless it recognizes and meets this obligation, it is not properly protecting the long-range interests of its stockholders, its employees and its customers. 

Last year some legal cobwebs, which many lawyers previously had thought stood in the way of such corporate gifts, were brushed aside by the decisions in the celebrated Smith case in New Jersey. There the legality of an unrestricted gift by a New Jersey corporation to Princeton University was upheld in 1953 by the Chancery Division of the Superior Court of New Jersey, a ruling which was unanimously affirmed by the Supreme Court of New Jersey, the highest court of that State. (The A. P. Smith 

Manufacturing Company v. Barlow et al., 98 A.2d 581). Last October, the Supreme

Court of the United States declined to hear an appeal because of lack of a substantial Federal question.

Perhaps the best way to elaborate on the relationship which exists between business and higher education, and which the New Jersey courts regarded as adequate justification for

this corporate gift to Princeton for general purposes, is to quote some passages from the decisions in this leading New Jersey case.

Judge Stein of the Superior Court, in sustaining this contribution to Princeton, said: "I cannot conceive of any greater benefit to corporations in this country than to build, and continue to build, respect for and adherence to a system of free enterprise and democratic government, the serious impairment of either of which may well spell the destruction of all corporate enterprise. Noth ing that aids or promotes the growth and service of the American university or college in respect of the matters here discussed can possibly be anything short of direct benefit to every corporation in the land.”

The Superior Court Judge then went on to say: "The college-trained men and women are a ready reservoir from which industry may draw to satisfy its need for scientific or executive talent. * * * A long-range view must be taken of the matter *** industry cannot function efficiently or enjoy development and expansion unless it have at all times the advantage of enlightened leadership and direction * * * nothing conducive to public welfare, other than perhaps public safety, is more important than the preservation of the privately supported institutions of learning which embrace in their enrollment about half the college-attending youth of the coun try 

Continuing, Judge Stein made this arresting statement: "I am strongly persuaded by the evidence

that the only hope for the survival of the privately supported American college and university lies in the willingness of corporate wealth to furnish in moderation some support to institutions which are so essential to public welfare and therefore, of necessity, to corporate welfare. What promotes the general good inescapably advances the corporate weal. I hold that corporate contributions to 

Princeton and institutions rendering the like public service are, if held within reasonable limitations, a matter of direct benefit to the giving corporations, and this without regard to the extent or sweep of the donors' business***. Such giving may be called an incidental power, but when it is considered in its essential character, it may well be regarded as a major, though unwritten, corporate power. It is even more than that. In the court's view of the case it amounts to a solemn duty." 

Justice Jacobs, speaking for a unanimous

Supreme Court of New Jersey, in affirming the judgment of the lower court, said: "It seems to us that just as the conditions prevailing when corporations were originally created required that they serve public as well as private interests, modern conditions require that corporations acknowledge and discharge social as well as private responsibilities as members of the communities within which they operate. Within this broad concept there is no difficulty in sustaining, as incidental to their proper objects and in aid of the public welfare, the power of corporations to contribute corporate funds

within reasonable limits in support of academic institutions. But even if we confine ourselves to the terms of the common-law rule in its application to current conditions, such expenditures may likewise readily be justified as being for the benefit of the corporation; indeed, if need be the matter may be viewed strictly in terms of actual survival of the corporation in a free enterprise system ***. In the light of all of the foregoing, we have no hesitancy in sustaining the valid ity of the donation by the plaintiff * * *.”

The Supreme Court of New Jersey then expressed its conclusion in these words: "We find that it was lawful exercise of the corporation's implied and incidental powers under common-law principles and that it came within the express authority of the pertinent State legislation. As has been indicated, there is now widespread belief throughout the nation that free and vigorous non-governmental institutions of learning are vital to our democracy and the system of free enterprise and that withdrawal of corporate authority to make such contributions within reasonable limits would seriously threaten their continuance. Corporations have come to recognize this and with their enlightenment have sought in varying measures, as has the plaintiff by its contribution, to insure and strengthen the society which gives them existence and the means of aiding themselves and their fellow citizens."

It may be argued that the decision in the 

Smith case primarily concerns the powers of a New Jersey Corporation under the New 

Jersey corporation Law. On the other hand,

P it must not be overlooked that the highest court in New Jersey held that the unrestricted contribution by the Smith Company to Princeton

University was a "lawful exercise of the corporation's implied and incidental powers under common-law principles" as well as coming within the express authority of the New Jersey statute. This would appear to be a most significant ruling so far as other States are concerned, as I should not suppose that the application of common law principles of this character is confined to cases arising in the State of New Jersey, but rather that such principles are applicable in all other States where the common law prevails. Certainly the decision in the Smith case is one which it seems fair to assume will be given careful consideration by the courts of other States having to determine a similar legal question. 

It is my understanding that thirty-five States, including New Jersey, have now adopted statutes expressly permitting corporations organized under the laws of those States to make contributions, under certain conditions, for philanthropic or general welfare purposes.

Some of these statutes specifically authorize corporate gifts in support of education. A list of the States which have adopted legislation of this general character is appended. I am told that a statute of this kind is presently under consideration in at least one additional State. 

Counsel for any corporation proposing to make a gift in aid of education should readily be able to advise his client as to the existence or applicability of such a statute, and generally on the legality and propriety of the proposed corporate contribution. ་ In conclusion, the foregoing comments may be summarized as follows:

1. The trend in this country seems to be definitely in the direction of a constantly increasing recognition by business of a direct interest in, and a corresponding financial responsibility toward, higher education - this being regarded as a matter of proper corporate concern. This trend is evidenced by the many corporations which in recent years have recognized such a responsibility through authorizing contributions of one kind or another in support of education. 

2. Earlier legal doubts about the power of a corporation to make a reasonable gift in support of a college or university, without imposing a personal obligation upon its assenting directors, are rapidly disappearing. While it is true that each case must be considered and determined upon its own facts, corporate executives and their counsel are more disposed today than formerly to take into account a corporation's over-all responsibilities as a member of the community or other area in which it operates and to look upon a proper discharge of these responsibilities as being in the best interests of the corporation and its stockholders. 

It is my conviction that the recent decision in the Smith case in New Jersey has done a great deal to dispel the old notion, often advanced by a few individuals as a more or less conclusive objection (sometimes, I gather, without too much regard for the facts) that directors might become personally liable through voting for a contribution in support of a college or university, even though reasonable in amount. So far as I know, no court in any State has reached a contrary conclusion in recent years. Furthermore, legislation expressly permitting corporate philanthropy has been enacted during the last few years in many 10

States. These recent statutes and the Smith decision furnish further evidence of the distinct trend in favor of corporate aid to education - a trend which I regard as fully justified and which I am led to believe is now reaching impressive proportions and will go much further.
