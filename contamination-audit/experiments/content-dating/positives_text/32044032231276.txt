UNITED STATES TARIFF COMMISSION Washington Safety Pins (December 1959) Report to the President, Under Paragraph 1 of Executive Order 10401, on Developments With Regard to Safety Pins Since Modification of the Trade-Agreement Concession on December 30, 1957 

The tariff concession granted in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade on safety pins, classifiable under paragraph 350 of the Tariff Act of 1930, was modified by Presidential Proclamation No. 3212 dated 

November 29, 1957, effective after the close of business on December 30, 1957, and the import duty on such safety pins was increased from 22-1/2 percent ad valorem to 35 percent ad valorem (3 CFR, 1957 Supp., p. 51).

This action was taken by the President following an investigation by the 

U.S. Tariff Commission under section 7 of the Trade Agreements Extension 

Act of 1951, as amended; a report to the President on January 30, 1957, of its findings and recommendations; and a supplemental report submitted · 1/ to the President, at his request, on September 30, 1957.

Executive Order 10401 of October 14, 1952 (3CFR, 1949-1953 Comp., p. 901), prescribes procedures for the review of "escape clause" actions with a view to determining whether a trade-agreement concession that has been modified or withdrawn may be restored in whole or in part without causing or threatening serious injury to the domestic industry concerned.

keep under review developments with regard to products on which a tradeagreement concession has been modified or withdrawn under the escapeclause procedure and to make periodic reports to the President concerning such developments. The first such report must in each case be made not later than 2 years after the modification or withdrawal of the concession. 

This is the first report on safety pins under paragraph 1 of the Executive order.

Description of articles 

The safety pins covered by this report are those made of base metal not plated with gold or silver and not commonly known as jewelry. The entire imports, so far as known, and much the greater part of the domestic production consist of all-metal safety pins. In addition two concerns in the United States produce a small amount of plastic-capped safety pins, but statistics cannot be published without revealing the individual operations. The information contained in the remainder of this report is confined to all-metal safety pins.

Domestic industry 

The domestic industry producing safety pins consists of six concerns:

The Ideal Manufacturing Co., Beacon Falls, Conn. 

The F. Kelly Co., Derby, Conn. 

The Oakville Co. Division of the Scovill Manufacturing Co., 

Oakville, Conn. 

William Prym, Inc., Dayville, Conn.

The Risdon Manufacturing Co., Waterbury, Conn. 

The Star Pin Co., Shelton, Conn. 

The Star Pin Co.

became a producer only in 1957, upon acquisition of the safety-pin assets of the DeLong branch of the Oakville Co. Division of the Scovill Manufacturing Co.

These assets had been acquired by the

Oakville Co. Division, together with other assets of the DeLong Hook &

Eye Co. of Philadelphia on April 1, 1955. Disposal of the safety-pin assets of the acquired concern was in compliance with an order by the 

Federal Trade Commission. The Oakville Co. Division of the Scovill 

Manufacturing Co., in its plant at Oakville, Conn., is by far the principal manufacturer of safety pins in the United States. 

Two of the producers, the Risdon Manufacturing Co. and the Scovill 

Manufacturing Co., operate other plants than those in which safety pins are manufactured. The Scovill Manufacturing Co. is one of the largest brass-manufacturing firms in this country, and the Oakville Co. Division, where its safety pins are manufactured, is only one of seven operating divisions of this concern. Of the remaining producers, one, William 

Prym, Inc., is among the principal importers of safety pins.

Four of the six producers of safety pins are members of the Pin, Clip and Fastener Association, to which sales by members are reported. The two non-member concerns (The Ideal Manufacturing Co. and the F. Kelly Co.) have been excluded from statistics given in this report, as in the 1956 report of the Tariff Commission, to avoid disclosing their operations. 

If the Commission were able to publish figures for all domestic producers, the record of the domestic industry with regard to production, sales and employment would appear somewhat more favorable in recent years than is shown in this report. 

Domestic production and sales

The production of safety pins by the 4 members of the Pin, Clip and Fastener Association was about 9.6 million gross in both 1956 and 1957

(table 1 in the appendix), and was somewhat less than the annual average (10.6 million gross) in the 3 years 1953-55. Production declined in 1958 to 7.5 million gross, notwithstanding the increase in the duty on imports in that year. In the first 8 months of 1959 total output of the four concerns was over 7 million gross, or at an annual rate of 10.6 million gross.

Annual sales were less than output in both 1956 and 1957, but they exceeded production in 1958. In the first 8 months of 1959 sales were 

6.5 million gross compared with output of 7.1 million gross. The value of annual sales ranged from $2.7 million to $3.1 million in the years 

1956-58. The average unit value of sales has undergone little change in the last several years; it was 31 cents per gross in 1956 and 33 cents in 1958. 

Employment and wages

Total employment since 1955 in plants manufacturing safety pins has ranged between 1,200 and 1,300 persons (table 2). As indicated by the man-hours worked, the proportion of employment which could be attributed to the production of safety pins declined from 23 percent in 1955 and 1956 to 20 percent in the first 8 months of 1959. Average hourly earnings of production workers on safety pins, like those of workers on all products in plants where such pins are manufactured, increased gradually during the period under review.

Imports 

Compared with nearly 5 million gross in 1956, and 6 million gross in 1957, imports in 1958--following the increase in duty--were 3 million

gross (table 3). Imports were at an annual rate of 3-1/2 million gross in January-September 1959. The high level in 1957 is attributable in part to imports in anticipation of a higher duty. Imports in December 

1957 alone, following proclamation on November 29 of the higher rate to become effective after the close of business on December 30, were 

1,344,000 gross.

The unit foreign value of imported safety pins increased from 12.5 cents per gross in 1956 to 13.8 cents per gross in 1957 and to 15.0 cents per gross for the period January-September 1959 (table 4). As indicated by information received directly from importers, prices on safety pins offered for sale by foreign suppliers have increased since 1956. 

The United Kingdom and West Germany are the principal suppliers of the safety pins imported into the United States (table 4). The United 

Kingdom supplied approximately 60 percent of imports for both the 2-year period (1956-57) before the rate change and the 21-month period (January 

1958-September 1959) following the change. West Germany supplied 39 percent of imports in the former period and 37 percent in the latter. 

Safety pins from Czechoslovakia have been dutiable at the full statutory rate of 35 percent ad valorem since November 2, 1951. From that date through December 30, 1957, during which period imports from other sources remained dutiable at 22-1/2 percent ad valorem, imports from Czechoslovakia declined sharply. With the increase in the rate on imports from other sources, the imports from Czechoslovakia have begun to increase. 

In 1958 they increased to fourfold the amount entered in 1957, and for the period January-September 1959 they came in at an annual rate more than double that for 1958. Czechoslovakian safety pins, however, still comprise a small proportion of total imports (table 4).

6. 

Consumption 

The apparent consumption of safety pins (domestic sales plus imports, less exports) since 1950 has averaged 14 million gross a year, and has shown no apparent trend. In 1957, apparent consumption was 15 million gross, but in 1958 it was only 11 million gross (table 5). The difference in consumption in the two years was due in part to large imports in 1957 in anticipation of the higher duty resulting from modification of the concession, which occurred at the end of the year. Apparent consumption in the first 8 months of 1959 was at an annual rate of about 13 million gross. Exports have been small for many years, and imports have supplied a varying proportion of apparent consumption, ranging from one-fifth in 1953 to somewhat less than two-fifths in 1957. In 1958 and the first 8 months of 1959, imports supplied slightly more than one-fourth of consumption. 

Prices 

All the steel pins that are produced in the United States and all those that are imported in bulk packages are made of hard steel, but many of those that are imported in retail packages are made of mild steel. All steel pins, whether of hard steel or mild steel, are nickel plated. The brass pins in the smaller sizes are given a gilt finish, and those in the larger sizes are nickel plated. 

Safety pins are put up in bulk for use by laundries, hospitals, and industrial establishments, and in bunches or on cards for distribution at retail. There are wide differences in unit prices for steel pins and brass pins and for the same kinds of pins in different forms of packaging.

All the domestic producers sell safety pins of various descriptions and kinds of packaging. With only minor variations, the prices and types of packaging for pins sold by different producers are the same. The different importers, on the other hand, sell safety pins at different prices and in different kinds of retail packages. Users of safety pins generally prefer domestic pins, and domestic pins at present, as at the time of the Commission's investigation in 1956, uniformly command a higher price at wholesale than imported pins of like description (table 6). 

Nickel-plated steel pins and brass midget pins, in bulk, comprise a large part of the total imports and somewhat more than half of the total number of safety pins produced in the United States. The prices of such pins increased between 1956 and 1959, following the increase in duty, but the spread between the price of the imported and that of the domestic pins remained the same.

The unit price for domestic nickel-plated steel pins and brass gilt pins in retail bunches was increased in 1957, before the increase in the duty on imports, but was subsequently reduced to a level equal to or below that which prevailed in 1956. The reduction below the 1956 price accompanied replacement of the smallest sized domestic bunches, designed to sell at retail for 10 cents, by larger bunches to retail at 15 cents, with a more than proportionate increase in the number (and a consequent reduction in the unit price) of the pins per bunch. This price

decrease by domestic producers was not accompanied by a significant change in the price charged by importers for pins in retail bunches. 

On nickel-plated brass pins, of which there have been only occasional imports, domestic producers reduced the price in bulk but increased the price on retail cards between 1956 and 1959. Although the increased price per card was accompanied by an increased number of pins per card, the increase in the price was greater than the increase in the number of pins.
