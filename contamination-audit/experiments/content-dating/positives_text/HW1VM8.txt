FIRST CHAPTER

CHAPTER ONE 

Notwithstanding the old familiar proverb certainly events are frequently very negligent in casting their shadows before. The Days continually offer their morning greetings without a suggestion as to what they are bringing with them. 

They are mysteriously close-lipped, and their hands are behind them. They deceive us by their calm and quiet approach and their utter failure to drop even a hint of what they intend to present to us before the clock strikes the midnight hour. 

Yes, surely, Emerson was right. They are "hypocritic Days" and they come "Muffled and dumb like barefoot dervishes."

Virgil Buckminster slowly opened his eyes in his large front room on Beacon Street in his native city of Boston. He had slept well but he was stirring somewhat earlier than usual as it was not wholly light. In passing from sleeping to waking [ 7 ]

his thoughts cleared quickly and he remembered that it was the ninth of November, the ninth of 

November, 1872, his seventieth birthday. 

Strange, he thought, how much older seventy seems than sixty-nine.-Yet I do not feel any older.

He reached toward his bed-table for his watch with its key fastened on its chain. The dimness of the room prevented him from telling the time at a distance. As he reached he tipped over a small bottle of cough syrup and then nearly dropped his watch. These accidents filled him with mild disgust. Yes, that's one way I show my age. I'm getting clumsy with my hands and unless I keep my mind on what I am doing I'm continually dropping things. It's most annoying! Why yesterday at dinner I dropped my fork as I was taking it up. What good did it do to see Dr.

Putnam about it? He only said that some of the automatic nerve centers were getting old and tired, that I must put up with it just as one had to put up with grey hair. Getting old, he said. Well 

I suppose I am, but I'm only seventy today. Why

Titian was painting The Battle of Lepanto at ninety-eight!

If it wasn't for this annoying drop- [ 8 ]

ping business I should feel about as young as I did ten or fifteen years ago.

As the room was growing lighter Virgil Maro Buckminster sank back on his pillows and reflected upon the many birthdays which had gone before and upon the days in between. 

Whatever possessed my well intentioned father to give me the name of Virgil Maro? It's one thing to be passionately fond of the classics but it's quite another matter to burden one's only son with such a name. I'm glad I had sense enough to drop the Maro. But perhaps I should be thankful that my father didn't give me his hero's full name,-Publius Virgilius Maro Buckminster. I escaped that anyway. Why don't people stick to old family names? They mean something whether you like the sound of them or not. I'm glad we had sense enough to name our only child, Richard.

It had the endorsements of three or four generations-probably more. 

Seventy years! How vivid my boyhood days have been growing these last years! Or is it that much later events are often dimmer? Why I remember distinctly that fourth of July when I wasn't quite four, when Father took me across [ 9 ]

the street to the Common to see a man fight with a bear. To my sorrow he hurried me away before it was finished as he thought I was getting overexcited. Let me see, yes I was about nine when it was a daily interest, for weeks, to watch the men dig away the top of Beacon Hill until they had made it about sixty feet lower. Father didn't like the idea at all, and when the old brick and stone monument, designed by Bulfinch, which marked the site of the old beacon light, was demolished he thought it was a desecration. If he had lived to see the fine old John Hancock House, close by, taken down in sixty-three, his distress would have been a real sorrow. 

As a little boy I remember so well walking with him along the street in front of the fine old house. 

I had hold of his strong but gentle hand with its long tapering fingers. We were on the Common side of the street and just after we had passed the house he stopped and, turning towards the rugged 

English elm next us, said: "Virgil, I want you to remember this, I saw the great John Hancock plant this tree. He did it with the help of one of his men. I think it was the year that Cornwallis surrendered at Yorktown." Then we walked on. [ 10 ]

Father seemed to be quiet and thoughtful until we reached Walnut Street when he said, half to himself, "Those were great days. I am glad I lived through them." Strange I remember it all so vividly. I couldn't have been more than five or six. 

One Sunday morning, when little Richard was about the same age, he and I were walking in front of the old house hand in hand, as I had walked with my father years before, and as we stood next to the old tree I told him all about the incident with my father and what he had said. Then I said, "Richard, I want you to remember that your grandfather saw the great John Hancock plant this tree." When I talked about the Declaration of Independence and how Hancock signed it first, he asked, "Did Grandpa sign it too?" 

How Father scorned the title of "Bunker Hill" for the battle at Charlestown! "Bunker Hill," he almost snorted. "Why it wasn't on Bunker

Hill. Why cling to the name just because it was 

Bunker Hill which had been ordered to be fortified?" He always called it the "Battle at Charlestown." How well I remember that old print of the battle which hung in Father's study. At the [ 11 ]

bottom it said in large letters, "An Exact View of the Late Battle at Charlestown June 17, 1775.'

The chief points of interest were numbered and the key was in the upper right hand corner. I had to get up on a chair to read it. I remember 

Number 1 was the tower of the Old South Church across the Bay and Number 9 was General Putnam on his horse. I used to gloat over all the dead British lying about. I wonder what ever became of that old print. I'd like to see it again. 

Memory lifted the curtain upon many earlier scenes. Some of them trivial but very distinct.— 

I shall never forget those two cold summers, one after the other, in my early teens when there was a frost here in Boston every month in the year. 

No wonder they called those years, "eighteen hundred and freeze to death."

And my marriage in King's chapel,-it's as fresh in my memory as if it had taken place last week. I felt quite old for I had reached the advanced age of thirty-five. I was ten years older than dear Sylvia. I remember overhearing somebody say that it was too bad I was so much older than she. My! she was beautiful with her delicate [ 12 ]

charm and gentle look of high breeding, but even then she was very frail.

Thus, as the room was growing lighter Virgil's thoughts were flying back and forth through the many years of his life-years full of deep tragedy but also of many wonderfully happy days.

Then the present asserted itself—as it always does. Well, thank the Lord the election is over with all its slander and abuse. Glad I shan't have to continue to hear about Credit Mobilier. Old 

Horace Greeley has got his comeuppance. According to the returns yesterday he carried only a very few States. Of course Grant hasn't been as good a president as he was a general but certainly he will be better than carping, critical Old 

Greeley. Why Greeley says he hasn't had a good night's sleep for fifteen years—what kind of a 

President would that kind of a man make! It takes more than a good editor to be a good Presidentof course it takes more than a good general, as well, but of the two men certainly Grant is better. 

I wonder how Senator Sumner and General

Banks feel now that election is over. Rather sick to their stomachs I'll wager. What will they do 1 [ 18 ]

now? Greeley will go back to his paper, which he never should have left.

The room had grown light and Virgil's eyes fell upon the portrait of a young boy-his boy

Richard. It was a distinguished, happy-looking young face with a good mouth and forehead.

Even now it bore a resemblance to his father which the years would have emphasized. 

As Virgil looked at the portrait his own strong face, with its deep marks of sorrow, grew tender. 

He began to count the years since this only child of theirs had been suddenly taken from them. 

Yes, born in fifty-one. He was killed when he was ten-that was in sixty-one-the year the war began. This is seventy-two, why, he would have been twenty-one-it doesn't seem possible. 

Fifty-one, seventy-two,-yes that's twenty-one.

Why if he had lived he would have voted this year. We would have gone to the polls together and cast our ballots for Grant. Twenty-one.

Yes that is true.-But I shall always think of him as only ten-my boy of ten! Ten happy years. 

But the day Richard was born, that was an awful day if there ever was one. Sweet, gentle

Sylvia was so frail I was anxious, so anxious from [ 14 ]

the moment the doctor said a baby was coming. 

But I put on a bold and cheerful front for her sake. "Of course it will be all right," I told her. "It's a perfectly normal thing to have a baby. We ought to be very happy that we are going to be blessed with a son or daughter." During the months I told her this many times and she had such confidence in my judgment that I think she fully believed it,—at any rate she seemed cheerful, and confident everything would be all right.

During the time of waiting I was distressed beyond words. The worst of it was the doctor did not seem over hopeful himself when I spoke to him privately....

When at last the final day of torture was over Virgil went into Sylvia's room to see her and the baby boy. The room seemed abnormally silentso silent after the long hours of suspense and suffering which had filled it from floor to ceiling with almost a shattering vibration of agony. He sat reverently by the bed and took Sylvia's delicate hand. How beautiful she looked and yet how unspeakably frail. She had just strength enough to gently close her expressive fingers over [ 15 ]

his and faintly smile, as she whispered: "I hope he will look more and more like you."

Virgil was so overwhelmed with the indescribable pain of birth, and by a devastating sense of the unfairness that it had been fragile Sylvia who was forced to bear it, instead of him, who was so much better able to do it, that he could not say a word. He only stooped and reverently kissed her upon the forehead and left the room so she could sleep. Notwithstanding that the delicate thread which held her to life seemed momentarily ready to break-she lived.

It was a week later that Virgil and Sylvia talked of a name. "No, no, not Virgil," he said at her suggestion. "Wouldn't you you like my father's name, Richard?" Yes, she would, So it was easily settled. 

Thus the ten glad years had begun. Years of happiness with little Richard. Years of planning for his education and his coming youth and manhood. He shouldn't go into his father's brokerage office on State Street unless he really wanted to, Virgil and Sylvia spontaneously agreed.

But as Virgil's memory led him on, he suddenly realized that it must not make him forget the time [ 16 ]

of day. Surely he should not be late for breakfast on his birthday 

even though the faithful

Hannah probably did not remember the anniversary. He rose hastily.

Later, as he was fastening his black stock before the mirror he remembered that Sylvia had wanted him to give it up. She said that stocks had gone by and that he should be modern and keep up with the times. She had said the same thing about his old fashioned "barndoor" trousers, but he had retained both. Perhaps it was a mistake. Probably gentle Sylvia was right. 

When he had dressed he stood at one of his windows and looked across the Common. How closely it had been associated with his life through the years. He remembered when the cows used to pasture there, and how in 1830 this was no longer allowed. He had seen the iron fence built around it five or six years later. Before that the 

Frog Pond had been enclosed with a stone curbing. He was glad that the fancy name of Crescent

Pond, which it was called for a time, had been dropped and that the good old name of the Frog Pond had endured. At one time, Virgil recalled, the boys were fined for bathing in it. [ 17 ]

And his associations with the Common connected with Richard were not all tragic. As a little boy they had walked together innumerable times under the friendly trees. Sometimes Sylvia went with them. Once they all sat together and saw Sam Rice's elephant bathe in the pond. How he spouted and enjoyed it, and how Richard laughed. The number of balloons they had seen go up were too many to remember. 

And Richard used to enjoy the coasting. The "Long Coast" from Park and Beacon Streets to the West Street gate and beyond was the most exciting especially when the course had been sprinkled at night and was icy the next day. As the boys went under the bridges which had been built over the speedways they were watched by interested spectators till eleven o'clock at night. 

As Virgil stood remembering the happy memories of the Common his eyes inevitably drifted up towards the Walnut Street steps and the tragedy of the Common came to him with all its accustomed force. Whatever happened, the years could never obliterate that fearful autumn day.

They had sent the carriage for him in hot haste at the office. When John came in Virgil knew [ 18 ]

something serious had happened. At first John only said that Richard was badly hurt. As they hastened to the carriage-Yes, they had sent for the doctor. On the way to the house John told how Richard was running down the steep stone steps at Walnut Street; how he had his new sail boat raised high in both hands as he hurried towards the Frog Pond; how he tripped and fell to the bottom striking his head on the lowest step.

Virgil knew, before he reached home, notwithstanding John's silence, that Richard was dead. 

He asked, "Did you see his mother?" "No, I didn't, Sir." 

When they brought the dead body of Richard into the house to Sylvia she had collapsed. She was in bed for weeks with a serious mental breakdown. Virgil always tried to forget those dreadful weeks-but he never could. They had dragged on and on. When at last Sylvia was up and about she was as sweet and gentle as ever.

She seemed perfectly normal except for one thing. 

Every now and then a passion for buying things would seize her buying things for Richard. 

Clothes, neckties, toys. Not long before Richard was killed, he spoke of wanting a microscope. It [ 19 ]

was one of the first things she bought. All her purchases were carefully put away in Richard's room just as if he were still occupying it. 

At first Virgil was distressed at this sign that the brain was not wholly normal. He protested gently but Sylvia only sadly smiled and then burst into tears. But one day, after the buying had gone on for several months, they were sitting together on one of the benches under the Hancock 

Elm in the Common. Sylvia had arduously gained back much of her strength, but she was still like a frail spring flower poorly able to withstand the March winds. As Virgil sat beside her it seemed to him that her delicate, high-bred beauty was rarely ethereal and unworldly. He stretched out his hand and took one of hers.

After a pause Virgil asked very softly, “Sylvia, precious, when you buy things for Richard, do you really forget that Richard is-that Richard is dead?"

Her face assumed a puzzled, confused expression as if she were trying very hard to interpret her mental reactions when the purchasing mania came on. Then she said, "You know, Virgil, if 

I stop and think I realize that Richard is dead, but [ 20 ]

I want to buy something for him just the same and the impulse is so strong I cannot resist it. I just have to do it." She paused, tears came into her eyes and her mouth quivered. "But," she went on, "I'm so sorry, Virgil," she faltered, as she clung tightly to his hand, "I am so distressed, Virgil. I know how it troubles you. I have tried to stop it. You are always so wonderful to me. 

Oh, I have tried so hard, but it's never any use.

It's like trying to swim against an irresistible current." 

As Virgil looked at Sylvia a great wave of compassion and understanding swept over him. He realized that a new bond of sympathy had sprung up between them, adding one more to the many which already existed. He reached forth his other hand and placed it over the one he already held and said, with tears in his eyes now and with a trembling voice, "Sylvia, darling, don't let it ever worry you again. When you feel the impulse coming on go and buy something for Richard. 

I know you buy because we both love him. He isn't dead to us but is always here. If you like I will go with you and we will buy things for him together." [ 21 ]

That is what they often did and Virgil grew to enjoy the little excursions prompted by a mutual understanding of each other and a love for Richard.

Sometimes, when Richard's things had accumulated too much in his room, Virgil would say, "Sylvia, don't you think it would be a good idea to give some of Richard's clothes and toys to the Charitable Society of the Hollis Street Church? It knows of many people in need who would, I am sure, appreciate them." Sylvia would answer, “I think it is a lovely idea. I am sure Richard would want to. I will speak to Mrs. 

Kitridge about it next Sunday." 

And so the years had gone on, nearly ten of them, years sweetened and purified by a sanctifying understanding of each other.

As Virgil looked back, his mind filled with the thoughts and emotions which his memories had awakened; he spoke almost aloud,-Well I am glad I gave up the office and came home and devoted myself wholly to her. It was better that way. I was sixty and notwithstanding the protests of my friends it was time to retire. Especially as Sylvia needed me more than the office. I wasn't [ 22 ]

much good there anyway as I was always wondering how things were going at home. I shall never forget the radiance in Sylvia's face when I told her that I wasn't going to the office any more. 

She threw her arms about my neck and kept saying, "Thank you, Virgil. Thank you." I hadn't told her that I was giving up business on her account, but I was glad she understood. 

Then last year the final blow came. I didn't bear it very well, but I tried to remember what 

Father told me many times, "Courage, Virgil, is a supreme virtue which introduces you to many others." 

Sylvia seemed to just drift away. I used to sit by her bed and hold her hand, hour after hour, and try to keep her with me, but it was hopeless. 

Towards the last she said, with one of her gentle smiles, "It's no use, Virgil, you must let me go, even if we do love each other so completely." 

After that I didn't try to keep her. At the end she slipped away as peacefully as the sunbeam which was in her room. There was not even a sigh, or a tremor as I held her hand. Gentle 

Sylvia went as she had lived.

That was nearly a year ago and here I am just [ 23 ]

trying to pick up the pieces and live on in a way worthy of her blessed memory; but when one has been trying to live and breathe for the person one loves, for so many years, one feels completely lost, and completely useless, after she has gone.

SECOND CHAPTER

CHAPTER TWO 

Virgil rather dreaded going down to breakfast. 

It was his first birthday breakfast without Sylvia.

As he descended the stairs he thought-There will not be any red rose on the table next my plate. 

For over thirty years Sylvia had never forgotten to have it there. She always went herself for the glorious rose, and never failed to make a profound secret of the object of her errand. Even last year, notwithstanding her failing health, she journeyed to Galvin's, near King's Chapel, for the beautiful red rose. With the full-blown flower she always had in the vase with it a charming rosebud and never failed to say, "That's for you to grow on 

Virgil, dear."

But to his great surprise as he entered the dining room and glanced at the table, there was the red rose and the rosebud in the vase with it by his plate, as they had been for so many years. [ 27 ]

He turned to Hannah with his courtly manner and said with deep gratitude, "Why, Hannah, you have not forgotten the rose and the rosebud. 

Thank you and thank you again." 

Hannah, blushing with pleasure, replied, "Why, Sir, how could I ever forget them when dear Mrs. Buckminster has put them there with her own lovely hands on this ninth of November, every year since I have been in this house." "Certainly, Hannah, it is a satisfaction to me that there is at least one person in the world who knows when my birthday comes." "Thank you, Sir. I could never forget it." "But Hannah," Virgil asked as he sat down for breakfast, "what year was it that you came to us?" "I don't remember the year but I know it was the day after a great storm and the famous Old 

Elm on the Common was badly damaged. When

I walked by it to come here my first morning there was quite a crowd around the tree and many were taking home wood for relics from the great branch which had blown off." "Let me see, Hannah. Yes, that was during 

June, in sixty. I remember Richard and I went [ 28 ]

down to see the old tree and I told him of the even greater storm of 1832 which had nearly destroyed it." "Yes, 1860, that was when I came." "You came then twelve years ago. That was the year before Richard-fell." "Yes, Sir. Shall I ever forget it and they brought him in dead without warning, and Mrs.

Buckminster was in the hall and she dropped with a groan, and we thought she was dead too." "Yes, Hannah, and you and Sarah and John looked after us so well. We always felt we were most fortunate in having such devoted helpersand now Mrs. Buckminster has been gone nearly a year and you have been very good attending to everything in the house.' 99 "Thank you, Sir. We have tried to do what we could. We all loved her too, you know." "Yes, Hannah. Everyone loved her." "Yes, Sir. How could anyone help it!" "Oh Hannah, have you gone over Richard's room lately?" "Oh yes, Sir. I go in there every day and fix everything just as if Richard were still here. [ 29 ]

Mrs. Buckminster used to do that and I know she would wish me to continue." "Yes, I'm sure she would. Do you think we should send more things to Rev. Mr. Chaney? 

I am sure Richard would like to have them used by those who need them." "I don't know, Sir. I will look them over again in a few days.'

THIRD CHAPTER

CHAPTER THREE 

After Virgil had finished his breakfast he went into the library taking his rose and rosebud with him, bearing the vase in the spirit of reverent formality as if he were approaching the altar with a lighted candle. He had done this for over thirty years. Hannah was watching to see if he followed his usual custom and was pleased that he did.

Placing the rose and rosebud upon the library table he sat down nearby in his usual chair in front of the fireplace. Often in former years on the ninth of November there had been a bright fire upon the hearth, but this morning it was not needed. Outside it was a mild Indian summer day. The night before people were strolling in the Common and sitting on the benches as if it were September.

After a few moments of pause as he looked into [ 33 ]

the lifeless fireplace he reached for the Daily Advertiser on its accustomed spot upon the table. 

He did not have to look to see if it were there. It had been there every morning for many years.

Abijah Ellis, murdered in Cambridge on Tuesday, election night. How could a human being commit such a crime! Cut up the body and put the four parts in barrels and cast them into the 

Charles River! It's past comprehension! "The 

Election." Rejoicings in New Hampshire.

At the sound of a band Virgil went to the window. It was playing "Oh dear, What can the matter be?" As Virgil suspected he saw a large loaded truck drawn with ropes by many men and boys, led by the band. This had been an occasional sight during the last two weeks, while Boston was suffering from the devastating epidemic among the horses. At its worst the streets were without horses. The limited traffic was drawn by oxen and men and boys. The street cars ran very infrequently if at all. Even hacks were drawn by men, the fleeter their feet the better. Trucking firms hired men and boys to haul their carts and now and then in the spirit of gayety, especially when the load was unusually heavy, they were led by a band. Even the fire department was without horses to draw their engines and hook and ladder trucks. Volunteers of men and boys were enlisted to do the necessary pulling and running when an alarm sounded. 

But now the horses were gradually getting [ 37 ]

better and more and more appeared on the streets, but many were still weak and in a poor condition. 

Even the day before yesterday the Society for the 

Prevention of Cruelty to Animals had stopped the running of the horse cars because of the unfit state of the horses. The horses in the fire department were the last in the city to recover, perhaps, some thought, because of their fleshy condition. 

Even now man-and-boy power had to be almost wholly depended upon.

FOURTH CHAPTER

CHAPTER FOUR 

Virgil left the window.-Well if I am going down to see about that ticket at the Hartford and 

Erie I'd better get started. The doctor says I ought to walk more and I guess he is right about it. As he went down his steps and turned up 

Beacon he walked off with a vigorous, springing gait. His well proportioned and erect form with his well-shaped head gracefully set upon his shoulders showed that he was unbowed by the weight and sorrows of the years. "Who says I am seventy?" he murmured. At Park Street he stopped a moment to glance back across Beacon 

Street to the Common.-My! it's a rarely beautiful Indian Summer day! How peaceful the old 

Common looks in the gentle sunlight.-Virgil could not even faintly dream that before the city clocks struck the hour of midnight it would be a seething multitude of excited inhabitants of [ 41 ]

Boston. As he walked along Beacon Street the city itself was going about its accustomed affairs wholly unaware that this was to be one of the most tragic days in its colorful history. Everything seemed so normal and regular without a hint of what was to come. Even if Virgil, on that calm, clear Saturday morning of November 9th, 1872, had visited every one of the nearly a score of clairvoyants who everyday advertised in the Boston papers, he would have left them all without the realization of the great catastrophe which the day was to bring to Boston. And Virgil 

Buckminster walked along Beacon Street, past 

Park, towards Tremont, without dreaming that the Day held something in store for him which would completely change the remaining years of his life. 

At Tremont he turned to the right, went by the

Tremont House, and passed under the old Paddock Elms in front of the Granary Burying- 

Ground. I always like to walk under the old trees, he thought. I remember Father saying they were planted in 1770, the year of the Boston 

Massacre, by Achino Paddock, who was an overseer of the poor. Achino, I wonder where his [ 42 ]

father found that name?-and why should an overseer of the poor plant trees? But it was a fine idea and I am glad he did it. 

That was a clever idea also that a New York paper advanced-that most of the Boston hotels have graveyards attached to them. Parker House is near King's Chapel Burying-Ground; Tremont 

House overlooks Old Granary; the Pelham is close to the Common graveyard and the St. James is near the Blackstone Square Cemetery. Some of it is rather far-fetched, but the other remarks from the facetious New Yorker are amusing- "Whether these burial places are the natural result of the too rich cuisine of the various establishments, or whether people who do not pay their hotel bills are summarily disposed of in Boston, is a matter we leave to conjecture."... There goes a horse-car. The horses surely do look weak and fagged, but the epidemic is certainly about over. 

I have only seen three yokes of oxen so far, and more horses are drawing the trucks and wagons.

The hackmen seem to be the first to get their horses out. Still there are a good many handdrawn vehicles. I really think that some of the [ 43 ]

boys will miss their new occupation. They seem to have enjoyed it.

He crossed Tremont at Park Street and started to walk down Winter and hesitated.-No, I won't go by way of Winter to Summer and take the shortest way to the station. The day is so mild and beautiful-and the doctor says I must walk- 

I'll keep on and turn down West Street;—an apparently trivial decision, but it caused a most revolutionary change in Virgil's coming days and years. As he turned at West he was only thinking, I mustn't forget to ask Hannah to sew on that button on my waistcoat that is getting too loose to be safe.

He crossed Washington after waiting for a large truck, drawn by about two dozen shouting men and boys, to go by, and started down Bedford, planning to join Summer Street at Church 

Green and go on to the Hartford and Erie at the bottom.

When passing the large, square, granite building of the Boston Latin and English High School the stern, picturesque master of the Latin School, Dr. Francis Gardner, came out. They bowed to each other as he turned up towards Washington [ 44 ]

Street. Something unusual must have brought him to the building on Saturday, thought Virgil.

Virgil woke the next morning about ten. It was the intense smell of smoke that roused him. 

Going to a window he saw the smoke was so dense he could not see the sun and only dimly the nearby trees on the Common. 

When he got downstairs he asked Hannah: "Have they got the fire under control yet?" "Not yet, Sir, but they are more hopeful now.

They think the blowing up of buildings is going to check it. But there is a good deal of opposition to this use of powder. Many think it is needless. 

At first they didn't seem to know how to do it. 

Sometimes the explosion just blew out the windows and didn't destroy the building at all. They have saved the Old South Church, but it was a close shave. It nearly went several times." "I am thankful for that, Hannah, and the old

State House, that is safe too-and the fire didn't get across Washington Street?" [ 95 ]

"No, Sir, it didn't get across Washington

Street, but John says it's gone way down to the water and that it crossed Milk Street near the new unfinished Post Office building, and went on north by Congress and Kirby Streets past Water and Central, nearly to State Street. It surely doesn't seem possible, does it, Sir?" "No, Hannah, it doesn't. Think how proud we have been of our fire department at its annual parades. Why think of the last parade! Our people lined the streets to see it go by. How fine the engines looked, all brightly painted and the metal so highly polished you could see your face in it if you were near enough." "Yes," said Hannah, "and what an impression those great bunches of flowers made which filled the tops of the smoke stacks!" "Yes, Hannah, and how fit the horses were and now most of them are sick and cannot be used. 

How proud we all were that we hadn't used handtubs for twelve years and yesterday we were glad of all which came in from the small towns. And in the procession how gay the hose-carriages looked, all decorated in their bright buntingand the marching firemen, how confident they [ 96 ]

appeared in their red shirts as they were led by their foremen proudly holding their silver trumpets. They all seemed ready for anything, and the great crowd cheered and cheered. But the people of Boston are not cheering this morning. 

We have all discovered that we cannot throw strong enough streams of water to the upper parts of some of our buildings." "Yes, Sir. That's true, Sir, but the fire department is not to blame for the wooden mansard roofs, is it, Sir?" "Of course it isn't, Hannah, nor for the poor water supply; but the city is, and I hope we have learned our lesson. But it's been a very expensive one, and the fire isn't controlled yet." "But, Sir, they say it's going to be this afternoon." "I hope so, Hannah, but the fire department should have secured well horses to take the place of the sick ones. Then the response to the alarms would have been normal." "Yes, Sir. John says everybody is saying that, but even then the fire would probably have been as bad, as there were still the other things to cause its spread." [ 97 ]

"Yes, Hannah, it was caused by a combination of circumstances. . . . But, Hannah, I must go out and keep looking for that boy. The worst of it is I haven't any clues. I can only wander about and ask people if they have seen him. Until after this dreadful fire is over there is no use in going to the police. They haven't any time now to look for a small boy. I am going now to the places where they are feeding people. The boy must eat."

He went first to the vestry of the Park Street Church nearby, where they were feeding the hungry by the hundreds. Then on to 277

Tremont Street where the Children's Mission was sending homeless children to its Home. This seemed like a hopeful place to find Hilary and Virgil looked carefully over the lists of children who had been taken care of; but his efforts were unrewarded.

He dropped into the Christian Union where Mr. Baldwin had long tables covered with food for the firemen. Helpers were also continually going out with food for those who could not leave their stations.

The city had employed the caterer, J. B. Smith, [ 98 ]

to furnish the firemen and soldiers with crackers, cheese and coffee. Waitresses from Mrs. Harrington's Dining Rooms on School Street carried coffee and refreshments to the fire fighters.

The chapel of the Hollis Street Church, Virgil's Church, had opened early in the morning to receive food. The Sunday School teachers were sent out, street by street, to gather it; many families sent their Sunday dinners. Rev. Mr.

Chaney, the minister, with two boys, started Sunday morning with a handcart filled with refreshments and distributed them to the firemen at their posts. Virgil looked up Rev. Mr. Chaney and walked along with him and told him about Hilary and asked his aid in the search, which was gladly given.

Certainly there was food enough. There was no need for Hilary to starve. But where was he?

ELEVENTH CHAPTER

CHAPTER ELEVEN 

Notwithstanding the excitement of the fire the churches were crowded Sunday morning. Trinity 

Church held its service in the hall of the Institute of Technology. Phillips Brooks discarded the sermon he had prepared and preached extemporaneously from the text: "If any man's work shall be burned he shall suffer loss, but he himself shall be saved, yet so as by fire." It was a deeply moving sermon. Virgil wished he could have heard it.

Dr. Manning of the Old South held his morning service in the old South Chapel on Freeman 

Place. In the afternoon, however, a soldiers' service was held in the Old South itself. There were no cushions in the pews, the glass of the windows was broken on the side towards the fire, one whole window sash was missing. The floor was strewn with the litter of a soldiers' camp [ 103 ]

for once more, as in Revolutionary days, the soldiers were encamped in the historic old building. At the service Dr. Manning and Rev. W. H. 

H. Murray took part. 

Rev. Robert Collyer, of Chicago, was exchanging with Rev. Edward Everett Hale. The South

Congregational was crowded to the doors to hear him. A year before his own city of Chicago had also known the tragedy of an even greater conflagration.

Early Sunday afternoon the fire was brought under control. Virgil had spent the day in a fruitless search for the boy who had picked up his cane yesterday morning. Was it only yesterday! 

It seemed days ago.

Sunday night he went to bed early as the long strain was beginning to tell on him. Just after midnight he was awakened with a start by a loud explosion. Shortly after, the fire alarm sounded from box forty-two. What could it mean? Were they blowing up more buildings? Had the fire broken out again? Hannah had John go out to see. He returned in a short time and reported that there had been an explosion of accumulated gas in the building occupied by the jewelers, 

[ 104 ]

Shreve, Crump and Low, at the corner of Washington and Summer Streets. The buildings on the south side of Summer next Washington Street were burning. The gas pipes were leaking in many places and the gas all over the city must be turned off. After this interruption, weary 

Boston-the part which was not still occupied with the fire-went to sleep again.

It awoke Monday morning to its great task of rebuilding over sixty acres in the very heart of the city. But it awoke to an unusual daily life. 

Marines had come from the Navy Yard, a squad of sailors armed with carbines from the steamship 

Tallapoosa, soldiers from Fort Independence and Fort Warren, over a thousand men from the State militia and police from Cambridge and Providence. These were protecting the city from the many ruffians and thieves which had descended upon the stricken town like vultures. 

The trains were also bringing in thousands of sightseers. Drygoods houses which had been burnt out were arranging their wares for sale in hotel dining rooms and halls. Reports were coming in regarding the fire from outside. It had been seen sixty miles away up in the New Hamp- [105]

shire hills, and the brilliance of the sky was noticed by sailors three hundred miles at sea.

Pieces of burned paper had been carried by the wind for miles. Burned parts of check books and ledgers were found in East Weymouth and a badly discolored fifty dollar bill was picked up in 

South Abington twenty-one miles away. 

The city was without gas until Wednesday night and candles and kerosene lamps were in great demand. The theatres were all obliged to close. The liquor bars were also closed day and night-not because of lack of light but because of the great crowds which had come into the city --many of them for no legitimate purposes. One of the disgusted visitors was heard to exclaim, "What's the use of having such a hell of a fire in 

Boston if a feller can't get a drink!" 

But the city was trying valiantly to get back to normal. Although the new Transcript Building was destroyed with its contents the paper came out as usual Monday afternoon but, through the courtesy of The Globe, used that paper's long article on the origin and course of the fire. 

Virgil interrupted his search long enough to go to the crowded meeting in Tremont Temple [ 106 ]

at noon Wednesday to consider measures of relief. 

Mayor Gaston presided. Governor Washburn was on his right and Senator Wilson on his left, and the hymn, "God is a Refuge of His Saints,' was led by Dudley Buck.

When he reached home Virgil read in his 

Wednesday afternoon Transcript, that Bret

Harte, having refused to keep his contract with the Boston Lyceum, the managers have secured 

Oliver Wendell Holmes who will lecture in Music 

Hall tonight-Wednesday-November 13. 

Froude was to lecture the next day in Tremont

Temple on "Ireland under the Tudors and 

Stuarts." And, Virgil continued to read, "The weather for three days has been of the finest autumn ever furnishes."—"Moonlight has been on hand to supply the place of gas light, and for the most part the wind has taken the smoke out to sea."-"Gas is promised the city tonight. This is indeed good news."-"The Theatres will resume their performances this evening if the gas is turned on as it is promised."

TWELFTH CHAPTER

CHAPTER TWELVE 

The finding of Hilary still continued to be, for Virgil, a supreme motive. Everything else was thrust into the background. No stone must be left unturned in the search for him. No effort was too great and no expenditure of money too excessive for the achievement of the desired end.

But a strange thing had happened to Virgil. 

He felt that he was searching for his boy Richard instead of for Hilary. He had only seen Hilary for a very short time. All he could recall about his looks was his smile and it was so like Richard's that he could no longer distinguish the two. As the excitement of the days went by, Hilary sank more and more into the background while the reality of Richard was always present. Of course intellectually he knew he was searching for 

Hilary, but emotionally he was looking, ever looking, for Richard. He did not try to check [ 111 ]

this feeling. He even encouraged it. He was seeking for his own flesh and blood,-his own child who had been lost, not merely for a few days, but for over ten years. 

At last there was a ray of hope. John heard that a boy and a dog had been seen that morning in the Fitchburg Railroad freight-yard. Nobody had seen him get into a freight car but what was he doing there unless he intended to do this?

Virgil hurried down to see his friend, C. L. Heywood, the Superintendent of the road. It did not take long to explain his mission. In a short time the wires were rushing orders from the head of the road to Ayer Junction, to Fitchburg and to 

Greenfield: "Search all cars carefully," the wires hummed. "Search all cars carefully for small boy and black and white dog. Report me personally every two hours until midnight." 

Virgil waited till midnight with Mr. Heywood. 

The reports came in regularly. "Search going on. 

No boy and dog found. Search will be continued according to orders, etc., etc." Virgil's hopes were unrealized and at midnight he walked wearily up 

Bowdoin Street on his way home. Strange, strange. Here a week has passed and not a single [ 112 ]

clue to the whereabouts of the boy since the fireman had seen him rush out of the burning house with his dog. 

When he opened his Beacon Street door

Hannah was waiting up for him as usual and at once went out to the kitchen for some hot milk. "It's a discouraging thing, Hannah, but we do not seem to be making a bit of headway; yet we must keep at it. Eventually we must be successful. I am going down again to the police in the morning. Until today I have been searching only in Boston. I haven't thought the boy would leave the city. Tomorrow I am going to begin a State-wide search." "You'll find him, Sir. I'm sure of that, if you keep on; unless," Hannah hesitated, "unless, Sir, he was killed in the fire. You know a good many people were killed and several are still missing. 

With the falling walls and the dreadful heat and smoke and the excited crowds, a small boy might easily have been severely injured or killed." 

Virgil's face became drawn and grey. "Yes

Hannah, I have thought of that. I have already been around to all the hospitals to see if any small boy who had been injured was in any of them. [ 113 ]

I did find two boys who had been hurt. One had jumped out of a second story window and broken a leg and the other had a brain injury from a flying piece of stone. They had dismissed two boys from the City Hospital, but neither of them was anything like Richard—” he hesitated, "like 

Hilary, I mean." "Of course, Sir, the boy's body would probably have been found if he had been killed." "Yes, Hannah, unless it's under fallen walls, or unless," Virgil could hardly say the words, "unless it was burned up."

It was time for Hannah to try to take control: "It's late, Sir, surely you should be getting to bed. You have had a hard day. Many hard days. 

Unless you are careful, you will not be able to go on with this. If you want to find the boy you will have to try and get more hours of rest.' 

"Yes, I suppose so. But I can't seem to stop,

Hannah. I am so anxious to find him I have to keep going." "Yes, Sir, but you can't keep going unless you save yourself some." "Well, good night, Hannah. We'll hope for better things for tomorrow."

THIRTEENTH CHAPTER

CHAPTER THIRTEEN 

When the morrow came Virgil went first of all to the head of the police. He gladly agreed to send out requests to the police in the various cities and towns of the State. "Tell me about the boy," the Chief said. "Well, he was ten years old," Virgil answered, “and he had a small black and white dog." "But what kind of clothes did he have on?" Virgil could not tell him. "Greyish, I think,” was all he could say. "And what did he look like?" But when Virgil tried to think, it was Richard's face and Richard's smile he saw before him. "Well," said the Chief, "about all we can say is, 'Small boy, ten years with small black and white dog.' Perhaps that will be enough—that is, if the boy and dog only keep together." Soon all over the State, even down to Provincetown on the Cape and across the mountains to North Adams [ 117 ]

and Pittsfield, the police of Massachusetts were looking for a small boy and his dog.

From the police headquarters Virgil went to call on the editors of all the Boston daily papers. 

He knew most of them personally and when he asked for their coöperation in finding Hilary there was not a moment's hesitation. As the presses of the afternoon papers were rolling they were printing words which thousands of eyes would read that very afternoon. "Look for small boy with his dog. Lost in the great fire. Last seen running out of burning house with his dog in his arms. 

That night the morning papers were setting up in their composing rooms the full story of Hilary; of his dead mother, of the rescue of his dog and of the complete disappearance of the boy ever since he had rushed out of the burning house. Bright and early the next morning the people of the State were reading and talking about the disappearance of Hilary and his dog, and the State began looking for them. "Surely, Hannah, with people, men, women and children, all over the State looking for the boy he must be found-unless-" Virgil did not [ 118 ]

finish the sentence. Yes, Hannah thought, unless he was killed in the fire. When several days passed and nothing came of the State-wide publicity, Hannah said to herself-he must be dead.

Virgil was silent; silent and grave. As a last resort he went to see his old lawyer who through the years had often given him good advice. 

As he entered the familiar office, and waited after he had sent in his name, he thought to himself: This is my last chance. If Joseph can't advise something I must give up the search. I have done everything in my power.

Virgil did not have to wait long. He was shown in almost immediately and greeted cordially by Joseph. "What's all this, Virgil, about a lost boy I hear you are trying to find?"

When Virgil told him, Joseph-who had known Virgil ever since they went to the Boston Latin School together and who loved him like a brother-asked, "You are resolved to find this boy?" "I must keep on searching for him until I [ 119 ]

either find him or I'm convinced he met death in the fire. Joseph, it's imperative with me." "Are you willing, Virgil, to spend a good deal of money in this search?" "Money, Joseph, is a secondary matter. I must find my boy." "Very well, Virgil, then I advise you to send an adequate telegram to Pinkerton's National

Detective Agency, 66 Exchange Place, New 

York City. They are able to start a trained investigator at work tomorrow in every city and town in the State." "Why, thank you Joseph. Why didn't I think of that in the beginning? I will go at once and send the message. Goodbye Joseph, and thank you again." 

The next morning the man sent from New 

York by Allan Pinkerton came for an interview.

After that there was nothing for Virgil to do but wait to wait as patiently as possible-to wait with the constant dread that the boy would not be found because he had been one more victim of the fire. Hannah was convinced that this had happened and in her wise, quiet way she tried to prepare Virgil for this final tragedy in his life. [ 120 ]

Virgil fought hard to go on in the even tenor of his way. But Hannah, who had been in the same house with him for many years, and had seen him meet the greatest sorrows mankind is called upon to bear-meet them with quiet dignity and selfforgetting courage,-Hannah knew what Virgil was passing through. She was not deceived by his outward calm and perfect self-control. And her heart ached for him. The passing years had taught her to almost worship this restrained, chivalrous man who increased in gentle reserve as the darts of fate cut the deeper.

FOURTEENTH CHAPTER

CHAPTER FOURTEEN 

And so the days came and went-morning, noon and night; morning, noon and night. Outwardly everything went on as usual, but the house was filled with a suspense which became harder and harder to bear. Hannah had given up hope sometime ago. Virgil was clinging to it as a drowning man clings to a straw. 

Then, as he was eating breakfast one morning, a telegram came. Virgil read: “Boy found. Come to Greenfield today eleven o'clock train from 

Boston. Will meet you."

Virgil looked up at Hannah and simply said, "They have found him." Hannah burst into tears and went into the kitchen to hide her lack of self-control. She returned later with more muffins, wholly self-contained. "Hannah, I am leaving on the eleven o'clock train from the Fitchburg station. Please tell John to be ready to take [125]

me. I wish you would pack my smallest bag.' "Yes, Sir." "I hope we will be back tomorrow afternoon. 

If not, I will telegraph."

The next day after Virgil had shown a small boy up the stairs to the upper hall, which led to Richard's room, he paused an instant at his wife's door and said, half to himself and half aloud: "Dear Sylvia, Richard has come home."
