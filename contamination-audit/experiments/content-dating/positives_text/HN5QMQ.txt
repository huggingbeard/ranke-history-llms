INTRODUCTION 

You are on the threshold of another season of golf. In fancy you can see yourself on the first tee, the other members of your favorite foursome gathered behind you, your own ball teed up, your driver in your hand. You have taken your stance, soled your club, the fairway stretches green and inviting before you you you—you are about to begin. What will the rest of the story be?

Will your drive sail true and far down the middle, will your long iron shots come sparkling off the club, your approaches land smack on the green, hop once and snuggle down tight to the pin for an easy single putt? Will your scores come creeping down and down, and the joy of steady and sure improvement be yours throughout the year? Or are you just going to go through all the same old grind again, never better, often worse, hooking, slicing, topping your way around the course, looking up, shanking into the tall uncut, growing twenty years older in ten minutes blasting in a bunker. your golf game a source of mirth to others, of mental anguish to yourself. 

The answer is up to you, and the right answer is in your hand. Within reason, you can make the future of your game of golf what you like, Nine

and the author of this book is the man above all others who can help you to make it something you will really like. The combination in one individual of a top flight golfer and a superlative teacher is so rare as to be very nearly unique. Roland Wingate combines a knowledge of the game of golf, an analytical comprehension of it from fundamentals to finest shades of finesse, with a life time of experience in teaching it and an unparalleled gift for imparting his own knowledge to others.

There are thousands of good golfers, and there are thousands of good teachers, but not of golf. 

The number of those who are both is small indeed, but the writer of this book is par excellence one of them. You may take it from one who has proved it in his own person that the author of Saving Strokes has no superior in the gentle art of showing you how to do that very thing.

AUTHOR'S FOREWORD 

In writing this manual on golf it has been my intention to bring about a better understanding of the game, to endorse those principles which have been proven by time and experience, and to take issue with some other commonly accepted ideas.

During the past eight or ten years the popularity of golf has increased tremendously. In addition to our private courses, we have in many sizeable communities Municipal courses operated by the towns and cities, where one may utilize the facilities of the course by paying a small fee, and numerous golf clubs which on certain days and hours solicit the patronage of the public. The result has been that there are now many more golfers, both men and women, who have a keen desire to improve their game, but have found that competent instruction was too expensive to be realized. This book is designed for them. I am sure that careful study of the following instructions and practise based on them, will have a very beneficial result. 

If this manual proves to be an instrument in improving the game of the reader, or in any way helps to popularize the game of golf, I will feel that my object has been achieved. Eleven

WINGATE GRIP 

If the reader will carefully study Illustration. 

No. 1, I will first explain the position of the hands, which is known as the "grip." The one which I am about to describe, is known as the "WINGATE GRIP," and is a combination of the interlocking and overlapping grips. These two have been for years the standardized methods of holding the club, but with all due deference to tradition, I feel that this new grip is a decided improvement on both. It was arrived at only after a great deal of study and experimentation, and while I know of no other instructor who teaches it, I have convinced myself of its merits only after the most thorough tests, both by myself and by my pupils. 

First, place the left hand only on the club, see that the club face is square to the ball, in this position the player must be able to see three knuckles of the back of the left hand. The thumb is directly against the right side of the shaft, and the tip of the thumb and the knuckle Fifteen

of the forefinger are directly opposite one another, with the ball of the thumb pressed firmly against the shaft. Open up the forefinger of the left hand. Now bring the right hand to the club, sliding the right little finger around between the left forefinger and second finger, so that its tip comes to rest on the web between the forefinger and second finger of the left hand. The natural groove of the heel of the right hand must be fitted firmly into the curve between the big knuckle and the joint of the left thumb. Next, still carefully keeping the left forefinger wide open, close the three fingers of the right hand around the shaft, pulling them up as snugly as possible against the left hand. This should bring the ring finger of the right hand slightly under the forefinger of the left hand, which you will now close. It is particularly important to note that this left forefinger does not come in contact with the shaft at any point, but rests entirely upon the little and ring fingers of the right hand.

If you have carefully followed the directions up to this point, you will recall that when we had finished placing the left hand on the shaft, the tip of the left thumb and the knuckle of the left forefinger were described, as being directly opposite one another. Now that you have come to finishing the placing of the right hand, you should find the tip of the right Sixteen

BACKSWING. 

I have often been asked the question, "What is the correct way to start the backswing?" 

One says start it with the left wrist, another says drag the club back, the third will say start the backswing from the left hip, the knee, the arm, the shoulder, in fact any part of the anatomy you care to mention. My own con- Twenty

ception of the beginning of the backswing is that the body should operate as a unit, without any joints or hinges, but that the mind must be consciously concentrated on the functioning of the left side. Continue this motion as far as possible, which should bring the club head to approximately the height of your waist. At this point the nature of the swing changes of necessity and the arms take on the function of carrying the club further back and up. The two movements will flow naturally together, the second simply being a continuation of the arc started by the first motion. 

At this point the left arm should be as straight as it is comfortably possible, avoiding like poison any tendency to lock the elbow. Here also we have reached a second natural physical limit, with the club approximately straight up and down the body turn, extended by the arm swing, has gone to its fullest extent. The further development of the back swing is taken over by the wrists which in their turn, reach a natural limit when the club has been swung to a position approximately parallel with the ground. It is advisable for the player to remember that at first better results are obtained by not overdoing the full development of the back swing. See Illustration No. 4. Here we come to grips with one of the great outstanding myths of the game of golf, the over-emphasis of the importance of the follow through. It will be impossible for the player to have anything to do with golf without being the recipient of an ocean of well intentioned advice directing him to concentrate his attention on developing his follow through, by which is meant the progress of the club head after the ball has been hit. It is wise for the player to bear in mind that the club does not maintain contact with the ball for more than 1/16 of an inch, no matter how fast it is traveling when the initial contact is made. This statement is indisputably proved by tests carried out under rigid scientific control with super speed motion picture cameras. It is perfectly obvious. that having generated a club head speed of somewhere around 90 miles an hour, this club head is bound to continue to go somewhere after the ball has been hit. The natural collapse of the resistance which we have previously built up in the left side of the body must now begin to take place.

When the back swing and the downswing have been correctly performed the follow through will naturally and inevitably take place. Due to the method of starting the down swing which I have taught you to employ, the club head is bound to approach the ball from a point somewhat inside the imaginary line of flight. This is called "hitting from the inside" and is probably the one most nearly indispensible good habit for the player to cultivate. The vast amount of harm done to the average player by the over-emphasis of the importance of the follow through, results from the fact that in concentrating his attention on this item of the swing, he really begins his follow through before the ball has been hit, which makes hitting from the inside an utter impossibility. After a correctly completed backswing and down swing, he can no more prevent or distort a proper follow through than he can deflect the course of a rifle bullet by mental suggestion.

POSITIONS FOR SHORTER IRONS. 

The address and swing for the shorter irons requires different treatment. The question which I am most commonly asked in regard to these clubs is, "How far should I swing back?" The player should not anymore than is unavoidable know how far he swings back with any club. The fact that these irons are shorter in length than the clubs heretofore described will to a great extent govern the length of the back swing for you, as is clearly indicated in Illustration No. 9, which shows the top of the backswing with the No. 4 iron, and Illustration No. 10, which does the same for the No. 5 iron, or mashie. What is far Twenty-eight

more necessary than any conscious effort to control or graduate the length of the backswing with the short irons is a clear conception of the growing importance of a correct address. 

The reader will recall that emphasis was placed on this before beginning the full swing. 

Great as this importance was, it becomes even greater as the player progresses to the use of the short irons. The most important modification of the address as the short irons come into use, is the progressive opening of the stance.

The best way for the player to accomplish this position is first to assume the original address for the wooden club. Standing in this position advance the right foot about 3 or 4 inches immediately toward the ball, and toe out about 2 inches with the left foot, as in Illustration No. 11. In doing this, the player will notice that his hands have automatically shifted into a position slightly in front of the club head. 

The instinctive reaction of the player when he notices this position of the hands, is a feeling that it must be wrong, and a corresponding tendency to pull them back onto a line with the club head and the ball. This tendency must be resisted. The hands are quite properly ahead of the club head in addressing the ball for shots with the shorter irons. The player must also resist any tendency to compensate for the shorter length of the shafts of these irons by reaching out with his arms. He must Twenty-nine

make up for this difference in length of shaft by adjusting his position so that he is actually standing closer to the ball, and he must see to it that his hands are kept in the same reasonably compact relation to his body that they were in when he addressed the ball with the longer shafted wooden clubs. BACK SWING, that is to say at the instant that the club head starts to move away from the ball, the player must devote his attention to keeping the sole of the club as close as possible to the ground for as long a distance back as he can comfortably do so. THE CHIP SHOT 

We now come to an entirely different type of shot, generally spoken of as a chip shot, a term which, while it serves admirably to cover the general classification of shot which it is used to describe, may be said to be in itself a trifle misleading, in that the impression conveyed is that of a shot produced only by a very short, sharp blow. The true nature of the chip shot, the characteristic which serves to set it apart from other golf shots, particularly those Thirty-five

heretofore considered, is the fact that it is a less than maximum shot, only called for when the distance that the player must obtain with the shot is a negligible factor and extreme accuracy of direction and placement has become the controlling requirement. 

The player will now do well to consult Illustration No. 14. He will at once be struck by the general impression of compactness which is given by the position assumed in the address for the chip. While the stance as would naturally be expected, has been opened up, the feet have been brought materially nearer together. 

The heel of the left foot is now seen to be resting against the base line, with the toe opened out to an angle of approximately 45 degrees to the left. The right toe has been brought up to within a matter of 3 inches of the base line and is now pointing directly at the ball. The left arm is a perfect continuation of a straight line drawn right up the shaft of the club, through the left forearm and up to the point of the left shoulder. It is not to be assumed for a moment that, because I call attention to the straightness of this line from the heel of the club to the point of the left shoulder, I intend to convey any impression of rigidity. The exact opposite of tension as regards the left arm itself must be your object. 

The elbow must be flexed, never by any chance locked back or tense. The under side of the Thirty-six

top of the right forearm is another important point in the address for this shot. The elbow itself is distinctly flexed, and while the forearm is by no means to be pressed or glued to the right leg, it must be kept very close indeed to this leg, in fact so close that the sleeve is actually brushing, even though very lightly, the clothing of the player. 

Again attention is called to the position of the head. Once more the further opening up of the stance may well lead to an unconscious relaxation of care in this particular. Deliberate attention must be given to resist any tendency to allow the head to turn toward the left with the body. The chin must be kept back and in, and emphasis placed on its being pointed at the ground behind and inside the ball, as we have described its position before. 

The balance of the body may now be defined as being two thirds on the left heel. In a word or two the best method for executing this shot successfully may be outlined as follows. Be careful of the position of the sole of the club in addressing the ball, take the club back low to the ground, keep steady but not rigid on your feet, and in bringing the club head down and through to the ball, allow your right hand and arm to have much the same sensation of steady rhythmic sweep that it would have if, instead of playing a golf shot, you were starting a bowling ball down the alley. Thirty-seven

Because of the fact that in addressing the ball for the chip shot I have placed the player in this open position, he often feels completely at sea as to the direction which the ball is going to take when it leaves his club. If he will bear in mind his imaginary line of flight, see to it that the line of the sole of the club is placed perfectly at right angles across that line, and if he will take a club head back low and straight along this line of flight, the ball will infallibly fly on that same line.

The instructions here given will serve to initiate the inexperienced player into the making of simple straight forward chip shots. It is my intention to develop more fully at a somewhat later point some of the subtler varieties of this fascinating golf shot. It is also my intention to reserve for discussion later in a separate section of its own, the principal remaining type of golf shot, the bunker shot.

PUTTING 

In spirit we have now taken the player from the tee, through the use of the wooden club, the long irons, the short irons, and the chip shot to the green where he now makes his first acquaintance with the mystery of mysteries of the game of golf, a department which has been reverently and blasphemously referred to, as a game within a game, Putting. 

There is no particular mystery to putting. Thirty-eight

Thirty-nine

While the results may be as weird and mysterious as you like, the method employed is simplicity in itself, and the physical ability required entirely within the scope of a six year old child. All you have to do it to take the smallest, shortest and perhaps the lightest club in your set, select a line from your ball to the hole, stroke the ball with a maximum amount of effort of a few ounces, or a minimum amount of practically nothing at all, and propel it from wherever it lies on the green into the cup. Putting is as simple as that. In endeavoring to accomplish this childishly simple stroke, you may assume any position to which the human body can accommodate itself. You may put your feet where you please, you may grasp the club in your hands in any manner which seems good to you, you may look at the ball, or you may look behind the ball, or even at the hole. Every individual golfer is a law unto himself, and to nobody else when it comes to putting. In a few seasons of playing the game you will probably adopt and discard more putters, more putting stances, more putting techniques, and more general putting fuss and feathers than all the other clubs, stances, methods and ideas that you will use in all the other parts of the game put together.

I have now taken the player through a rough outline of the swing and the principle fundamental shots of the game. I have taught him the proper positions for his feet, his hands, his head and his body in detail, and I have analyzed the various parts of the backswing, the down swing and the finish of the swing. I will now assume that the player has progressed through the various stages taken under consideration up to now, and has acquired a game of golf.

From observation, it is my conviction that the vast majority of golfers belong in the 90 to 100 

or "Business Man's Golf" category. The nov ice or inexperienced player will naturally be keenly interested to know how long it will take to arrive at this point. Depending somewhat on the capacity of the individual, it is safe to say that with a reasonable amount of attention to the game, a couple of seasons will suffice to develop the novice into a golfer of this general class. Many individuals will be able to record as much progress as this in only a single season; others again may learn a little more slowly, but nearly all should find it possible arrive at the point of being able to break a 100 and to score somewhere in the 90's by the end of their second season of play. It is my intention as the title indicates to devote myself to this great body of golfers in the present section of the book. Forty-five

From the point of view of the novice, the development of sufficient proficiency to enable him even once to break a hundred, let alone the ability to score consistently in the nineties, seems like a goal almost beyond achievement.

To the actual 90 to 100 player, this range of scoring seems like a slough of despond, out of which he will never be able to extricate himself. Now, it does not seem to me, that even though the business man golfer has his business, and the woman has her innumerable duties to perform, either one or the other should feel forever more tied down to this particular range of scoring. It is apt to be the impression of the player of this calibre that his game is about as good as his business will allow it to be. The woman golfer also may feel that she cannot spare as much time as she would like to the game, and that consequently it is practically impossible to register any further improvement in her game. I feel very strongly that, while we must grant that the game demands a certain amount of time and practice, the attitude which I have here described is primarily only an excuse for allowing the game to remain at this stage, which is that of let us say, a player with two season's experience. It is really something even less worthy than an excuse. It is, if the truth be frankly admitted, only another of golf's innumerable alibis, Forty-six

Another very common idea is that, if the player started the game at anything but an early age, only a limited standard of proficiency can possibly be obtained. Hundreds and thousands of exceptions prove conclusively that this idea is also a fallacy. One does not have to be in a position to devote hours and hours, and days and days to practice; nor must one have had the good fortune to have been initiated into the mysteries of the game of golf at the socalled "plastic" or imitative age. Improvement over whatever degree of ability you have already attained can always be made by the application of proper methods of thinking to the use of whatever amount of time you may have at your disposal, and the 90 to 100 player probably has a better chance of making this improvement in a concrete. tangible, and unmistakable manner than has any other player in the game.

One of the things which probably contributes more than anything else, except the general defeatist attitude referred to above, to the difficulty of getting this player out of his rut, is the fact, that after he has been taught the fundamentals of the game for a sufficient length of time to allow these actions to become as nearly as possible mechanical, he fails to progress mentally as far beyond his novice condition as he has progressed physically. To be explicit, it now becomes necessary for him to forget Forty-seven

somewhat the mechanical detail of what is happening during his swing, to divert his complete concentration away from the complicated maneuvers which he has been taught to perform, in taking the club head back and in bringing it down, and to focus this complete concentration on the one really vital essential of the whole business HITTING THE 

BALL. It has been proven by experience that the result of a shot is never good when the mind is overloaded with thoughts regarding the mechanism employed in making it. It is difficult at this stage of the game for an instructor to convey to his pupil the knowledge that the reason that the pupil has been trained to think carefully of all of his physical positions up to this time, was that he might later be allowed to play the game in peace, and not with a mind cluttered up with a jumble of ideas regarding his elbows, knees, feet, etc., yet this should be the very reason for the emphasis which was quite rightly placed on all this physical detail at the beginning. It can only be learned through the mind, it must then be taught by the mind to the muscles, and it should then be forgotten by the mind. The muscles never forget.

Contrary to the almost universal estimate of the game, the short game is invariably the good golfer's strongest weapon.

From the tee to the green the game of golf divides itself into two major sections. The first, the long game, may be characterized by the quality of over-spin. In all shots where maximum distance is the principal end to be achieved, the player's object is to hit the ball so that it is spinning away from him, in order that desirable extra yardage after the ball has landed will be contributed by the roll. The second, the short game, is primarily characterized by the quality of backspin. Here maximum distance is no longer required, accuracy and control having taken its place as the desired objects. To obtain these objects, the player must endeavor to hit the ball in such a Fifty-eight

THE BUNKER SHOT. 

While it is not my intention, and is very far from my desire to wish the player any hard luck, he will inevitably get into a bunker sooner or later. When the average inexperienced player finds himeslf in this predicament, his immediate reaction seems to be one of complete panic. There is no real necessity for this feeling at all, and it is far more often the panic which brings about the disastrous results of his attempt to extricate himself from the position in which he finds himself. than the inherent difficulty of the shot itself. True, a bunker is a nasty place, no one wants to be there, but if the player will approach his problem just as calmly as he would a simple fairway shot, armed with a perfectly steady set of nerves, and the calm assurance that an equally simple shot is certain to succeed 9 times out of 

10, he will experience no more difficulty in Seventy-two

getting out of a bunker than he would playing from a good lie on the fairway. 

I desire right here to address another word direct to the women golfers. It has been my observation that they are particularly susceptible to bunker panic, and it is my conviction they believe that owing to their slighter physical power, being bunkered is about as nearly a fatal catastrophe as can befall them in the playing of a round of golf. I want to say that everything which I have just said in connection with the bunker shot in general, applies to them ever more specifically. It is only very rarely indeed that any exceptional amount of power must be expended in executing a recovery shot. By far the vast majority of bunker shots call only for care, skill, and above all perfect coolness and confidence. 

When you are confronted by a bunker shot, there is no earthly necessity for immediately putting yourself down for a penalty of 2 or 3 shots. Instead of doing this let it be your mental attitude that you are not only going to get out of that bunker at your first attempt, but that in addition to this you are going to place the ball as nearly as possible exactly where you want it. This means that if the hole be within reach from where you are bunkered, you are going to attempt to put the ball close, and I mean very close to it. You will probably Seventy-three

The address in general bears a marked similarity to the address for the chip shot; the feet a very little wider apart, the heel of the left foot about on the imaginary base line, the right foot a trifle further back from the base line with the right toe more nearly parallel with it. The position of the head and body are approximately the same as in the chip shot, the balance, however, to be kept steady on both heels throughout the entire shot. The right forearm should be a little freer from the body. Avoid by all means allowing any tension to develop in the body or in the arms. Merely because you are faced by what habit leads you to consider a difficult shot, do not allow this largely imaginary difficulty to lead you into the dangerous error of tightening up in order to meet and overcome it. Your care should be directed toward achieving the absolutely opposite condition. The shot simply does not call for any such do or die preparation, and as a matter of actual fact, cannot be successfully executed if it is approached in this manner. Seventy-five

It is wise for the player to notice at this time the position of the club face. The club is well laid back, the face very open, and for the first time in addressing the ball for any shot whatsoever, we depart from the fundamental principle of squaring the sole of the club across the line of flight. Here the toe of the club is distinctly pointed away from the back of the ball. Another item of very considerate importance is the fact that the ball is no longer opposite the center line of the club face, but is approximately half way out from that center line toward the toe of the club. This is done in order to lessen the risk of shanking, which is the most frequent cause of failure when attempting bunker shots. Placing the ball toward the toe of the club helps to counteract the tendency, which we all have, to lean forward in the act of hitting the ball. Should we do this when the ball is so placed it gives just that extra amount of club face which can cross the line of flight and still result in a hit with the true center of the face. Even if we do not thus fall in on the ball, and the ball is struck with the exact portion of the face with which it was lined up in the address, a satisfactory shot will still result.

While we are on this matter of shanking let me remark that, on most occasions when a player is convinced that he has failed to negotiate a recovery shot from a bunker because Seventy-six

he has topped the ball, the true explanation is that he has really shanked the ball. Owing to the confused feeling resulting from hitting in sand, and the general further confusion resulting from his collapse in morale, he is not able to make the clear distinction which would be almost automatic with him were he analyzing the cause of a shot missed in the fairway. 

Needless to say the club cannot be soled in addressing a ball for this shot. Its position must be adjusted as carefully as possible, slightly in the air. The beginning of the backswing must still be kept low, but in view of the fact that cut or backspin must be imparted to the ball in order to execute the shot, the line of the back swing will vary somewhat, according to the requirements of the individual making the shot. Some players are able to get all the height and cut required when the club is taken straight back along the line of flight, just as in playing an ordinary straight away pitch shot. Others will find that it will be necessary for them to start the club back on an angle slightly outside of the imaginary line of flight. The amount of angle at its very greatest should be very slight, and the amount required to meet your individual needs can be determined only by actual experiment. 

Above all things bear in mind, that you must not under any circumstances pick the club up straight away into the air from behind the ball Seventy-seven

in a wood-chopping motion. To do so will only lead to burying the club head in the sand and produce nothing in the way of results. 

Keep the start of the backswing low. Do not attempt to pick the ball clean when using this method. On the other hand rid yourself of the extremely common notion that it is necessary to go delving down into the sand to extreme depths in order to execute the shot.

Obviously the sole of the club must go under the bottom of the ball, but if you make sure of accomplishing just that much, you have done enough in the way of excavating. This is a cut shot. It is neither a clean shot nor an explosion, and should not have any tendency in either of those directions. It is not necessary to get hold of any large amount of sand in order to make sure of getting hold of the ball. The natural spin which vou impart to the ball in this method of playing the shot will give you all the height and stop you need.

THE FOUR IRON 

We now come to the No. 4 Iron. This club is the modern development of the old time "jigger," a club which had a very shallow blade, and was used when the mashie would not quite make the distance required. Our present day No. 4 iron is an improvement over the old "jigger" as the blade is just enough deeper to allow us more margin for hitting the ball without encountering the danger of slipping the club head under the ball, when the lie is such that the ball is sitting up. The average player will find the club one of his favorites, as it will meet the demands of a wide range of situations, as well as play its correct distance to the green. I would advise that when you feel that the lie you have in the rough is not good enough for a spoon, and there is no chance of getting home. the best and safest club to play is a No. 4. Don't allow yourself to be tempted in a situation like this into using the 1, 2 or 3 irons, on account of any slight extra distance you feel you might get with them. The No. 4 will get you out and a little bit more than that, without running the Eighty-one

risk of making a complete failure of the shot in an effort to do too much. 

Your method of hitting with this club should not be changed from that used with any other iron. Remember that all iron clubs require the employment of the same fundamental principle a descending blow. As you work up to this club from the shorter irons, the address will of course have become much less compact, and it is not necessary for you to picture to yourself any new ideas other than those recommended for the No. 5 iron. All that will be needed is the simple, comfortable adjustment, to the greater length of the club. 

THE LONG IRONS 

The No. 1, 2 and 3 irons seem to be the most difficult clubs in the bag for the average player to handle. It does not seem to me that there is any adequate reason for this, except possibly for the fact that these clubs are not used quite as often as the shorter irons, or even as the wooden clubs. There is a point in connection with the use of these clubs. which may be of interest. Practically everyone seems to worry about the proper length of the back swing when using these clubs, even to the extent of conceiving that it should be intentionally cut down. This is wrong. Since these clubs are shorter in the shaft than the wood clubs, the address position which they bring you into is Eighty-two

naturally more compact and will tend automatically to take care of the top of the back swing for you. Bear in mind at all times that the most dangerous thing for you to concentrate your attention on, in any shot you play is the top of your back swing. When you focus your mind on the top of your backswing you are removing it as far as possible from what must be your true target, the ball.

In this talk of the long irons, the reader might well assume that I would naturally go into a fair amount of detail regarding these clubs. 

Instead of going over all this ground again, it will be quite enough for us to bear in mind merely the clubs and positions which have previously been analyzed completely. Do not attempt to do anything different with the mechanical detail of your swing when using these clubs. Leave all this back in the sub-conscious mind, remember only that all these iron clubs call for the same type of swing. Make a careful study of the positions of address in the illustrations. THE WOODS 

When we come to the wood clubs, we arrive at a part of the game which probably produces the biggest thrill for the majority of golfers.

We are all familiar with the feeling, which comes to us when we are 25 yards out ahead of the other three players in our foursome.

Perhaps not as familiar as we would like to be, nevertheless we know it. 

Let us look into all this business of terrific driving and brassie work, and try to find out what it is really worth. It must be granted that we should all like to have the long game as good and as long as is possible, but we must bear in mind the fact that we all have a natural limit beyond which we cannot expect to hit the ball, no matter how well the swing is performed. It is also a self-evident fact, that a 250 yard drive is no use to the player who cannot keep it under control. The grip which 

I have given you will be of great assistance in controlling the wood shots, for when the punch of the right hand side of the body is applied to generate power in the club head, to obtain a strong shot, the left hand, wrist and arm are in an ideal position to withstand this pressure, and keep the base of the swing and the club face under full control. You will Eighty-five

notice that both hands have their duty to perform, which leads me to call attention at this point to a very common misconception of the game of golf. You will hear a great deal said. about the dominant importance of the left hand in golf, and you are apt to come to think of the game as a one handed affair. I cannot too strongly insist that golf is very much a two handed game. No player can achieve his own maximum distance if the right side is not doing its full share of the work. You will also hear a good deal about the desirability of not attempting to knock the cover off the ball. 

This is a very good thought and in first learning the game it has stood you in good stead, but at this stage of your development, I would suggest that you put into your full swing all the natural abandon that you can not with the thought of distance uppermost in your mind, as this will make you force the shot, but with the feeling that you are giving all you have got in developing speed in the club head, while still keeping a proper amount of rhythm. 

Let us imagine that we are standing on a tee, with a narrow fairway in front of us, bounded on both sides by woods, and let us try to find out what goes on in the mind of the average golfer. His first reaction is of course to try at all costs to keep the ball straight, and not slice or hook. When this is the picture which he has drawn for himself, his chances of pro- Eighty-six

ducing a good shot are slim at best. It is impossible to "steer" a ball straight for the reason that, with this only in mind the player becomes anxious, his muscles tense, and his swing chokes up. Simply because he has not the right idea in mind when he attempts to play the shot, no amount of advice I could give him regarding his swing would be of any use to him now. 

The next time you are faced by such a situation, do a little correct thinking about it. Call to your own attention the fact that this swing of yours has hit the ball reasonably straight before now, and that you will give it the fullest chance to do so this time, by not holding it back. Pay special attention to the thought that only the shots which are hit with a sense of abandon are really good, and that you have learned to swing correctly especially in order to make use of this valuable freedom. Remember, the only shot in the game that is really hard to play is the one that looks easy. 

The situation which I have here imagined presumes that the driver is the club in question, but it might just as well be the brassie or the spoon, as there is no difference worth mentioning between any of these clubs, as far as the method of swing is concerned. In this connection, there are a couple of thoughts which occur to me which may settle some of your doubts. When the average player is not driving well it seems, strangely enough, that Eighty-seven

the last thought which will occur to him is that his brassie will do a better job for him. 

There is nothing in the least incorrect about falling back temporarily to the brassie for your tee shots, when your driver has begun to fail you. It is the logical and sensible thing to do, as the extra loft of the face will give you a feeling of increased confidence, which will be followed by a freer swing, leading to a better shot, and I doubt very much if you will notice any appreciable loss of distance from the tee. 

To my mind, the spoon, the junior member of the wooden club family, is a grand club. It seems able to do almost anything you demand of it, whether the shot is to be played from a good lie in the fairway or from a middle distance bunker. Naturally in the last instance, the ball must be away from the bank, and have a reasonably clean lie. It is possible that nine golfers out of ten would name the spoon as their favorite club, and with good reason. 

Do not feel when you see another player using a No. 1 iron, that there is any reason why you should not be just as accurate with your spoon, and never allow yourself to be shamed out of using it when it is the club you really want. 

COMMON FAULTS 

In turning now for a while to a discussion of some of the common faults in golf, let me say Eighty-eight

a preliminary word of general advice. When a player develops an outstanding fault, that is to say, when the same type of error manifests itself so consistently and frequently in his game as to give indication of becoming a habit, the only thing for him to do is to get out on the practise tee and go to work in a deliberate effort to eradicate his failing. If he does not know the reason for his failing, he should get someone to look into it and tell him. Above all things, he should not let such a peristent weakness definitely develop into a habit, as it will only take longer to cure it, the longer he lets it run.

THE SLICE 

Unquestionably the commonest fault which the average golfer manifests is the slice. There are many reasons for slicing a ball, but far and away the principal one of these is an incorrect groove line in the swing, i. e., the player cuts across the ball from the outside to the inside, causing the ball to have a left to right spin. This is commonly the result of the player's anxiety to hit the ball, which brings about what is known as "hitting too soon."

When this happens, the downward arc of the club head is thrown out and forward. This extends the player's arms long before they should be, and the only possible thing he can do now is to pull the club in toward him. Of

THE HOOK 

There are some players of this class, whose trouble is the direct antithesis of the slice. 

They are troubled by the tendency to hook, and for their benefit we will consider the cause of and the remedy for this fault. First let me say that there are two kinds of hook, good and bad. There is the correct controlled hook, which is a highly desirable shot, and a distinguishing mark of the truly proficient golfer.

A ball which is hit for a controlled hook, starts on its flight a little to the right of the direction line, and when nearly at the end of its flight, turns to the left, coming back to the line and landing with an over spin. This is the ideal shot to use from the tee, and is not to be confused for a moment with the hook which spells disaster. The latter is either the turned or the smothered hook. 

The turned hook comes about in the following manner. The swing line is the same as that Ninety-two

which produces the slice, from the outside in, but in addition to this there has been a slight collapse of the left side of the body at impact, and the result is a ball which, while it may feel like a good shot to you will fly off the club face in a straight line to the left. The ball will not describe any arc in the air whatsoever, and for this reason the player will often think that he has simply faced in the wrong direction. 

This, however, is not so. The turned hook is a faulty shot in its own right, and will fly unmistakably to the left of the direction in which the player faces. The cure for this fault lies in a determined return to the principle of hitting from the inside, plus a strong left hand.

In the case of the smothered hook, the initial error is the same an incorrect groove line, coming from the outside in. To this is added the premature collapse of the left side, and now, worst of all a break down of the left wrist at the moment of impact. This, of course, allows the club face to turn over, and the result is a shot which at best will only just get up in the air for a moment, and will more often stay on the ground entirely after the manner of a topped shot. The best method of preventing this kind of shot, or of curing it after it has once developed, is a faithful adherence to the grip which I described at the beginning of this book, which is specifically designed to build up the strength of the left wrist. CHILDREN 

One of the questions which I am most frequently asked is "What is the right age to let children begin to play golf?" I should say that the age of ten is about as good an average as could be struck. depending of course somewhat on the individual youngster, the amount of interest already manifested in the game, and the physical development for the age. I do not believe that a lot of instruction is good for children. This is apt to defeat its own purpose and spoil their interest in the game at such an early age. I would not on the other hand allow them to go along on their own sweet way, without some proper conception of the fundamentals of the swing, and of their importance. This age is, of course, the best of all possible times to begin, as it is common knowledge that a correct swing developed in any child at this time will "stick." Even if the child should happen not to play any more golf for, shall we say, another ten years. the first 

One Hundred and Five

correct instruction will still be of great advantage, and there wlil never be any necessity of beginning all over again.

In most of the better clubs and schools of today, class instruction is available, and I believe that this is not only a splendid thing for the children, but that it merits the favorable consideration of their parents on the ground that it is much less expensive than individual instruction. In my own classes I have found that interest is greatly stimulated owing to the fact that no one child cares to be behind the rest of the class, which results in much better attention than when he or she is receiving private instruction.

My usual method of procedure with children is of the simplest kind. First I have them learn how to grip the club, second how to place the feet in relation to the ball, and third I tell them to look at the ball, and to keep the left arm as straight as possible. These three or four ideas are enough, and I do not at this time go into any detail of swing. From these positions carefully observed, the child will develop a natural swing, and it is not desirable to spoil his or her fun by attempting to do the impossible in the matter of detail. Swinging a golf club comes as naturally to children at this age as throwing a ball, or skipping rope, and´ a lot of detail of action will only spoil and distort this natural adaptability. It is a great 

One Hundred and Six

pity that it is so commonly forced upon them. 

They are certain at this imitative age to acquire a great deal more by natural absorption than can be dinned into them by tuition. One of the principal points of my own early training, which I recall most distinctly, was my father's insistence that I go and watch every possible tournament, and since at this time I had only a rough idea of the fundamentals of the game, I feel certain that the thing which helped me more than anything else was the time devoted to watching the sound methods of developed players. 

What better training could any child have than golf? In addition to the wonderful physical benefits, control of temper, patience, and sportsmanship are only a few of the byproduct advantages derived from the game.

WOMEN'S GOLF 

It would not be proper to complete this little book without a word about the remarkable advance that the women have made in golf. 

Only a few years ago golf was a game which gave relaxation and pleasure only to men, but there is no doubt that today the women get, if anything, more fun out of it than anyone else, and I know of many cases where the usual winner in the family single match is the wife, and not the husband.

Their golf in team matches is much more of 

One Hundred and Seven

an event of importance to the women than it is to the men. They are much keener in making use of every possible means to improve their game, and they are usually more willing to devote as much time as possible to practise. 

Golf has become a thing of considerable significance to them and not just another way of passing the time. One very strinking difference between men and women golfers, is the lively interest of the latter in any kind of competitive golf. You will find that any club tournament scheduled for the women will be well attended, whereas most of the men have to be coaxed to enter their own tournaments. 

From an instructor's point of view, women are really much easier to teach than men. There is no feeling of self-consciousness to hinder progress, and their own natural grace and control are great assets for the acquisition of a correct swing. Once they have a good conception of what is to be done they will stick to it until proficient. 

The average woman has, however, one rather amazing shortcoming to which I feel that her attention should be directed. That is her short game, by which I do not mean the shortness of her long game. There is no earthly reason why a woman should not be as good as a man or even better, in this department of the game. 

And since she cannot in any but the most exceptional cases aspire to compete with a man

I was an interested spectator during the Women's National Championship held at the Salem Country Club in 1932. I was tremendously impressed by the strict attention which these players had clearly given to every department of the game. Their play was a delight to watch from the standpoint of correctness of swing, good judgment and keen competitive spirit, and I must freely admit that I carried home with me a lot of new ideas which proved to be of great value to me in my work of instruction.

It must be remembered that we have seen the coming and the development of only the first generation of women in all branches of outdoor sport. What will the future bring? It seems to me that in closing this little manual there is no more significant point to be made in connection with the entire field of sports than that which I wish to put myself on record as predicting, namely that the next few years will see the greatest improvement recorded by the women golfers.
