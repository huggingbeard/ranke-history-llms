OUTLINE OF A THEORY OF LINGUISTIC CHANGE.1 

The present theory, in its precise uncompromising form, is being proposed here for the first time, so far as we know. It is based almost exclusively on the data of the phonology of Italian, Spanish and French. However, it may probably apply to other languages and possibly to all languages.

The speech of man is an important function and it is of the greatest aid to other functions, but life demands much more than our ability to communicate (in the broadest sense of the word) with one another. Hence, man has perceived unconsciously (and, only to a slight degree, consciously) that it is well for him to make his speech as efficient as may be necessary to do the things that speech can do, but that energy (and time) are needed for other vital tasks. Consequently, every language which has ever been used in the history of the world has always strived to do whatever language could do (in its time and circumstances) and it has always strived to do its task with a minimum expenditure of effort. In those remote days when man first began to speak in the (for him) simplest combinations of sounds, we can be sure that his language was the most efficient language that he could devise for the occasion. It did all that man could make it do, and in the easiest way. (The fact that the "savages" of our own day have languages which are often highly developed and complicated is no argument against the assumption that the language of the earliest man was of a very simple type. Every language used by man is as old as man himself (1.e. it 1.

By the above remarks, we do not mean to imply, for example, that the language A is necessarily the most efficient language that the speech-community A' could possibly have. What we do mean, however, is that the language A is the most efficient language that the community A' was or is able to devise and/or make use of under the circumstances of that community's existence. 

However, let us suppose that the language B of the community B' is imposed upon the conquered community A', and that, judged from a hypothetical ideal standard, language B is more efficient than language A, i.e. that 

B is capable of accomplishing more for A' than A can accomplish, and that it can do so with an equal or less expenditure of effort. In that way, by "accident", A' is provided with a more efficient language than the one it had. In the meantime, of course, even though B (as used by A') is inevitably modified somewhat by A, B will always continue to strive to attain its ideal of maximum accomplishment and minimum expenditure of effort. 

In simple terms, our theory may be stated as follows: Every language strives to be as clear (unambiguous) and forceful as is necessary for the welfare of the linguistic community, and it strives to be so with a minimum expenditure of effort.

At first glance, it seems that linguistic change is due to three main forces: 1. assimilation (simplification); 2. the reaction against assimilation; and 3. the need for forcefulness (vividness). However, it immediately becomes apparent that the necessity for causing a linguistic change in order to produce added forcefulness would never arise unless the constant trend toward assimilation had resulted in making certain speech-units so much alike each other or one another that the necessary forcefulness was lacking. Hence, it becomes apparent that linguistic change due to the need for forcefulness is merely a reaction against assimilation or threatened assimilation. Furthermore, it is obvious that a threatened assimilation can only exist when (and as a result of the fact that) some degree of assimilation has already been started. Hence, threatened assimilation and assimilation are the same phenomenon. It

follows, therefore, that our theory can be stated in the following very simple terms: Linguistic change is caused by assimilation and the reaction against assimilation.

As long as the necessary (for the welfare of the community) forcefulness (effectiveness, clarity, vividness, value) of the language is not interfered with (disturbed), the urge to simplify (conserve effort and time) constantly manifests itself. Every linguistic change which can not be explained as a result of the urge to simplify must be attributed to the need for maintaining effectiveness or for creating a new effectiveness. Maintaining effectiveness (maintaining an effectiveness which has always been sufficient in the past; the retention of the linguistic status quo; the resistance to change) is basically the same as creating a new effectiveness (creating an effectiveness for which there is a need; the altering of the linguistic status quo; the need for and the creation of "spontaneous" change). Both can only be a reaction against an excessive leveling (simplification) which is causing or is threatening to cause the language to lose effectiveness.

It. fatto; 0.It. cielo > It. celo (although it is still spelled cielo); and monophthongization (the simplification or blending of the two members of a diphthong) is well known.

The attraction of an i or u(for example) to a relatively stressed vowel is really a step in the process of assimilation. Sometimes the assimilation is partial (the two vowels do not blend to one), and sometimes the assimilation is complete (the two vowels blend), but in either case the phenomenon is that of assimilation. 

The process of umlaut is an obvious type of assimilation. "Dissimilation" of the type V.L. Augustus > V.L.

Agustus is really assimilation, i.e. the u of au is completely assimilated to the u of the following syllable.

Changes commonly explained as "by analogy" are really changes due to assimilation of one word to another. 

Psychologically the words are contiguous. An example of this type is the case of V.L. grassus from V.L. crassus plus the analogy of grossus.

4 Analogical changes (assimilation) may take place in cases which do not necessarily involve a semantic (or emotional, conscious or subconscious) association. Such changes, for convenience, may be described as being due to auditive association or acoustic (or physiological) analogy. The phenomenon of auditive association (acoustic analogy) may manifest itself in many ways. A common type is the making similar (the leveling) of somewhat similar sounds in the whole body of a given language. 

The principle of one type of auditive association may be stated as follows: A phonetic change which has started, for whatever reason, in certain specific situations (in certain phonetic positions in certain words) tends to extend itself, i.e. tends to bring about a similar or identical change of the same sound (or even of related sounds) in other situations (in other phonetic positions). For example, the stem of a word may be stressed (modified) by the need for clarity. The change thus brought about may induce similar (not necessarily exactly similar) changes in the same sounds in other places than in the stem of the word, or even in other words. If assimilation by acoustic analogy takes place we assume that the change was the result of the ever present urge for simplification. If it does not take place, we assume that its taking place would have resulted in over-ambiguity, i.e. that the words in which the assimilation might have taken place would lose their effectiveness (would become too similar to other words in the language).

A type of analogy similar to the one indicated above may be described by the following statement which seems to be justified by the evidence from Romance phonology: A changing sound from a source of relatively frequent occurrence tends to determine the phonetic course (1.e. the manner of further phonetic development) which is followed by the same sound from a source of less frequent occurrence. If such a tendency (a variety of assimilation) does not manifest itself, we likewise assume that the words in which the assimilation might have taken place would lose their effectiveness (would become too similar to other words in the language).

However, homonymy frequently results from assimilation. In many cases, the process of simplification of

a sound or sound-combination (in a given phonetic situation) involves such a great conservation of energy, on the whole, that it is felt (consciously and/or subconsciously) to be justified (to make the language more efficient) even if some words become ambiguous and have to be replaced by others, as a result of the general application of the rule to all sounds in the given phonetic situation. Homonymy may also result from the reaction against (or resistance to) assimilation. In many cases, the reaction against assimilation produces an increased clarity (effectiveness) which is so needed by many words in a given phonetic situation that the resultant change is felt to be justified, on the whole, even if some words become ambiguous and have to be replaced by others as a result of the general application of the rule to all words in the given phonetic situation. 

Dissimilation of the type L. peregrinus > pelegrinus and Fr. orphenin > orphelin is a reaction against assimilation, i.e. against assimilation of non-contiguous sounds. Such types threaten to lose a syllable and thereby to become oversimplified. 

Another example of consonantal dissimilation as a reaction against assimilation is the case of V.L. *lendine (Cl.L. lendem) which became Sp. liendre. In the V.L. group nd'n, the second n threatened to become completely assimilated to the first n, a process which would have resulted in oversimplification, especially since the interconsonantal d was also in danger of being involved in that assimilation. By changing the second n to the similar dental or alveolar "vocalic" consonant r, there was produced the group ndr which has thus far resisted oversimplification. 

Similarly, V.L. anima became It. alma, instead of the normal *amma which would be confused with ama amat.

Sp. alma is explained in the same manner.

A consonant may shift its position (the phenomenon called metathesis) as a reaction against oversimplification. In its new position (in its new phonetic combination) it is no longer in danger of undergoing an assimilation which would destroy the identity (clarity, effectiveness) of the word in which it occurs. For example, there is definite evidence that the V.L.S. What seems to be a striking example of a resistance to a threatened oversimplification is the case of V.L. placuit which became It. piacque. In this change, the V.L. u (w) was retained, contrary to the general rule.

It seems almost beyond doubt that that u was retained in order to avoid a threatened confusion with piace placet.

One type of resistance to oversimplification is exemplified by the change of 0.Sp. m'r to 0.Sp. mbr. It appears that this m'r must be considered an unfavorable group, and the danger was the threatened assimilation of those two continuants. The first step in averting the danger was the lengthening (doubling) of the m to mm. 

Next, the second m was denasalized by assimilation to the r.

The result was a consonant group which, up to the present time, appears to be no longer in danger of oversimplification. Looked at in another way, this change might be explained as follows: the people became aware of their tendency to produce an ambiguous word when they did not pronounce the m and r distinctly (when they succumbed to the normal urge to simplify their speech). They therefore "overpronounced" the m (lengthened it) to prevent the assimilation. But, apparently, there was still danger of assimilation. (We say "apparently" because we know that the reaction, against assimilation, continued). By the partial assimilation of the second m to the r there resulted a group which, apparently, was favorable. (We say "apparently" because there was no continuance of the reaction against

assimilation.)

This type of change affords us a very clear illustration of the whole process (all varieties) of phonetic change which is induced by the reaction against assimilation (oversimplification). However,

In the above example, notice that the interpolated sound is the result of the exaggeration (intensifying, elaboration, extension) of the changing sound. it is true that in many such changes (reaction against assimilation) the interpolated sound has phonetic characteristics which are similar to the sound from which the changing sound is moving (i.e. similar to the sound which threatens to bring about assimilation). In this connection, notice the interesting case of the change of V.L. nkr (kis mediopalatal) to 0.F. ntr. in which the changing sound is a combination (blend) of n and § (<), and, at the same time, is articulated in the same place as is r. 

In all changes of this type, the process may be stated as follows: Sound A gradually becomes assimilated (partially) to sound B. Just before the assimilation becomes complete, A becomes overpronounced. Overpronunciation probably always causes lengthening (doubling), at least for a short time. Next, the doubled A or one half of the doubled A starts to become differentiated from B. This differentiation takes place gradually (as does every linguistic change, whether it be assimilation or differentiation). The differentiating sound will take on the form of the nearest stable sound in the language, or will approach the nearest stable sound as soon as possible, i.e. it will follow the path which affords the shortest (easiest) escape from the threatened oversimplification. It will then (if necessary; if there is still danger of assimilation) continue to change until the danger of assimilation is past. But if, on its course, other oversimplifications are threatened, it will, in turn, undergo a further differentiation (flight). This continued flight of the differentiating sound from the sound which originally threatened to cause an assimilation or from any other sound which, being encountered in the original flightmovement, might also cause an oversimplification (assimilation) is the cause of what appears to be a kind of phonetic momentum. To this phenomenon we shall give the name of "the flight from assimilation". Notice, however, that the flight is only so far (i.e. the dif-

ferentiation is only so great) as to avoid the assimilation. The urge for conservation of energy continually manifests itself, and to such a degree that the differentiating sound frequently is able to avoid assimilation while retaining or acquiring some phonetic characteristics of the sound which threatens to cause the assimilation.

With respect to the above discussion concerning vowel-breaking in the Romance languages, we obviously do not intend to imply that the selection and adoption (on the part of the linguistic community) of a given diphthong (out of the many possible diphthongs which theoretically might have been selected) was the result of a sudden and deliberate decision. 

Indeed, as in the case of all linguistic change, we assume that the ultimate selection of the new sound was made over a relatively long period of time by the method of trial and error exercised by all the members of the linguistic community. It is highly probable that many "irregular" spellings which are found in linguistic material (old documents, etc.), and which are so readily labeled "dialectal" or "scribal errors", represent pronunciations which were temporarily used by some members of the linguistic community, but which were ultimately supplanted by the better (more "efficient", in accordance with our theory) selections made by the linguistic community as a whole.

Spelling pronunciations are isolated (relatively rare) phenomena which, in a sense, are not a part of the normal development of a living language. However, we assume that such changes are always analogical, and that basically they are manifestations of the trend toward simplification (conservation of energy). In adopting a spelling pronunciation, speakers replace the normally developed sound of one phonetic position or linguistic period by the (similarly spelled) normally developed sound of another position or linguistic period; or they replace the sound of a dialect word or any borrowed word (which is taken into a given language) by the (similarly spelled) sound which exists in a given language at the time of (or sometime after) the borrowing.

It is not probable that pronunciations which are the result of physical defects or idiosyncrasies of a king or other prominent person ever persist for any conside-

rable time in a language, even in the pronunciation of a small group of a community. The only "abnormal" pronunciations which may have started in the pronunciation of a king or other prominent person are spelling pronunciations, which, as we have seen, are really manifestations of analogical change.

It is beyond the scope of the present book to discuss questions of morphology, syntax and semantics. It may be stated, however, that the data of those branches of linguistics provide strong evidence in support of the present theory.

In summing up, we may state that the basic cause of linguistic change is the interplay of the two urges (almost wholly unconscious), the one for effectiveness (clarity) and the other for conservation of energy.
