HARVARD COLLEGE LIBRARY VARIATIONS IN THE ROTATION OF AZIMUTHS 

M Walton Azimuths may have different directions of rotation and irregularities of \motion, including changes of direction, dpending on the relation of the observer's latitude to the declination of the body. A consideration of some of the particular types of motion dependent upon the above factors reveals some very interesting aspects. 

Since one side of the azimuth angle is the arc of meridian between the zenith and the pole, and since when the zenith and pole coincide this arc diminishes to zero, then, to an observer at the Pole, there would be no such thing as an azimuth. 

Location of a body could be by Hour Angle and Altitude (which equals Declination). 

The stars would rotate in concentric circles about the celestial pole and remain at the same altitude above the horizon. Other celestial bodies, for all practical purposes, would approximate such motion during the course of any one day. At all latitudes an observer will find that bodies of zero declination, rise with an azimuth of 90° and set with an azimuth of 270°. If the observer be in north lạtitude, the azimuth of such a body will rotate clockwise from 90° to 270. If the observer is in south latitude the rotation will be counter-clockwise, from 90° to 270°. The reason for these limits of azimuth for bodies of zero declination is because the arcs of any two great circles bisect each other. The two great circles concerned are of course the horizon and the particular diurnal circle under consideration. In north latitudes bodies with north declination less than the observer's latitude rise with an azimuth less than 90° and set with an azimuth greater than 270°. The azimuth rotates constantly clockwise through more than 180° of arc. In south latitudes bodies of south declination less than the observer's latitude rise with an azimuth greater than 90° and set with an azimuth of less than 270°. azimuth rotates constantly counter-clockwise through more than 180° of arc. 

In both hemispheres, bodies with declination numerically greater than latitude will have azimuths which rotate first one way, than the other way. In north latitudes azimuths of rising bodies in this category will first rotate clockwise to a certain limit; reverse and rotate counter-clockwise across our meridian to a corresponding westerly limit; then reverse to clockwise again. The circumpolar stars best illustrate this change in the direction of an azimuth's rotation. A corresponding reverse in direction of azimuth rotation occurs with stars between the observer's latitude and the pole in the southern hemisphere, except that the rising stars have first a counter-clockwise motion of the azimuth. 

The critical altitude at which this change in the direction of azimuth rotation occurs varies between two limits. As the value of the declination approaches 90°, the value of the critical altitude decreases toward the lower limit which is the altitude of the elevated pole. As the difference between the declination and the observer's latitude approaches zero, the critical altitude of change approaches the upper limit which is 90° (and azimuth of 90°). 

Bodies with declination same name and equal to latitude will of course pass through our zenith, with the azimuth suddenly changing from 90° to 270° as the body transits our meridian. In the northern hemisphere the azimuth will increase clockwise in rising up to 90°; then decrease clockwise from 270°. In southern latitudes the azimuth will decrease counter-clockwise to 90° when rising, and then decrease counter-clockwise from 270° in sctting. Of course to an observer at the equator bodies of zero declination will have only two values of azimuth; 90° and 270°. Gnd 1.

The rate at which the angular values of azimuths change is interesting to note. 

As they change from one direction to the other, the rate of change becomes very slow indeed. In contrast & body gets to our zenith when it transits our meridian, the more rapid will be the rate of change in its azimuth. 

Another interesting aspect which aids us in visualizing the paths of bodies through the sky is a consideration of the diurnal circles. The diurnal circles are the pparent paths of the stars, and for practical purposes the other bodies as they move across the heavens. The sc circles are equivalent to bends of celestial latitudc. 

The diurnal circle of a body of zero declination is the only one which is a great circle; all other circles are small circles. All diurnal circles are concentric with the axis of the celestial sphere, and all are in different planes which are parallel to each other. 

Now then, consideration of two aspects of these diurnal circles in relation to the plane of the horizon gives us . better concept of the apparent motions of the heavenly bodies. 

One factor is: How much of the diurnal circle is above the horizon? 

The other is:

What degree of angle docs the plane of the diurnal circle make with the plane of thċ horizon? To in observer at the equator 180° of arc of each diurnal circle is above the horizon, and the planes of these circles are perpendicular at the plane of the · horizon. Therefore as the bodies progress westward in the sky, they rise vertically from the horizon. To an observer at either pole, 360° of arc of cach diurnal circle in that particular hemisphere is visible, and the planes of these diurnal circles are parallel to the plane of the horizon. As bodics progress westward they remain t constant altitudo. To an obscrver at, for example, latitude 30° more than 180° of arc of cach diurnal circle of declination same no.me as latitude is above the horizon; and this amount above the horizon increases with the declination of the body until we get to the circumpolar stars which have 360° of their paths visible. 

Also cach diurnal circle of declination contrary name to observer's latitude will have less than 180° of arc above the horizon with decreasing amounts visiblo as the declination of that name increases. And at latitude 30° the angle which the plane of the diurnal circle makes with the plane of the horizon is equal to the colatitude or 60°. So to an observer at this latitude, the bodics do not rise vertically from the horizon as they procede westward but rise and seem to swing south in rising as they follow a path at an angle of 60° with the horizon.
