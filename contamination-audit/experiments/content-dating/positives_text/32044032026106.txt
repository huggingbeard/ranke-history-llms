I. REPORT OF CHIEF CONSULTANT 

October 21, 1948.

Hon. Herbert Hoover, Chairman, 

Commission on Organization of the Executive Branch of the Government, Washington, D. C.

DEAR MR. PRESIDENT: 

While the staff and consultants as well as the distinguished advisers who have been invited to join the Public Works Task Force are by training and experience particularly interested in major public and quasi-public works, we have tried conscientiously to bring to our study and report sufficient detachment and objectivity to make our conclusions valuable to the Organization Commission, and consistent with the findings of the various other task forces whose functions to some extent overlap and even conflict with ours. 

Functions of the Public Works Task Force 

We are neither propagandists nor special pleaders for a Works Department, but are called upon to give disinterested patriotic advice to the Commission on the basis of our experience. It is not our duty to tap the political barometer, anticipate opposition not based on merit, and offer compromises to conciliate possible opponents or critics. This is the province of the Commission, not of its technical advisers.

We have heard the comment which obviously comes from uninformed sources-that this task force was organized and committed in advance to some sort of governmental engineering heaven in which one group of professional men would have a department to themselves, with direct access to the President and Cabinet. Nothing could be further from the truth. We are all busy people, attempting to render a public service without bias. 

The fact is that the same captious criticism could be made of the Department of Justice because it is composed largely of lawyers, the 1

Department of Defense because it has a concentration of men trained in the military profession, the Department of State because it is composed largely of diplomats and foreign officers, the Labor Department because more and more it represents labor, the Commerce Department because it represents business, the Department of the Treasury because it has a concentration of accounts, or the Department of Agriculture because it is run by those who are trained in farming. 

Any logical consolidation of public and related works will involve many professions other than engineering. Consolidation of works functions in a new department of Cabinet rank is either a good thing in the public interest, or it is not. Lugging in extraneous and irrelevant arguments contributes nothing to a decision on the merits. 

Congress recognized the value of centralizing public works in the reorganization and streamlining of its Congressional committees in 1946 when 48 committees of the House were reduced to 19 and 33 committees of the Senate were reduced to 15. There is today only one public works committee in the House and one in the Senate, where previously these functions were scattered among half a dozen miscellaneous committees. The same logic should prevail in the 

Executive Branch of the Government. 

General Considerations

In spite of world events and the general tendency to increase national Government powers and activities-a tendency which must in the long run be reflected in the structure, personnel, and functioning own Federal Government-we have proceeded upon the assumption that this should continue to be a country of private enterprise, and that national Government here should not compete or interfere needlessly either with local administration of the States and municipalities or with private initiative and enterprise. Ours is a rather inflexible system of delegated executive Government compared to a totalitarian autocracy on the one hand and to the sensitive British parliamentary system on the other. Today ours is the oldest surviving democratic and republican Government in existence. It is true that the makers of our Constitution could not have anticipated all the strains and trials to which our Government has been subjected, but it is astonishing how far into the future they were able to look and how valid their basic philosophy is today. 

It is not necessary to change the fundamental principles on which our Government rests. Your Commission is called upon only to modernize both structure and operation within the framework provided by the fathers of the Constitution, and particularly in the wing which houses the Executive Branch.

The President does not under present laws and practices have an organization which can meet these requirements. He is harassed 2

and overworked; he lacks power over agencies in his branch of the 

Government and cannot command complete loyalty. He is in a considerable measure held responsible for confusion and waste beyond his control.

An unfortunate result of the present scattering, independence, and irresponsibility of numerous agencies in the Executive Branch of the Government is that these agencies establish their own personal relationships with Congress, and thus drive a wedge between the 

Executive and Legislative Branches of the Government. Another tendency, manifest since about 1932, has been to make the office of the President at the White House a sort of catch basin for all sorts of new agencies and officials outside of the Cabinet and not ordinarily included among the immediate secretarial and administrative staff of the President.

The main purpose of the reorganization of the Executive Branch of our Government, therefore, is to provide a responsible, economical Government, with the President as the real instead of the nominal head, and with a rearrangement of departments and personnel such that the President will be free to deal with major problems of all sorts, and will not be burdened unduly with administrative detail. Agencies of Government outside of the Cabinet circle, conflicting with established departments and only theoretically reporting to the President, should be brought into proper relation with the major departments headed by the President's Cabinet advisers. It is, no doubt, too much to expect that Cabinet officers will be selected solely on account of their ability to manage the great departments of Government without reference to geography, politics, etc., but it certainly should be possible to make selections within a framework of expert and professional requirements based upon administrative responsibilities. 

National Defense and Democratic Government 

Unfortunately the battle for sound democratic Government in a republic is not to be won by slogans. The triumph of the democratic principle is a fine objective. So are efficiency and economy. The two are not always synonymous and when the logical and inevitable slogan of national defense, "win at any cost," is added, we have introduced an objective which is essentially undemocratic and wasteful. 

The reconciling of these three objectives is our big problem in what remains of the twentieth century. This fact was brought home by the spawning of more or less independent alphabetical agencies before and during World War II, coupled with the inevitable necessity of establishing a small inside war Cabinet because of the impossible number of administrative heads of important agencies. 

It must be admitted that in preparation for possible war, and of course in the actual conduct of war, many normally and ordinarily

sound principles of both business and Government organization must yield to the common sense, special logic, personalities, pressures, and other exigencies of the moment. Nevertheless, the aim should be to reorganize the functions under the President so that they can be operated well at all times, and to avoid the hysterical creation of all sorts of new alphabetical agencies whenever an emergency arises. 

Whatever world conditions may be, and whether war is remote or threatening, we are entering a highly competitive age in which our world responsibilities are becoming more exacting and pronounced. 

We are approaching an era in which, if we are to avoid totalitarian, government, we must bring into the Executive Branch the best brains we can muster and provide these brains with the powers, facilities, organization, leeway, and encouragement so that they can function with something approaching the atmosphere of the best and most efficient private enterprise.

Advance Plans for Depressions and Public Works 

Even among persons familiar with public finance, there are few who realize the tremendous obligations which the Federal Government must assume to stimulate employment, promote recovery, and prime the pump of private enterprise in times of recession and depression. 

The obligations are quite inescapable and it must be assumed that they will recur from time to time in the economic cycle. The advance planning and promotion of public works for such periods should be recognized as a continued responsibility of the Federal Government, working in cooperation with States and municipalities. It is senseless to proceed on the theory that every major slump in business and employment is an unexpected Divine visitation not to be anticipated and to be dealt with only on the basis of ineffective, wasteful, and hastily improvised emergency measures.

Full realization of the economic interdependence of the world will no doubt cushion the shocks of cyclical bad times. Meanwhile, the depth, dimensions, and duration of a depression can be greatly reduced by intelligent advance planning which will both control inflation in periods of boom and reduce deflation in times of recession. 

An uncontrolled, prolonged depression can run Government into the red almost as far as a war without even providing full employment for its duration.

During the depression of 1932 to 1938 the huge sum of $24 billion represented the total cost of the public works relief program; of this, $18 billion were Federal funds; the balance, State and local. These funds were of course raised from current revenues as well as through borrowing.

Let us break down some of the figures aggregating this staggeringtotal. Of the $24 billion total, $16 billion was spent on work relief

including the WPA, the CWA, the FERA, the CCC, and other alphabetical manifestations of made work. The balance of $8 billion was spent on loan-and-grant projects of the RFC and the PWA. The cost per capita per year for labor and equipment was $1,000 for WPA projects and $3,000 or more for PWA projects. A total of 8½ million individuals obtained employment at one time or another during the period of the program throughout the Nation. In New York City alone over 700,000 persons were employed throughout the 7-year period, reaching a peak in 1935 of 260,000 at one time. At this peak over 80,000 individuals worked on the New York City Park Department program alone. 

Because of lack of advance plans worth while projects were delayed or abandoned. The money was there, the men were ready to go to work but the blueprints were not available. In New York City the 

Park Department hastily assembled an emergency technical staff of 2,500 individuals in order to prepare plans and inspect the work of its relief program. Throughout the Nation makeshift devices were resorted to in an effort to minimize boondoggling and find skilled work for skilled labor. This led to all sorts of freakish permutations. For example, skilled laborers in New York and other cities were not permitted to work more than an average of 5 days a month in order to keep within the $84 maximum permitted per month. This meant recruiting crews of three and even four shifts in order to carry on the operation without interruption. Some of these men were not even eligible for relief and some had other jobs.

All intelligent students of government prefer permanent, wholly or partly long-range self-supporting improvements. Theoretically WPA is cheaper per man but practically it is a poor way of employing people. 

PWA on the other hand promotes the employment of indirect labor in the mine, the factory, and in transportation. Throughout the chaos of all these alphabetical agencies the RFC stands out as a shining example. Its guiding principle was the use of every conceivable means to put a project on a loan-and-grant basis before resorting to a straight grant and the performance of as much of the work as possible by contract as distinguished from force account.

If all the brains, energy, and ingenuity of private enterprise are brought to bear; and if labor is offered fair rewards, industry will no doubt be prepared to take up much of the slack which necessitates Government depression spending. It is inconceivable, however, that all needed employment in bad times can be provided by business. 

Public works admittedly can take care of only a fraction of the depression-employment problem, but it is an extremely important fraction. It is a marginal area in which men out of work will stew around helplessly unless the Government is ready to meet their problems. 

Few States and municipalities are geared to turn out detailed postwar public works plans and specifications within any reasonable time,

even assuming they know what they want to do and have public as well as official opinion back of their program. With a few notable exceptions they lack sufficient regular engineering design forces. 

Most of them are opposed to hiring private firms, consultants, and experts. 

There is a complete misunderstanding in most quarters as to the practical difficulties of designing major public works. Even though private consulting firms are used, the time required is from 6 to 18 or more months depending upon the complexity of the problems. These firms, although they pay more and have greater ffexibility, also have trouble in finding additional competent men especially where there is a sudden demand.

Another difficulty lies in scheduling public improvements, as distinguished from designing them. Scheduling means fixing the order of advertising and letting contracts, so as not to interfere with each other. 

Such scheduling must depend upon the anticipated availability of material, equipment, bidders, and inspectors. If too many public works projects are started at the same time in one neighborhood there would be serious interference with traffic, business, and ordinary living conditions. There are not enough contractors to guarantee real competition if too many contracts are advertised at once, and not enough men in certain skilled trades.

There is not a State, city, or municipal subdivision in the country which can, on its own, finance a depression-construction program sufficient to make a real dent in the employment problem. Federal assistance is required. The alternatives are greatly increased soldier bonuses, pensions, insurance, and other allotments, another WPA program on a very large scale; an American security program approaching the British scale recommended by Sir William Beveridge, but in terms of American money and on the basis of our enormously higher scale of living, and finally, as a last resort, home relief and the straight dole with all of its crushing implications of failure and futility. 

Idleness and home relief are the worst depression expedients; made work is a shade better; genuine needed, durable, public improvements afford honest, dignified employment and permanent benefits; works which are wholly or partly self-supporting are at the very top of the list. These distinctions are palpable, and smart advance planning always will have them in mind.

Their experience in the last depression brought home to Federal and local officials the importance of advance planning for future emergencies. To date considerable progress has been made by the 

FWA on a shelf of plans for future use, although aggregating nothing like the total required. At present we have available through Federal, State, and local appropriations a reservoir of projects estimated to cost about $5 billion, although many States have not taken full advantage

of Federal advance planning funds, nor provided appropriations of their own for this purpose.

The theory that the volume of construction undertaken by the Federal Government should bear a close relation to the national production index is practical only with many reservations. Only projects which are not indispensable or urgent but which are desirable but not at the moment essential can be put on the shelf of plans. 

Municipalities cannot hold back vital sewer and incinerator, housing, school, hospital, health, transportation, and other "must" projects until there is a depression. In certain instances it may be possible to tie the shelf of plans to the national production index insofar as projects which can be postponed are concerned. That is precisely the kind of study which should be made by the Division of Planning in the proposed Department of Works. Unfortunately the purely automatic accounting devices and formulas to control public spending which have all the charm of simplicity, exactness, and ease simply don't meet the human, the unexpected, and the political contingencies as they arise.

Doubts have been expressed as to the validity of advance plans after a lapse of several years and the question of obsolescence has been raised. Experience has, however, taught us that if the projects are selected intelligently and represent a continuing need, and if the plans are made by competent technicians and checked after the preliminary stage, there is no reason to anticipate any substantial loss through obsolescence, variations in taste, new inventions, and higher standards. Even if 15 percent of some designs must be revised, advance planning is fully justified. Projects ideal for advance planning purposes include streets, slum clearance and low rental housing, roads and highways, hospital and other buildings, sanitation and water supply projects, airports, port facilities, recreation, and the huge regional development programs which include the improvement of navigation, flood control, reclamation, power, and irrigation projects. 

Recent studies show that over $100 billion will be required to insure reasonably adequate public facilities in the Federal, regional, State, and municipal fields. One government economist, basing his figures on apparently reliable estimates issued by the various Federal departments engaged in public works, lists the cost of bringing our present highways up to a reasonably workable system at $30 billion, public buildings at $12 billion, recreational facilities at $7 billion, regional development works at $11 billion, and sanitation, water supply, and similar facilities at $6 billion. Motor vehicle registrations today total 39 million and it is estimated that this will increase to 45 million in 5 years. The great increase in the production of motorcars, trucks, and busses does not make sense if we do not make plans for the repair, expansion, and modernization of our highway system,

including so-called throughways, expressways, and parkways in congested areas and adequate parking facilities. 

Normal public works activities of the Federal Government involve 200,000 employees and annual expenditures of $3 billion. Any reallistic analysis of the enormous sums needed to bring our public works up to date, to prepare.advance plans, and to pay for programs for recessions, establishes in our opinion a strong and almost indisputable case for a single consolidated Federal Works Department of 

Cabinet rank.

Civilian Defense-Another Argument for a Unified Works Department 

In preparing civilian defense programs and in planning for protection against wartime bombing, evacuation of population, and other emergencies, there is need of a single Federal agency to plan for and cooperate with local governments in providing the facilities to meet these emergencies. It is entirely within the realm of possibility that future city planning will have to be guided to some extent as to concentration of strategic industries, plant dispersion, transportation, roads, and location of airports by anticipated war contingencies. 

The official records of the United States Strategic Bombing Survey as to the effect of the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki emphasize certain signposts of danger and stress the need of shelters and other civilian defense works as well as active military defense. 

While there is no need of becoming hysterical on this subject or suggesting that we disband our cities and go underground it would be foolish to ignore these signposts entirely and do our advance planning without these lessons in mind. 

No doubt the Department of Defense is best equipped to outline the dangers to be guarded against, but it is not organized to determine the changes in civilian public works to meet these dangers. As the matter now stands, under the present organization of the Executive Branch of the Federal Government, problems of this kind will continue to be met by improvisation.

As an illustration of the desirability of a competent, centralized Works Department let us recall the confusion which resulted during the war in congested war industry areas. The Army and Navy became so disturbed over housing, transportation, sanitary, recreation, and numerous other problems in these congested areas that a special study was made looking toward a single program which would bring under one direction the conflicting efforts of numerous Federal, State, local, and other agencies which were attempting to wrestle with these problems. Obviously, if there had been a single Works Department, this department would have been responsible for assisting local officials in the solution of these wartime problems.

The Works Department would also be of great assistance to the National Security Resources Board in its task of planning and organizing resources essential for the national security. The Secretary of 

Works should be added to the membership of this Board. 

Scientific Research and Reporting 

The functions of the Coast and Geodetic and Geological Surveys and of the Bureau of Mines and Bureau of Standards which involve engineering, physical and related observation and research, testing, standardization, and report belong logically in the same Cabinet department and are distinctly Works activities. Considering the huge sums which are spent annually on works and developments of a public or quasi-public nature, and the rapid, current technological advances, inventions, and discoveries in this field, disproportionately small sums are spent on applied as well as basic research in the interests of both immediate and ultimate efficiency and economy. The establishment of a major research division in a consolidated Department of Works should go far toward remedying this neglect. No doubt some research would continue to be carried on in other divisions of such a department, but most of the efforts in this direction would more and more be concentrated in the research division.

Atomic Energy Commission 

The functions of the new Atomic Energy Commission are primarily to unite the scientific brains of our universities with the know-how of great corporation research laboratories under Government auspices and to produce weapons of war, new forms of power, and incidental byproducts useful in medicine and many other fields. War for the time being comes first; the harnessing of atomic energy to supply power is tremendously important but manifestly a long way off; and medical and additional uses may be expected to develop currently. 

Both laboratory and manufacture of atomic products are fraught with dangers as well as benefits and entire communities with all their people, plans, and works are more and more vitally affected. 

The evils of separation of such an agency from the President, the Cabinet, Congress, and from the ebb and flow of public opinion, are so great, however, that serious reflection will show that there must be a closer tie with the Executive chosen by the people to govern them, and through the Executive with the other branches of government and the electorate. It would seem possible to resolve this particular phase of the problem by providing for a compromise under which one or more Cabinet officers or their representatives would at all times be ex officio members of each of the technical agencies, and in this way to provide for the flow of current information to and from the Execu- 817148-48- -2

tive and the harnessing of the new sicentific agencies to the traditional administrative ones. 

For these reasons we favor representation on the Atomic Energy Commission of the Secretary of Works as well as the Secretary of 

Defense. 

Consolidation of Functions Relating to Water Control and Development 

A fair consideration of the consolidation of water control and development functions must begin with a detached and objective study of the Interior Department. This department began in the frontier period and its purpose was to open up and promote new western territory. The considerations which from time to time have governed the selection of Secretaries of the Interior by the various Presidents reflect confusion as to the kind of talent required in the head of the Department which more and more calls for qualifications ordinarily associated with public works. We do not know the extent, limits, and duration of our most important natural resources measured by modern demands, and we are therefore equally ignorant of the need for development of existing resources or encouragement of substitutes and imports. 

Certainly, some Cabinet official supported by highly expert advisers should speak with familiarity and authority in the Cabinet on this immensely vital subject.

The name "Interior" has made less and less sense as time has gone on, and as all sorts of other functions, such as insular affairs, including the future of Alaska, Hawaii, Puerto Rico, and the Virgin Islands were added. Other Interior Department activities such as the functions of the Bureau of Land Management would be more at home in the Department of Agriculture, except as to engineering service which might well be a duty of the public works and engineering administration, however organized.

An argument can be made that the Bureau of Fish and Wildlife should go into the Department of Agriculture but the close relationship between the national park and recreation program and the Fish and Wildlife Service indicates that this service might better be included in the central works agency. The Office of Indian Affairs is similarly unrelated to other Interior Department offices. When all functions not properly placed in the Department of the Interior are redistributed, little remains but incomplete public works functions, and the way is clear for a consolidation with a Department of Works. 

Placing in one agency reclamation and conservation arbitrarily and artificially separated from the various agencies devoted to rivers and harbors work, the TVA, flood control, and power development, has made it impossible for the Interior Department to function either as 10

an integrated works agency or as a genuine department of development.

We considered carefully a partial consolidation of public works in a division or bureau in the Department of the Interior. This is regarded by us as the least satisfactory of the various alternatives to the present scattered agencies. Shoveling Federal responsibilities of growing importance into an outmoded department as one of its bureaus would only bolster up an organization which palpably becomes a shell in any genuine and honest redistribution of Federal functions. For this reason we discarded this idea. 

Public projects which involve the impounding and distribution of water may include water supply, flood control, irrigation, navigation, and private, public, quasi-public, and mixed power development at the source with distribution over transmission lines. Such projects may also run into the field of recreation. Not only publicly and privately owned lands may be needed but also parks, Indian reservations, and other lands held under special conditions. The question is bound to arise as to which of these objectives is the main one and this, in turn, involves conflict and often compromise between and among the numerous agencies, Federal, regional, State, and local, which are called into play.

After careful consideration our Task Force agreed unanimously that the Tennessee Valley Authority should be included in the Department of Works as an integral part of the Division of Water Control and Development. We see no fundamental difference between this agency and others of a similar character included in this Division. There are two alternative methods of transferring the 

TVA to the Department of Works. Under one, a managing director would be set up; under the other, the present three-man board would be transferred bodily. We prefer the first alternative, but if the second should be decided upon, we believe the three-man board should be appointed by the Secretary. One of the members should be chairman and chief executive and should be appointed at large. The other two should be qualified persons residing in the Tennessee Valley region. They should have reasonably long, overlapping terms. In the proposed legislation we have indicated the second alternative, namely, the transfer of the board as a unit. 

A well-selected, expert, central engineering group, representing the President and respected by Congress and the country generally, as well as by scientists and technicians, would be invaluable in passing initially on disputed projects like the Nicaragua Canal, Florida Ship Canal, St. Lawrence seaway and power installation, Missouri Valley development, and Passamaquoddy. Untold sums have been spent already for plans and hearings and, in the case of Passamaquoddy and the Florida Ship Canal, for actual construction. It is true that each of these projects involves other considerations besides engineer- 11

ing, such as diplomacy, local politics, trade, shipping, defense, and banking, but the basic test of feasibility remains an engineering matter. 

It would be worth a great deal to the country to have a thorough factual, unbiased report by the sea-green incorruptibles of the engineering profession on all major construction projects, especially if such a report were couched in plain, ordinary Anglo-Saxon English, understandable by the average layman. We have therefore recommended, as a most important feature of the Division of Water Control and Development in the new department, a board of three experts to be known as the Board of Impartial Analysis. The members of this 

Board would be appointed by the Secretary of Works and they would be responsible for a complete detached investigation of all aspects of every major proposal affecting water development and control, promotion, and conservation of natural resources. At the same time, the banking aspects of such projects should be commented on by the Secretary of the Treasury and in this way all technical and financial considerations would be covered fully. 

Central Engineering Advice to Domestic and Foreign Loan Agencies 

The proposed Works Department would offer centralized engineering advice not only in the domestic loan field but also in the field of foreign loans. This is another strong argument for its establishment. Such engineering service could be furnished to the Export-Import Bank, RFC, and other loan agencies which for banking guidance should lean upon the Department of the Treasury. The Works Department also would be in a position to cooperate with loan agencies and the Treasury to stimulate the financing, wholly or partially by revenue bonds, of large engineering projects here or abroad, in times of depression as well as prosperity.

The use of the central engineering staff on loan projects should not preclude or even interfere with the employment of a small staff of engineering executives and consultants by the loan agencies themselves.

Special Problems Relating to the Assignment of Army, Navy, and Air Force Engineers 

While the Department of the Interior grew in a haphazard way, other agencies functioning in the same general sphere preempted parts of the field and have clung to their possessions with pleas which are sometimes convincing, but usually only plausible or even specious. 

Some of the current arrangements between engineering agencies and services, including especially the Bureau of Reclamation and the Army 12

Engineers, smack of collusion rather than cooperation, and of neat devices to avoid competition in getting into the congressional pork barrel.

The Army Engineers continue to control part of the rivers and harbors and flood-control spheres at a time when reclamation in the broad sense, power development, and other phases of engineering work involving rivers and harbors should be part of the same program. To make matters more complicated, this function is shared by other agencies, some of them wholly outside of the Cabinet and, for all practical purposes, beyond the reach and purview of the President himself. It is also a curious fact that a good deal of river and harbor work under the Army Engineers is, if anything, a Navy function, with no relation to Army strategy but with some bearing upon navigation. 

Other phases of this work have no real relation to either the Army or the Navy and involve questions only of aid to private, or at any rate, commercial development and usage. 

The argument that river and harbor work can be directed only by the Army Engineers becomes even more absurd when it is realized that less than 200 Army Engineers are involved and that the remainder of the personnel under their control, numbering over 30,000, are civilians who supply most of the detailed knowledge and continuing direction. 

If the Army Engineers supply unusual ability and obtain invaluable training by contact with this responsibility, there is no reason why the same and even better results cannot be obtained by assigning them and corresponding officers of the Navy and Air Forces, on a proper, dignified, and respected basis, to a central, consolidated Works Department. 

The Secretary of Defense temporarily should assign to the Secretary of Works engineer officers of the Army, Navy, and Air Force who would direct and be engaged in public-works tasks commensurate with their rank and experience. In this way, particularly, junior officers would obtain varied training and experience. The Secretary of Defense would continue, as he does now, to prescribe regulations relating to service, rotation of duties, and promotion of these engineer officers, with full power to withdraw them from the Department of Works during times of emergency. The Corps of Engineers of the Army would continue in close contact with the best civilian engineering brains in the country to perform functions of a military engineering nature under the Secretary of Defense. Only the civil functions of the Corps would be transferred to the Works Department under the proposed plan. 

This subject is far too important to be approached from the point of view of old-school-tie tradition. A detached and scientific spirit is required. There have been recent reports of efforts on the part of the 

Corps of Engineers to preserve the old system and even to reach out for the work of the Bureau of Reclamation. Dams, of course, involve all kinds of considerations-flood control, power development, reclamation, conservation, recreation, and navigation; not to speak of 13

Federal, regional, and State quasi-public and private financing. It is inconceivable that these and related subjects would be placed in the Department of Defense, which is already a very big institution. 

Question of an Exclusive Engineering Service 

Department

We have canvassed a new Works Department of Cabinet rank with only service and research as distinguished from operational functions. 

We believe too much emphasis can be placed upon the benefits of a complete consolidation of engineering activities for service to other departments only and almost exclusively in the interest of better budget making and economy; that is, on a single exclusive engineering advisory agency which permits no engineering services to be rendered elsewhere in any other department or establishment. 

In all frankness, it must be stated that there are arguments against any wholly exclusive Government service agency, not only in the field of public works but in other fields, such as law. There is a tendency on the part of highly centralized service agencies to give insufficient attention, interest, and ingenuity to the solution of peculiar and unusual problems of other departments, and thus to furnish these other departments advice and facilities which do not meet their needs fully. 

Problems referred in a routine way to a mere service agency must take their turn. There is also a disposition in service agencies to measure results by percentages rather than by intrinsic needs. 

Therefore we believe that other departments whose engineering needs can in the main be met by the proposed central public works force could continue to have a few engineering advisers of their own to insure proper consideration of their needs and smooth cooperation with the central engineering agency.

These considerations indicated to us that if we overemphasize the benefits of a single exclusive engineering service agency, and one which has no responsibilities whatever for operation and administration, we may establish a form of government which merely parallels and even duplicates functions of the Budget Bureau and performs more perfectly on paper than in practice.

The chief arguments against a new purely engineering service department are: First, that it does not make intelligent provision for operation of public works which would be entrusted to other departments such as operation of public buildings after they are constructed, management of power and reclamation projects after they are completed, maintenance of national roads and parks after they are constructed, and similar cases; and second, that it represents an arbitrary separation of design, construction, and operating phases of big public undertakings which belong together in one place from the point of view of logic, efficiency, and economy. 14

It seems to us altogether unlikely that the people would support and the Congress would establish a major department of government headed by a Cabinet officer to discharge only service engineering functions. Under these conditions such a service agency would either degenerate into a mere bureau in the Interior Department or would be a minor and completely uninfluential and independent establishment like the present Federal Works Administration. 

The Federal Aid Question in the Field of Public Works 

It is difficult to conceive of a valid argument against a consolidation of public works which will bring together in one agency the officials responsible for Federal aid for highways, airports, and housing. 

These are not operating functions. They involve the distribution of 

Federal money for projects of national significance and the setting of standards which will insure uniformity, and permit taking full advantage of current technical improvements. 

The arguments against Federal aid in certain engineering fields seem to us to rest on theoretical rather than pragmatic reasoning. 

Admittedly Federal highway aid is indispensable to the less populous and wealthy States. It is justified for regional and national thoroughfares and for a program of defense highways and access roads such as the one promulgated in the last war. States like New York hardly can be expected to contribute to others and to get nothing in return. 

The assertion that a comparatively wealthy State like New York contributes 25 percent or more of Federal taxes and gets back only 10 percent and therefore would be better off if it paid for all its own roads does not stand analysis, unless we propose to repeal, wholly or in part, Federal taxes, such as the income tax, and substitute local ones. Similarly the contention that States and municipalities pay a huge fee for "brokerage" in connection with Federal aid, is not provable in the case of highways, where central expert service and the establishment of national standards are worth all they cost. More 

Federal as well as State experimentation and research is needed in the field on lighting and marking roads, in types of pavement surface, and in disabled car shoulders. We must have more study of parking facilities, especially in cities, and of bus terminals; and we must decide to what extent private enterprise can provide the answers. 

We are being choked to death by traffic and certainly it is a lot cheaper to meet this probem by modernizing our street and highway system. than by completely decentralizing urban communities, the only theoretical alternative.

If we come to the 500-pound, 5-passenger, 100-miles-an-hour-onone-gallon-of-gas plastic car, purchasable at every good filling station at $500, where will we be with our roads? There is no sense in building trucks too big and heavy for the roads and roads too flimsy 15

for the trucks, and in loading trucks and buses with heavy freight and passengers better carried on rails. Why should the manufacturer's interest stop at the salesroom and the highway engineer's concern begin there?

Public officials, on the other hand, must think of the roads of tomorrow, of advanced highway design, and of improved materials, methods, and equipment, without which the new passenger cars, trucks, and buses are worthless. We must have some idea of what production the industry has in mind in order to be able to schedule road construction and repair intelligently. 

Much has been said about transcontinental highways, but figures have proven that there is not as yet any substantial amount of transcontinental as distinguished from regional and local travel, excepting, of course, a few main routes from coast to coast and from Canada to the Gulf.

Most of our travel originates and ends in cities and when we bypass the cities we simply duck around the entire problem and thrust it upon crowded communities which cannot meet it without help. 

Standards for ordinary streets, country highways, and secondary roads are fairly well established. It is the congested urban and suburban main artery that requires our clearest thinking and best judgment, and both Federal and State aid.

Every American wants a durable, cheap car, and he looks to the automobile industry to provide it. He won't wear that car out quickly on a broken and obsolete road system. He wants good roads and expects the Federal Government to help pay for them. 

How shall our highway improvements be financed? Some will be paid for out of matched Federal and State funds; some by bond issues; some out of license and gas taxes; some out of other current taxes; and some by assessment. Others will be wholly or partly self-liquidating by means of tolls and other service charges. For many years the American public paid tolls on turnpikes as well as bridges and ferries. But the backbone of our new national highway system cannot be made out of toll roads. It will be devised and financed on a joint, cooperative Federal, State, and city basis. 

We already have substantial Federal matched moneys for design of postwar highways, and the more progressive States and municipalities are taking advantage of these inducements and supplementing them with funds of their own. The Federal program will be sound and successful as long as Federal highway officials continue to allow it to develop locally, do not interfere with local initiative, and demand only that the projects be feasible and the work well done. If there should be an attempt to run the entire national highway system from Washington, local initiative and support would disappear. We would then have the same cumbersome, overmanned, bureaucratic Federal machine in the domain of public roads which we now have in many 16

other fields. There is no better example of nonpolitical, effective, and prudent Federal, State, and local cooperation than that afforded by the Public Roads Administration for almost 30 years under the respected leadership of Commissioner Thomas H. MacDonald. 

How much can we afford to spend on our new arteries? There is no use hiding the figures. An ordinary four-lane concrete highway runs to $275,000 a mile without counting the right-of-way. A typical rural section of four-lane parkway with only a few grade-separation bridges costs $400,000 a mile; a typical suburban section of four-lane parkway, $700,000 a mile; a mixed urban traffic artery with six lanes, $1,250,000 a mile; a six-lane city parkway or expressway through expensive and often built-up areas, $3,500,000 a mile; arterial improvements with six lanes and a service road along built-up waterfront, involving reconstruction of plants and industrial and commercial structures, at least $4,000,000 a mile; elevated expressways with surface lanes below in cities average about $5,000,000 a mile. 

Another road problem is railroad grade crossings. There is a curious assumption, not substantiated by either the courts or common sense, that the elimination of such crossings is something separate and apart from modern highway construction. The United States Supreme 

Court has recognized the fact that the automobile and not the train has caused the danger at such crossings; the elimination of railroad crossings is therefore primarily a highway problem which requires Federal as well as State aid. A large part of the problem of elimination of dangerous railroad grade crossings is transcontinental or regional in origin, and on that basis alone is entitled to substantial Federal aid. New York State has gone out in front to meet this problem through large State bond issues but even in this State nothing like a complete job can be done without Federal aid. Most States are unable to make a dent in the program with local funds. Efforts to make the railroads pay the bulk of a large percentage of the total costs have generally failed, and result only in prolonged and usually futile litigation instead of construction.

There is no generic distinction between a big bridge and a little one; and toll or free the bridge is an integral feature of the road system and should be planned as such. Similarly, at water gaps too wide to be spanned by bridges, large, steady, fast auto ferries should be just as much the road engineer's concern as culverts, drainage, or curbs. 

North and south Michigan are tied together by the Mackinac Ferry, run by the State Highway Commission.

A better argument can be made against public and quasi-public housing subventions in normal times than in an emergency period following a great world war, a period in which housing shortages, scarcities, and high prices are directly attributable to the war itself, and in which the immediate solution is beyond the powers and means of States and municipalities. Arbitrary administrative separation of 17

the FHA from other Federal housing and building functions, because FHA is an insuring underwriting agency, seems to us unsound. FHA insurance of loans on what is virtually a 100-percent basis is essentially indistinguishable from slum clearance loans and grants, and from the administrative point of view is closely related to all forms of national control over building standards, construction materials, and public building. Sound financial advice to FHA on all banking as distinguished from building aspects of FHA underwriting can be assured by placing the Secretary of the Treasury on the National Housing Council.

All Federal housing activities should be in one division in a Works Department because they require honest analysis by one set of experts who know the building business from the ground up and, as to subsidized dwellings, the precise income groups which require Federal emergency help. Incidentally, one of the most scandalous difficulties arising out of the housing emergency lies in the different and varying methods of cost accounting used by the FHA, the PHA, the HOLC and Veterans Housing. As a result, costs are not at all comparable and many are hidden. It is impossible to get a true picture of construction financing because there is lacking one overhead agency to coordinate, compare, and analyze these cost figures on an honest uniform basis.

There is no real analogy between a farm loan and a housing loan. 

One is a loan for business expansion to men who have a business which they know, the other is a subvention to help families who know nothing of the business of building to enable them to put a roof over their heads.

Certainly the Federal Government is responsible for providing permanent housing for veterans and their families now temporarily quartered in Quonset huts and left-over wartime demountable houses. 

The whole thing is an emergency matter which will be alleviated within 10 years. It is nonetheless too vital a matter to be kicked around by competing and overlapping executive agencies and congressional committees.

Although highways and emergency housing may seem more vital, Federal subsidies are needed for airports. The flying industry is still in its infancy. It is in poor financial condition and vitally related to the national defense program. Moreover, regional locations, specifications, and standards for airports need Federal aid. Most of the air lines are in serious financial difficulties and these will continue even if fares, postal and freight rates are raised. The place of airports in the proposed centralized public works organization should be much the same as that of public roads. The new Department would take over the airport construction program but would have nothing to do with control of air travel and airways or regulatory and other forms of 18

administration. These are matters for the Department of Commerce to continue to regulate and supervise.

The subject of Federal aid is not to be disposed of by catch phrases. 

Generally speaking, Federal aid on a matched basis is preferable to complete Federal aid without State or local contributions, because genuine local interest and concern are manifest only when the local citizen knows he has to pay part of the cost out of local taxes. Somewhere between the extreme socialist philosophy of Beveridge with his cradle to grave national concern for every individual and his contempt for American States' rights and municipal home rule on the one hand, and, on the other, the Bourbon philosophy of some of our own American reactionaries which would leave everybody to shift for himself, lies the sound middle of the road pragmatism which bids the Federal Government supplement the State and locality to raise the general level of the Nation in a limited number of fields in which such aid cannot sap the vitality of the State nor cripple the initiative of the individual. All conservative students of the subject agree that we should keep Federal aid within strict bounds, and that only sufficient subventions should be made from the Federal Treasury to enable the States and their subdivisions to achieve definite purposes of national significance. Competition between Federal, State, and municipal governments in the same tax fields should gradually be eliminated to the end that double and treble taxation will disappear and States and local governments will have additional sources of legitimate revenue to meet their own obligations.

It is obviously silly to be dogmatic about the precise dimensions of the proper Federal aid fields. The whole problem, like that of establishing social equality, is one of evolution. All virtue does not lie in the States, nor all good government and true economy in the grass-roots and villages. Grass-roots and sidewalk taxpayers may be able to watch carefully and perhaps intelligently the dimes that go into local school taxes, but they are no authorities on atomic research. 

Nor can we safely assume that crossroads wiseacres know all the answers to other international and scientific riddles. The concept of weak Federal Government supported by strong States has no more validity than that of a gigantic national bureaucracy undermining the States and subjugating the municipalities. Our own native common sense will dictate the compromises which will preserve our American system and still meet changing world conditions. 

The Case Against a Cabinet Department of Transportation 

The allocation of highways, airports and canals, waterways, pipe lines, and other facilities to a new Department of Transportation seems to us unwise and undesirable. Many of the Federal functions 19

in this field involve regulation of private enterprise and quasi-judicial functions. For example, public highways in a Transportation Department might require a certificate of convenience and necessity for every road and Federal regulation of every individual flivver driver. 

Bitter controversies, delay, and confusion in road building would result with no ultimate advantage even to the railroads. The same logic applies to airport and waterway construction. Air travel and the transportation of freight by air do not seriously compete with the railroads and will not for some time. No possible advantage to the public can be secured by attempting to consolidate Federal planning and supervision in these two distinct fields merely because they are both forms of transportation.

As to waterways, it is only necessary to remember that some of our largest seaports are in fact not seaports at all but rather harbors on rivers usually reached by approach waterways. The ship canal at 

Houston, the Mississippi River at New Orleans, Puget Sound at Seattle, the Columbia River at Portland, and the Delaware River at Philadelphia are examples. Placing these approach rivers and canals in a department of transportation because of possible competition with the railroads would be a ridiculous piece of doctrinaire legislation. 

No doubt the public service aspects of rail and perhaps air-line transportation have been overshadowed by quasi-judicial regulation, but this overlooked aspect of transportation does not justify the establishment of a new and unnecessary Department of Transportation. 

Problems of Personnel, Incentives, and Compensation Where engineering activities are concerned, and no matter how the new Department of Works is organized, the traditional tendency to build up a large permanent civil service force, in the absence of a foreseeable and continuing need, should be opposed and counteracted. 

Civil service forces in many Government engineering bureaus invite justified criticism of unnecessary multiplication of permanent personnel and overhead expenses for specific projects which would be better and more cheaply designed and supervised on a consulting fee basis. 

We need competent top engineers in civil service, but it is only human nature for the rank and file who are paid out of limited project funds to string out the work and make it last as long as possible. Adoption of a policy to retain qualified engineers engaged in private practice, for specific purposes on a fee basis would expedite work, reduce overhead costs, afford an opportunity to secure specialized personnel for such specialized work and would encourage professional pride without weakening the "esprit de corps" of the permanent civil-service personnel. 20

Adequate salaries and competent personnel are vital to the proper functioning of any organization. Salaries in the Federal Government, always notoriously low, have not kept up with those in large-scale private enterprise, and it is often impossible to obtain the best qualified people to accept or continue in positions of responsibility with the Federal Government. The number of employees in any Government department should be kept at a minimum but the best people should be obtained and held by adequate pay and other rewards and inducements. We do not recommend a large technical staff to plan, design, construct, and operate public works. On the contrary, we strongly recommend an adequate staff and the employment of outside consultants for special tasks as they arise. Substantial savings in cost of design and construction will result. 

We should aim at a department with the smallest reasonable number of regular employees. Employees of professional training and rank should be absolutely first-rate people, on a par with the best in private employment, and competent to engage and direct the activities of consultants and contractors in private business. It should be noted that in World War II, military and related establishments more and more adopted the practice of employing outside consultants for specific tasks of limited duration. They got away from the old practice of building up an immense permanent civil service staff for projects performed better, more quickly, and more cheaply by private corporations. Among such corporations should also be included university and other research groups; laboratories and research units of large corporations, as well as engineering, architectural, and other professional firms. The Federal Government will enjoy the up-todate services of engineers on the latest developments in their particular fields. It will permit the selection of professional firms familiar with the particular problems of the locality, physical difficulties at the site, problems of local building codes, and questions of availability of local labor.

As Government expands rapidly into fields hitherto preempted by private enterprise the need of first-class talent in Federal agencies becomes ever greater and the deficiencies more glaring. The most ambitious find greater rewards elsewhere and it is extraordinary not that we do not attract more of them into public service but that considering the draw-backs we have so many. It is important to provide incentives for Government service such as pensions and other benefits which make it attractive. It must be remembered that large progressive private corporations also offer pensions as an inducement; no longer is this an exclusive Government characteristic of public employment. The privilege of working for the Government is its own reward but even that argument cannot be labored too much. 21

Plan Recommended 

Our Task Force studied and analyzed every reasonable arrangement and consolidation of the functions of public works presently scattered throughout the various Cabinet departments and independent offices. We narrowed the area of possible controversy by exchange of information and informal conferences with other task forces and through the secretariat. We concluded that the only plan of integration, which would accomplish the results aimed at in the law establishing the Commission on Reorganization, would be a genuine complete integration in a new major Department of Works of Cabinet rank of all scattered engineering and related functions, including operational as well as service and research functions. Under this plan there would be a reorganization and consolidation of what is left of the Interior Department with Public Works after various functions now discharged by the Interior are redistributed to Agriculture and elsewhere. In place of the Interior Department this would result in a new Department of Works headed by a Cabinet officer. 

We have given some thought to the appropriate name of the proposed new department. This comprehensive function of Government might be described by the word "Works" or "Development" rather than by the meaningless word "Interior," or the limited term "Conservation." Traditionally our major departments have a single name, and there should be a good reason for departing from this sound practice. Moreover it is the connotative, not the denotative meaning of a word in such a context which ultimately gives it significance. 

We therefore recommend the single name "Works" for the proposed new department, preferring it to the name "Development" largely because the latter, although arresting and significant, carries with it some implications of speculation, spending, and private business enterprise which might be misunderstood.

Proposed Legislation 

Reorganization of the type here proposed necessarily involves the ultimate amendment of many specific laws relating to existing administrative agencies and projects. In order to expedite the reorganization process, it is proposed that there be presented to the Congress an interim administrative code providing for the continuance of existing functions and regulations within the framework, intent, and spirit of the new organization. A draft of such a law appears in the appendix. This draft is modeled to a considerable extent on the interim Executive Department's law used in the State of New York at the time of the reorganization of its scattered departments and offices, a device which served admirably to insure the continuity of Government while the detailed statutory revisions were being codified. 

A draft of a bill establishing a Department of Works also appears in the appendix. This provides for the appointment and compensation of the Secretary, Under Secretary, and four Assistant Secretaries; for the transfer of the various agencies to be absorbed within the new Department, including the distinctly civilian functions, personnel and property of the Corps of Engineers and its chief. II. SUBCOMMITTEE REPORTS Division of Planning and Scientific Research BUREAU OF MINES 

History.--The Bureau of Mines was established in the Department of the Interior by an act of Congress approved May 16, 1910, to promote safety in the mineral industries, conserve mineral resources, and conduct investigations on the use of minerals. In 1925 the Bureau was transferred to the Commerce Department but returned to Interior in 1934.

Activities. Since its creation the Bureau has pursued numerous activities related to the basic functions of the act. It has introduced new safety practices, improved techniques in using minerals, and published much scientific, technical, and economic information vital to the progress of the mineral industries. 

The organic functions of the Bureau have been repeatedly supplemented by additional legislation. In 1927 the Bureau was designated as the sole agency entrusted with the production of helium, including the acquisition of gas lands, drilling of wells, and construction of plants. 

In 1939, the Bureau was authorized to investigate possible domestic sources of strategic minerals. Public Law 290, approved April 5, 1944, authorizes the construction and operation of demonstration plants to produce synthetic liquid fuels from coal, oil shale, and other substances.

The Bureau employs approximately 3,400 persons, of which almost half are assigned to the Fuels and Explosives and the Metallurgical Divisions. The Bureau has requested over $20,000,000 for 1949 operations as opposed to the $12,000,000 authorized for the preceding year, with almost the entire increase earmarked for the synthetic liquid fuels program. Recommendation

The Bureau of Mines is carrying out a program of scientific research which not only is of considerable importance to industry but will contribute substantially to the national defense. As a technical service agency, the Bureau logically belongs under a cabinet officer primarily concerned with the engineering, construction, and research phases of governmental operations. Operating under such an organization would assure the Bureau of more effective coordination with 27

-- other research bureaus, all consolidated in a Division of Planning and Scientific Research. It is, therefore, recommended that the Bureau of Mines be included as a unit in the proposed Department of Works. 

GEOLOGICAL SURVEY 

History. Surveys conducted by the Federal Government prior to the Civil War were chiefly exploratory in character. Most of them were made by the Army Corps of Engineers and the U. S. Coast Survey.

After the Civil War, surveys were pushed with increased vigor. 

Four surveying expeditions were put in the field, two by the War Department and two by the Department of the Interior. By 1879 Congress, convinced that there was unnecessary duplication, approved an act which established the Geological Survey under the Department of the Interior. In 1898 the Survey's functions were enlarged to include a division of mines and mining but most of these activities were transferred to the Bureau of Mines in 1910. The Survey continued to publish statistics of mineral production until 1925 when that function was also transferred to the Bureau of Mines. 

In 1894 the Survey was directed to gage the streams and study the water supply of the United States. This work has continued to date. 

Many of the early reclamation studies were made by the Survey but in 1907 this work was transferred to the newly established Bureau of Reclamation. Similarly the Survey examined and mapped forest reserves from 1897 to 1905, when this function was transferred to the General Land Office and later to the Forest Service. The Grazing 

Service of the Department of the Interior took over all grazing studies in 1934.

Activities.-The Geological Survey is presently organized into four scientific and engineering operating branches as follows: 

1. Geologic Branch-the geology, mineral resources, and mineral possibilities of the country. 

2. Topographic Branch-topographic surveys and maps in the 

United States, its Territories, and possessions. 

3. Water Resources Branch-investigation of water resources, both surface and underground, of the United States and its territories and possessions. 

4. Conservation Branch-classification of public lands, specially their mineral and water resources, and supervision of private operations for mineral development on leased public and Indian lands, and determination of output and royalty liability. 

On December 31, 1946, the Geological Survey had a total of 5,872 employees, of whom 962 were departmental employees and 4,910 field employees. Of the latter, 2,246 were part-time gage and well readers. 28

On July 1, 1947, the total number of employees had increased to 6,705. 

The appropriation for 1948 fiscal year was $10,091,340. 

The Survey reproduces its own maps, charts, and diagrams and does some similar work for other Federal agencies. It should be noted, however, that the Coast and Geodetic Survey performs similar functions, duplicating more or less the map production, printing, and other reproduction facilities of the Geological Survey. Recommendation

Although the Geological Survey does no construction work, all of its functions are closely connected with engineering. It definitely should be included in the proposed Department of Works. Its functions should be merged with those of the Coast and Geodetic Survey in the new department with other related agencies engaged in planning, mapping, and research.

U. S. COAST AND GEODETIC SURVEY 

History. In 1807 Congress directed that a survey be made of the eastern coast and the islands and shoals offshore. The President assigned this work to the Treasury Department which created the "United States Coast Survey." Delay in procuring precise instruments, combined with the War of 1812, resulted in postponing actual field work until 1816. The Survey has continued operations since, except for the periods 1818-32 and 1834-36.

In 1871 the Congress authorized the work of the Survey to be expanded sufficiently to make a geodetic connection between the Atlantic and Pacific coasts and to provide the States with geographic positions for control of their topographic and geologic surveys. The designation was then changed to "United States Coast and Geodetic Survey." On July 1, 1903, the Survey was transferred from the Treasury 

Department to the newly created Department of Commerce and Labor. When that Department was split in 1913, the Coast and 

Geodetic Survey was placed in the Department of Commerce where it is assigned now. 

Two additional activities have been assigned to the Survey in recent years seismology, transferred by Congress from the Weather Bureau in 1925, and the preparation of aeronautical charts, delegated to the 

Secretary of Commerce in 1926.

Activities. The Coast and Geodetic Survey renders a variety of technical services for the marine, aviation, commercial, and industrial interests of the country. It produces nautical and aeronautical charts and related publications, topographic maps, control surveys, tide and current tables, geomagnetic surveys, and carries out seismologic investigations.

Charts and maps are printed in the Survey's printing plant. Other publications are printed at the Government Printing Office. 29

District offices are maintained at Boston, New York, Norfolk, San 

Juan, New Orleans, Los Angeles, San Francisco, Portland, Seattle, and Honolulu. During World War II the Survey cooperated with the 

Army and Navy on several major military survey projects. 

During 1946, the Survey operated 13 survey vessels. 

On June 30, 1946, the total personnel of the Survey was 2,117, of whom 13 commissioned officers were serving with the armed forces. 

On June 30, 1947, the number of employees had increased to 2,442.

For the fiscal year 1946 operations, a total of $7,980,493 was received; for 1948, $9,550,000. Recommendation

The activities of the Coast and Geodetic Survey are fundamentally engineering. Survey and other data necessary for the proper study, design, and construction of projects which come under the jurisdiction of a Department of Works can best be obtained by the Survey. 

The Coast and Geodetic Survey belongs in the new Department of Works. Its work should be merged with that of the Geological 

Survey which also should be placed in this Department, along with other planning, mapping, and research agencies. 

NATIONAL BUREAU OF STANDARDS 

History. The Department of Commerce and Labor was created in 

1903 as one body. On March 4, 1913, the labor activities were separated and transferred to the newly established Department of Labor and the Department of Commerce was constituted. 

Additional duties have been assigned from time to time by new legislation and by Executive order.

Included as a part of the department was the National Bureau of Standards which was established by Congress on March 3, 1901. 

Activities. Functions of the Department of Commerce are to foster, promote, and develop foreign and domestic commerce, the mining, manufacturing, shipping and fishing industries, and the transportation facilities of the United States. 

Under the Office of the Secretary, the department is broken into 13 bureaus, offices, and administrations, including the National 

Bureau of Standards mentioned above.

The National Bureau of Standards is the principal agency of the Federal Government for fundamental research in physics, chemistry, and engineering. It has custody of the national standards of physical measurement and general responsibility for development of improved methods for testing materials and equipment. It determines physical contents and properties of materials. 

A large part of the work of the bureau is establishing specifications for the purchase of Federal supplies and rendering advisory service to Government agencies on scientific and technical matters. Coopera- 30

tion is extended to States, industries, and national organizations in the development of specifications and engineering and safety codes. 

An example of the service rendered to other departments is the work done for the armed forces in the research of guided missiles. 

The research activities are so broad that the Bureau is broken into 

14 divisions.

The normal budget of the National Bureau of Standards seems to run from $7,000,000 to $10,000,000, but it also has assigned for its use sums of money from other departments sometimes greater than the direct appropriation For instance, in the year 1947, the direct appropriations were $6,800,000 but they were supplemented by $10,- 800,000 in funds transferred by the Army, Navy, National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics, the Office of the Secretary of Commerce, the Bureau of the Census, and other Federal agencies. Appropriations for actual construction are a minor part. Out of a total of 2,800 employees, approximately 40 percent are professional scientists and engineers, and an additional 20 percent are technicians and aides. Recommendation

This Bureau is essentially a technical scientific service agency to the several Government departments and organizations as well as private industry. A great many of its activities in research on construction materials and formulation of specifications and codes belong directly in a Department of Works under an assistant secretary heading a research and planning division. It could, as a service agency, supply the same information to the Department of Commerce and other agencies as it does at present. Therefore, it is recommended that the National Bureau of Standards be included as a unit in the proposed Department of Works and consolidated with other research units.

Division of Water Control and Development RIVERS AND HARBORS 

History. The first act of Congress relating to improvement of rivers and harbors dates back to August 11, 1790, when Congress assented to certain acts of the States of Maryland, Georgia, and Rhode Island, levying a duty on the tonnage of ships for the purposes of protecting and preserving rivers and harbors.

The first substantial Federal aid was provided by Congress early in the nineteenth century, mostly in the form of subsidies for the construction of canals. In 1824 grants of land to aid in canal construction and river improvement were made to a number of States. 

For more than a century Army engineers were given by Congress the responsibility of investigating proposed work and of carrying out authorized civil work projects. In 1879 the Mississippi River Commission was created by an act of Congress, to consist of seven members, three of which were to be officers of the Corps of Engineers. 

An act of June 13, 1902, authorized improvement of any navigable rivers by persons, corporations, or municipalities and specified that such work must conform with the general plan of Government improvement and that plans and specifications therefor must be approved by the Secretary of War and the Chief of Engineers. 

The same act created a Board of Engineers for Rivers and Harbors, providing for the organization in the office of the Chief of Engineers of a board of five engineer members. The act further directs that all reports on surveys of river and harbor projects be reviewed by the Board prior to submission by the Chief of Engineers to Congress. This directive was later amended to apply also to flood control projects. 

In 1920 under the Transportation Act the Board of Engineers for Rivers and Harbors was charged with the additional duties of investigating water terminals, consulting with local interests relative to location and planning of terminals, and with the compilation, publication, 32

and distribution of statistical data and information concerning water transportation.

Activities.-The River and Harbor Division is presently a branch of the Civil Works Section of the Corps of Engineers. It is headed by the Assistant Chief of Engineers for Civil Works who is responsible directly to the Chief of Engineers. The Civil Works section consists of four divisions: Administrative, Engineering, Flood Control, and 

River and Harbor.

Administrative and Engineering act as service bureaus for the 

Flood Control and River and Harbor Divisions. The general adminis trative functions of the Branch for Civil Works (fiscal, legal, personnel, safety, etc.) are performed by the administrative organization servicing all the various divisions of the Corps of Engineers. 

The River and Harbor Division consists of the following subdivisions: a. Miscellaneous Civil Branch: Supervision of all matters relative to administration of laws for protection and preservation of navigable waters and information to mariners. This branch also includes the supervision of the New York Harbor (under a special act). b. Operations Branch: Direction of construction, operation, and maintenance of navigation and allied improvement works. Project estimates, surveys, liaison with Navy Department and with other components of the office of the Chief of Engineers. 

c. Planning and Special Projects Branch: Direction of field studies requested by congressional directives or by governmental agencies. 

Directing planning of multiple-purpose projects for conformance with law and policies. Reviewing cost allocations for multiple-purpose projects. Survey of the St. Lawrence seaway. 

d. Technical Data Branch: Research work. Supplying technical information on port facilities, harbors, etc.

The present program for Federal improvement of rivers and harbors includes projects located throughout the United States, Puerto Rico, Alaska, and the Hawaiian Islands; a total of approximately 1,150; estimated cost to complete, about $1,580,000,000. In 1947 the Corps of Engineers spent $89,651,092 on river and harbor improvement. 

Total personnel in the Civil Works Divison of the Corps of Engineers 

(including both flood control and river and harbor improvements) consists of approximately 37,000 civilian employees and 125 officers, assigned for primary duty. 

An important phase of the work included in the River and Harbor Division is the exercise of jurisdiction over pierhead and bulkhead lines.

In addition to river and harbor improvement and flood control, it has been committed to certain other work, as for instance: Water 33

supply, maintenance, and repair of the aqueduct, Washington, D. C.; reports of international boards on operations affecting international boundary waters; survey and charting of northern and northwestern lakes; and development of landing areas for Civil Aeronautics Administration, etc. Recommendation

The character of the work performed by the River and Harbor Section of the Army Corps of Engineers is civil rather than military and since these activities fit in with those of the proposed Department of Works transfer to that Department is recommended. The additional activities listed in the previous paragraph likewise should be integrated into the proper divisions of the proposed Department of Works. By including it in the Department of Works, Rivers and

PANAMA CANAL 

History. The American phase of the construction of the Panama 

Canal started in 1904 and the first ocean steamer passed through on August 3, 1914, although the canal was not declared open formally until 1921.

An act of Congress, approved on August 24, 1912, established the operating organization for the Canal and the Canal Zone in essentially the same form as that existing today. Under this act authority of the 

United States Government in the Canal Zone is exercised through a Governor appointed by the President with the approval of the Senate, for a term of 4 years. The Governor controls the Canal proper and also all branches of civil government in the zone. 

In time of war or national emergency, the entire Panama Canal establishment can be placed under the jurisdiction of the Army, as was done during World 

War II. 

Activities. The various departments functioning under the Governor of the Canal Zone are responsible for all civil governmental duties in the area, including the operation of the Canal and auxiliary enterprises. There is also a United States District Court within the zone. 

The Panama Railroad is wholly owned by the United States Government and operates, in addition to the railroad, a variety of industries including a steamship line, harbor terminal, coaling plants, commissary, dairy farm, two hotels, telephone system, bakery, and various other units.

The total capital investment of the United States Government in the Canal Zone up to the beginning of the fiscal year 1946 was $705,356,000. Average prewar net revenues from the Canal and all business operations were about $15,000,000 per year. Because of the sharp reduction in toll-paying commercial traffic, the Canal was operated at a deficit during the war years, but for the fiscal year 1947 there were net revenues of $1,404,325. This does not take into account any return on capital investment. 

As of July 1, 1947, 25,556 persons were employed of whom nearly one-fourth were in the executive, professional, and skilled-trade categories. Recommendation

The functions performed by the Panama Canal administrators are primarily the operation and maintenance of the Canal, its auxiliary services and a number of Government-owned business enterprises which are necessary to the normal life of the community. 

It is recommended that jurisdiction of the Canal be placed under the Department of Works in order that these functions may be carried out most effectively and to provide technical services for studies and construction of any possible future routes. No change is recom- 36

mended in the existing legislation which provides for placing the entire Canal Zone under the direct authority of the National Defense Establishment in time of emergency.

BUREAU OF RECLAMATION 

History.

The Bureau of Reclamation was established within the 

Department of the Interior by the Federal Reclamation Act of June 17, 1902. This act made the irrigation of arid lands a national policy and authorized the Secretary of Interior to survey, plan, and construct. works for the storage and diversion of water to be used in irrigation and for the conservation of the waste water of streams. 

Under the provisions of the act the Bureau of Reclamation has become the principal agency for developing irrigation in the arid States. 

The Bureau is headed by a Commissioner with headquarters in Washington, D. C., and an engineering office under the supervision of the 

Chief Engineer is located in Denver, Colo.

The law requires irrigation projects to be self liquidating and repayments are made under a number of different arrangements provided by various amendments to the act. The Bureau also is authorized to construct and operate hydroelectric plants, the costs of which are to be repaid from power revenues. Other related improvements for beneficial purposes, such as flood control, navigation, and conservation, may be undertaken without prospect of reimbursement. 

Activities.The Bureau of Reclamation constructs and operates irrigation works, makes surveys, and provides engineering services for same; collects annual payments from water users for the costs of irrigation; promotes knowledge of irrigation methods, suitability of crops, availability of markets, and improvement of farm homes. It also has developed many hydroelectric plants and other related improvements. 

Among the Bureau of Reclamation's better known projects are the Colorado River Basin development with its Hoover Dam and the Columbia River Basin project including Grand Coulee Dam. At the present time the Bureau is engaged in the large-scale development of the Missouri River Basin. Approximately $1,000,000,000 has been expended on construction by the Bureau since its inception. It was granted appropriations in the amount $136,644,000 for 1948 and $245,566,000 for 1949. The Bureau currently employs approximately 

16,000 persons. Recommendation

The Bureau of Reclamation's activities involve a very large volume of construction conducted continuously over the years. As the 

Bureau also maintains and operates these projects, design features can be effectively coordinated with construction, future alteration, and maintenance problems. Activities of the Bureau cannot be separated 37

readily and it is recommended that the entire Bureau be placed under the proposed Department of Works. 

It should be noted that the Bureau of Reclamation's activities in irrigation and power developments are related closely to the floodcontrol activities of the Corps of Engineers. In such cases as the 

Columbia River Basin and the Missouri River Basin projects, the Bureau and the Corps of Engineers have had responsibility for different parts of the same general development. Consolidation of these activities under a single control will eliminate conflicts, friction, and duplication of effort.

BONNEVILLE POWER ADMINISTRATION History. The Bonneville Power Administration was created by an act of Congress approved August 20, 1937. It was set up within the Department of the Interior and the Administrator is appointed by the Secretary of the Interior. 

Activities. It was originally created for the purpose of marketing the power generated at the Bonneville Dam on the Columbia River in Oregon and Washington, and is directed by statute to encourage the widest possible use of electric energy and to provide market outlets therefor by constructing, operating, maintaining, and improving electric transmission lines and substations. It is now the agency for marketing the energy generated at the Grand Coulee Dam, also on the Columbia River. It also will market power from several other dams in that section of the country which are to be constructed if and when funds are available.

The Bonneville Dam is operated by the Corps of Engineers and the Grand Coulee Dam is operated by the Bureau of Reclamation. 

Some of the proposed dams will be constructed and operated by the Bureau of Reclamation and others by the Corps of Engineers. 

Transmission systems will cover parts of the States of Oregon, Washington, Montana, and Idaho.

This Administration appears to confine its efforts mainly to the marketing of power and only ventures into other fields where required to encourage the sale of this power. 

Facilities at both dams are recorded as having been operated at overload conditions in order to keep abreast of the consumption requirements in the area served and the Bonneville Power Administration is urging completion of additional installations of generating equipment: In 1947, the two plants produced nearly 9 billion kilowatt hours of energy. This energy was sold by the Administration to 89 wholesale customers of which 54 were publicly owned utilities, 6 were privately owned utilities, 11 were military establishments, and 

18 were industrial users. 38

Recommendation 

It is recommended that the maintenance, operation, and construction of all projects built or proposed to be built within the area served by the Bonneville Power Administration be taken over bodily by the new Department of Works. Such a proposal, if carried out, would eliminate the competition between the agencies responsible for the construction and operation of the various projects and would permit one agency to supervise all activities from the inception of the program to the completion of the transmission lines, including the sale and distribution of power.

TENNESSEE VALLEY AUTHORITY 

History. Agitation for activity in the improvement of the Tennessee River for navigation started in the administration of President Monroe in 1824. This culminated in the creation of the Tennessee 

Valley Authority by an act of Congress on May 18, 1933. The 

Authority is composed of a board of three directors appointed by the President with the approval of the Senate, with the chairman designated by the President. The act very carefully requires that the members of the board cannot engage in any other business, but must devote their time to the work of the Authority. It also requires that they have no financial interest in a public utility, fertilizer company, etc. It also requires that each member shall be a person who professes a belief in the feasibility and wisdom of the act. Some of the actions authorized in the statute require direct approval of the President of the United States. Under the act, the President of the 

United States is authorized to do certain other things with or without the approval of the Authority. This is mentioned to show that it is the intention of this law to have this established as an agency directly responsible to the President.

Activities. The Tennessee Valley Authority has custody of the 

Wilson Dam and the appurtenant plants at Muscle Shoals, Ala. It is also responsible for the development of the Tennessee River and its tributaries in the interest of navigation, the control of floods, and the generation and disposition of hydroelectric power. By executive order, it was authorized to conduct investigations to aid in the proper conservation, development and use of the resources of the region. 40

The development of the Tennessee River by the Authority has been most extensive. It has developed 16 dams in addition to the 

10 that previously existed, and coordinated them into a single system. 

This system is developed for flood control, navigation, and power as well as other secondary purposes. These include tourist business, park developments for local communities, as well as for private resorts. 

Conservation of wildlife, timber, and soil is one of the byproducts of this work. Improvement of navigation has resulted in a steady increase in the amount of freight handled on the river with a consequent increase in terminal facilities. All terminals were built with the advice of the Tennessee Valley Authority and in addition, the Authority itself operates some terminal facilities. 

As directed by statute, the Authority made its plants and its organization available to the war effort. It produced nitrates which were used for explosives and for fertilizer, phosphorus for chemical warfare and for fertilizer, and calcium carbide, an ingredient of synthetic rubber. During the war, the Authority as agent for the Federal 

Public Housing Administration built a number of houses at Muscle Shoals for war workers, and it now operates 430 housing units chiefly for veterans and their families. 

It operates a large, power-producing system having a capacity of more than 2,500,000 kilowatts. Eight of these hydroelectric plants producing more than 300,000 kilowatts are owned by the Aluminum Co. of America, but the operation is directed by the Authority. They provided great quantities of electric energy for the war effort, including that required for the atomic project at Oak Ridge, Tenn. As in the case of other Government hydroelectric developments, the Authority is required to dispose of its surplus power in such a way as to permit the widest possible use of electricity and at the same time to assist in liquidating the cost of the project. Preference is given to cooperative associations and municipalities in the disposition of this power, with the balance being sold to industrial customers. As an example of how this works, in 1947, contracts were outstanding with 92 municipalities, 47 cooperatives, and 3 privately owned utility companies, representing more than 700,000 ultimate consumers. In addition, contracts were outstanding with 9 industrial concerns for bulk sales of power. The distribution system covers substantially all of Tennessee, northern 

Alabama, northeastern Mississippi, northwestern Georgia, southwestern Kentucky, and parts of North Carolina and Virginia. In these areas, the Authority has bought out many of the private power companies and has worked with municipalities and cooperative associations in the purchase of many others.

In addition to producing materials for fertilizer, the Authority is engaged in an experimental program for the development of new and improved plant food products. It also experiments and develops new processes and advises on the modernization of plants. It also tests 817148°-48- 4 41

its newly developed products. This takes the Authority into the field of soil conservation and watershed protection. It also operates an educational program with the farmers in its area. This work is carried out in conjunction with the Agricultural Extension Service. 

Up to January 1, 1947, such test demonstrations on farms have been established in 30 States on nearly 60,000 farms. 

The program of the Tennessee Valley Authority for 1948-49 requires a budgetary appropriation of $107,351,600, a substantial part of which will be for the construction of new improvements. Recommendation

Good organization would dictate that this Authority be treated in the same way as the Bonneville and Southwestern projects and placed on the same basis in the Division of Water Control and Development of the Department of Works. However, this Authority is so much a part of the Tennessee Valley area that to radically change its set-up would unnecessarily disrupt this whole area. We therefore recommend that this Authority be taken over bodily by the Department of Works, and that the only changes made in the organization be that of eliminating the direct tie to the President of the United States, the members of the Authority to be appointed by the Secretary of the Department of Works instead of by the President, with the members reporting to the Secretary.

Cong.) It builds and maintains forest highways and national park highways in cooperation with the Forest Service and the National Park Service. The comprehensive public works activities of the Public Roads Administration make it clearly a unit to be included bodily in the proposed Department of Works. Any program designed to alleviate a future depression will certainly include. a large volume of highway and road construction. Road building also plays an important part in wartime as uninterrupted transportation is vital for war production purposes as well as military operations and civil defense. 43

NATIONAL PARK SERVICE 

History. The National Park Service, an agency in the Department of the Interior, is responsible for the supervision, development, and protection of 177 Federal areas comprising 22,000,000 acres of national parks, monuments, and reservations throughout the United States and its territories, administered in accordance with the National Park Act of August 25, 1916, as amended. Activities.-The congressional act establishing the National Park

The activities of the organization cover the provision of adequate service and facilities for visitors including supervision of concessions, development and protection of new areas, extensive maintenance and repair of facilities including 4,500 miles of roads, 6,600 miles of trails and 3,700 buildings, cooperation with the States in park and recreational planning, intensive protection of wildlife and of almost 10,000,000 acres of forest land in the parks against fire, and dissemination of public information on the park system.

The balance of the money spent by the Federal 

Government on the national park system is for administration, maintenance, and operation. Recommendation Since the program of the National Park Service includes many activities of an engineering and technical nature, involving virtually all types of construction and maintenance and requiring the employment of a large technical force, it should be included in the proposed 

Department of Works. 

Advance plans for worth while projects designed to cushion the shock of another depression will certainly include a large amount of work in the national parks as expansion of recreation facilities has proven to be one of the best means of providing useful work for the unemployed. 44

Over recent years, during which use of the national park system increased enormously, relatively little major improvement to physical plant was undertaken. To meet present demands for facilities, a large program of construction work must be undertaken. Much of this work will unquestionably be done, as in the past, by the Public Roads Administration or other construction agencies to be integrated within the proposed Department of Works. 

The inclusion of the Park Service in the Department of Works will facilitate a close working relationship between the Park Service and the agencies cooperating in its public works program. This is also true in connection with maintenance activities where the Public

Recommendation

History. The Bureau of Indian Affairs, created in 1824 as a part of the War Department, was transferred to the Department of the Interior in 1849. There is no other department of the Government where the Bureau of Indian Affairs can logically be placed, once the Department of the Interior is abolished. HOUSING AND HOME FINANCE AGENCY 

History. The major housing activities of the Federal Government were placed in the Housing and Home Finance Agency by Reorganization Plan No. 3, effective June 27, 1947. This plan permanently grouped the functions previously carried out by 17 different agencies and administrative units.

Activities. This reorganization plan set up an Administrator to supervise and coordinate the functions of three main branches, which are: (a) The Home Loan Bank Board, (b) the Federal Housing Administration, and (c) the Public Housing Administration. 

The Home Loan Bank Board consists of three members appointed by the President with consent of the Senate, not more than two of whom can be of one political party. It has taken over the functions of the Federal Home Loan Bank Board in supervising the Federal Home Loan Bank System and the Federal Savings and Loan Associations and in administering the Federal Savings and Loan Insurance 

Corporation, the Home Owners' Loan Corporation, and the United 

States Housing Corporation. The first two provide a credit reserve and insurance for thrift and home financing institutions with the basic purposes of strengthening the home-financing industry, improving mortgage-lending practice and placing thrift in home ownership on a broader, more secure basis. The Federal Savings and Loan 

Insurance Corporation insures accounts of shareholders of Federal and State chartered savings and loan associations up to $5,000. From 

1933 to 1936, the Home Owners' Loan Corporation refinanced the mortgages of more than 1,000,000 home owners involving a total investment of $3,492,759,260. Since then its chief function has been liquidation of its assets, including collection of loans made in connection with these refinancing operations and the sale of the houses which it acquired through foreclosure. The United States Housing Corporation, established to provide war housing during World War I, is in the final stages of dissolution.

The Federal Housing Administration is headed by the Federal 

Housing Commissioner, who is appointed by the President with the consent of the Senate. Its general purpose is to insure private lending 48

institutions against loss on loans secured by mortgages. Authorization for additional mortgage insurance is increased by Congress from time to time to cover existing needs. Under the National Housing 

Act as amended, its functions include the following: a. Title I: To insure loans to convert or rehabilitate existing structures or to build small new structures to provide additional living accommodations and to alter, repair or improve other existing structures. b. Title II: To insure mortgage loans on home and rental housing to achieve a sustained, long-term residential construction program based on an economical credit basis. c. Title VI: Since May 1946 the application of this title has been for needed housing restricted to veterans' priority and hardship cases. d. Title VII: To encourage the building of rental homes for moderate-income families through the guarantee of yield up to 24 percent on the equity. 

The basic mission of the Public Housing Administration is to carry out the low-rent housing and slum-clearance program authorized by the United States Housing Act of 1937. This involves Federal loan and subsidy aid to local public authorities to provide housing within the means of low-income families living in slums. Other functions include disposition of the subsistence homestead and suburban resettlement projects originally developed by the Farm Security Administration for low-income nonfarm families; the final liquidation of the Defense Homes Corporation and the Home Owners' Loan Cor poration; the liquidation of the national defense and war-housing programs and the execution of the veterans' temporary postwar housing program.

There are a few housing activities which it has not been feasible to place under the Housing and Home Finance Agency. Some of them are integral parts of other broad programs, such as loans to veterans through the Veterans' Administration. Therefore, a National Housing Council was created within the Housing and Home Finance Agency to coordinate these functions. This council is composed of the Housing Administrator as chairman, the heads of the 

Home Loan Bank Board, the Federal Housing Administration and the Public Housing Administration, the Administrator of Veterans' Affairs, the Chairman of the Reconstruction Finance Corporation, and the Secretary of Agriculture or their designees. Recommendation

The consolidation of major Government housing functions provides an opportunity for the coordination of the housing activities in aid of private enterprise with those related to the field of public housing. 

Unless this coordination is accomplished successfully at departmental level, the Office of the President will be burdened with this additional function. The primary function of the Agency is to encourage and provide the construction of good and economical housing and this basic objective may be achieved most readily by the inclusion of the 49

Agency in the department where its personnel can work closely with the specialized engineering and architectural staff concentrated there. 

It is concluded therefore, that the Housing and Home Finance Agency should be placed under the Department of Works.

Activities.-The Public Health Service functions under the immedi ate direction of the Surgeon General. In its organization are publichealth specialists including sanitary engineers. It advises the Federal Works Agency on requirements regarding the construction of marine hospitals, laboratories, and other facilities. It will conduct somewhat similar activities, in cooperation with the Federal Works Agency, in aiding State, interstate, and municipal agencies under the water-pollution-control law. As a measure of the size of this latter program, the law authorizes in each of five fiscal years (July 1, 1948, to June 30, 1953): a. Loans up to $22,500,000 limited to $250,000 or one-third cost of each project. b. One million dollars in grants for investigations, research, studies, and surveys. c. One million dollars in grants for engineering and planning limited to $20,000 or one-third cost of each project. d. Eight hundred thousand dollars to erect, furnish, and equip buildings and facilities at Cincinnati, Ohio, for research and study of pollution and training Public Health Service personnel. 

The most important present activity is the Federal-aid hospital program to States and other governmental subdivisions, on a matching basis, for civilian hospital construction. This will provide 841,- 

000 new hospital beds and more than 4,000 new health centers. Only preliminary planning has been done to date.

Agreements are being progressed with States allotting Federal funds for planning local hospitals and making tentative allotments for construction from funds now authorized. As of February 1948, 124 such construction projects had been approved. This program contemplates appropriations of $75,000,000 of Federal funds annually for five successive years, a total of $375,000,000, in Federal grants to match $750,000,000 of State and local funds making a total of $1,125,- 58

COAST GUARD 

History. The Coast Guard was established as the Revenue Cutter 

Service under the Treasury Department in 1790 by act of Congress, to assist in the enforcement of custom and revenue laws. The peacetime activities of the Coast Guard have expanded as the Government from time to time has enacted laws requiring enforcement at sea. 

It has assisted the Navy in every war in which the country has been involved. In 1915, by act of Congress, provision was made to transfer all personnel and equipment of the Coast Guard to the Navy during time of war and to continue it as a branch of the Treasury Department during time of peace.

Activities.-The peacetime functions of the Coast Guard relate to law enforcement, maritime safety, and military readiness. The Coast 

Guard is the specific enforcement agency for many laws and regulations relating to navigation and merchant shipping. It is also an auxiliary enforcement agency for laws relating to customs and revenue, immigration, quarantine, protection of fish and game, and matters under the jurisdiction of other Federal agencies which require marine personnel and facilities for enforcement. 

Its marine-safety activities include the safeguarding of life at sea and upon the inland waterways; the maintenance of rescue and assistance service such as life-saving stations, lighthouses, rescue vessels, and aircraft; the maintenance and construction of aids to navigation; ice breaking on inland waterways; the destruction of derelicts and other obstacles to navigation; the inspection of merchant vessels and safety equipment; and the regulation of noncommercial vessels. 

The military readiness program consists of the development, in cooperation with the Navy, of plans for the mobilization of the service as part of the Navy in time of national emergency.

During time of war its activities are expanded and it is assigned specialized naval activities, including convoy and patrol service, certain operations in heavy ice areas, and the manning of transports and landing boats in amphibious operations. 60

The Coast Guard has prepared an extensive program amounting to nearly $400,000,000 for the repair and expansion of its shore facilities and for the purchase of new vessels and aircraft for the years 194853. Of this, $55,000,000 is for the construction of new cutters and lightships and $211,000,000 is for aircraft. The $140,000,000 remaining is for the construction of buildings, water supply systems, sanitary facilities, docks, and bulkheads which are not of specialized marine character and which do not require unusual training and background of experience. The actual appropriation for the work in 1948 amounted to approximately $3,000,000. Recommendation

It is recommended that the design and construction responsibilities of the Coast Guard covering buildings, water and sewer facilities, docks, bulkheads, and similar projects of public works character be assigned to the Department of Works as a service agency. 

SOIL CONSERVATION SERVICE 

History. The Soil Erosion Service was established in 1933 as a temporary agency in the Interior Department to afford unemployment relief. In early 1935 the Service was transferred to the Department of Agriculture, expanded to include erosion-control experiment stations, erosion nurseries and the erosion-control work of the Forest Service. Its work proved so valuable in checking erosion that Congress, by act of April 27, 1935, specifically established the Soil Conservation Service in the Department of Agriculture to develop a longtime program of soil and water conservation. In the beginning most of the work was done on Federal lands but in 1937 the Service began to cooperate with soil conservation districts organized by farmers under State laws.

In 1940 the Service was transferred by Presidential order to the Department of the Interior but shortly after the declaration of war, it was made part of Conservation and Adjustment Administration, only to be moved to the Food Production Administration in December 1942 and later to the War Food Administration. 

On June 29, 1945 the Service went back to the Department of Agriculture.

Activities. The Soil Conservation Service studies water flow, sedimentation, soil erosion, and similar problems in over 100 watersheds in order to reduce flood-water and sediment damages. Its operations on 11 major watersheds are carried on in cooperation with soil-conservation districts and other local and State organizations. Rotation of crops is emphasized. Conservation farm plans have been prepared for a total of over 190,000,000 acres. 61

The Service actually applies soil-conservation measures only to Federal lands. Its work with repsect to private lands is primarily of a consultative character. 

On July 1, 1947, the Service had 12,781 employees. Its appropriations for the fiscal year 1948 totaled $40,648,000 and these were increased to $49,035,000 for 1949. Less than 14 percent is for direct construction work. The Service does its own design but handles all its construction work by contract. Recommendation

As most of the work of this agency consists of maintenance and advisory services to agricultural interests, it is recommended that the maintenance and operational functions of the Soil Conservation Service be continued in their present form. Engineering and new construction projects, however, should be handled by the new Department of Works as a service agency.

FOREST SERVICE History. The present Forest Service under the Secretary of Agri culture was established pursuant to the Agriculture Appropriation Act of 1906. It is responsible for the conservation and administration of 152 national forests comprising about 180,000,000 acres in the United States and its territories, and promotes the protection and economic use of the Nation's forest lands as a whole. 

Activities. In supervising and developing the national forests, the 

Service protects them from fire, guards against insects and disease, and controls their resources. Scientific methods are developed in growing timber; regulation of livestock grazing; supervision of water sheds with respect to floods and soil erosion; and the general protection and conservation of water, wildlife, and other resources. The Service also conducts forest research and experimentation, studies forest economics, and advises the States as well as private owners of forest lands in the scientific application of forest conservation and economic use.

The Forest Service has 10 regional offices and 13 experiment stations. 

It employs approximately 22,500 persons. The annual budget averages about $50,000,000 practically all of which is used for administration, management, research, and maintenance. In addition to these nonpublic works appropriations, the Public Roads Administration is authorized to spend approximately $20,000,000 a year for forest highways under the Federal Highway Act of 1944, and the Department of Agriculture has allotted $1,500,000 to the Forest Service for flood control. 62

Recommendation 

One of our most vital crops is the raising of timber. The Forest 

Service is primarily concerned with the administration, protection, and economic use of forest resources with public works representing a minor phase of its program. The Forest Service should be left in the Department of Agriculture, and not included in the Department of Works. However, since its road program is being successfully administered by the Public Roads Administration and since from time to time it has other engineering and public works of a conventional nature to be performed, the Forest Service should use the proposed 

Department of Works as a service agency. 

BUREAU OF LAND MANAGEMENT 

History. The Bureau of Land Management was created by Presidential order effective July 16, 1946. It was formed in the Department of the Interior by merging the Grazing Service, formed in 1934, and the old General Land Office. 

Activities. The Bureau of Land Management administers the 

Federal grazing, forestry, and other laws relating to the public domain which comprises three-fourths billion acres in the continental United States and Alaska. It carries out the leasing laws on land of the 

Department of Agriculture, supervises the Federal ranges, conducts surveys, and classifies lands as to their best uses. 

The Bureau operates from a headquarters in Washington through seven regional offices. Under each regional office are three specialized types of field offices: (1) District grazing offices (53), (2) Public survey offices (11), and (3) District land offices (25). 

For the fiscal year 1948 total obligations incurred were $13,305,064 against $14,280,665 estimated for 1949. Collections from sales, rents, and royalties were over $21,000,000 in 1947. Less than 2 percent of the total appropriations was for direct construction. On July 1, 1947, the Bureau had 1,311 employees and as of January 1, 1948, that number had been reduced to approximately 1,000. Recommendation

Because its forestry and grazing operations together with important lease administration responsibilities are closely related to the Department of Agriculture, the Bureau should be transferred to that Department. The research, mapping, and engineering facilities of the 

Department of Works will undoubtedly be most valuable to the Bureau in the carrying out of its work and it is therefore recommended that the proposed Department perform such services for the Bureau as it may request. 63

RURAL ELECTRIFICATION ADMINISTRATION 

History.

-This Administration was created by Executive order of 

May 11, 1935, under the authority of the Emergency Relief Appro priation Act of April 8, 1935. Statutory provision for the agency was made in the Rural Electrification Act of May 20, 1936. It was made a part of the Department of Agriculture. The Administrator is appointed by the President.

Activities.-The Rural Electrification Administration provides financing on the basis of 100 percent of the cost for the construction of electrical facilities to serve rural people, who do not have central station electric service. The law provides that preference shall be given to public bodies, cooperatives, and nonprofit or limited dividend associations. Recent reports indicate that the bulk of loans have been made to cooperatives. More than a billion dollars has been authorized for loans, most of which has been borrowed from the 

Reconstruction Finance Corporation. The borrowers use the funds for construction of distribution, generation, and transmission facilities, and for the purchase and installation of electrical appliances, for the wiring of houses, etc. The agency does provide some engineering and legal consultation to new borrowers, but in the main it is a loaning agency. Recommendation

Since this is primarily a loaning agency, the Rural Electrification Administration is not recommended as an agency to be included as a part of the proposed Department of Works. It is recommended that the Department of Works provide such engineering services as may be required by the Rural Electrification Administration. 

RECONSTRUCTION FINANCE CORPORATION 

History. The Reconstruction Finance Corporation was created by an act approved January 22, 1932. The Corporation is managed by a board of five directors appointed by the President with the advice and approval of Congress. As quoted in the act, the purpose was "to aid in financing agriculture, commerce and industry, to help in maintaining the economic stability of the country and to assist in promoting maximum employment and production * * 99 Activities.-The activities of the RFC are basically that of a banking institution as evidenced by its diversified loans to banks, trust companies, insurance and mortgage companies, building and loan associations, credit unions, railroads, business enterprises, drainage and irrigation districts, and other public agencies. 

It functioned as a war agency in the operation of the Defense Plant 

Corporation, Office of Defense Supplies, Office of Rubber Reserves, and the Office of Metal Reserves.

It also was authorized to make 64

subsidy payments in connection with certain commodities, to purchase marketable securities held by the Public Works Administration, and to loan money to States for aid in the acquisition of real property required for highway projects. Recommendation

Inasmuch as the responsibilities of the Reconstruction Finance Corporation are described specifically as being financial in character, it must be considered as separate and apart from the proposed Department of Works. To the extent, however, that the RFC may require expert technical advice on engineering and design matters in connection with the extension of credit for capital construction, it should be required to look upon the Department of Works as a service agency and make use of its facilities. 

EXPORT-IMPORT BANK 

History. The Export-Import Bank was established in 1934 as a banking corporation under the laws of the District of Columbia. It became an independent agency of the Government under the Export- Import Bank Act of 1945. The board of directors consists of the 

Secretary of State and four full-time members appointed by the President by and with the advice and consent of the Senate. 

Activities. The purpose of the bank is to facilitate through financial help exports and imports and exchange of commodities between the United States and foreign countries. The policy outlined in the act requires that the bank supplement and not compete with private capital. It also requires that the loans should be for specific purposes and offer reasonable assurance of repayment. The bank is authorized to have a capital stock of $1,000,000,000 and may borrow from the United States Treasury on its own obligations not more than two and a half times its authorized capital stock. The bank may have outstanding at any one time loans and guarantees not in excess of three and a half times its authorized capital stock. Recommendation

As the name indicates, this is a banking agency and as such should not be included as a part of the Department of Works. However, such technical engineering advice as it may need in carrying out its purpose should be obtained from the Department of Works.

CONCLUSION 

The two reports are not irreconcilable. We have included with this memorandum three charts, one (chart I, p. 23) covering the recommendations of the Public Works Task Force, the second (chart II, p. 74) based on the report of the Natural Resources Task Force, and the third (chart III, p. 75) indicating how most of the recommendations in the two reports might be reconciled and their conclusions combined in a new Department of Works and Conservation. 

It does not seem to us to be at all practical to have two new departments in place of Interior-one for Natural Resources which arbitrarily separates the most important, difficult, and technical of all engineering functions (namely that related to water control and development in all its phases) from a Works Department in which the engineering talent of the country would be concentrated; not to speak of splitting up physical research, duplication of scientific work, and the inadvisability of placing directly in the President's Office the analysis of highly technical and professional functions; thus paralleling functions of both the Works Department and Budget Bureau, and lifting a most important duty from the Cabinet officer who should be responsible for it and creating another independent body which the President could not personally supervise. Therefore, the creation of three new overlapping and in some respects duplicating departments in place of Interior, one of them right in the White House, seems to us to promise neither efficiency nor economy nor responsibility.

"(c) Officers and employees of a department, board, commission, council, or office, the functions, powers, and duties of which are assigned and transferred to a department, or of an institution of which the supervision is so transferred and assigned, shall be transferred without examination to the department to which such assignment and transfer is made, subject to the power of the head of such department to abolish unnecessary office or positions or to transfer officers and employees from one position to another, or to change the title or designation of the office or employment subject to the civil-service laws and the Classification

Act of 1923 as amended. "(g) The assignment and transfer of any of the functions, powers, and duties of a department, board, commission, council, or office to a department shall not affect any action or proceedings, civil or criminal, pending at the time of such assignment and transfer brought by or against the department, board, commission, council, or office, functions, powers, and duties whereof are assigned and transferred, but the same may be prosecuted or defended in the name of the department to which such assignment and transfer is made, or the appropriate authority in such department having jurisdiction, and such department or authority, upon application to the court, shall be substituted as a party. "(h) Any proceeding or other business or matter undertaken or commenced by a department, board, commission, council, officer, or employee, functions, powers, and duties whereof are assigned and transferred to a department, in relation to a matter jurisdiction whereof is vested by such assignment and transfer in the department, and pending at the time of such assignment and transfer, may be conducted and completed by the department, or the appropriate authority in such department having jurisdiction, in the same manner and under the same terms and conditions and with the same effect as if undertaken or commenced and conducted and completed by the department, board, commission, council, officer, or employee, the functions, powers, and duties whereof are so assigned and transferred. "(i) Whenever a department, board, commission, council, office, officer, or employee, is referred to or designated in any law, contract, or document, such 77

references or designations shall be deemed to refer to and include the department to which the functions, powers, and duties whereof are assigned and transferred, or the appropriate authority therein, so far as such law, contract, or document pertains to matters which are by reason of such assignment and transfer placed within the jurisdiction of the department to which such assignment and transfer is made."

A BILL 

To establish a Department of Works, and for other purposes Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of 

America in Congress assembled,

SECTION 1. That (a) There shall be at the seat of government an executive 

Department to be known as the Department of Works (hereinafter referred to as the "Department") and a Secretary of Works (hereinafter referred to as the "Secretary"), who shall be the head thereof to be appointed by the President, by and with the advice and consent of the Senate, and whose term and tenure of office shall be like that of the heads of the other executive departments. (b) The Secretary of Works shall cause a seal of office to be made for the said Department of such device as the President shall approve and judicial notice shall be taken thereof.

SEC. 2. (a) There shall be in the Department of Works an Under-Secretary with compensation at the rate of Twenty Thousand Dollars ($20,000.00) per annum, who shall be appointed by the President, by and with the advice and consent of the Senate. (b) The Secretary shall appoint four Assistant Secretaries of Works, with compensation at the rate of Fifteen Thousand Dollars ($15,000.00) per annum, and such other officers and employees as may be necessary to carry out the provisions of this act and as may be provided for by Congress from time to time. There shall also be in the Department such other officers and employees as may be transferred to the Department under the provisions of this act. 

(c) There shall be in the Department of Works the Office of General Counsel for the Department of Works. The General Counsel shall be appointed by the 

Secretary of Works, and shall receive compensation at the rate of Fifteen Thousand Dollars ($15,000) per annum. The Secretary may appoint such other Assistant Counsel, with compensation at the rate of Ten Thousand Dollars ($10,000) per annum, not to exceed five, as he may deem necessary. (d) The Secretary may prescribe duties for the Under Secretary of Works, the General Counsel, the Assistant Secretaries, and the Assistant Counsel, as well as for all the other employees in the Department, and may make changes and transfers therein, when, in his judgment, it becomes necessary. 

SEC. 3. It shall be the province and duty of said Department generally to investigate and make plans and recommend measures for the conservation and promotion for the proper use and development of the natural resources of the United States and to supervise the acquisition, design, construction, maintenance and operation of public works, and to develop in advance plans and programs of deferred public works designed to furnish employment in times of depression or other emergency and to promote national security. 

78

SEC. 4. The duties of the said Department shall include the preparation of programs and cooperation with State and local governments in the planning, expediting, and financing of necessary deferred public works in periods of recession or depression, the furnishing of advice to State and local governments on plans for national defense in emergencies, the analysis and preparation of reports upon the economic and engineering feasibility of proposed major public improvements of a national or regional character, the furnishing of advice and cooperation in public works undertaken by State and local governments which are of national or regional significance and involve Federal aid, advice, allocation, or control, and the furnishing of engineering services to other departments, bureaus and agencies.
