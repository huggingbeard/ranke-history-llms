FOREWORD 

It should be noted that there is no attempt to present the subject of Business Ethics completely. Two-thirds of the content deals with historical and psychological facts which should form a basis or background. The other portion represents an effort to define the business application of the subject of ethics, simultaneously connecting the social and political influences exerted upon the individual to the point where each person constructs his separate code of ethics. This code should be practical and inclusive enough to enable him to justify his actions to the satisfaction of himself, his business associates and society in general. 

Special topics which are not always included in the study of ethics are considered briefly. Students who become interested in these subjects are enthusiastically encouraged to continue study upon their own initiative. The bibliography contains information regarding material and each source will provide additional references.

THE ROMANCE OF PIRACY, by E. Keble Chatterton, published in London by Seeley, Service & Co. Limited, provided an interesting and exciting source for the first chapter. Behaviorism deserves additional careful consideration by each student. 

Suggestions will be welcomed from those who are using "An 

Introduction to Business Ethics." 

No foreword is complete without its apology. Passing from the obvious one of audacity for an attempt to outline such a perplexing and elusive field of knowledge, I plead guilty to the charge of jotting down thoughts without revision or due regard for English, grammar or coherence.

Chapter I PIRACY 

We are led to believe that the Stone Age Man employed a unique method of settling business disputes. When he coveted an article possessed by his neighbor he bargained with him and tried to barter, offering an article for the desired one. But this bickering was of short duration. The trader owned very few pieces and the very fact that he showed an interest in the prize prompted the owner to place a higher value upon it. From the scant amount of reconstructed evidence gathered through history we can imagine the termination of this incident. A quarrel arises and the possessor of the greatest physical strength, the person with the most endurance or the shrewdest mental reasoning during the combat becomes the legal owner. Personal prowess was the factor which determined the extent of the wealth of the period. That individual who could battle a sabre-tooth tiger, clad only in animal skins and equipped with no more than a short knife fashioned and sharpened from stone, and a stout knobbed club from the forest, became the leader and capitalist.

Down through the ages there has been a tendency to accept might as the determining factor of possession of property. 

Piracy was at one time a great and noble profession. This statement may well startle us today and bring forth a large amount of incredulity. As a profession, it never consisted of more than robbery by sea or on the adjoining coast. A pirate was regarded as a gentleman and more honorable than his neighbor who tried to wrest a livelihood by trading or fashioning commodities. Commerce on the ocean was conducted in a stealthy manner, simply because the corsairs were alert enough to trap the unwary. Shipping resorted to the expediency of bluff and disguise to elude the vigilance of the sea robber. Black sails replaced the white as soon as harbor was cleared. Lookouts were placed on the highest mast to watch for sails and even the most innocent craft was given a wide berth. Silence was strictly enforced. Not even a dinner bell was used to call the crew to eat.

Public opinion pictured the pirate as a brave man, partially on account of his thrilling exploits and romance but mainly because he conducted his feats openly and aboveboard, heartily advertising the fact that he had overhauled and boarded the cowardly merchant ship. It is reported that when two ships met on the high seas the

common query was "Are you a trader or a pirate?" Responses were given truthfully and the corsair gave his answer more proudly than the merchant.

Piracy originated due to the spirit of adventure, a motive similar to that attributed to the Yankee Traders. Man has always had an urge to go to sea, which has been manifested largely by exploration and colonization. Piracy, from the standpoint of pure adventure, was even more fascinating and dangerous than either of these. One success led the pirate sailor to pace his deck with a fierce determination to gain greater glory and renown, while each pirate victory filled the merchant with fear and alarm. He met the pirateer with considerable trepidation but the pirate cheered heartily at sight of the merchant. Pirates carried no heavy cargoes and they had the advantage of stalwart and experienced oarsmen. 

Their speedy crafts were enabled to overtake the cumbersome merchantmen sailboats and the passage-at-arms found the corsairs overwhelmingly successful.

In addition to the individual snatching of ships indicated above there were organized piratical expeditions. At times there was little thrill in waiting for lone merchantmen and bands of pirates united and lay in wait for the large fleets of commerce and even laid siege to wealthy estates on shore or to harbors where merchandise was gathered together in preparation for loading on the ships or to form supplies for caravans.

All merchandise seized was sold as a regular course of business. Fishermen were apprehended as they returned with a fresh catch. The cargo was taken into the same port and sold to the merchants who would have bought it from the fishermen. Pirates gave better values than traders and were careless with regard to maintainance of prices. When in need they marked down their products to a fraction of the former figure. They became restless if goods did not move rapidly enough to satisfy their spirit of wanderlust and they disposed of the entire remaining stock, with a grand and benevolent gesture. The poverty-stricken were favored with bargains or charity and they waited with impatience the advent of the goodnatured and free hearted corsair, shunning stalls of the traders.

A young male of ambition, given the choice between a position aboard a ship in the navy or a pirateer, invariably chose the latter.

Later in life, he sought employment in the navy. 

Organization among the pirates became classical. They had no other duties than the perfection of an expert fighting unit, with

skilled officers and leaders. Strict rules were observed and strict subservience was demanded. Expert oarsmen and battlers developed between encounters with merchant ships. No mutiny was brooked and discipline became more perfected than in an army or navy. Each participant shared a definite percentage of the spoils, as our stockholders recover a share of the corporate profits today. 

Expeditions sent against them were usually defeated and the members were captured, then sold as slaves. Threats of punishment did not deter these adventurers. The sport became all the more spirited and hazardous. Piracy was not carried on to produce wealth any more than our army of hoboes anticipate the gaining of a huge fortune by their tramping. An interesting diversion consisted of inducing ships sent against them by the King to turn pirate temporarily. They assisted in pilfering, then returned innocently to the King. Many centuries elapsed before nations united against piracy and built great naval fleets. International agreement helped to curb activity but the nineteenth century found cases of sea robbery recorded. Piracy prospered for more than two thousand years.

The Liparian Isles were infested by Greek Corsairs as early as 580 B. C. Ionians and Lycians engaged in the lucrative profession. 

The Aegean and the Adriatic formed excellent waters for the trade. 

Cretan, Etruscan and particularly Sicilian bands terrorized the earliest traders in Southern Europe. In the North the Angles, Saxons, Jutes and Vikings were all classified as pirates. Cinque 

Port seamen were permitted to plunder any vessel except those owned by the English. In retaliation, French, Irish, Welsh and 

Scotch engaged in the pastime to the north in the English Channel. 

The latter part of the thirteenth century England greatly augmented the fleet, forming protection to her developing commerce. 

The Hanseatic agreement between Henry IV and the cities of 

Bremen, Gripeswold, Hamburg, Lubec and Sund at the beginning of the fourteenth century dealt with the settlement of piratical plundering on the seas. Breton seamen were experts and some of them naturally pillaged British ships during the first half of the sixteenth century. Turks and Moors in Africa joined the list about the same periods. Saracens worried Italian enterprise. Moslem and Barbarian corsairs demoralized shipping. The romance of the renegade Barbarossas (Uruj and Kheyr-ed-din) is extremely interesting. The first became Sultan of central Barbary early in the sixteenth century and formed the nucleus of those Barbarian "Wasps" who continued their depredations until the nineteenth century. He was killed about 1518 but his brother became even more powerful and waged the famous wars between Christians and Moslems.

Englishmen had their opportunity in the Elizabethan period when the richly laden Spanish galleons of King Philip returned from America.

In 1648 pirates from Ireland harried English shipping. 

Both France and Holland joined England in sending punitive expeditions against the pirates of Tripoli and Algeria during the latter part of the seventeenth century. 

Malay prahus bothered shipping and Chinese junks operated during the last part of the nineteenth century. 

Our own histories tell us about the difficulties which our business men experienced with shipping on the Mediterranean. 

Personalities stand out sharply during the development and outlawry of this form of business enterprise. 

Sir Henry Morgan attacked only the recognized enemy ships of England. This buccaneer showed a strange mixture of coldbloodedness and loyalty. "Black-Beard" Edward Teach left England and engaged in a long series of captures, involving understandings between the Governor of the Bermudas and the Governor of North Carolina. 

Captain Kidd was appointed captain of a ship which was to capture French vessels. Meeting with no success in this venture, he turned buccaneer and wrested a huge fortune from the unfortunate ships which he encountered. A considerable part of his gold, silver and jewels was concealed in America. 

Captain Avery was born in Devonshire. A career of piracy gained for him a huge fortune but it was wrested from him in his own Devonshire and he died without enough money for burial.

Captain Bartholomew Roberts was a Welshman who became a gentleman of fortune. He was killed by a British man-of-war. 

John Paul Jones was a Scot. He commanded an American warship but looted ships and countrysides. His death occurred in obscurity in Paris. Remains have been brought to this country. 

Charles Gibbs was an American pirate from Rhode Island. 

His activities were confined to the Atlantic Coast. In 1831 he was condemned to death on the scaffold. 10

It is necessary to explain the meaning between pirate and privateer. Several of the gentlemen mentioned above were regularly commissioned as masters of ships, with authority to capture vessels of enemy nations, seize contents and destroy the ships. By exceeding authority and apprehending any bark which came along these gentlemen became corsairs.

Piracy was condemned during the fifteenth century but certain extenuating circumstances permitted exceptions to the punishment meted out and failure to enforce regulations encouraged its continuance until the dawn of the twentieth century. Staunch pirate leaders were nearly always fearless, resourceful and inventive. A similar amount of energy, devoted to constructive and ethical business ventures, would have enriched the world's wealth, welfare and knowledge. Lasting happiness was not gained by any of the buccaneer heroes. They were killed in action; executed; or died penniless, hunted and fearful of apprehension. 

One race which has an intriguing history is the Gypsy race. 

Its origin is uncertain but India is usually accepted as the country of their birth. One account relates subjugation and subsequent exile for a period of seven years, with the stipulation that settlement for a considerable period of time was not to be permitted. 

The author of the account pictures the band as coming from Egypt. 

They permitted any person to join the group who followed their customs. New members consisted of criminals who had been driven from the cities. When the seven years had elapsed the original Gypsy band returned but the more recent members carried on. We read a story of stealthy travel, trickery and robbery. None of the members attempted to do any productive labor but told fortunes and tried to wrest a living from the community in which they found themselves. Fairs and holiday festivities drew bands of this wandering race. One town solemnly met and considered the thefts suffered by a roving band of Gypsies. It was decided that those who lost articles would be permitted to steal an amount equal to their losses from the Gypsy band and they captured a drove of fine horses from the camp. Two wrongs thus made one right. 

A famous "Cours des Miracles" sheltered the strangest family ever chronicled. It was so named because the lame, the crippled and the blind suddenly became well upon passing the gateways after a hard day of work begging on the streets of Paris. Many drew from their ragged clothing a ham or piece of beef which had been picked up when the proprietor's back was turned. Ragot was the leader who gave us the following definition of his family: 11

matois-sharpers mercelots-hawkers gueux-a general term for beggars and swindlers The Cagoux were captains who trained newcomers until they were fit to go out and beg. Arms were strapped up and crutches fitted to healthy persons. Artificial sores and darkened glasses deceived passersby, who gave liberally. These Cagoux became skilled workers and soon had a well organized hold-up game in operation. As soon as the task was completed the apprentice was sent to the street and the result automatically decided his professional name. Some of them are unusual enough to list. The highest class beggars were called Argotiers. Down the roll we find archisuppots, marcandiers, malingreus, pietres, sabondeux, callots, coquillards, hubins, polissous, francs mitous, ruffes, millards, capons, convertis, petite flambes, narquois and drilles. Many were clothed in rags to excite sympathy. Again we find that a huge amount of energy and ingenuity was expended to avoid honest occupations. Wanderlust prevented them from placing proper value upon property and their conception of right and wrong is hard for us to understand. We can scarcely understand the hardship inflicted upon a member who was ordered to leave the band. 

He became an outcast, desired by no town or community. 

Business attitudes have undergone many changes similar to those indicated by this outline. Orthodox seizure of property gradually became condemned and considered unlawful. Mere outlines of several types follow: 

TITHES: Requirement of payment of one tenth to an official. 

HAVAGE: Official permitted to put his hand into each load of grain brought into the market and retain as much as he could hold in that hand. Later he used a sanitary wooden spoon. 

ROBBER-KNIGHTS AND FEUDAL BARONS: Lords engaged in the sport of waging warfare on their neighbors. They burned buildings, confiscated wealth and cut down trees. Peasants were maltreated and owners carried away into slavery or to be held for ransom. Merchants encountered enormous difficulty because of the impassable roads and the slowness of travel under good conditions. Lords made extortions of all kinds, including iniquitous fines and tolls in return for safe pasage over their domains. When this tax was not paid instances were recorded of robbery and pillage of the entire caravan. This form of neighborly salutation has dis- 12

appeared, except for an occasional case of bridge toll which still survives. Motorists describe some tolls as "highway robbery."

The Hohenzollern Dynasty is one example of nobles who arose from the ranks and by conquest and arranged marriages gained the titles of Lord, King and Emperor.

TYRANNY: Ure is responsible for the statement that one and all of the great tyrants of ancient and medieval history were business men. They secured offices because of their great wealth or through the efforts of friends who hoped to gain favor and recognition.

GYGES was the first known tyrant. He ruled Lydia in 700 

B. C. The origin of the word tryant appears to have been a commercial one.

MEDIEVAL FAIRS, or MARKETS: States granted pro tection from robbers. One instance is recorded of a merchant who shouted "He is a robber" when a colleague quoted a price to a customer.

USURY: Any amount charged as interest in the Middle Ages was regarded as usury. Interest charges on loaned money was considered immoral. The fact that it was loaned indicated that it was not needed by the owner and all that was necessary was sufficient security for its return when actually needed. 

Shakespeare gave us an idea of the contempt with which Shylock was regarded by society. 

One of our ethical problems is the amount which is a fair return for the use of capital. We have now advanced so far that it is illegal to charge more than $12 per year interest on a loan of $10 in the State of New York. Ten per centum is legal per month for four months, when the loan must be renewed. This applies to loans made by pawnbrokers. 

SLAVERY: Slaves at one time were regarded as an article of property. Their masters selected partners for them in marriage and were permitted to take their lives if they felt so inclined. Various forms of slavery have prevailed through history and today it has not been stamped out entirely.

DECEPTION: Countless methods of deceit have been prac tised. Some practices contain no elements of deception but are based upon a misunderstanding of conditions or expectancies. 13

Governments have issued "fiat" money, knowing full well that in time it would depreciate in value. Our early advertising was stigmatized by the thought of trickery. Salesmanship has passed from the stage where anything was said or done which might lead to a sale. The Boston Tea Party involved destruction of wealth because of a business disagreement. 

Today there are many regrettable phases of business which might be changed. Horse traders still blink their eyes when a prospect examines the animal. Their theory is that he should know what he buys. The State of Connecticut has its title of "Nutmeg 

State" and wishes that it were possible to obliterate that memory. 

Church fathers register sanctity on Sunday and drive as close a bargain as ever the other six days of the week. Ponzis and Get- 

Rich-Quick-Wallingfords prosper for a day and then pass out of circulation. Radio broadcasting continues to copy ideas of other stations. Prize fights and horse races are questioned. Patent medicine and circus side-shows have not discredited Barnum as a prophet. Stocks and bonds are bought on faith, while repentance follows more leisurely.

In short, errors in the conduct of business are perceived by everybody. Two persons who direct their affairs in totally different manners believe that the other person uses the wrong method. But both may be correct. It is only when a vast majority of the population rebels against an institution that we begin to examine it closely and ask ourselves the question with which this course is concerned, namely "Is this an ethical practice?" The ensuing solution would be known as a group solution. We must engage in a more fascinating sort of problem. No one may give us assistance. 

The answer is purely personal, but is based on societal factors and fundamental facts. 14

Chapter II MODERN BUSINESS ATTITUDES 

Today, more than ever before, there is need for a more minute classification of that great mass of information which is being translated into the science of human conduct. There is distinct. realization that it belongs to the realm of science as urgently as the older exact branches, such as Chemistry, Physics and Mathematics. 

We are now faced, as always, with the problem of finding a starting point or base on which to build. Later chapters will indicate attempts which have been made to isolate the field of ethics from closely allied subjects. Most of these essays have failed in segregating the subject but nearly all have shown that their original ideas contributed something of value. They have merely accentuated the interrelation of our actions with economic laws, the contributions of history, old and new theories of philosophy and logic, the existence of sociological and psychological factors, as well as a borrowing from the exact sciences. Legal and political progress influence our conduct. Any moral values obtained without due regard to all these determining forces will contain errors. life:

Leo Jacobs found three types of practical ethical movements: 

1. Religious ethical movements. 

2. Social ethical movements. 

3. Pure ethical movements.

William DeWitt Hyde builds upon five great philosophies of 

1. Epicurean pursuit of pleasure. 

2. Stoic self-control by law. 3.

Platonic subordination of lower to higher. 

4. Aristotelian sense of proportion. 

5. Christian spirit of love.

Writers and thinkers are collaborating in contribution to general knowledge which may be collected and indexed at some indeterminate future period. Many facts have been culled out which may form a foundation. For example, the industrial problem is not solely a question of how much wages a worker may demand or the amount of labor which an employer forces from him. Man has not 15

only the right to work to gain a living but he has also the privilege of refusal. Questions of insurance and pensions have arisen without adequate settlement. We differ on the subject of strikes. Seasonal employment and "productivity" of each worker attract our attention. Minimum wage scales, "yellow dog contracts" and arbitrary subscriptions are endured rather than accepted as legal perquisites of the boss. There is the "dole" in England and the "dot" in France. Private and public employment bureaus quarrel instead of assisting each other. Bricklayers are able to lay a minimum number of bricks per day while employed by one type of corporation but they become exhausted and lay far less when working for a city or state.

Cripples, persons worn down by service with a concern, dullards and laggards all deserve consideration from the business which employs or refuses to hire them. A man who is hired during the rush period feels injured when he is laid off at the first indication of slackness. Has he a right to expect permanent employment? 

We say that the worker should receive a "living wage" or a fair reward for his productivity. What is the amount? How are we to decide either point without prejudice?

Thus we find several factors confronting us in one of the economic problems of ethics. The list noted above brings out only a few of the weighty opinions which are conflicting and have not been satisfactorily settled. There are dozens which have not been mentioned which should be included under the employment heading. 

Enough have been suggested to indicate that much preliminary work should be done to find out existing facts concerning relations of the capitalist and his workers before we could even begin to consider any moral questions. Ethical conduct is so involved and so intricate that every minute detail must be placed before us in order to determine the proper interpretation of that conduct. It is not enough to know one side of the argument. A complete record of both parties is still far from sufficient. Even then the arbiter cannot proceed with his task. He is too close to the scene of conflict. 

It is necessary to go far away and find an objective viewpoint. His decision involves not only the workers and the employers but the unemployed in that industry, others employed by that industry and the effect upon society in part and in whole. Should his judgment be affected by social and economic considerations or is there a need for ethical thought as well?

Business today is based on the system of private capital. Considerable attention has been given to safety devices and to efficiency and scientific management. Machine technology and financial 16

manipulation have progressed miraculously. Employee representation and ownership, as well as profit sharing, uncovered unsuspected possibilities for the solution of uneasiness and disinterested labor. 

Some return of the feeling of pride in workmanship which distinguished the master craftsman is noticed when we work for OUR company. The greatest progress is noted in the attitude which the most alert capitalist is displaying. He is just as interested in making a profit. Competition with his rivals is much keener than ever before. But he has found that supposed concessions to workers have had the boomerang tendency. Employers have benefitted by human kindness to workers to a greater extent than the employees themselves. When the twelve hour day was abolished there was a large amount of uneasiness. Ten hours of labor would mean that five instead of six articles would be produced. Imagine the relief, surprise, even joy, with which industry noticed that the SAME AMOUNT was produced in ten hours! Seasonal workers were trained to do similar work during the dull season. Employers discovered that they actually saved money by keeping their help throughout the year. In addition to this, workers became more satisfied and loyal. Payment of better wages induced a superior type of workman to seek employment and the manager discovered that the drones were forced out of his organization. He obtained selection of the more desirable men and again found that instead of spending money recklessly he had made a wise investment. Many other social and managerial experiments became economic benefits to capital. Attention to methods of helping the customer also aided the manufacturer. It was found that service was the main factor in such success. Instead of producing an article and then looking for a prospective purchaser business has sought for the need of a customer and then made the article which was needed. Justice is displacing trickery. Truth has been found to be more enduring than deception. Faith has brought about an unbelievable volume of installment sales, and credit buying far outnumbers cash sales today. Honesty is at a premium today more than ever before.

Advertising and selling were at one time regarded as the black sheep in the business field. It is true that Wanamaker and Wrigley spent all their available capital in advertising and believed in it thoroughly. Wanamaker was one of the pioneers who laid the honesty policy for future advertisers but there were hundreds who deliberately misused it and their conduct was responsible for the suspicion with which the public regarded early advertising. There is a large amount of unethical advertising in existence at the present time. The following quotations are representative of modern business attitudes toward advertising. 17

Toner M. Overley, managing director of the Better Business 

Bureau of Indianapolis, believes that advertising has taken the business world out of the days of the hitching post and put it into the days of "No Parking" signs. 

Quoting Elbert Hubbard, who once said: "Truth is an asset and a lie a liability," Mr. Overley continued: "He spoke well, for business interests have found that it is a good business policy to tell the truth about their products. Success in advertising must be based on truth in advertising. The average person is honest. I do not believe that more than half of one per cent of our business concerns are dishonest." "There is a growing tendency of honor in business and even among competitors there is a feeling that it is better to recognize friendly competition than to lie about competitors and their products. The general public and the merchants are being educated rapidly to better methods and practises in doing business.' 

PRINTERS' INK, August 21, 1930. 

Another quotation comments upon Edward L. Greene's announcement that there would be a Joint Committee on Advertising Ethics and Standards, composed of representatives of a dozen leading advertising organizations, cooperating with the National Better 

Business Bureau, Inc.

Humphrey, called together a group of periodical publishers and asked them to consider the subject of handling further fraudulent advertising from the publishers' standpoint, and if possible to formulate a definite plan that might be submitted to the publishing industry. 

On October 9, 1928, at a trade practice conference in New York

City, the Commission and the publishers met and discussed the situation thoroughly. After the conference the publishers drew up a set of resolutions in which they recognized, "the fact that the 18

National Better Business Bureau. 

is the most competent agency to the businesses of advertising in preventing frauds in advertising and selling," and requested the Bureau to "advise periodical publishers generally, and whenever deemed advisable, any governmental agency, whenever advertising which is being published, or is likely to be offered for publication" is established by the 

Bureau to be fraudulent.

Although it was the original suggestion of Mr. Humphrey to treat the problem by attacking individual publications, the Bureau and the publishers finally adopted a plan whereby the problem was taken up by industries.

After the first eleven months of operation of the plan the Bureau was able to report that in a group of publications studied there had been a decrease of 25 per cent in deceptive and questionable advertising. Careful investigation was made of eleven fields and basic reports issued on these fields to publishers. In order to eliminate the worst spots first the Bureau investigated those fields which were notorious for their fraudulent and deceptive advertising. The reports contained detailed information concerning the names of unethical and fraudulent advertisers and suggested remedial methods.

It has long been the opinion of leaders in advertising that fraud is not nearly so dangerous to the welfare of their profession as is that type of unethical advertising which, although quite within the law, seeks to tear down the public confidence in truth in advertising. 

Frauds can be stopped by due legal process. The unethical advertisers are more difficult gentry to deal with. 

It was apparent that anything which threatens the credibility of advertising threatens the welfare of all groups within advertising and that any single group, such as periodical publishers, working alone, not only is carrying the burden for the entire industry but also has much of its work nullified by the fact that the unethical advertiser, driven from one group of mediums, will find safe haven in another group. Because of this an effort was made to bring together the most important groups in advertising in order to accomplish the right type of cooperative work. This effort has finally borne fruit.

It is significant that in the present announcement no mention is made of individuals serving on the committee. It was the feeling of the members of the committee that it would be better to submerge individuals and place the emphasis on the organizations which are backing the movement. 19

According to Mr. Greene the committee does not mean the creation of another organization, but just what its name implies, a flexible, not too formal committee appointed to consider and deliberate upon any matters pertaining to national advertising ethics and practices and to develop and foster the utmost co-operation between the Bureau and any and all business interests in advertising and among those interests themselves. 99 "The committee and the Bureau will work interdependently,' said Mr. Greene. "The Bureau, for instance, may make investigations and refer the results of these investigations to the commtitee for advice. On the other hand the members of the committee are equally free to call to the attention of the Bureau practices or individuals which they believe will be harmful to advertising. "We are doing in a large way what has been so effectively accomplished by the local Better Business Bureaus. Where such bureaus are functioning properly they have the backing of the business leaders in their communities and their recommendations are listened to with respect by those individuals and firms who are stepping out of line. Hitherto the national organization has lacked the type of backing which has made the work of local bureaus so effective. In the joint committee I believe we have such backing. "The preliminary work already done has been highly encouraging. We have found almost all of the leading groups in the industry eager to co-operate in any movement to clean up advertising. All recognize the magnitude of the task and the paramount fact that only by united effort can lasting benefit be accomplished." 

PRINTERS' INK, February 27, 1930. 

There is a similar corrective suggestion in the radio field. Dr. 

Lee DeForest, a pioneer in the radio industry and a firm believer in unique methods of selling, as evidenced by sales of his audion tubes in the corner drug store, prophesies that radio broadcasts of direct sales talks must be curbed. This is the task of the station or the advertiser rather than the duty of the government. The ethical solution appears to be a housecleaning in the broadcasting field before the federal agencies become compelled to make a clean sweep, using restriction, regulation and modification. 20

Chapter III STAGES OF MORALITY 

As the individual makes contacts through life he finds certain situations confronting him which must be met. Frequently he prefers to avoid them but many stand directly before him and further progress is impossible without meeting the issue. 

At other times, he travels along, blissfully unconscious of the presence of the issues. They are near him and if he were alert he could easily grasp their significance but since his energies are dormant or focused upon an absorbing incident of the moment, their presence is not detected. These settings are unimportant or time enough elapses to render action on the part of the individual ineffective.

Three automobiles were moving in the same direction. The operator of the first machine noticed an intimate acquaintance on the sidewalk and waved a hand. At the same instant he stopped his car and stepped out, walking the three feet to the curb, where he enthusiastically engaged in conversation. 

But the driver of the second car had been in a hurry. He had wondered why the first car did not move along faster. When it came to a complete stop so suddenly he was unprepared and almost bumped it. However, his four-wheel brakes were good enough to enable him to stop within six inches of the first car, but in doing this he swerved out until he was seven feet from the curb. The third car was not so fortunate. The driver had charming company and the first indication that anything was wrong came to him when he noticed the sickening sensation of a crash with another car. An argument followed, in which the third driver accused the second of stopping too far from the curb, while the second operator insisted that the third was following him too closely. During the heated airing of views, the first operator noticed the two machines which were damaged and remarked to the girl, "Can you imagine that?

An accident! That driver wasn't watching the road. Have to be careful these days. Well, let's go." And they drove away, bearing a genuine feeling of innocence regarding the smash. 

Was the first driver under any ethical obligation to signal his intention of stopping? Did the success of the second operator in stopping his car eliminate driver number one from all responsibility for the accident? 21

If a check were made upon the number of occasions where decisions regarding a situation were needed but where the individual failed to recognize their existence, the result would be surprising.

In addition to the problems which must be faced and the type which are not recognized, there is a third group which is more common and about which we have more knowledge and information than the other two. The distressing fact is, while we have the opportunity of registering a success with this type, we all too frequently fail as utterly as though it belonged to the second group of the series. This type of situation is characterized by the recognition of the problem to be met but a corresponding hesitation on the part of the doer to act. It is distasteful for him to come to a decision. It is too difficult. Tomorrow will be soon enough. There is not enough information. The decision involved is so important that it should not be formulated without due deliberation and further fact-finding. Consequently, for some easily explained reason, action is delayed and frequently postponed permanently. 

Methods of Meeting the Situation. 

Mental activity of the individual has an important bearing on the way in which he solves problems as they arise. In the preceding groupings we may eliminate the second possibility, since he is not fully aware that the necessity of coming to a decision faces him. 

In the two remaining groups we find that there are three stages of morality, or conduct, which the individual may employ in the solution of his problem. The first is known as Instinctive Action; the second as Personal, or Reflective Conduct; and the third as Group, or Customary Behavior.

INSTINCTIVE ACTION: 

Returning to the first group, where action must occur before the person may resume his normal routine, we find three divisions of Instinctive Actions: 

First. The condition which arises may be an emergency and require instantaneous solution. Like a flash the mind seeks a solution and the entire nervous system appears to assist the person without any counter-interest. These instinctive judgments are very frequently excellent settlements of the problems. Many times they are far better than the long-thought-out solution. One reason for this is, undoubtedly, the urgent need for the mind to concentrate on the one thing. It may be a matter of life or death for the 22

individual. Possibly it is in his field of interest and he already has enough information to assist him in his hurried "snap judgment." 

Second. The situation may involve a gratification of the senses. Here the condition of the mind definitely assists in instinctive action. The person who reaches out his hand and takes an apple from the public fruit stand as he passes is acting in an unforeseen situation because of one of two possibilities: 

1. Hunger is so acute that he desires the apple regardless of probable discovery. 

2. His attitude concerning right and wrong is so flexible that he instinctively took advantage of the opportunity, without the guiding and restraining check of an alert conscience. 

Third. This type is closely kin to the second and may be called a sensory motive. It is given a separate classification because the motivating forces appear to be emotional and lack the tinge of rationalization which accompanies the desire for food, the wish to excel in athletics, the attainment of some social or political or mental pedestal where others may look up and worship. This act may be the result of a desire to please a member of the opposite sex. Perfectly rational males render idiotic performances and actually expect to receive smiles of approbation from the feminine observers. 

Ladies instinctively reach for a powder-puff and hasten to clothe an already-well-powdered nose when the proper setting demands it. 

All three instinctive forms of action follow a well defined course when the person's characteristics are known. However, indulgence in intoxicating liquors, the use of drugs, or the arousal of active emotions (e. g. Anger) causes the mental condition to vary and instinctive reactions to the given set of circumstances tend to become inconstant and unreliable.

In general, in the first group, instinctive responses occur when there is little or no time for deliberation or when the individual recognizes fundamental needs and desperately comes to a decision without weighing all the facts. 

The third group of circumstances does not contain many possibilities for instinctive action. The baby who notices the bright color of a flame and reaches out his hand until it touches the fire must be classified in the first group because he does not recognize the conditions when they occur for the first time. After he has been burned it is possible for the third group conditions to appear but 23

it is more probable that the second group plays as important a part and the child warily avoids action, even while noticing the brilliant color of the flame. When this occurs, there is a good example of instinctive action in the third group. It is a negative one, namely the instinctive care with which the infant determines that he shall not be burned again. This situation also represents the good results of the hesitation or reflection of the third group, as opposed to the usual unfortunate conditions which arise because of continued procrastination. Dumb animals ordinarily practise instinctive reactions to stimuli but when the stimulus has been repeated there is a tendency to recognize the set of circumstances. Action to them is still instinctive but it has been transferred from the first group to the third because of knowledge which has been acquired. 

The distinction between first and third group instinctive acts is the degree of experience which the individual has acquired, the variety of other situations in which he has participated, the ability to adapt himself to circumstances, or the versatility of his mental concepts. 

This first stage in morality, the Instinctive, concerns itself very largely with results. Most acts in this class are founded on very little mentality and intelligence. Since there is no effort devoted to securing an ethical result but merely the desire to attain that end in the quickest and easiest possible manner, we may classify the Instinctive Stage of Morality as being the lowest of the three and characterized as containing almost no element of Ethics. Some emotions are on the borderline and cannot be classified very readily. 

Parental love may be almost entirely Instinctive but at the same time may form the basis for an emotion that will rank very highly in the second or even the third stages of morality. Thus the Instinctive acts of mankind are extremely valuable in the formulation of the laws and principles of Ethics and should be used and studied as a foundation upon which to build up individual ideas and ideals of Ethics.

On occasion, this Instinctive motive force which we all inherit becomes so strong within us that it overthrows forces which have. been developed through the ages or through our personal experiences. When this occurs, society is usually benefitted if we have been thoroughly grounded in the principles of modern Ethics, particularly if we are one of a large group reverting to Instinctive action. But if we blindly fall back upon Instinctive conduct, we must pay the penalty exacted by society and our acts become a check instead of an aid to it. Instinctive conduct is not subject to praise or to blame when a person is recognized as living on that level but when a more cultured individual acts instinctively and the result concerns another, society subjects him to censure. While it is 24

natural to avoid painful experiences and to seek pleasurable ones persons on the higher planes recognize duty as a controlling element.

We must recall that it is necessary to classify the Instinctive Reactions. For example, in addition to the indicated group including reactions to disgusting situations, to anger and to hate we must add the opposite class: instinctive admiration, tenderness, devotion, loyalty, sympathy and pity. 

Similarly, if we search for ethical standards, we are prone to place varying values on instinctive actions of strangers, casual acquaintances, close friends, relatives and, particularly on our own acts. Although each group is confronted with the same conditions. and reacts in an identical manner, it is difficult for us to accept one reaction as fully as another. We find ourselves, our friends and our intellectual equals reacting in the orthodox way of which we approve. 

Another classification includes the question of whether the instinctive effort contained an attempt to achieve an ethical or aesthetic result.

Again, the extent of the instinct may be considered. When a man kills a large rat before our eyes, we undergo one degree of instinctive feeling. If the object were a dog we become more distinct in our reaction and it makes some difference whether it is a mangy cur or a thoroughbred animal belonging to a breed which we believe to be universally valuable. The question of expediency tempers our horror or approbation. But when one kills another human being we instinctively pass judgment. There are no extenuating circumstances, even though the slain person attempted to murder the first party. Our instinctive action becomes very violent, whereas the killing of the rat aroused only torpid or faintly curious feeling.

Definition and explanation of instinct. 

James defined instinct as: "The faculty of acting in such a way as to produce certain ends, without foresight of the ends, and without previous education in the performance.' 99 James frowns upon the division or classification of instinctive reactions as such but the indicated division may be justifiable when the above definition is recalled. Instinctive reaction may be ex- 25

pected when a person has never undergone the particular experience or when his knowledge, acquired previously, is not utilized. 

There is a question as to how far instinctive acts may be modified by experience and still remain instinctive. Occasionally two sets of instincts occur at the same time and once again it is difficult to decide which is stronger, and if we determine which wins out with the intellect there is still the point of explaining why it prevailed. 

A man is hunting a wild bear. Suddenly he decides to turn and run from the bear. Why does he stop, appear undecided, and then run, allowing the animal to become the pursuer? 

Instincts appear to ripen or mature and after a time to decline in intensity. When a certain age is reached the child no longer has the desires of childhood. Dolls are discarded. The tender care which dressed them warmly for winter weather seems to be converted into an opposite manifestation when we see the same girl pommeling boys who dare to speak to her without due pomp, ceremony and formality. Still later, it is difficult to understand her treatment of the same sex, remembering the determination with which she pulled hair.

PERSONAL, or REFLECTIVE CONDUCT: 

This stage of conduct requires the undivided attention and interest of the individual. He is conscious and has his actions under control. Grave and prolonged deliberation enable him to give the direction to his actions which seems proper under the circumstances. His choice is made regardless of the setting and in spite of the strength of his desires and impulses, which are strictly watched. 

Everything which appears to be unworthy of consideration is discarded after making sure that it has no value. 

Personal conduct should come last in the list if we have in mind the sequence of acts or if we are concerned with the importance of our conduct. The present classification places it between. the actions which are inherited or which are merely automatic activities and those in the third stage, which represent the acquired habits. Little or no intellectual effort is needed when the first stage stimuli are encountered. Acquired habitual acts require more thought and analysis but after one solution has been gained there is a tendency to act in the same manner when a like situation occurs. 

Personal conduct requires the most thought and the greatest consideration. It is placed second in the list because of the middle ground which it occupies between inherited and acquired characteristics of moral conduct. 26

Profound thought is the main requisite of personal conduct and that brief statement gives the solution to the question: "Why is there so little reflective conduct in the world?" We seldom find the time or energy to devote to extended consideration of our own acts, although many persons are quite capable of passing judgment on the conduct of others. Protracted thinking is painful. It is not the shortest way out. We consider life too short and decide that unless a situation is of vital importance the quickest spur to action is the best one. Thinking is a scientific task-an experiment. The answer is known, but, as we see the magician perform his stunts and observe the result without knowing the steps in the presentation, we are prone to look for the solution to ethical problems without the proper contemplation of the facts leading up to our conclusion. Instead of being a mere observation of a finished product, thought is the constant and painfully gradual emergence of an idea which is built up and nursed and studied and tested until it is brought to the nearest point approaching perfection. It is this willingness to test new thoughts which arise; this perfect freedom to trust to individual judgment which forms the basis of personal conduct. GROUP, or CUSTOMARY BEHAVIOR: 

Group behavior may be called the most usual stage of human conduct. It is to be expected. It is ludicrous. It is laughable. 

Two boys stand on the car tracks of a busy city street. Traffic is halted and hundreds of curious and eager persons gather around them and look up into the sky. The two culprits disappear from the crowd and enjoy the scene from the safety of the sidewalk. 

No one knows why he is looking up into the air. No one knows that he is entertainer instead of audience. One may ask another what it is all about and why they are standing there. 

Side-shows at a circus employ "barkers" to shout the news of the marvelous performance. Actors may be displayed on the stage outside the tent. At the proper moment, the barker announces that the show will start, he quotes the price of admission for the fifth or sixth time and at the same instant paid "pushers" elbow their way toward the ticket booth. They have been on the outside of the 27

crowd and all now work in concert, pushing everyone in one direction until the curious individual on-lookers find to their surprise that they have bought tickets and are now inside the tent forming an audience. They have followed the line of least resistance and literally "gone with the crowd."

A fire started in a crowded theatre. The audience was composed of former Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, pupils trained in fire drills and successful business men and women. How many remained cool and assisted others? How many were able to calm their own fears?

Persons who successfully solve conduct problems when allowed time enough in the privacy of their own homes are frequently unable to think as efficiently when emergencies arise. They are ordinarily classed as individuals who practice personal conduct but at the important point in their lives, under great mental stress, they revert to Group or to Instinctive action. Nine out of ten who fail to achieve real Personal conduct lose out because they are unable to adjust themselves to situations rather than a lack of intelligence enough to meet them. They simply are not adaptable. If society remained static from year to year there are many persons who could attain the highest stage of conduct and retain it. However, the world is constantly changing; the individuals move from one section of the country to another, or to other countries; new habits and customs are introduced; and the perfect personal conduct is never performed for any considerable period of time. Our greatest achievement is the noting of facts and the arrangement of them in a manner consistent with our individual requirements, permitting no criticism from others. Then Personal conduct has been attained for us. When we act in any manner other than the free and conscious mental thinking, we must be classed as members of the Cus tomary Behavior Group. 

Habitual behavior, when built up consciously and analyzed carefully, tends to become Personal conduct. Instinctive action may be encircled by careful training, bringing modified results even when an entirely unknown situation arises. But occasionally the action, instead of being toned down, is carried to the greatest extreme because of the inhibitions which have been imposed upon the individual.

Society plays its part in shaping and forming the mind and character of each person. Thinking in the right way will tend to bring into sharp relief a conception of value and ideals-of right and wrong-for each individual. 28

Conduct is regarded in one of two ways. If we pass judgment upon an act we try to discover whether the party who performed the injury did so intentionally or whether it was a pure accident. 

Another basis for decision is the extent of the damage, or the fact of actual benefit or injury.

Bozo, a tame bear, loved his master and was happiest when near him. One summer afternoon Bozo found his master asleep in his comfortable chair on the porch and was extremely annoyed to discover that a large mosquito had lighted upon Mr. Harris' head. Bozo had frequently noticed that his master was very averse to a visit from this type of pest and decided to pay him a mark of respect and kindness, as a token of his solicitation and affection. 

He raised his paw and succeeded in exterminating the mosquito. 

Unfortunately, he was too vigorous and Mr. Harris suffered a broken skull. Was Bozo's conduct ethical?

Samuel George Forbes is a licensed aviator. He has become attached to James Dexter, a boy of fifteen. Sam offers to give Jim a free ride and the latter is given reluctant permission by his mother to take the trip. While they are up in the air we have an interesting ethical point to decide. Intention places judgment of actions purely upon the basis of individual subjective action. Morality can be approved or condemned before the act occurs. Result looks along the line of action, 29

gauges the act and also its effect before deciding whether conduct is right or wrong. Legally this method has been tried and adopted as the most logical manner of weighing evidence and passing judgment. Certainly the problem of ethical conduct would be a simple one if we could wait until all the evidence had been gathered and then pass on to a conclusion. On the contrary, the majority of our ethical situations are met and must be settled before we know what the result will be. It is entirely possible that we meet a problem, solve it, act, and THEN decide that we have erred in ethical reasoning. It is fortunate that intentions and results travel along the same footpath. As we gain skill in working out our good intentions we are enabled to prophesy that a certain percentage of the results will conform to our energies and efforts. 

BEHAVIORISM: 

Watsonian Behaviorism has obtained many followers in this country in the past decade and the vast majority appear to be young people who will soon become leaders in the business and ethical fields of endeavor. This new theory has secured recruits in our country, rather than in the European countries where psychological research has been conducted so vigorously for many decades. 

In our study of human conduct Behaviorism may not be ignored. 

An examination of the debate between Dr. Watson and Professor 

William MacDougall, entitled "THE BATTLE OF BEHAVIOR- 

ISM" brings out the information listed below. 

Dr. Watson gives us the keynote: "Given a certain object or situation, what will the individual do when confronted with it? Or the reverse of this formulation: 

Seeing an individual doing something, to be able to predict what object or situation is calling forth that act. 

Behavioristic psychology, then, strives to learn something about the nature of human behavior. To get the individual to follow a certain line, to do certain things, what situation shall I set up? 

Or, seeing the crowd in action, or the individual in action, to know enough about behavior to predict what the situation is that leads to that action."

Dr. Watson discards the basic principle of the psychologist- 

CONSCIOUSNESS. William James defined psychology as: "The description and explanation of states of consciousness as such." 

Watson declares that even James' definition of psychology assumed that we knew the meaning of consciousness, while as a matter of 30

fact it is something intangible, something meaning less to one person than to another, something which must be defined before there may be a workable definition of psychology. 

Behaviorism discards intangibles, such as sensation, perception, image, desire, purpose, and even the original meaning of thought and emotion. Research has been confined very largely to work upon infants and children. It denies the existence of any purpose in the increasing complexity of behavior as persons grow from infancy to maturity. Fear behavior was found to be present at birth, in addition to rage and love indications. Sneezing, smiling, feeding and grasping were among a list of other impulses noted. Fear was stimulated in one of two ways: A loud sound given behind the head of the infant or a loss of support accomplished by jerking the blanket on which he rested. Another conclusion Behaviorism outlined: It may be achieved and planned with the whole body and may be carried on even when the world of objects are not present to the senses. Thinking more than doubles our efficiency. Our day world may be carried to bed with us and manipulated at night. 

Professor MacDougall points out that there are three forms of Behaviorism. First, Metaphysical, or Neo-Realism. Second, 

Watsonian Behaviorism, or the form now under discussion. Third, Behavioristic Psychology, which he claims to have originated. 

He agrees with Watson that Psychology has been concerned with the "science of consciousness," but showed that John Stuart Mill attempted to work out a new science called "Ethology," and Dr. Charles Mercier started his "Praxiology," both recognizing that psychology as studied was not the complete solution in the study of human conduct. Both hoped to establish a new science, based on behavior, conduct, or human action. MacDougall contends that this work should be attempted as part of the science of psychology, making use of all available data heretofore gathered rather than attempting to bring into existence a new science, totally unrelated to psychology. He defines psychology in this connection as: "The science of human nature." 

Professor MacDougall contends that when Behaviorism ignores human efforts, desires, ideals, and strivings: "it is futile and inconsistent to talk of and to strive after selfdiscipline, or the moral training of our children, or social betterment, or the realization by our efforts of any ideal whatsoever." 31

He states that the Behaviorist mechanical idea of conduct cannot explain the homing of animals, urging that it results from intelligent learning akin to human acquirement of knowledge. The charge is also made that the submissive and self-assertive tendencies noticeable in social contact have been overlooked by Behaviorism. 

Obviously, Behaviorism ignores intention entirely, and concentrates its effort on result. 

These questions involving the extent and nature of inborn or native tendencies and mechanical causation versus purposive striving will be left for future psychologists to decide. Their decisions and findings should prove helpful to the study of Ethics. 

INNER AND OUTER THEORIES: 

Man is eternally playing Dr. Jekyl and Mr. Hyde. He definitely has two theories of conduct concerning himself and at least one concerning other humans. While we may not have noticed the fact, one person acts two parts and forces us to believe that his performance is logical. It is only when we investigate thoroughly that we discover that his actions do not conform to the same standard or test.

A husband was given the rather dubious compliment: "He is perfectly wonderful WHEN HE IS SOBER." The wife still thinks of the good qualities which manifested themselves when they were married. However, it is of interest to call in the neighbors in this family opinion. Their opinions may be summed up in the phrase "He is NEVER SOBER." Neighbors tend to ignore his excellent characteristics and take them as a matter of course, while his wife tries to see only those actions which are acceptable to her. 

Mothers similarly invent excuses for their children who misbehave and often fail to recognize that their offspring have now become adults and are no longer children.

A scientist is accepted as a genius by one group and ridiculed because of his eccentric behavior by another. Teachers who concentrate too closely upon their work become adept in that field of action, but tend to place less attention upon everyday tasks. Many professional men are known to be woefully absent-minded. Children who have been "under their mother's apron-strings" have been known to get out from under and show an astounding degree of executive ability. When they return to their homes they docilely permit the mother to again assume charge and it is difficult to recognize the same person who dictated company policies to other officials. Persons may be ethically immoral in a lust for power, or 32

goid, in regard to sensuality or religion, and at the same time perform tasks in a moderate, kindly, moral manner at home and in society. We may recall the Puritan who was so devout on Sunday and drove as hard a bargain as possible for the other six days of the week. Many business men skimp and save while at business. but throw caution and economy to the winds while seeking pleasure or where home affairs are concerned. 

This lower level of conduct can thus be readily observed. The other standpoint is illustrated by the fine friendships which are formed by the splendid work achieved in scientific pursuits, by the martyrdom of religion, by the serious attention given to noble objects and the free and spontaneous assistance granted to the oppressed or unfortunate.

A continual conflict is waged between the forces represented by these two levels of conduct within the individual. Natural desires and natural instincts, when merged with natural environment, develop the person to a realization of the beauty and fullness of life. 

One must act as a foil for the other. When one is crushed and the other permitted to flourish, an individual emerges who terrorizes society or dominates it. In either case, his inner attitude determines his conduct.

Inner Conduct places the entire responsibility upon each person. He must decide for himself without any influencing factors whether his act will be moral or not. It is to be commended, insofar as it strengthens judgments and prevails upon individual thinking out of problems,even though the number who practice it may be limited. Law does not recognize the inner theory on most occasions, although the Law of Contracts considers the intent of the parties as one of the essential features. When placed in positions of doubt, we should strive to exercise the Inner Theory of Conduct, since the result is uncertain. If our motives appear right to us, and if we are familiar with the society in which we are located, motive may be the sole determinant of action, and should bring about satisfactory results.

Man's treatment of others is the subject of the Outer Theory. 

Law accepts it when motive is not the more important consideration. Result is more readily weighed. The Golden Rule of conduct toward others is always a safe ideal. We should be just in all our dealings. We should be kind. Unfortunately, it appears to make a difference whether we are dealing with one person or another. 

We are perfectly willing to call the colored man "Brother" but hesitate perceptibly when the opportunity arises to accept him as 33

"Brother-in-law." A business deal with a favorite customer, involving a great bargain for him, becomes a profitable enterprise when compared with the treatment given to an old college chum. 

Kindness to one person becomes cruelty to another. Selfishness arises from the very effort to perform a kindness to another. One individual is favored but it may be at the expense of some colleague. 

The Outer Theory has a more social aspect, since it considers others as well as the individual, and since those others are in a position to observe the acts of the person and the effects of those actions. 

Observation may play as important a part as experience in effecting a change in the decisions involving Outer action. An observer who notices three individuals attempt to go over Niagara Falls becomes somewhat reconciled to the refusal of authorities to permit him to needlessly risk his life. Result becomes more important than intent.

There is a tendency at present to condemn too much thinking which fails to include action. A protracted thinker may become too morbid or too one-sided. It is maintained that the person who considers just a little, makes the attempt but fails, has achieved as much morally from the experience as one who has done nothing but think it out.

Possibly we could adopt the middle viewpoint. Life is completely filled with moral issues and we must not neglect our daily duties. It is patently impossible to devote our full attention to every situation. The more logical plan is to make sure that we do select certain ethical problems and study them to the fullest, permitting events which are commonplace to continue their normal course without our contemplation of them. Intelligent conscientious consideration of important events should enable us to meet less weighty ones with some tiny degree of preparation. 

Mental activity consists of a desire and deliberation for the sake of determining what shall be done. Outer activity desires the carrying into effect of whatever action is deemed necessary. No clear-cut dividing line is drawn between the two theories. Apparently we adopt the activity of action without spending a great deal of energy on the mental phase of the subject. Extraordinary occasions force us to concentrate mentally to a greater degree than usual, but even then we feel injured because of the necessity. 

Will is connected exclusively with the "inner" side. Consequences are the important feature of the "outer" side. If a person were able to persuade his will to conform to ethical standards, it 34

would appear that nothing else were needed to permit perfect conduct. But each person goes through life surrounded by objects and persons who permit no opportunity for extended operation of the will. Again, while it is possible for the individual to accurately gauge his own will, he is unable to determine the effect of his actions upon others, or the reception which will be given to them by his superiors. Knowing that his will to action must be criticized by others may induce action concerned with consequences rather than that provoked by his will. He thus escapes the need for justifying his will conclusion. 35

One author defines folkways as: "individual behavior merging into multi-individualistic or pluralistic behavior into co-individualistic becomes folkways if repeated often enough." 

Sumner has written a monumental work and we will borrow his simple description: "If we put together all that we have learned from anthropology and ethnography about primitive men and primitive society, we perceive that the first task of life is to live. Men begin with acts, not with thoughts. Every moment brings necessities which must be satisfied at once. Need was the first experience, and it was followed at once by a blundering effort to satisfy it. It is generally taken for granted that men inherited some guiding instincts from their beast ancestry, and it may be true although it has never been proved. If there were such inheritances, they controlled and aided the first efforts to satisfy needs. Analogy makes it easy to assume that the ways of beasts had produced channels of habit and predisposition along which dexterities and other psychophysical activities would run easily. Experiments with newborn animals show that in the absence of any experience of the relation of means to ends, efforts to satisfy needs are clumsy and blundering. The method is that of trial and failure, which produces repeated pain, loss, and disappointments. Nevertheless, it is a method of rude experiment and selection. The earliest efforts of men were of this kind. Need was the impelling force. Pleasure and pain on the one side and the other, were the rude constraints which defined the line on which efforts must proceed. The ability to distinguish between pleasure and pain is the only psychical power which is to be assumed. Thus ways of doing things were selected, which were expedient. They answered the purpose better than other ways, or with less toil and pain. Along the course on which efforts were compelled to go, habit, routine, and skill were developed. The struggle to maintain existence was carried on, not individually, but in groups. Each profited by the other's experience; hence there was concurrence towards that which proved to be most expedient. All at last adopted the same way for the same purpose; hence the ways turned into customs and became mass phenomena. Instincts were developed in connection with them. In this way folkways arise. The young learn them by tradition, imitation, and authority. The folkways, at a time, provide for all needs of life then and there. They are uni- 36

form, universal in the group, imperative, and invariable. As time goes on, the folkways become more and more arbitrary, positive, and imperative. If asked why they act in a certain way in certain cases, primitive people always answer that it is because they and their ancestors always have done so. A sanction also arises from ghost fear. The ghosts of ancestors would be angry if the living should change the ancient folkways. 

The operation by which folkways are produced consists in the frequent repetition of petty acts, often by great numbers acting in concert or, at least, acting in the same way when face to face with the same need. The immediate motive is interest. It produces habit in the individual and custom in the group. It is, therefore, in the highest degree original and primitive. By habit and custom it exerts a strain on every individual within its range; therefore it rises to a societal force to which great classes of societal phenomena are due. Its earliest stages, its course, and laws may be studied; also its influence on individuals and their reaction on it. 

It is our present purpose so to study it. We have to recognize it as one of the chief forces by which a society is made to be what it is. 

Out of the unconscious experiment which every repetition of the ways include, there issues pleasure or pain, and then, so far as the men are capable of reflection, convictions that the ways are conducive to societal welfare. Those two experiences are not the same. 

The most uncivilized men, both in the food quest and in war, do things which are painful, but which have been found to be expedient. Perhaps these cases teach the sense of social welfare better than those which are pleasurable and favorable to welfare. The former cases call for some intelligent reflection on experience. When this conviction as to the relation to welfare is added to the folkways they are converted into mores, and, by virtue of the philosophical and ethical element added to them, they win utility and importance and become the source of the science and the art of living.' 99 Individuals acquire habits and groups follow custom without consciously attempting to do so. When attention is called to them we notice them but even after constant observation we seldom appreciate their true value. We place a higher value upon eyesight when it has once been impaired and full use has returned. Folkways appear in their natural powerful role only when our intelligence has wrestled with them and a higher plane of mental activity reached. Primitive races do not have the slightest idea of any strange folkways. Residents of sectional communities comment upon peculiarities of speech of other parts of the country, not realizing that those natives are in turn amused at their own mannerisms and speech. The leader of an American Indian Band, a Carlisle 37

College graduate, was playing in a New York City theatre. After the performance a Jewish lady who had recently become wealthy asked him: "And how do you like OUR COUNTRY?"

Folkways are not will creations. They come into existence because of needs and as a product of natural forces. Experience moulds them and they are passed down from father to son with no variation except that needed by changing natural conditions. Only the more important habits and customs survive to become folkways and mores. These regularities in social life are highly important in spite of the fact that they are universally overlooked. Men ordinarily pull on one leg of their trousers first but few can say whether it is the right or the left. The right shoe or stocking is selected first. In shaving, there is a regular formula and perhaps the side of the face nearest the light is more smoothly shaven than the darkened section. Professor Willey sketches activity for one day. Compare it with your daily routine. "Within an hour of seven o'clock in the morning most of us rise. Our first step is to perform a fairly elaborate toilet. The entire body, or at least certain parts of it is washed. If we are men the face is shaved, for hair upon the chin and cheeks is not generally acceptable. We dress: for men and women each a standard type of garment, pressed and folded in the latest fashion. From season to season this may differ, but always we attempt to change in conformity with thousands of others who are like ourselves. 

Dressed, we descend to breakfast, where the ubiquitous morning paper greets us. Our food is of a standard type, served in a standard order, prepared in a standard way. Never does a soup or roast appear upon our table for this meal; never do we have dessert. There is a formula to which we rigidly conform, unquestioning, unthinking—a series of breakfast habits that has us in its grip. 

We start to our school, or our work. If it is winter, we put on our felt hats and topcoats, all of a kind; if it is summer, a straw hat is enough. Day in, day out, week by week, month by month, and year by year, our lives run the accustomed course prescribed by our social groups. Customs of education, customs of business, our part as citizens and voters, our family customs, religious customs, our social customs, our play customs, and many others-to all of these we conform in a manner identical with our adherence to the food customs. Nowhere are things haphazard; the procedure in courting a member of the opposite sex is prescribed by rules and customs as invariable as the rules that cause certain foods to be served at certain times, or definite clothes worn for various occasions. Every aspect of life, from morning until night, and from the ceremonies of birth to those of the funeral, is dominated by a formula of behavior that exacts compliance from us all. These ways of doing things are not altogether our own; they were not initiated by us; they are group ways that we have acquired. 

This somewhat detailed though sketchy description of certain obvious behavior should serve to show that the characteristic thing about human behavior is not sporadicity, but orderliness. The thoughts we think, the things we do, and the things we employ in doing them, are not left to us to develop at the moment according to our own whims or inclinations, but, for the most part, are given to us ready made; we absorb them. The result is the great preponderance of habitual or conformitory performance over unique or individualistic behavior. This does not mean, as we shall see, that social behavior is a thing of drab uniformity; it does mean, that it is a thing within which orderliness prevails. 

Orderliness in social behavior is a characteristic that is universal. It is found among the most primitive of societies as well as in the most advanced. Nothing could be further from the truth than the conception held by many people of primitive men as naked savages, running wild in forests, scalping each other at will, each man being a law unto himself, or held in check by the sheer brute force of the strongest. The network of custom that holds modern society in check, binds with equal strength the society of the Hottentot or Eskimo." 39

Mores begin where folkways end. When folkways are recognized as being absolutely essential and when pressure is brought upon members of the group to abide by certain folkways in order to avoid disaster, mores emerge. They exert a greater influence upon society than the folkways. Most of our social behavior is based upon some indefinable folkways and clearer mores which lead us to consider the effect of our acts in attracting attention of others to us. 

Folkways are more variable than mores and no two groups abide by identical behavior patterns. Mores are more basic and have a tendency to become the same or to become quite similar, even when distance separates the two societies under consideration. One noticeable feature is that an individual who leaves one society and enters another group ordinarily "feels his way" and watches closely before entering wholeheartedly into the activities. He finds himself subject to the folkways and mores of the new group and those to which he has been accustomed may call forth comment or surprise from his new associates. "When in Rome do as the Romans do" is based upon comfort derived from a strict observance of community customs. Man must not only adapt himself to these customs but to natural environment. In frigid and temperate climates he clothes himself differently at various seasons. He is well able to adjust himself to physical environment at the present time. Mixing with other people is not quite so simple. They have constructed many things which are not natural and all must be considered. A modern city forces pedestrians to observe and obey the stop and go signals. The group is favored at the expense of the individual. 

Thousands of man-made things exist to which the uninitiated individual must adapt himself before he becomes one of the group. 

These produced specimens are known as the social environment, as contrasted with the general environment. Common customs have received the general term "culture." Tangible articles present no difficult problem ordinarily, unless the costly "trial and error" method is applied. The infant who reaches out his hand to touch the flame has undergone a painful but at the same time a priceless experience. But he could just as readily discover that fire is hot without touching the flame. More caution shows him that heat becomes greater as his hand approaches the bright light and he may stop before he is actually burned. Natural phenomena are accepted without any question and ordinarily without any complaint. 

Intangible culture is built up gradually. We have religious beliefs, we say "Good Morning," we carry a rabbit's left hind foot, we show respect when elders talk. Folkways and mores are established before we are born or are fashioned without our knowledge or consent. 

We consider it wise and expedient to conform, whether the custom is approved by us or whether we find it somewhat distasteful. Our codes of action and our mental attitudes have been prescribed by 40

our forefathers and if we follow the line of least resistance there will be few changes. Centuries of experience are not broken down very readily. Changes which occur during the life of one person are not always perceived. Today there is a complaint that younger people are not the same as those in the good old days. Man is variable, changeable and flexible, and his memory permits the change of mores or the establishment of new folkways without a realization of the fact. 

Our social heritage is thus accepted as orthodox and we do not notice changes. Neither do we perceive the folkways or mores of other groups, unless they are extremely fantastic. When they come under observation we have a distinct feeling of superiority. Our own culture is far superior to the other. This belief is universal and is known as ETHNOCENTRISM.

Culture is a human creation. It includes belief, knowledge, art, morals, materials, laws and characteristics of the particular society. 

We find it and accept it. Pure chance enables us to boast of our culture. A person born in 1912 has a broader cultural background than one who was born in 1812. An Asiatic, born in Buffalo, and associating with neighbors and their children, will obtain a cultural development which is a marked contrast to that of his brother born in the Orient and educated there for a similar period of time. Culture in a primitive stage of development is called convention. Social approval is given to acts but the test of time is lacking. Folkways are temporary and connected with recreation, economics and social relationships, while mores have received ethical sanction and our performance is obligatory.

Chapter VII THE STATE, SOCIETY AND THE INDIVIDUAL IN BUSINESS 

A modern State includes all machinery of political control organized to improve the economic, moral and cultural conditions of its population. Our State includes all the forces of government from the tiniest settlement or village up through the towns to the wards in the largest cities. In the Middle Ages the Church assumed the function of a moral and economic governmental agency. That attempt, while it was actuated by admirable motives, became a colossal failure and a good working rule was established, namely, that the Church has a gigantic spiritual task to perform and some other power must control our economic welfare. State and Church together are building up moral welfare. Present indications lead us to believe that the Church retains some political and economic factors which might be discarded, while the State has not yet achieved the proper proportion of spiritual characteristics. We shall leave to the Church the solution of its problems and pass to a consideration of the State. It is now in the transitional period and change has been so rapid that it is useless for us to expect a satisfactory solution to the myriad problems confronting it. A State, as we pointed out, has primary political interests. Society's sphere is social relations. Individuals are mainly concerned with economic problems. There are close interrelations. Our State controls society and individual; society dominates the individual and urges State policies; and the individual works upward and shapes societal conditions and approves or alters State control. Initiative rests with the individual but the State, when given authority, exercises absolute control in its sphere. 

Activities of a small population may be controlled with no small degree of efficiency by a personality or by the group. Large cities are making excellent progress today under the direction and guidance of one city manager. But huge scattered populations require another type of supervision in order to promulgate general social and economic well-being, and our State functions to fulfill that control. It operates by modifying one or more of the geographic, inherited, and acquired or cultural interests. At present our government functions in geographic activities by restricting immigration and by deportation of aliens who are found to be undesirable. There is agitation toward the conservation of forests and other natural resources. Water transportation and irrigation projects, preventive flood measures and harbor planning are urgent 50

problems confronting federal bodies. Agricultural research and experiments are aiding all parts of the country. 

The inherited phase of control is more difficult to classify. 

Laws are enacted, forcibly breaking down resistance to tradition. 

Education and the fashioning of public opinion tends to modify inherited characteristics. Environment and new customs, if enforced, will cause sentiment which leads away from the force of inheritance. 

Cultural factors offer the greatest opportunity for State control. Science, invention, teaching and learning all permit entering wedges which may prove effective in influencing conduct. Education becomes compulsory and undesirable opinions are criticised in the schools. While freedom of speech and of the press are granted, there has been restriction of freedom of discussion and several subjects have become taboo. Communist meetings have been broken up in Boston, New York and Buffalo. In the West, I. W. W. gatherings are prohibited or held under strict supervision when authorities know about the convention. 

For five centuries large territorial States have sprung up and expanded. They were natural results of dynastic or nationalistic tendencies of their citizens. Each State controlled commercial activities. Centralization was almost essential to easy control. Decentralization and localism characterized most governments and brought with them the question of the fitness of politics to dominate our modern States. Imperialism has increased and commercial combinations flavored with a political tinge have placed added responsibility upon the State. Internationalism apparently has made little progress. Intentions have been of the highest and apparently all States of the world were not only willing but anxious to settle once and for all time the perplexing problems and become brothers and sisters. But somehow there was discussion and discord and eventually each conference broke up without reaching final agreements. The recent Treaty is the first tangible accomplishment of value and may pave the way for speedy future understandings. 

The State has been triumphant in the solution of one problem. 

Protection of persons and of property has gained great headway. 

There are frequent violations of rules laid down but the system is workable, if not perfect. Improved health and the lowering of the death rate have made life safer and more worth while, both direct results of State activity. Our local and national highways may be regarded by taxpayers as cumbersome burdens, nevertheless one type of incalculable service has been rendered by their construction. 

Public improvements and services have kept step with progress. 51

Commercial progress has never been equalled and the standard of living is on a higher plane than ever before. 

Production methods and distribution of goods are so unusual today that the State has unique problems. In addition to conserving and exploiting resources, the State must become an all-seeing guardian to all of the conflicting interests and act impartially toward each. Unfortunately, this is an open problem. There are charges of graft and favoritism. Lobbying is common and many regard it as a lawful right. That individual with greater wealth has an indisputable advantage over his less fortunate rival. It is evident that government for productivity and government for economic welfare are not truly identical concepts, though they are somewhat related.

Hobson has summarized two suggestions whereby the State would make the largest direct and indirect contribution to human welfare: "First, a more intelligently ordered system of industry as a complex cooperative whole: secondly, an adjustment of this system to the demands of the humanly desirable, as embodied in accepted principles and policies of human welfare." 

A business today is not regarded as "belonging to" the individual who invests all of its capital. Workers who help to supply the product as well as the customers or clients who purchase the article or service must be included as part of the business. An industry without labor and unable to find buyers for the product is indeed "no business." Either the State should assume the function of economic government or the economic system should acquire a liberal human purpose and attitude, along with suitable power. 

Modern courtesy courses offered by large corporations represent the change from the "public be damned" policy formulated by a railroad magnate several decades ago. The suggested pure economic department would be under supervision and ultimate authority would be vested in the State, as representing the guardian of the general welfare.

Strengthening of the State on its economic side must be accomplished in some manner. Business consolidations and combinations have altered the aspect of competition in the past few years and the State lags behind present progress. Voters display moral or intellectual indifference in the selection of representatives in politics and in industry. Antagonistic attitudes have developed between industry and the State, instead of a common interest. The 52

State attempts to be aloof on the one hand and at the same time wishes to be regarded as a member of the family. Political activity often disrupts economic rights by demanding taxes for political purpose and by exacting them from business institutions. 

Criticisms of the State have increased. Many are justifiable in whole or in part. Political and economic influences undoubtedly sway official conduct and action. More powerful organizations dominate parties. The proverbial tangle of "red tape" still ensnares governmental machinery to some extent. Scholastic instruction appeals to many as one field into which the State should not intrude. One principle of our democracy is typified by free discussion of questions which relate to public interest. That measure of personal liberty of which we are so proud may be modified to a greater extent than we consider ethical. Private enterprise has become more efficient in many ways than governmental activities in economic channels.

However, these shortcomings of our State offer another excellent field of ethical inquiry. We must, as individuals, develop our 

State to the point where there are less conflicting elements. One task is to discover the dividing line where the State does not function so well as other agencies. If we could relieve it of that duty and concentrate its energies upon factors which improve the political machinery and develop our social plane, the State might be justified as the greatest historical agency of mankind. 

The State aids security and welfare to human life by four definite policies: 

1. Economic protective: compulsory insurance, physical and moral conditions of employment, abatement of public nuisances, inspections of goods offered for sale and the safe-guarding of investments by the general public. 

2. Development: investigation, preservation, and prolonga tion or renewals of resources. Contributions toward the aiding of health, education and the advancement of science. 

3. Equal apportionment: products of industry, available work, care of the destitute, price-fixing of necessities and the fixing of maximum profits. 

4. Raising of standards: public expenditures of the economic surplus to further cultural advancement. The avoidance of waste. and injustice. Arousal of good will, efficiency and contentment. 53

The white races would probably become extinct everywhere. A few primitive races might live on fruit and small animals in those fertile tropical regions where the human species was originally evolved until they had slowly accumulated a new social heritage." 54

While society borrows indiscriminately, it is surprising to discover how truly backward it is in developing knowledge which is within reach. We all know the difficulty with which an inventor is faced. His brain-child brings forth amusement, sardonic derision, incredulity, suspicion,-anything but approval and sympathetic assistance. But once his findings are verified there is a veritable rush to benefit by the new discovery. A research worker ignores suggestions of the mere layman and thereby frequently fails to attain a faster solution. The layman retaliates by delaying the utilization of the scientifically evolved product of the scientist. 

Parents have more knowledge today relating to the training of children than ever before, yet how many take the time to supervise, to become chummy and to sacrifice without making their own lives a burden? How many physicians diagnose ailments of their patients and subsequently fall victims to the disease which they have successfully combatted in others? Is it the well-informed person who becomes the best religious convert? Society and each individual combine in transmitting and applying knowledge by communication and explanation, by public opinion and other types of social pressure, by law, by economic necessity, by study and practice and observation.

Society could aid in developing an ethical code for the individual by practising tolerance, by exercising cooperation and by replacing ignorance with knowledge. It could lay upon the individual the responsibility of educating himself to the point where he acts reasonably from the social viewpoint. By enforcement of these ethical ideals it could attach a social stigma upon unethical deeds that would prevent all but the most reckless individuals from transgression. Ethical standards require new interpretations and society is bewildered when required to sanction divorce, impartial justice, punishments for crime and kindred problems. It is not capable of passing judgment which involves not alone knowledge of social situations but of economic conflicts and of political intrigues.

Leaders are prominent in society and with them we come to the consideration of the individual in business. Our leaders have been trained persons, or they have been well educated, or they have possessed a magnetic personality which gathered others about their standards, or they have had a knowledge of people and their desires, or they have had ambitions enough to forge ahead. More recently, they have shown an expert's ability in some field or they have displayed an impressive balance sheet.

Individuals may try to find a dividing line between the State and civil society. They are not divisible without great difficulty of 55

classification. One test for the latter is the exercise of guarantees and enforcements of rights and duties of individuals. It may be more scientific than the former. A test for the State is the defining of basic methods of exercising activities of individuals. In business every individual assumes some obligations willingly. He has a certain restricted freedom of choice. But while his performance of this obligation may be voluntary he has placed himself under an engagement to another person or has contracted with another person to serve him. Thus he is no longer a free agent in his business relations but is under an obligation to observe current ethical standards in his actions in business. 

Our next chapter considers in detail qualifications of the individual in business and outlines expectations of his employers and associates. Let us turn here to a phase of business which is seldom considered in its full importance. Going to school is a business. 

The school is one of the largest of business organizations. Consider the amount of the payroll, including teachers, janitors and assistants, clerical help and the amounts earned by students outside of school hours. Which business in town has a larger payroll? 

Consider the capital investment in buildings, equipment and training. A study of education budgets will prove astonishing. Reflect upon the amount of time which is devoted to education. When an individual enters the business field he reckons his worth in terms of hours devoted to tasks, in addition to capital investment. Why should he not reckon up the time invested in the gaining of an education? Individuals who leave school and work for awhile are acutely aware of the cost upon their return. College students may state expenses at the figure of $1000 per year but they have not thought of their time and energy which has been devoted to study instead of to earning power. Figuring six hours to the school day and thirty-six weeks in one year, does not the average high school graduate spend fifteen thousand hours in the classroom and from five to ten thousand additional in home study? From an economic viewpoint the student is engaged in business and his investment will be measured and gauged for the remainder of his life in terms of achievement. Scholastic society admires the businesslike student to a greater extent than "big business" applauds success. Business expects capable performance and wastes no time in praising completion of ordinary tasks. In fact, many young persons complain that their extra work and brilliant achievements in the business field are taken as a matter of course and that one slight error will offset weeks of painful and earnest concentration. A student who becomes businesslike while in school will find that he does not become homesick in the business world. It is difficult to change overnight and the student who has done careless work in school but who 56

has been able to "get by" must become a veritable genius in order to avoid the temptation to continue his carelessness in business. 

One definition of a genius is one who takes infinite pains, which does not leave much hope for the inaccurate, indifferent student. 

Students, not employees, beg for more time in which to finish an assigned task. 

Exactness, concentration and efficiency alone will not enable the individual to become a genius in business. He may work hard and have every honorable characteristic and remain a clerk for life. 

His education may be unusual and complete, yet another point must be included. No person will become a phenomenal success in business without imagination, without initiative and ambition which permit him to finish his present task while dreaming about future possibilities. The drudge is dependable and valuable and wanted in every business but he will not do justice to himself. He must acquire cheerfulness and originality. He must seize an opportunity when it arises. If there is no possibility of an opportunity arising he must construct one all by himself without waiting a lifetime for one to "happen." 57

Chapter VIII PERSONAL QUALIFICATIONS FOR THE BUSINESS WORLD 

HONESTY: There is no need to mention huge thefts which will be discovered within a short time or actions which most of us readily recognize as being right or wrong. It is the little things in business which determine honesty, rather than those which result from intentional deliberation to commit a dishonest deed. The individual who reads the paper while there is nothing to do will innocently ask you "Is there anything wrong in that?" He will work hard on his personal correspondence during office hours, using pen, ink, paper, stamps and time for which his employer is paying him. Perhaps he will justify his acts by recalling the hours of overtime for which he was not paid. At present there is dissention concerning some forms of "honest graft." The term is contradictory. 

Graft is never honest. Tipping may be ethical when a service is performed greater than the value of payment but suitable payment should be made for all service rendered. 

LOYALTY: Since you are engaged in a business which is ethical, you are proud of that fact and anybody who makes slighting remarks about it is really insulting you. You may deplore certain undesirable features but you would never dream of injuring your business in order to secure revenge. Advancement at the expense of an associate or a business could never be ethical. Any secret planning or understandings with members of the organization are clear violations of the term loyalty.

MORALITY: Clean living is not confined to a business or to business hours. Dissipation outside the office leads to a sapping of energy while at work. Dissipation need not be vicious to be immoral. The clerk who plays eighteen holes of golf and then reads for six hours is guilty of no moral crime other than that of undermining efficiency which his employer justly expects. 

TEMPERANCE: Excess is always harmful. One purpose of education is to permit the individual to adjust himself to his environment and any intemperance is evidence that his education has been defective. One college fraternity "cuss-box" was emptied periodically due to two enthusiastic, likeable young fellows who had been admitted and who soon prevailed upon the older members to use language which other members characterized as "strong." Intemperance may consist of too much work as well as too much play. 58

The person who applies the correct formula for mixing his varied activities has accomplished an enviable record.

PERSISTENCE: It is seldom that a person stumbles upon a fortune in business without hard work and many experiences. 

The task requires slow building and the foundation work appears to proceed slowest of all. Often the progress report does not contain a single word. Soon after results begin to appear there is a steady gain and early labor shows returns. Again, too much persistence may overlook some point. This trait must be directed and constantly stimulated. There may be short-cuts but steady persistent endeavor usually brings a higher reward than brilliant spasmodic work.

PREPARATION: Experience is frequently the "Open 

Sesame" to business opportunity but today corporations invade the schools in the same manner as athletic scouts, looking for the graduate who has shown promise in studies. No experience is required, instead, the individual who made one beginning is avoided because of the attention necessary in order to insure his proper start with the new concern. Preparation, then, consists of the record made in school with regard to some of the personal qualifications mentioned in this chapter. One of the greatest assets under this heading is a keen enthusiasm for the business or concern for whom you wish to work. 

CHARACTER: Most of us have "company manners." Under certain conditions we act thus and when other conditions arise we act so. A real test of character is action which involves mental processes and which is not stimulated by tangible factors. Too many employees waver and falter at the very occasion when they have an opportunity to mould character. A person may be making a notable character advance built upon a business failure. Mere success is no more than a guide. The individual who gains fame without boasting or the person who quietly accepts an unfortunate loss without moaning, has constructed character. A wealthy businessman who enjoys no reputation for honor has failed if his purpose was recognition of character. 

SERVICE: Business is built upon the thought of service. The theory is that reward follows service. Henry Ford has stated that service has been and is his chief policy. McCormick said that the farmer was not buying a machine from him but grain which was harvested and placed in the barn. This is another way of saying that service performed by the machine was the main requisite. Patterson sold cash registers because merchants did not know they 59

needed any until Patterson rendered them the service of explaining just how they were needed. Too many neglect the basic principle of providing for wants of others. In business, that method of thinking will be found to produce. Alertness may uncover a task which no one has considered. The person who does a job which should have been completed performs invaluable services. He should be convinced that that task has not been assigned and it should not conflict with regular duties. Assistance to an overworked associate need not result in a heavier schedule assignment to you and he may be grateful enough to help you out in your emergency.

TACT: While modern business ordinarily knows the latest procedure the business initiate may enter an office where he immediately recognizes that there is a great deal of waste motion and confusion. He believes that he could regulate conditions better than they are now found. But, being tactful, he watches and notices whether there is a reason or whether it is simply a case of neglect. At times the smoothest working office does not produce the highest returns and minor details are brushed aside in order to reach the main objective. All sort of persons are found in business and the business man must know how to approach each one. It is possible to say too much but difficult indeed to stop when exactly the proper amount has been said. A tactful individual tries to discover the mental attitude of the other person in order to avoid talking on an entirely different plane. Disappointment is encountered but the tactful person meets it without a grouch. He does not offer impossible excuses. He is content to take any blame without trying to divert it to a fellow employee. He is a gentleman after the other person has forgotten. Observation sometimes aids knowledge far more than countless questions. Novices should ask questions but the elimination of useless ones evokes respect and admiration. 

MORALE: Employers maintain that they are entitled to work well done, but in addition to that they can demand a gracious performance. Employees who permit an expression of sadness or disappointment or resentment to appear upon their faces at the time a task is assigned show clearly that they are figuring employment on the time or job basis rather than the position attitude. 

Cheerfulness is never out of place, when controlled. Any clerk who smiles in the morning or who beams a contagious "Good

Morning" has contributed more to the morale than the dour head bookkeeper who furtively opens the windows and examines each worker as he sets about his daily duties. That official, who expects cooperation and plainly shows that he trusts every member of the force, will be able to secure more efficient work and may show a 60

better spirit of wellbeing than the avowed taskmaster, who constantly radiates suspicion of soldiering on the job. 

ECONOMICS: Let the first essential be a BUDGET. Plan and abide by that plan. Determine the amount necessary for rent, food and clothing. Save, no matter if it is only $1 every two months. The amount should be so small that you never disturb it. 

A separate bank account should be used for surplus savings and withdrawals and credit established for occasional emergencies.

Charge accounts must be settled as agreed and personal loans returned even sooner than that. Borrowing and lending have strained many friendships and should be avoided. Investments in health, education, and insurance precede financial investments and security buying comes next. Speculation, when practiced, is an uncertain venture and should be undertaken with the expectation of no returns, consequently, funds which will not be needed for any other purpose might be applied. Travel, recreation, and incidentals complete the budget list. Try to save ten per cent of your income.

When you gain promotion, act as though you still received the old salary. Limit purchases until you have earned the money to pay for them. Do not incur debts for either pleasure or personal appearance. Your budget planning and operation will become a fascinating game. Calculations will be faulty at first and laughable errors will afford amusement, but a few months of experience should permit you to become exceedingly accurate. Individuals who have never kept an exact budget may regard you as a hoarder, but your friends discover that you always pay your way, though you may refuse to join spendthrift parties. Earned interest promotes more satisfaction than earned income. 

PERSONALITY: Be yourself. No person is able to work out your thoughts so well as YOU. Perfect mechanical details, after you have been given an assignment. Cheerfulness has been cited as one aid to personality, but there are countless other factors. 

Courtesy is another. Originality which is not carried beyond bounds is heartily welcomed by associates and superiors. Gain a reputation for stability. Complete an unusual duty so quickly that the assignor is astonished. BE ON TIME. Promise so seldom that you are not compelled to break any. Notice when an unfair amount of work is given to you and make such a protest that the air reverberates, but be certain that the favoritism was intentional. A joke or story, given at the right time and place, breaks up any monotony. Imagination relieves tedious tasks and current history is always a valuable asset for any person in business. Personality consists in gaining command over the clock, rather than permitting it to dictate. Personality is endowed with eyes that see. Some 61

objects are to be observed, others are commented upon and many should "pass in one eye and out the other." Personality urges the still small voice to repeat "I can" and discourages the term “I am afraid." Personality is jealous of friends and does everything in its power to keep them. Individuals smothered with a sense of futility decide to quit, but the thought that friends are watching them and believing in them gives just enough spark to their ambitions to encourage them to carry on. Personality recognizes the difference between consumption of products and waste of articles. The ideals of personality shine forth from the eyes of the individual and stamp him as a person who possesses character, common sense, and the ability to face an issue. Personality prevents the individual from becoming satisfied with present conditions. Calamity consists in having no desire to press on to greater and higher goals. 

PROFESSIONAL ETHICS: 

Engineer: Duty to public above that to his own interests. 

Faithful performance includes loyalty and fidelity to clients, avoidance of any connection with materials to be used in construction, refusal to accept tips or commissions in return for recommendations favoring a supplying firm, and concentration upon the immediate project. Any conflicting interest or employment should be reported to the employer before final acceptance. Any opportunity to benefit himself at the expense of his profession should be rejected. 

Price and job competition detract from professional dignity. Sealed bids or those sought when another engineer has been informed that his estimate is too high, are distinctly unethical. Any interference with other engineers is unfair. An engineer deals with material factors, while the teacher comes into contact with human beings, in a different sense. An engineer works with tangible matter and meets humans only in contractual or social senses, while the teacher faces intangible workmanship and must connect it with social, contractual, sympathetic and conducive interrelations. 

Teacher: First duty is to the student, rather than to the state or himself, or the parent. This involves violation of training as well as of principles, in many cases. Dress is no longer a personal matter. Advancement is ethically understood, typified by graduate courses. Commissions and tutoring in the same set are definitely barred. The teacher should keep secret confidences of students but must not conceal questionable conduct of other teachers from proper examination. He should not apply for a position which is not available without forcing another teacher from employment. 

To what extent should he participate in society and community activities with students and parents? The American Association 62

of University Professors has a workable code of Ethics and the organization is gaining in power in the upper scholastic field. 

Teachers who discuss faults of their students and teachers who criticise the school in a public place lessen their own dignity and subject themselves to the danger of creating a false public opinion concerning the profession, as well as a certain diminution of respect and cooperation.

Lawyer: Primary duty again is to the client. Society may conflict with client's interest but lawyer's knowledge must remain concealed once he has promised to take the case. Questionable cases should be rejected, and such indication forms parts of the oath of office. Attorneys should keep the peace and encourage law observance, advocating compromises and avoidance of litigation, educating the general public to the point where the law, lawyers and judges are respected and honored. Unlike the teacher, who instructs young persons of good repute, the lawyer meets those who have come into contact with the law in either an innocent or criminal sense. There is an instinctive fear of the law and legal advisors may accomplish much by removing some of the ignorance and obsessions. They may refuse to consider cases which involve conflict with other statutes and should go so far as to avoid those which are strictly legal but are tinted with offensive morality. Morals are sometimes regarded as parents of law, originating from the MORES mentioned earlier. One distinction is that the first breaking of a moral law entails serious qualms of conscience, while subsequent violations become less and less bothersome. In law, the first offence is condoned to some extent but punishment becomes more vigorous as repetitions occur. 

Physician: Law is accepted as a guide by the doctor as well as by the lawyer. Each has difficulty in attaining skill and completing required duties satisfactorily without "wasting time" figuring out moral problems which extend outside the legal sphere. One moral concept regards the physician as the guardian of public health, improving public morals. Punctuality becomes as important for the physician as for the teacher. Communications remain secrets unless society will benefit from the explanation. Criticism of the work of another physician is derogatory, while interference is not to be tolerated, though accompanied by urgent requests from the patient. Temporary relief from suffering is the extent of such services without the consent of the attending physician. However, the physician who discovers a difficult case should refer the patient to a more expert diagnostician where there is any doubt as to the type of ailment or the seriousness of condition of the patient. Patronizing attitudes of physicians toward nurses and other assistants is 63

one of the most alarming features of the "inhuman" condition into which many successful practitioners permit themselves to lapse. 

The nurse contributes her indeterminate share to every case on which she is called and it is patently unfair to shunt responsibility toward her when her actions are so sternly limited by the physician, who, nevertheless, does not permit scruples to prevent him from leaving the scene of activity before the task is completed, placing the nurse in charge and requiring her to do the most disagreeable portions of the job for which HE is paid. This callousness is not peculiar to one type of professional person but is a constant menace. 

The physician hears so many pleas of alarming symptoms that he refuses to become alarmed over an individual case. Excuses are so common to the teacher that they are accepted without an effort to remedy conditions. Lawyers are prepared for the statement that circumstances alter cases and that in this particular instance the act was proper. All professionals find the layman advising, criticising and condemning them without a knowledge of the most fundamental principles of the business, and pay little attention to a valid suggestion when it arises. 

Remuneration acts as a basis of contrast between the professional man and the business man. A lawyer or physician performs service without knowledge of financial standings of clients. Teachers continually give outside work and assistance to students which is not stipulated in contracts. But a business obligation tends to become much more definite and clearly defined. The engineer thus comes closest to receiving payment on a business basis. A well defined line of demarcation should exist between the duties of a professional man and his social obligations, in order to avoid a conflict which might normally result. Fee-splitting and reciprocal understandings are discountenanced by all professionals and each has his definite code of ethics. Professionals were the first to adopt a standard code of ethics, medical members having established codes long before the strange Hippocratic Oath. Physicians today believe it more worthy to treat patients BEFORE they become ill rather than waiting to cure diseases which have become established in the system. Lawyers act in advisory capacities more than ever before and find it more enjoyable than defending a client against criminal charges.

The business man engages in industry in order to gain a profit from the business of rendering service where needed. He now realizes that any business which does not devote itself to service must be temporary and cannot be built upon a firm foundation. 

Stability is one characteristic of success and reputation and goodwill have been valued in dollars and cents. The trade-marked name 64

of one product of one company is priced at ten million dollars. 

Products of other concerns or other services reckon good-will in terms of millions of dollars. An unethical merchant discovers that his priceless good-will depreciates instantaneously when practices are revealed. Our business man wishes freedom to pursue his calling. He is disturbed in such a variety of ways that his working day becomes shortened. A salesman spends less than twenty hours each week in actually selling. Acute antagonism saps the energy of the merchant and he discovers new situations each morning as he arises. 

No field of endeavor is so expansive and so chameleon-like. At the same time he is urged to be original, to get away from wornout ideas of the past. He should have expert training comparable to that of the professional man and should study constantly. There is a constant struggle between activity of business, which is social, and the individual motive of gain, which is selfish. It is an interesting fact that the highest ethical standards are set by business houses who are now successful. Add to that the point of their STATED actions, which may have no direct relation to their PAST PER- FORMANCES and the realization that conditions have changed during their business lifetimes. These codes apply to them NOW but may be inadequate to the young person who is just starting his performance.

Competition is wholesome when it is conducted in the right proportions. But the difficulty lies in finding an arbiter who has worked out the right formula and the hardest task of all is to find one who will apply that rule once he has discovered it. The beginner in business must, as in the classroom, work out his individual salvation and construct his personal code of ethics. He is justified in having one code for personal conduct and one for business conduct. Both should be based on the Golden Rule as far as possible. 

Both should realize that actions affect the other fellow. But coordination requires genius.

Many business enterprises, particularly corporations, are accused of having no heart and no soul-no human impulse beyond that of the desire of returning a profit to investors. While there are no direct dishonorable dealings there are constant indications of a lack of sympathy for humanity and an occasional indifference toward general public interests. A collective responsibility arises, with no individual or group bearing the burden, and a subsequent feeling that any accidental wrong may be perfectly adjusted by the payment of the proper amount of money damages. 

The increasing number of women who enter business multiply ethical problems, which will not be considered in detail. A vast 65

majority are economic problems, involving wages, equal rights, unemployment of males, inequality of family employment opportunities and the standard of living. Social aspects, such as maintenance of the present family system, increasing emancipation of women and proper education of children are changing. Even the political organization of business and of the State may be affected. 

Into this business turmoil and economic center the young person today may bring a new moral concept and the individual who combines sound business principles with sympathetic ethical viewpoints will eventually find himself a leader with a considerable following. Never before has there been a greater need for businesses based upon public opinion, nor a more brilliant opportunity to gain success in this field. 66

Chapter IX ETHICAL PROBLEMS AND CODE BUILDING THE PROPERTY QUESTION: 

Professor Hobhouse indicates that the possessor of property may control persons as well as things: "Property is the material basis of a permanent, ordered, purposeful, and self-directed activity. On the other hand, property is a form of social organization, whereby the labour of those who have it not is directed by and for the enjoyment of those that have.” "Property, it was clear to the thinkers who introduced this conception into ethics, was a human institution. The gifts of nature, the land and its fruits, must originally be free to all men; appropriation was the act of man, and the institutions by which appropriation is regulated derived from man-made laws. Just as by nature all men are free and equal, so by nature they have a right to use the earth and its fruits for their own purposes, to apply their labour to them freely, and to enjoy the product at their will." 

The second paragraph is communistic theory. Such a condition exists when uninhabited country is settled. But a practical difficulty arises when a thickly settled community must decide the share which each person is to enjoy as a result of his right and labor. 

Public wealth may be owned and used in four ways: 

1. Privately owned and used. 

2. Privately owned and publicly used. 

3. Publicly held, but privately used. 

4. Publicly held and commonly used. 

Our ethical problem may be stated: If property is valuable morally, how many are enjoying it and how many deprived under the present system and is that ratio changing? Are any deprived of enjoyment by tremendous power influenced by the person who is constantly gaining greater wealth? Is it right for the billionaire to take wealth from poor who must purchase his necessity products, when he is legally entitled to the established price but has no need for the great profit? 67

St. Thomas Aquinas argued that stealing is not immoral if there is sufficient justification. If a capitalist's pocket were picked and the contents given to an orphan who was given great happiness, would the act be right?

One ethical interpretation of property rights is that man holds property not only for his own use but to further the welfare of humanity.

THOUGHTS UPON WEALTH: Personality is more im portant than wealth. A person who favors a single appetite at the expense of vitality or efficiency is immoral. Wealth should be regarded as a subordinate end. Wealth should depend upon activity. Public service is a noble activity for the wealthy. By serving humanity they become more self-respecting and feel greater satisfaction in living. 

BARGAINING: A four-day old chicken sandwich, offered to two starving shipwrecked aristocrats, will command a higher price than the identical article offered at the end of an eighteen hole round of golf. One famished person may bid against the other and offer any available amount of cash in return for the sole article of food. 

Under the caption "business is business" the price fixed was the highest that could be secured, but today there is increasing realization that a reasonable profit is secured more readily from an article selling at a popular price and that economic fact stimulates ethical bargaining. The instance noted is a rare exception. Collective bargaining is not confined to industrial workers. Commodities abound in variety as well as in quantity and the bargaining is conducted on a gigantic scale. Ethical considerations are difficult when the needs of such a huge group are being met. Bargaining becomes important when we remember that goods well bought are half sold, or, applied to individual purchases, cheap unnecessary articles are no bargain. 

LABOR: Problem is to obtain a common understanding and interest to replace the indifference, suspicion and antagonism of the mechanical, absentee-owner-capitalistic system. Efficiency systems aided production but had no thought for the worker. Scientific management started with humanitarian principles but lost them in the bustle and enthusiasm of success. Other ethical problems have been indicated previously.

ADVERTISING: One of the most difficult problems. There are so many factors involved that we can do no more than state the problem. Truth is the factor stressed as an essential in advertising 68

and by means of truthful advertising the building up of a public consciousness to the point where advertisements are respected and believed. It is not desirable to gain the old objective—a belief that everything which was stated in print must not be questioned-but real progress can and will be made to exert firm pressure upon unethical advertisers. One goal is the lessening of bankruptcy sales which recur monthly, "lost our lease" pleas, "our loss, your gain" slogans, inventory and pre-inventory sales, end-of-season and advance showing sales, remote location fire sales, flood and insurance opportunities and others which undermine the reputation of the advertisers and at the same time initiate public desire to engage in a bargain hunt. There are only 365 days in our year and we shall soon be reduced to the Irishman's "last holiday." We have Father's 

Day, Mother's Day, Independence Day, Labor Day and other reputable vacation periods at which wonderful advertising stunts may be performed. But we have the alarming fishless days and meatless days, sweet and bitter days, flower and sweetheart, springspree and smoke days, until we find ourselves in a veritable Alicein-Wonderland setting. Each advertiser urges us at our peril to forget the day. Any untoward results most certainly cannot be laid at his door.

EXTERNAL: Three vast groups of forces sway business outside of the immediate organization. They are the Law, the State and Public Opinion. Obedience to the first two are obligatory and evidence points to necessity for studying the last group even more than the other two.

INTERNAL: Continuing the grouping of three elements, we find inside the business productive, distributive and administrative activities. Production has received its modicum of attention for the past fifteen years and is quite well organized. In the administrative field there has also been progress in the last decade but activities of distribution are more recent and there we shall find the most ethically disturbing factors. 

COMPETITION: Modern business men repress bitterness toward rivals. Friendly rivalry may become the basis for renewed activity along business lines and eventually a line of departure may be drawn which separates strictly secret activities from information which may benefit both. Commerce reports aid one industry without a corresponding detriment to the one which gave out the information. An increasing number of institutions distribute periodic reports of conditions and trends which is rapidly becoming systematic enough to play an important place in business activity. 

Better methods are adopted by rivals and economic discoveries are 69

freely given to the world in general. Possibly the most bitter struggle at the present time is the conflict between independent retail dealers and the chains. Ethical retorts have been common and suspicion toward each other is vitally keen. But it would not be surprising to realize that one has bolstered up the other until each has its definite function to perform, when they may clasp hands and consider common methods of increasing service to humanity.

ACCOUNTING: This profession furnishes a good example of a business which is so recent that there has been too little time to organize members. Before we can expect ethical progress an opportunity must be given to perfect terminology, systems of thought and procedure and coherent unity of the profession. But, the extreme importance of duties of accountants has stressed attention to competency, to ethical performance and to a general tieup with public opinion which marks surprising advances. 

The FARMER: Agriculture is listed as a business enterprise. 

We admit that it is a peculiar one, as it is not based upon a capital expenditure. The farmer who borrows money to raise one crop in a year presents an entirely different problem from that of the industrial producer, who borrows a smaller proportion in order to obtain capital to complete production or to market his goods. The latter obtains short time loans and offers better security. His raw material will command a large percentage of the loan in an emergency while the farmer who loses his crop for the year has lost not only his raw material but also the opportunity to produce income in some other manner and must seek another loan before he may plant his acreage another year. Agriculture has the important risks of industry in addition to weather and internal problems. The Federal 

Reserve system had helped as far as possible, even going so far as to advance unwarranted sums but the growing of commodities is one subject of vital interest and deserves further careful ethical scrutiny. 

IDEALS and STANDARDS: Neither the subjective ideal, which considers motives, nor the objective viewpoint, which dwells upon results, is alone sufficient goal for a standard of business ethics. The latter criterion is of necessity employed but every person in business should glorify the person who tried to do right but failed. That desire by itself is not sufficient but it forms the basis for a correct beginning and is not so blameworthy as praise given to the individual who used wrong methods in gaining correct results. Useful portions of both views are necessary in building up ideals and standards of ethics in business. 70

In business, ethical standards revolve about the organization and the individual, with society's interests as an afterthought. 

Whenever possible, public needs should be the ideal objective, with individual members of the organization, who maintain allegiance to the business, working for the welfare of society in conjunction with their business activities. Such unselfish conduct permits devotion to business duties and the sacrifice of personal and social contacts is ordinarily offset by resulting economic rewards. 

Employment of convicts who seek honest labor is another question which has no universal solution. Some organizations refuse them arbitrarily while others go to the extreme of hiring every person who is physically capable. There is a proper ethical decision which every employer should reach. Morality and ethics of officials affect all workers in a business and responsible executives show great care in their selection of higher officials, knowing that their standards will prevail down through the list of employees. 

Occasionally we find cases of businesses who declare that objective viewpoints are the only essential considerations. One such contradictory example is the newspaper which accepts patent medicine advertising which leads to sales of the product with resulting injury to the purchaser who believed in the value of any article advertised in his reputable newspaper. 

Evidently, each business should clean house for itself in the adoption of ethical ideals. It may go so far as to adopt one code for members of its family, another for rivals, a third for customers and the last for strangers. But that brings up the questions: Are such distinctive standards ethical? Should there not be one ideal for all?

Of course, there is no need to discuss the double standard which exists in business. The question has already been settled and it is only the practice which remains.

Accuracy versus Speed: 

Time is an essential element in business. Tasks are assigned with a time limit. A practical problem in ethics finds the worker deciding whether the somewhat imperfect job should be rushed and completed on time or whether perfection is desirable regardless of the time element.

Codes of Ethics have been formulated and revised in every profession and in every department of industry and business. The shabby country practitioner who has attended penniless patients 71

with no thought of obtaining his fee is more respected than the city consulting surgeon who has attained a national reputation through delicate and hazardous operations upon wealthy clients who could afford his skilled services. Unsung and comparatively uneducated teachers have aroused a blazing intellectual spark in the breasts of students more frequently than the renowned college professor who lectures to thousands and relegates conference duties to quiz assistants. Each is following his code and each performs his valuable service.

Thinkers review the survival of the fittest and natural selection theories of Spencer and Darwin, the legal precepts of John Marshall and Roscoe Pound, the surprising reflections of Aristotle and Adam

Smith, the achievements of Michelson and Edison, the psychologies of James and Wundt with modern revisions, and then come to dwell upon the army of writers who suggest the latest modern reasoning. 

Any successful code of ethics must utilize current information thus painfully and lengthily amassed and train it upon our practical problems today. We are modern, medieval and ancient at the same time. At a given moment we may argue heatedly for emancipation and up-to-date motivation, including statistics in support of the argument which were compiled decades ago. Thinkers often lack high emotional capacity, while a man of profound feelings is often lacking in clearness of thought. In religion, for example, there are both intellectual and emotional functions. We may find the intellectual, emotional or ritualistic actions emphasized at the expense of the ethical. Nearly all of the primary modes of human functioning are present when an ethical question confronts us. Each individual has different conceptions of values. Moral values affect religion. If a religion demanded inmorality of us, we would fail to consider that religion ethical. There is an intricate system of action and reaction to every situation. Responses may be alike or dissimilar, slow or fast, equally or unequally persistent. Because of these many conflicting features, ethics cannot readily be reduced to a code. The interest is composed of too small a proportion of the practical and too much of the theoretical. One possibility for an individual code is to watch out for faults which have the tendency to recur habitually and by guarding them closely we may be able to avoid them entirely.

Business freedom to date has been accomplished at the expense of social freedom. Its ethical-social function should be to act as an intermediate agent between the state and the individual for the benefit of all parties concerned. The ethical situation for the individual arises when he is torn between conflicting loyalties to the various groups of which he is a member. Each is insistent that he 72

favor that particular group. His ethical conduct requires keen foresight in anticipating and controlling behavior rather than in moral reflections. Ethics runs the constant danger of concentrating on unimportant points, leaving many essential elements unsettled. 

Codes should first of all be practical, not written down as idealistic and something which may be read with a swelling of the chest and a smug complacency of being the most advanced sentiments in the field. A business should confine its ideal to policies and fall short in codes. Any code adopted should be followed without fail and a simpler code becomes more valuable than one which contains too many impossible rules of conduct. Standards may also produce some unfortunate results. While there is a tendency to improve the condition of every item below standard there is a corresponding urge to permit all above that line to fall back to normal. Thus ideals and standards may become the cause of the very condition which they seek to avoid-the existence of unethical situations. 

Too much should not be expected from codes. They are temporary expedients and exist for the purpose of revision and growth. 

The United States Chamber of Commerce has published a resolution relating to codes: "The function of business is to provide for the material needs of mankind, and to increase the wealth of the world and the value and happiness of life. In order to perform its function it must offer a sufficient opportunity for gain to compensate individuals who assume its risks, but the motives which lead individuals to engage in business are not to be confused with the function of business itself. When business enterprise is successfully carried on with constant and efficient endeavor to reduce the cost of production and distribution, to improve the quality of its products, and to give fair treatment to customers, capital, management and labor, it renders public service of the highest value."

Some parts of the code follow: "Waste . . . . . is intolerable and constant effort will be made toward its elimination.

Representation of goods and services should be truthfully made and scrupulously fulfilled.

The foundation of business is confidence, which springs from integrity, fair dealing, efficient service, and mutual benefit. 73

The reward of business for service rendered is a fair profit plus a safe reserve, commensurate with risks involved and foresight exercised.

Obligations to itself and society prompt business unceasingly to strive toward continuity of operation, bettering conditions of employment, and increasing the efficiency and opportunities of individual employees.

Excesses of every nature. .... are condemned. 

Unfair competition. . . . . is wasteful, despicable and a public wrong. Business will rely for its success on the excellence of its own service.

Business should render restrictive legislation unnecessary through so conducting itself as to deserve and inspire public confidence.'

Whenever Ethics is mentioned there is reference to the Golden Rule. But how is the person in business to discover a method of regulating his conduct along those lines? After the reader has said, "Yes, he BELIEVES that credo but he does not PRACTICE it," how may the merchant justify himself when a fellow merchant becomes involved in a rank violation of their code of ethics? Again, does Mr. Consumer have a suspicion that a code has been printed simply to obtain his sympathy and cooperation, and does he regard it as a tool or as a remedy? The student who wishes to continue study of business ethics may at this point branch out in three different ways: 

1. Divide business ethics from social, moral and religious connections so far as possible, building up a few basic principles in the form of generalities. 

2. Investigate codes of ethics and determine the degree to which they measure up to their own specifications. 

3. Select specific problems which arise in everyday life, find out all the facts, and work on the solutions. 75

It is the belief of the author that such codes as have been considered should not be subscribed to by the younger individuals who are starting business careers. That does not mean the encouragement of unethical acts but involves the thought that these codes have been advocated by large firms and successful merchants AFTER their periods of struggle and discouragement. The person who enters business has difficulty enough to keep his head above water without constructing generalities. A better plan is to evolve a personal code while preparing for the business world and checking up whenever there are any spare moments. When each contributes his share to the project there will not be many unethical practices which survive. Energy can be devoted toward ethical affairs instead of toward schemes which will bring temporary reward but subsequent financial punishment. 

One such code, which was prepared by a former student, includes many admirable factors:

CODE OF ETHICS 

for 

BRYANT AND STRATTON COLLEGE 

As a student of Bryant and Stratton College, it is my obligation: 

1. To deem my vocation worthy, offering me the opportunity of service to the school and to society as a whole. 

2. To improve myself, increase my efficiency and extend my service, thus living up to the motto of the college: "Aim High.” 

3. To endeavor to so standardize the vocation of business training that it may be termed a profession. 

4. To be at my best, conducting my affairs in a courteous, profitable and ethical manner, that every other student may be better and may emulate my example. 

5. To have no eye for those faults and differences of fellow students which I would long to have go unnoted in myself, and to hold as sacred the confidence of friends and fellow students. 

6. To remain firm in the belief that service is the basis of all progress and that I am willing to deny selfish pleasure for service to others. 76

7. To keep myself morally, mentally and physically clean at all times, maintaining honesty in my dealings with fellow students and teachers, upholding the honor and dignity of the school. 

8. To believe and incorporate in all my dealings the universality of the Golden Rule: "All things whatsoever you would that men should do unto you, do ye so unto them." 

9. To ever maintain an attitude of cheerfulness and pleasantness with all with whom I come in contact, remembering that a smile goes a long way toward giving others the right impression of our school and our fellow students a feeling of hope and encouragement. 

10. To do whatever is asked of me willingly, cheerfully and to the best of my ability. 

11. To never criticize a teacher or member of the executive force, maintaining the utmost respect for them at all times, mindful of the fact that they are here for the advancement of our knowledge and general welfare. 

12. To establish, maintain and adhere to the foregoing ethical code that we may be loyal to our school, to its members and to the high principles and ideals for which it stands and upon which it was founded."

ALETA HATCH 

In conclusion, it is more proper to include a definition of ethics than at the beginning. Ethics is a conscious rule of behavior supported by the sanctions of the appropriate authority. It continues from the point where Law leaves off. It is concerned with particular acts, which are not important by themselves. Ethics is limited to local authority and proper conduct in one business community may well become improper in another. Morals are merely customs imposed upon members of the group. Morals are based upon emotion. Ethics is one step higher, having a pinch of reasoning added.

Virtue consists in doing right without any ulterior motive and is called the only intrinsic good but it is not the complete good. The latter needs the appropriate amount of human happiness. Our right actions are controlled by exercise of the will. Will is stronger than intelligence or physical prowess, or good fortune. It faces misfortune with attention and a tightening of moral forces and the amount of will-power which the individual possesses at the moment 77

determines defeat or victory over the situation. Our morals and our ethics are determined very largely by the amount of effort which we make to control our actions. We might go so far as to say that our worth as individuals is measured by the will which we exert. In any given situation some one part will attract more interest than the whole and there is need to concentrate upon the part which is essential rather than the part which interests. 

Objects are necessary to start action, whether they are visible or mental but the pain or pleasure which action brings modify it. 

But will enters in. Who frowns for the pleasure of frowning? Do we become furious over a situation because of the pleasure to us? 

Evidently ordinary incidents do not control our actions from the standpoint of pleasure or pain. The greatest miracle of will is the ability to center attention. James tells us that "reasonable ideas take precedence over others IF THEY CAN OBTAIN A QUIET 

HEARING." Life is such a hurried affair today that the person is wise who has the ability to commune quietly with himself—to be entertained by nothing except his innermost thoughts-to decide for himself just how he shall act and conform to his religion, his morals, his peculiar system of ethics and the hundred and one other determinants to action. 78
