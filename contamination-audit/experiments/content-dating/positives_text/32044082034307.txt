THIS BUSINESS DEPRESSION ITS CAUSES AND REMEDIES 

How will we get out of this depression and by what course of events? It is apparent that our most influential business men and financiers are in utter confusion in this matter. Leadership of the type which has successfully guided us in the past has almost entirely disappeared. 

This fact is of appalling significance as to the maintenance of our economic system and its institutions. Our economic destiny is hopeless unless business men, who are the operators of our economic mechanism, attain a true and sound understanding of economic processes.

This article attempts to enlighten the business man in the problems of the business cycle through a process of thinking consistent with his experience and knowledge, by aiming to shift his point of view from that of self-interest in his direct profit or gain, to interest in the general business welfare, which, in the final accounting, reacts to his own advantage. An aggregate rather than a competitive point of view is taken. Conclusions are reached but they are not of more importance than the attempts made herein to disprove fallacious notions [S]

which tend to retard our recovery and also to throw light on business processes, which are generally misunderstood.

The practical mind of the business man usually rebels against anything in the nature of economic theory. Nevertheless it is essential that we have a true conception of certain abstract principles which are necessary for a sound understanding of this subject. We must know what "purchasing power" is and how individual expenditures and savings react on general business conditions.

PURCHASING POWER, WHAT IS IT? 

The force which keeps our economic mechanism going originates in the needs and desires of the people. If there could be compiled a list of the needs and desires of all our people, we would have a schedule of potential market demand to product which would require all available labor on full-time basis. The limits to which anyone can satisfy his needs and desires, depend on the extent of his purchasing power. 

What is purchasing power?

The prevailing notion that purchasing power is confined to money and deposit balances in banks is responsible for most of the controversies and fallacies which glut economic literature. While money in pockets and in bank accounts is purchasing power, such money is the smaller and least significant part of the [6]

total current purchasing power of the country. 

Purchasing power is mainly represented in the possession of marketable wealth and of current and prospective income from wages, salaries, rents, dividends, interest. 

Most purchases are made "on credit" (commercial credit). Credit ratings and the credit standing of corporations and individuals indicate their purchasing power. Every business man knows at any time the approximate extent of his purchasing power, and his cash on hand, as a rule, is a small part of it. As to his personal living expenses, he normally adjusts his budget to his current and prospective income. He does not hesitate to make commitments in excess of funds he has on hand at the moment. His household bills which accrue during the month, are paid from income which he receives at the end of the month. Less than ten percent of the volume of purchases are made on the "cash and carry" plan. More than ninety percent are made on commercial credit followed later by disbursement of funds in settlement. From banking figures it appears that for every hundred dollars of purchase commitments made and outstanding at any particular time, there exists not more than forty dollars of bank funds.

SAVERS VS. BORROWERS 

The average individual limits his budget of living expenses to his income. No responsible [7]

person would consider, in normal times, satisfying his current wants and desires for consumable goods out of principal, i. e. by disposal of past accumulations of wealth. Nor does such a person spend all his income for living expenses. From training and experience, he acquires the habit of saving. It is important that we do not conceive savings as necessarily withheld from expenditure for goods and services. 

Hoarding of money is negligible in normal times when our banking system is in sound condition.

Deposits in savings banks are not normally withheld from actual expenditure, for no bank can continue to accept such deposits unless it can loan such funds to borrowers, who, of course, expend them. Savings generally seek investment in capital enterprises which bring an annual income to the saver in the form of interest, rent, dividends and equities in profits. 

It was stated above that, generally speaking, expenditures for living and maintenance or, in other words, for consumable goods, are kept within the limits of current income, but for capital goods, expenditures are not so limited. 

They are based on anticipated savings from future income. For example, a man with a salary of $10,000 per year desires to build a home for himself and family, and needs $10,000 in addition to funds he already has to complete payments on his house and grounds. He executes a mortgage with his bank for this amount and [8]

agrees to pay $1,000 per year on the principal of his loan. The $10,000 he obtained from the bank represents the current savings of many depositors, who, practically speaking, own an equity in his property as well as equities in many other similar bank assets. This home builder spends $10,000 more than his current income during the year he builds his home, but for the following ten years, he will underspend his income $1,000 per year. The amount he pays each year on his indebtedness represents current savings of that year. 

Municipalities also overspend their current income in construction of public improvements based on future income, and obtain current funds from those who buy their bonds out of savings. The tax budget of every city includes sinking fund reserves to meet obligations made in previous years. Such sinking fund reserves represent savings from income by taxation. Corporations, likewise, do not hesitate to borrow funds through security issues for additional production capacity, provided they anticipate profitable operation of additional plant facilities. 

There are those who are consistent savers and who abhor indebtedness. On the other hand there are those who are alert for opportunities for maximum profits and who do not fear the risk of borrowing funds for capital enterprises. [9]

Between these two extremes are those who are sometimes in debt and sometimes out of debt. 

The latter class is represented by the case cited above of the man who borrowed $10,000 to build a home.

Probably the great majority of the people who save, invest without personal knowledge of the merit of their investments. They act on the advice of bankers, brokers and promoters. On the other hand we have corporations whose managements see the need for extensions of plants, and investment bankers and promoters who are constantly on the lookout for investment opportunities. Inasmuch as most people are induced to save only from desire to provide for the future, the rate at which savings accumulate has no relation to the need for current expenditures for additional capital facilities. 

When commitments for capital expenditures are in excess of the current savings of the people then the banking system is called upon to extend its credit. When such a situation arises, we face the danger of credit inflation in our banking system. By this same token when the savings of the people are in excess of the amount which our capitalists determine can be wisely spent in new capital enterprises there follows a retirement of bank loans and a consequent deflation of bank credit. [10]

TOTAL CURRENT EXPENDITURES EQUAL TOTAL CURRENT INCOME 

Inasmuch as the income of every individual is derived from the expenditures of others, the total income of all the people in any period of time equals the expenditures of all the people. 

By the term "expenditures" herein is meant outlays for goods and services purchased under commitments usually made previous to actual disbursements. It does not include disbursements for retiring bank loans. While our income and expenditures are affected by foreign trade in goods and services, our domestic or internal trade is reconcilable to the above equation.

Any change in the figures of this equation is initiated on the expenditure side. Those who receive income can not of themselves increase it, and they are not likely to reduce it on their own volition. However, with expenditures there is freedom of choice on the part of every individual to spend less than his income and if his "credit" is good, he may spend more than his income. If there is any reduction in the current rate of expenditures, there must likewise be a reduction in the current rate of income with a consequent reduction in production and employment.

Expectancy of increasing future income, whether justified or not, generally results in [11]

increasing purchase commitments, which actually increase total income. "Capital gain" or paper profits from the rising value of securities, whether such values are sound or not, represents additional purchasing power and, to the extent it is used for purchases, adds to the total income. By the same token, when pessimism prevails as to the future and security values are on the decline, purchase commitments are comparably reduced and a larger share of total income is devoted to savings. Under such conditions savings are largely used to retire bank loans or are allowed to accumulate in savings accounts in banks. In such times of depression, the banks find it necessary to maintain the most liquid condition and do not invest all their accumulated savings in ways which mean expenditures of the savings. To the extent that the savings of the people are not expended the total income declines with consequent increase in unemployment.

Increasing employment can only come about through an increase of total expenditures over total current income which means that individuals, corporations and governmental agencies, in the aggregate, must borrow or create liabilities in an amount greater than current savings. 

Inasmuch as the normal individual, particularly in times of depression, resists to the utmost borrowing funds for consumable goods, it follows that a higher rate of employment depends [12]

on the production of capital goods at a rate in excess of current savings.

CONDITIONS PRIOR TO THE DEPRESSION 

Now holding to the point of view and the notions above outlined, let us analyze the circumstances which confronted us in the period of prosperity prior to the collapse in security values in 1929. The spring of that year found this country in a most prosperous condition with labor well employed and production eclipsing all previous records. The people were better provided than ever before with homes, furnishings, equipment, automobiles, etc. The standard of living was at its highest for all classes of people. Municipalities had spent large sums for public improvements needed to accommodate the increasing population of our cities. Mills and manufacturing plants had been extended and equipped with the latest, most efficient machinery which made our production capacity ample to take care of all possible demands. All of the homes, buildings, public improvements and factories were financed out of the savings of the people. There was no credit inflation at that time. Capital expenditures for production facilities had proved extremely profitable. Industries had increased their sales and earnings and the values of their securities had steadily mounted. This situation naturally attracted savings to corporate securities. While the rate of savings had [13]

been ample in the past several years to provide all this capital, people were induced to increase their savings to take advantage of this rare opportunity to make stock market profits. Corporations had made large profits from which they accumulated surpluses and were thus able to pay for considerable extensions and betterments out of their own savings. There was little necessity for them borrowing the savings of investors.

In addition to the earned income of all the people, "capital gain" from the rising values of securities represented large additional purchasing power; and, as stated above, to the extent that it was used, added to total income. 

The Federal Government had been retiring Liberty Bonds at the rate of approximately a billion a year. Holders of bonds which were retired had these funds to reinvest, and probably the greater part of them sought investment in stocks.

EXCESSIVE SAVINGS-THE MAIN CAUSE OF THIS DEPRESSION 

All the above circumstances suggest that the rate of savings of the people together with the rate of retirement of the government debt was in excess of the need for new capital expenditures. Investors were seeking ownership of securities belonging to others, representing capital already provided, therefore, savings were [14]

not being expended in the creation of new wealth. As consequences of this came first, the inevitable inflation of security values due to demand exceeding supply and second, a reduction of total current income due to non-expenditure of savings. Among the holders of securities who sold to new investors were those who did so presumably because of high prices. They naturally hesitated to purchase other securities all of which were likewise inflated. It is this hesitation or delay in making purchase commitments that represents the first step in the reversal process from inflation and prosperity to deflation and depression. Delay in expenditures means a declining rate of income. 

All major depressions are the reactions of inflation; either of bank credit, of commodity prices or security values. In the inflation period preceding this depression, the initial rise in security values was warranted by increased earnings. Owners of securities were as much responsible for marking up values as buyers. 

Progressive increases in corporation earnings resulted in further advances in security prices.

Such a condition attracted increasing investment and speculation. Then prospective buyers of securities assumed dictatorship of values. 

Holders of securities were restrained to some extent from selling because of income taxes and consequently it was not literally a free market. 

When such a cycle process gets under way every [15]

factor intensifies every other factor and it becomes difficult to control. In past times stringency or inflation of bank credit stopped commodity price inflation, but in the inflation movement which ended in 1929 there was no inflation of bank credit. The outstanding volume of bank credit (demand deposits of banks) was smaller in proportion to the total volume of non-speculative business and exchanges than it had ever been previously. The large increase in loans and investments of our banks which had occurred through preceding years was represented by a comparable increase in savings deposits. The large increase in brokers' loans represented mainly the borrowing of idle deposit balances of corporations and savings banks which essentially were accumulated savings. While bankers had lost technical means of controlling inflation, they exerted every influence in their power to stop the inflation movement. The heights to which values went, in spite of the vigorous warnings of our Federal Reserve bankers, can only be explained by an excess of current savings of the people supplemented by the amount of the national debt which was retired.

The conclusion that our existing depression is due to excessive savings of the people directed into stock speculation is contrary to the notions of many of our influential bankers and business leaders, who have blamed our present condition [16]

on the extravagant habits of the people. It is, of course, true that there was a large number of people who were extravagant and who committed themselves for expenditures for consumable goods in excess of their incomes. However, the total of such over-commitments up to the fall of 1929 on the part of those who were extravagant was largely overbalanced by the total unexpended savings of those who were thrifty. In order to keep up the rate of expenditure and income, we need a spendthrift to balance every miser.

The thought that the savings of the people can be excessive is contrary to the traditional notion that the greater the savings the greater will be the prosperity of the people. Surely the prosperity of the individual depends largely on his thrift. Two or three generations ago when individual proprietorship predominated, as it does today in France, those who could and did save were in direct contact with enterprises which needed additional capital for extensions and improvements. The small factory, the small shopkeeper or farmer had a better chance for existence in those days, for he did not have the competition of large corporations with their labor-saving machinery and efficient distribution methods. Savers generally directed expenditure of their savings in their own enterprises whereas today the direction of savings is in the [17]

hands of a comparatively small proportion of the people.

OVERPRODUCTION VS. INADEQUATE SAVINGS 

The opinion is frequently expressed that this depression is due to overproduction, implying that we have a surplus of production capacity. 

Is it not desirable that we have surplus capacity in order to maintain competition, lower costs and prices, which results in increasing the real wages of labor, thereby increasing purchasing power and raising the general standard of living?

Surplus of production capacity and surplus of office buildings, apartments, homes, etc., provide for an automatic adjustment of relative values of consumable goods and services. 

Idle and useless factories, to be sure, represent economic waste, but such waste is negligible to expenditures of billions of dollars for goods and services such as in the bootlegging trade, which results in no lasting economic benefit to the people. Only by a surplus of capital producing capacity may we be insured against commodity price inflation. Only by providing for such capital producing capacity through adequate savings can we be insured against credit inflation. When demand for commodities exceeds the capacity of our capital facilities, due to insufficiency of savings, or restrictions in bank credit extension, it logically follows that we would have commodity price inflation. [18]

BUSINESS CYCLES AND SAVINGS 

Inflation may result from other causes than lack of balance between total savings and total capital expenditures. In the inflation of 1919, when market demands exceeded our capacity to produce, it was shortage of labor rather than inadequate capital facilities which limited supply. Political disturbances or Acts of God, such as floods, famine, pestilence, etc., may seriously disturb business.

The main contention made herein is that provided we start with a prosperous condition of business and employment and provided all other factors were favorable for continuous stable business, we could not expect to be free from inflationary tendencies unless in some uncanny way the total of the savings of the people plus the amount of public debt retired equaled the amount expended for capital enterprises. 

An excess of savings directed into speculative channels will produce inflation of capital or security values, and insufficiency of savings will lead to inflation in commodity prices and in credit extension.

FOREIGN TRADE 

Foreign trade is, of course, an important factor to be reckoned with under every condition of business in this country. Prior to the collapse in stock market prices in 1929, foreigners had invested in our securities, but their investments [19]

with us were exceeded by our investments abroad. Had this country been self contained in its business without imports or exports of capital, our surplus of savings would have been even greater.

The continuation of our operation in foreign trade with net credit balances can only be maintained in the long run by offsetting these credit balances through investments in foreign securities. With the large indebtedness of foreigners to us, with tariff barriers excluding their shipments of goods to us, with their depleted gold reserves and with foreign business conditions so unattractive for investment of capital, it is clear that the return of prosperity in the near future years cannot depend on much benefit from foreign trade. Cancellation or adjustments of war debts would entirely change the situation.

BANKING POLICY AND THE BUSINESS CYCLE 

Banking policy in respect to its control of current interest rates has little to do with the initial causes of business cycles. Purchase commitments for consumable goods generally based on current or prospective income are not concerned with interest rates. When savings are adequate to meet needs for capital, banks are not called upon to over-extend their credit. 

When savings are inadequate for capital needs the credit inflation which generally ensues is a [20]

resultant rather than a casual factor. Under the latter condition bankers are in a technical position where they can exercise positive control or checks against further inflation by strictly limiting their credit extension. However, when savings are excessive, as they were in 1929, high interest rates have little effect in checking security inflation. Bankers, however, have means of checking security inflation in their function of appraising values of collaterals. Assume that bankers generally had been so far-seeing and wise in 1927 and later years that they had limited values of securities for collateral loan purposes to the basis of the preceding five-year averages, the inflation of stocks would surely not have gone anywhere near the height it did.

Bankers would not have been forced to liquidate to the extent they did. Bank failures would have been minimized. Business recession would have been a comparatively minor affair. When deflation movements start bankers are induced to liquidate their loans and investments. Disbursement of funds for retiring bank debts takes just so much away from the total current income which further tends to reduce expenditures. It is the reduction rather than the increase of brokers' loans which absorbs "money" or purchasing powers.

The banker's function of appraising values on which he extends his credit has become of much more importance than the control of the [21]

amount of bank credit. Bankers should assume their inherent responsibility as arbiters of values rather than allow themselves to be at the mercy of the unstable moods of speculators. 

The most serious factor tending to retard our recovery today lies in the persistent decline of security values. Bankers are forced to demand additional collateral or liquidation of loans. This results in forced sales of stocks, which further depresses values. When a single bank takes steps to improve its own liquidity, the result is an impairment of the portfolios of other banks. Weaker banks have to close their doors and fear spreads to depositors of other banks, who start hoarding. This cycle process is the most vicious of any in the deflation movement, for it endangers a collapse of our credit system. Such a disaster would bring about almost complete stagnation in trade.

It is most urgent that immediate steps be taken to check further deflation of stocks. In past times, such situations have been met by closing the stock exchanges. With conditions obtaining today, such action would be only of temporary relief. It would not restore confidence in our banks.

If by some means bankers could be relieved of the necessity of forcing liquidation and lower values, prospective buyers might right the situation, inasmuch as the consensus of opinion seems to be that stock values are extremely low, [22]

if it were not for the adverse technical factors such as cited above. It must be recognized that when banks take action to put themselves in an abnormal liquid condition by accumulating large excess reserves, they virtually hoard cash. 

This forces other banks to follow suit and it indirectly induces hoarding on the part of individuals.

It would seem in view of all the conditions that bankers would be justified in reducing margin requirements on new loans and in declaring a moratorium on existing loans. If all bankers throughout the country would cooperate along this line, stock market prices would undoubtedly advance. This would mean a reversal in the cycle process as far as it relates to the condition of our credit and banking system.

Our banks are confronted with a serious problem in the fact that a large proportion of the billions of dollars they have in mortgages is "frozen." It has been suggested as an emergency measure that the Federal Reserve re-discount privileges be broadened so that the member banks can re-discount other sound assets than those now classified as eligible for re-discount. There seems no good reason why this suggestion should not be put into effect. 

FAVORABLE AND UNFAVORABLE INFLUENCES 

If the foregoing diagnosis of the causes of our depression is true, then, as stated above, im- [23]

proved conditions of business and employment can only come about through increase in total purchase commitments over total current income. Influences working toward this end are deficits of the Federal Government and of municipalities and of many corporations, and probably many individuals are spending more than their income. However, these over-expenditures in the aggregate seem to be more than balanced by the total savings of the people. The declining rate of business is proof of this. Savings to a large extent are directed to the retirement of bank indebtedness or to accumulations in savings bank. Our savings banks are not investing at the rate that their deposits are increasing. Hoarding of currency is also going on at an alarming rate.

To make matters worse for the future, governmental agencies are economizing in their expenditures. It is proposed that taxes be increased, which means further withdrawal of purchasing power from the people. It is proposed to raise funds to take care of the unemployed for the coming winter. The amount that is contributed to such funds represents a sacrifice on the part of donors who reduce their current expenditures, thereby throwing more people out of employment for dependence on charity or dole.

Our system of income taxes which produces surplus revenue in prosperous years and a deficit [24]

in depression years automatically applies inflationary and deflationary processes at the proper times.

The financial policies of the government, corporations and individuals can all be classed either as effecting inflationary or deflationary influences. Inflationary reactions follow when expenditures are greater than income and deflationary reactions follow when expenditures are less than income. There are times when it is just as important to encourage inflationary processes as there are times when it is necessary to apply deflationary processes. There is no justification for the widely prevailing notion that anything which tends to induce inflation should be discouraged at all times. The adoption of the most radical inflationary program today would result in only a slightly increased price level due to the elimination of "distress prices," but nothing approaching inflation would ensue until demand increased approximately to production capacity. When we approached such a condition a vigorous deflationary program should be adopted.

GOVERNMENTAL EXPENDITUREs as a REMEDY 

If from the aggregate of all economic units which include individuals, corporations and governmental agencies, the unexpended savings of those who save exceed the over-expenditures of those who are not balancing their budgets, [25]

then the problem of increasing business and employment resolves itself into the adequate expenditure of current savings in capital enterprises (obviously savings are not savings if expended for consumable goods). Under present conditions, little inducement exists for expenditures in private enterprises. Manufacturing plants, rental buildings, hotels, theaters, etc., are more than adequate to meet demands for some time ahead. To be sure all people desire better homes and furnishings, but as a whole they are not in financial condition today to satisfy such wants. Not until there is an actual scarcity of housing, inducing higher rentals, will capital be attracted to this field of investment. Depreciation and obsolescence of homes as well as of factories, buildings, etc., is a continuing process which in time forces demand for replacement. The rate of depreciation must exceed the rate of accumulated unexpended savings before total expenditures exceed total current income which is requisite for increasing business and employment.

It is not possible to estimate when replacement demand will develop to proportions which will start the trend of business upward, but thoughtful observation of our present conditions gives little hope for relief from this natural process for some time in the future. Natural economic forces, if given full play would eventually bring about a return of prosperity, but [26]

our difficulties today seem to lie in the time element. A continuation of declining business and employment for even a year or so more would undoubtedly force us into the dole system. It would be extremely difficult to extricate ourselves from this curse when once established. Continued lowering of values in securities may undermine our whole credit system. It would take years to recover from such a disaster. 

Large expenditures on the part of governmental agencies appears to be the only available means of relief for averting worse conditions than we are suffering from today. The most authoritative studies of the question of business cycles have resulted in recommending governmental expenditures in combination with appropriate fiscal policies as a cure for such a depression period as the present. 

It is, of course, difficult to determine to what extent governmental expenditures are necessary to get us back into our stride. It is estimated that we are running twenty-five billion dollars a year short of what might be considered normal production if labor were fully employed. Our monthly deficiency in production would therefore be $2,000,000,000. Expenditures on the part of governmental agencies at a much smaller rate than the above could probably be so directed that a steady improvement in trade would result. A distinct trend toward [27]

improvement would unquestionably reverse the general state of mind from pessimism to optimism, which is requisite to returning prosperity. Expectancy of increased income results in releasing current purchase commitments which actually increase income. 

It is extremely important that release of purchase contracts by governmental agencies be progressively made and so timed that no recession of trade will occur, for that would nullify the initial benefits. Reversal of the public state of mind again to pessimism would be fatal. In view of the great uncertainties the nation will face for several years, and the time needed for wisely planning such expenditures, it would seem wise to err on the side of extravagance in adopting a schedule of public works so that adequate purchase commitments may be released as occasion demands.

GOVERNMENTAL FISCAL POLICIES 

In advocating large expenditures on the part of governmental agencies, it is presumed that such expenditures would be derived from borrowed funds and not balanced by current taxes, for by the amount taxes are increased the purchasing power of the people declines. It has been stated above that the deficit of the government is a favorable factor and it is recommended here that the government does not attempt to balance its budget in the face of present [28]

conditions. All this sounds extremely radical.

The measures suggested are both artificial and inflationary.

There appears to be considerable opposition to the adoption of any artificial measures to stimulate business. The operations of the Farm 

Board are cited as a flagrant example of the futility of combating natural economic forces. 

On the other hand, it should be borne in mind that we have been operating for a number of years under artificial restrictions to individual profit incentive and competition. Railroad rates and wages have been under government control. Our Federal Reserve Banks, through legal restrictions in distribution of profits to stockholding banks, are governed with little attention to profit possibilities. They, therefore, exercise an arbitrary control of the money market. Many industries through trade associations apply restrictive influences against individual members for the benefit of their industries as a whole. Freedom of individual initiative has been the foundation of our economic progress and undoubtedly should be restricted as little as possible but when "mob rule" under our competitive system makes such a mess of things, it would seem reasonable to adopt sound artificial measures provided they were effective in correcting or counteracting harmful practices. [29]

No governmental agency, not even our Federal Government, could continue for long on such a policy as outlined above without facing bankruptcy. On the other hand the credit of the government is no stronger than its taxing power, which inherently rests upon the production and earning power of the people, individually and corporately. Probably most business men today would willingly pay double the taxes they paid in 1929, if the volume and profits of their business paralleled the condition at that time.

An increase in the indebtedness of the government even to a large amount might be considered a good investment, if it resulted in increasing the production of the country at a rate of twenty-five billion dollars a year, for obviously it is this marginal production of twentyfive billion that provides the most favorable basis for assessing needed increased taxes. It would be poor business policy on the part of any corporation to take any action which impairs the financial standing of the dealers and agencies on whom it is so dependent for distribution and income. By the same token, it is poor business policy on the part of our government to take any action which impairs the financial standing of its people, on whom it is so dependent for its income. [30]

EXTENT OF GOVERNMENTAL ACTION 

There can be no definite formula set as to the extent expenditures and finances of the government should be regulated. Improvement in business and employment requires that expenditures for capital enterprise and public works exceed the unexpended savings of the people. 

The more the expenditures, the more the gain in business, and the earlier the return of confidence and optimism. Too little emphasis has been given above to the public state of mind as the most powerful factor determining the rate of business. General confidence and optimism at times will prevail against serious adverse technical factors in trade and industry, just as in the stock market. If we can restore general optimism, manifesting itself in inflationary influences and actions, it will of itself work out the situation for us. [31]
