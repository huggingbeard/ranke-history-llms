PREFACE 

VERY American shares with his fellow citizens the ownership of much land in what is known as the District of Columbia. Of course, the District has many private landowners within its territory, but even these have no more actual control, in fact, they have less, than the millions of Americans who live in the various sovereign states. The matter is simple the Capital City of the United States is a federal territory, managed by the Federal Government. This situation has caused many authors, in writing about Washington, D. C., to emphasize the federal relationship at the expense of local history. However, there are some excellent books about our local history, but even these often fail to look upon Washington as a community in its own right.

This book has been written with the hope that it will acquaint the uninitiated citizen of our

PREFACE 

country with enough information about the City of Washington to cause him to realize that life, personal as well as official, has existed and today is very much existing in our Capital City. Another purpose is to demonstrate to the residents of the city itself what a great number of historic places we have here, in addition to those places usually pointed out by the professional guides. 

In recent years much interest has been aroused in American History, local and national, and 

RAMBLING THROUGH WASHINGTON is offered as an answer to many questions about Washington, and an awakener to those who would further pursue the study of Washington History. 

For as long as the author can remember, he has had an interest in Washington, and from time to time he has run across interesting facts and stories about the city and its inhabitants. It is his purpose here to relate some of them. Much effort has been spent in obtaining verification of what is told, and many anecdotes have had to be omitted because they had no background which could be substantiated by facts. Because the number of places to mention ran into the hun dreds, it was necessary to devise some logical plan for presenting the information, and, as a final solution, the trip method has been used. By this is meant a more or less connected series of sketches, pointing out places lying near together or along well-defined routes.

No attempt has been made to write about that

CHAPTER I. 

THE CAPITOL BUILDING AND GROUNDS The Capitol of the United States of America is a beautiful white building situated about a mile east of the geometric center of the original "Ten Miles Square" donated by Maryland and

Virginia for the Capital City of the Nation. The building itself is accurately oriented; that is, it faces due east, and its four sides are located according to the points of the compass. 

After Congress, in the early years of the government under the present Constitution, decided to build a brand new capital city instead of utilizing one of the several available cities offered for the purpose, it was found necessary to plan an entire city, with streets, public buildings, spaces for private residences, and all of the other component parts which make up a metropolis. 

Surveys were made, the first corner boundary stone was officially placed with appropriate 

Masonic ceremonies, and the Federal District 15

began to be a reality instead of a mere idea. An eminent French engineer, late of the Continental 

Army, Major Pierre Charles L'Enfant, was given the task of planning the city, and soon Congress realized that the United States of America would eventually have a permanent capital city. Skipping, for the time being, all of the details of the acquisition of land, assignment of reservations for particular public and private needs, etc., let us return to the story of the Capitol Building. 

In March, 1792, the three Commissioners ap pointed by President George Washington - Gen eral Thomas Johnson, Dr. David Stuart, and 

Daniel Carroll-advertised for competitive plans for the Capitol and the President's House. Three months' time was given for the contest, and a prize of five hundred dollars cash and a building lot in the new city was offered to the winner of each competition.

Of the numerous designs submitted for the Capitol, those of Stephen L. Hallet, an architect who was said to have studied under the famous 

English architect Nash, were the most promising.

The Commissioners did not approve of Hallet's plans as first submitted, but asked the designer to make revised drawings. After Hallet had set to work on his new plans, and after the competition had officially closed, another aspirant for the prize appeared. He was Dr. William Thornton, a native of the British West Indies and a physician, who was interested in architecture as a

THE CAPITOL 

17 side line. He later became the first Commissioner of Patents, and a noted inventor as well. One of his inventions, the use of which was not commercialized for many years, was a board made of sawdust, properly mixed with an adhesive. Undoubtedly, this was an ancester of our modern wall boards. Dr. Thornton was also an ardent admirer of horse flesh, and established the first race track in Washington. In spite of his late entry in the contest, Thornton won the prize, but

Hallet was given an equal reward in consideration of the extra efforts he had put forth. 

Dr. Thornton was not well enough acquainted with architecture as a profession to be able to make all of the necessary specifications, estimates, etc. for the new Capitol, so Hallet was employed to do this part of the work. The latter found fault with the plans of Thornton almost immediately, and insisted on changing them to conform with his own ideas. In spite of all of this friction, the Capitol was started in August, 1793. 

George Washington, as Worshipful Master of 

Alexandria Lodge, F. A. A. M., laid the cornerstone. He was assisted by the brethren and officers of his lodge and those of the lodge in Georgetown. James Hoban, who had won the contest for the design of the President's House, was soon made Supervising Architect in charge of the actual work on the Capitol, and Dr. Thornton was given more time to spend on his work in another official capacity. It is interesting to note that

Hallet's salary as assistant was four hundred pounds per year, equivalent to about two thousand dollars. By the end of a year Hallet was dismissed because he would not build the Capitol as directed-he continually attempted to revert to his own plan, and to discredit the work of 

Dr. Thornton. 

George Hadfield, an English architect, succeeded Hallet in 1794, but he in turn quarreled with the Commissioners, and was in 1796 succeeded by Dr. Thornton, who had made the original accepted plans.

To go any farther into the early history of the 

Capitol would be too tiresome, so let us content ourselves with a list of some of the later architects whose skill is responsible for the building that we all know: 1803-1817, Benjamin Henry 

Latrobe; 1818-1830, Charles Bulfinch; 1851-1865, Thomas U. Walter.

The original Capitol consisted of two separate rectangular buildings, which were later connected by a central portion covered by a low, squat dome sheathed with copper. The British burned these buildings when they visited Washington in 1814.

So great was the damage, especially to the interior, that a complete reconstruction was necessary. After the Capitol was restored, along the previous lines, it remained practically unchanged until the administration of President Fillmore, when the House and Senate wings were planned.

Not until the Civil War was the present iron dome completed; it was designed by Thomas U. 

Walter. This huge dome, one hundred thirty-five feet in diameter, reaches a height of some two hundred eighty-seven feet above the base of the Capitol. Visitors are permitted to climb to the top of the dome. President Lincoln thought of the Capitol as the symbol of Union, and he therefore ordered workmen to complete the dome-the wings were already finishedduring the trying days of the Civil War. While the iron workers were assembling the eight million nine hundred thousand pounds of of iron that make up the dome, other workers were busily baking bread in the basement of the building. 

At the outbreak of the Civil War, soldiers from out of town, who had rushed here by train at 

President Lincoln's call for volunteers, were quartered in and about the edifice-some even slept in the legislative chambers, it is said. 

To describe the exterior of the Capitol Build ing would not be worthwhile here, first, because the Capitol is the best known structure in America, and second, because only a very detailed description of all of the architectural features, such as columns, pediments, pilasters, porticoes, etc. could give a complete idea of the detail that has been worked out in this huge building. Huge, yes. 

The entire length is seven hundred fifty-one feet, and the greatest breadth is three hundred fifty

feet. Altogether, the building covers more than thirteen and one half acres.

Every American who has the opportunity to visit Washington should, of course, go to the

Capitol. The usual entrance for visitors is the main one, facing east. At the top of the steps which rise from the plaza on the east, a symbolic figure of Columbus holds a miniature globe. 

Formerly, an immense statue of George Washington, dressed in classic Roman toga, and seated on a huge stone throne, stood out on the plaza, facing the main entrance. Many people were amused by the coincidence that Christopher 

Columbus and George Washington, with uplifted arm, appeared to be playing ball. This was not the only criticism directed at the unmilitary-looking statue of the Father of His Country; many thought that the famous Virginian was not appropriately attired, especially for a great general. 

He was taken from the Capitol plaza years ago, and now sits uncriticized in the Smithsonian Institute. The base of the statue, a fine piece of stone, was used as the cornerstone of the Capitol 

Power Plant, on New Jersey Avenue, Southeast, but is now so covered with vines that it is hidden from view. 

All of the cast bronze doors of the Capitol 

Building are historically interesting, but no amount of description here will do them the justice that a personal inspection will. Passing through the Rogers bronze doors of the east

In this room are many historic paintings, busts, frescoes, etc. This is not an attempt to describe what a large volume should do, so we will not stop to examine and admire the many art works of the Capitol Rotunda. The Rotunda is ordinarily used only as an entrance hall, and for the display of the above-mentioned art works, but at times this circular room is used for very solemn and impressive rites-those for the honored dead of the Nation. With few exceptions, only the very great of the country have lain in state in the Rotunda; among those so honored were President Abraham Lincoln, Congressman Thaddeus 

Stevens, President James Garfield, Vice President Logan, President William McKinley, Major

War, President Warren G. Harding, Ex-Presi dent Woodrow Wilson, and Ex-President William

The black bier on which these historic remains lay in state in the Rotunda was constructed in 1865 to hold the coffin of Lincoln, and has been kept in the basement of the Capitol ever since, except when needed.

Directly over the center of the Rotunda, and surmounting the dome, is a nineteen foot bronze

statue of Freedom, which was erected in 1863. 

Crawford, the sculptor, originally planned a re presentation of Miss Freedom wearing a Liberty 

Cap, but the then Secretary of War, Jefferson

Davis, objected on the ground that such a headpiece was indicative of slavery in the past, so the statue was given a hat of feathers, emblematic of the freedom of the Indian warriors. 

The Supreme Court of the United States meets in a semicircular room on the main floor of the Capitol, just north of the Rotunda, and facing east. This is the Senate wing of the original 

Capitol, and the present Supreme Court Chamber itself was used as the meeting place of the Senate from 1800-1859. It is truly a beautiful room. 

The original gallery has been removed, but the general layout has not been altered much, except to provide a dais and bar. Around the room is a row of marble busts-likenesses of the former 

Chief Justices. 

In this room President Jefferson was twice administered the oath of office by Chief Justice

John Marshall, and here the Senate confirmed that memorable treaty with Napoleon Bonaparte which transferred the title to the Louisiana Territory to the United States. The much-discussed 

Monroe Doctrine was in this hall presented to 

Congress by President Monroe in 1823. While still the Senate Chamber, this room echoed the speeches of such orators as Daniel Webster,

By 1859 the new Senate Chamber was ready for occupancy, and the old room was therefore vacated. The next year the Supreme Court moved in, and has met there ever since. One of the most famous events which has occured there since the Civil War was the meeting of the Electoral Commission, which decided that Hayes should be declared President instead of Tilden, who apparently had a very justifiable claim to the disputed electoral votes.

The modern Senate Chamber, much larger than the old one, is at the north end of the Capitol Building. Many historic scenes have been enacted in this room also, but we will not attempt to describe them. The visitor should note the lack of paintings and other art works which are conspicuously absent in this room; the Senate itself has permitted only a few marble busts to be displayed in its home, lest the dignity of the place be marred.

The original House of Representatives Cham ber was called The Oven, both because of its shape, and its stuffy atmosphere in hot weather. 

It was early replaced by the present Statuary Hall, where the House met until 1857. This room, which is south of the Rotunda, is just as full of historic associations as the old Senate Chamber, and, like the latter, it still looks about the same as it did when used for legislative purposes. The

floor has been raised and made level with the main floor of the building, and the desks have been removed, but the architectural features remain unchanged. One of the best-known decorative clocks in the world is in this room; it is the beautifully carved time-piece representing The Car of History. In a winged chariot stands the figure of a maiden symbolizing the Genius of History.

The wheel of the chariot forms the face of the clock.

In this hall Chief Justice Marshall administered the oath of office to presidents Madison and Monroe, and here Lincoln, John Quincy 

Adams, Horace Greeley, and Andrew Johnson sat in the same Congress. Lafayette received his official welcome here from Henry Clay, who is also said to have penned Lafayette's reply.

After the House had moved to its new quarters, each state was invited by Congress to place in the old room two statues of favorite sons. Statues of sons, and daughters, too, have been erected here by the dozen, until today the hall is so crowded that it looks more like a salesroom for monuments than the Shrine of National Heroes. It is hoped that before long Congress will provide more adequate space for the ever-growing group of representative Americans from all states. 

The new House of Representatives Chamber, which is large, but not too large, is richly ornamented with paintings and other art objects, in sharp contrast with the plain Senate Hall. Never-

It is in the House Chamber, at the right of the Speaker's chair, that the Mace is kept. This ancient symbol of authority is carried by the 

Sergeant of Arms of the House whenever he carries out the orders of the Speaker. When the 

House is in session, the Mace is placed in a marble pedestal. Older than the room where it reposes, the Mace is more than a bundle of black rods bound in silver; it is the symbol of legislative power and of union.

The most interesting place in the basement of the Capitol is the crypt, a circular room directly under the Rotunda. A star in the floor marks the exact center of the building. Beneath the floor of the crypt, in the sub-basement, rests the black bier constructed to hold the coffin of Abraham Lincoln, when the remains of the Great 

Emancipator were laid in state in the Rotunda.

This location was intended to be the site of the tomb of George Washington, but Mrs. Washington preferred to let her husband's ashes stay on the estate he loved so well.

From the basement of the Senate wing of the Capitol a winding tunnel cuts under the plaza to the basement of the Senate Office Building, several hundred yards away, at the corner of Delaware Avenue and B Street. A queer little mono-

rail car, the delight of visitors, carries Senators and the general public through this miniature subway. A similar passage has been cut to the 

House Office Building, but as yet it has not been provided with a car. Two other tunnels enter the basement of the Capitol, but they are not open to visitors. One is a small passageway which goes to the Library of Congress. A basket-like vehicle carries books back and forth for the use of Members of Congress. The fourth tunnel brings the heat and electric power for the Capitol from the Powerhouse, located some distance away, down on New Jersey Avenue.

The hill on which the Capitol Building is situated was the home of one Mr. Jenkins in the seventeenth century, and from him got the name Jenkins Hill. Before the land was acquired by this man, it was owned by Francis Pope, and by the latter the lazy creek at the foot of the hill was called Tiber Creek. It is said that neighboring farmers chided Mr. Jenkins about being a 

Pope of Rome, with the old Tiber nearby. Through the years of Washington history, the names of Tiber and Goose Creek, an older name, have been used interchangeably, although the name Jenkins Hill has long since disappeared. Where the central portion of the Capitol now stands there used to be two old wells, which for many years supplied water to the families living in the vicinity. 

Today the Capitol Grounds are very beautiful,

Numerous landscape gardeners and architects have from time to time improved the lawns, until now they are very attractive, and are dotted with lovely trees and shrubs from all over the world. Particularly charming are the west front of the Capitol, and the long walk leading from the building downhill to Pennsylvania Avenue. On the sidewalk stands an imposing statue of Chief Justice John Marshall. It is of heroic size, and was erected in 1884. 

An old landmark which for many years stood at the west front of the Capitol is the Tripoli Monument, now at the U. S. Naval Academy, at 

Annapolis. It was brought to America in 1808 on the U. S. S. Constitution, which sailed right up the Potomac and the Anacostia Rivers to the 

Navy Yard. The Tripoli Monument remained at the Navy Yard until after the War of 1812, when it was placed on Capitol Hill, there to stand until 

1860. Northwest of the Capitol, a few feet from the road, stands a little brick grotto, in which cooling waters trickle. The grotto is not yet a century old, but the green vines that cover it make it appear to be a relic of the middle ages. 

Today the street cars from Pennsylvania Avenue pass around Capitol Hill and go up B Street with no apparent effort, but fifty years ago, before the day of the electric trolley cars, the horse-drawn cars had a hard time making the grade, especially when loaded. So steep was the grade that extra "hill horses" had to be kept

waiting at the bottom of the hill to help pull the cars to the top. These "hill horses", having helped the tiny cars up, were ridden down again by boys, to wait for the next car.

There are still numerous antiquated, decorated lamp posts arond the Capitol plaza and grounds, but they do not give sufficient light to satisfy the twentieth century demand for good illumination.

On each of the two wings of the Capitol are large banks of searchlights which shine upon the dome at night, making the Capitol visible from distant parts of the city, and to the numerous aviators who cross the city during the night. Other searchlights are located on the tops of lamp posts around the plaza, and illuminate those portions of the dome not reached by the great banks of searchlights. 

The plaza itself is a rather famous old place, not so rich in historic associations, to be sure, as some of the squares in foreign capitals, but boasting an abundance of historic scenes in the past hundred and thirty years of American History.

At inauguration times a wooden platform, during recent years covered with a canopy made to match the facade of the Capitol, is erected immediately in front of the main entrance. Citizens from all over the country stand in the plaza to witness the historic ceremony performed when the Chief Justice administers the oath of office to the new 

President of the United States. Here the inaugural parade starts, too. On pleasant summer

THE CAPITOL 

29 evenings the Marine Band often gives concerts in front of the Capitol. Other more spectacular but less historic events have also occurred on the plaza. "General" Coxey's army here assembled prior to its unsuccessful attempt to gain admission to the Halls of Congress. Not very long ago one of our most air-minded legislators, Senator Hiram Bingham, of Connecticut, was a passenger in a tiny army dirigible which landed on the Plaza. When the old high-wheel bicycles were the rage, back in the gay nineties, numerous attempts, some of them successful, were made to ride down the steep steps of the east front of the

Capitol. 

We have devoted relatively little space to the most important building in the United States, but the visitor should realize that it will take him many hours to roam about the Capitol seeing all of the sights. The best plan is to arrive a little before noon on a day when the Supreme Court and both houses are in session. Just at noon it is very awe-inspiring to watch the Justices file into their seats in the Supreme Court 

Chamber, and to hear the age-old cry of the

Bailiff as court opens. A few minutes spent in each of the Congressional galleries will give the visitor a sample of parliamentary procedure. 

Thus, with apologies for the brevity of this chapter, we will continue to the White House, by way of historic Pennsylvania Avenue.

CHAPTER II. 

PENNSYLVANIA AVENUE, FROM THE CAPITOL TO THE TREASURY 

The one mile stretch of Pennsylvania Avenue between the Capitol and the Treasury is one of the most historic thoroughfares in the world. 

Its intimate connection with inaugural parades of a century and a quarter makes it well known to all Americans. Some of these parades have been very pompous affairs-others have been ex-tremely simple, but all of them have marked important phases in United States history. Military reviews, particularly those following the 

Civil and World Wars, have turned Pennsylvania

Avenue into a great parade ground, resounding to the steps of hundreds of thousands of veterans. 

Many of these same veterans have also marched along Pennsylvania Avenue to the beat of muffled drums; for example, during the funeral processions of our martyred presidents, and of the Unknown Soldier of the World War, whose body was placed to rest in beautiful Arlington Cem- 31

etery during the administration of President 

Harding. 

It is very appropriate, therefore, that the first monument on Pennsylvania Avenue, going westward from the Capitol, should be the Peace Monument. Located at the foot of Capitol Hill where 

First Street crosses the Avenue, this monument is really a memorial to the brave sailors and marines who fought in defence of their country from 1861-65. Admiral David Farragut, naval hero of the War between the States, made the preliminary sketch of this monument, which was erected by Congress in 1878. 

For a decade after the Civil War, railroad tracks were to be seen on First Street, where they had been laid as a war measure, but Governor Alexander Shepherd, in his valiant attempt to beautify the Capital City, had the obnoxious tracks removed over the protests of the railroad authorities. Washington's first railroad "depot", the old Baltimore and Ohio Railroad Station, had been opened on the north side of the Avenue at Second Street with appropriate military band music and "customary banquet”, in the fall of 1835. Hungerford, in his History of the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad, quotes an early passenger as follows: ...

The conductors are active, watchful, and accommodating, and the cars, with their broad seats, with high, stuffed, well-inclined backs, are the most commodious we have ever traveled in, and we have been in a good many.

The corner of the Avenue and Second Street is not what anyone would call beautiful, but none can deny that it is historic. Here the old Tiber 

Creek formerly crossed, and made unsanitary marsh lands for civic leaders to complain about. 

The Tiber was at first crossed by means of a ford, but high water so often made the road impassable that a wooden bridge was erected in the very early days of the city's history. Today the ancient Tiber, its bed laid with brick, passes unseen through a huge tunnel on its way to the Potomac. No doubt the rich soil along the banks of this once navigable stream has made possible the luxuriant growth of trees and plants in the Botanic Garden on the south side of the Avenue, between First and Second Streets. The lover of 

Nature can, in this reservation, find many rare flowers to delight him, and as many oddities to arouse his curiosity, but we shall leave a description of the Botanic Garden and its statuary

and plants for another chapter. I wonder how many of the thousands of truck drivers who daily come up to the business section by way of Second Street know that they are riding over the top of the creek that was the highway for barges which in the 1790's brought stone from Aquia Creek, down the Potomac near George Washington's birthplace, for the beautiful Capitol of a new nation.

At the corner of Third Street are three ancient buildings which were once the stopping places of the elite, Gadsby's (the third of that name),

Made's, and the New Capitol. The last named was for over a hundred years a famous hostelry -today it is a sad sight, awaiting the wrecking crew which will soon destroy it to make room for a part of the new municipal center development. 

The New Capitol Hotel, first known as the St.

Charles, was erected between 1813 and 1819, on the northeast corner. The gray marble pillars and cornice ornamenting the Third Street entrance are particularly interesting, because they were once part of the Capitol Building, having been removed when the damage done by the 

British firebrands in 1814 was repaired. Under the sidewalk along the west side are the famous slave pens which have been pointed out to sightseers for generations. Soon they too will be but another memory of an age that has passed. The hotel may look shabby now, but it was considered wonderful a century ago, when it was a favorite

San Francisco and New York have Chinatowns that keep in the headlines of world newspapers because of horrible tong wars and narcotic scandals; Washington has had her Chinatown, also, but it was a very peaceful community, even compared to some of the other foreign settlements which lay in the shadow of the Capitol Building.

The several hundred Chinese who until 1931 lived along Pennsylvania Avenue are aligned into two rival organizations, the On Leon Tong, and the less powerful and influential Hip Sing Tong, but the local "clubs" have lived in comparative peace and quiet for many years, in spite of the fact that the two leaders were, at a time not so long ago, the only two private citizens of the District of Columbia legally permitted to carry pistols "for self-defence". In the past the Chinese settlement has had not a few colorful Oriental New Year celebrations, made picturesque by lanterns, and punctuated by the reports of countless packages of firecrackers. In at least one respect the Washington Chinese have adopted an 

Occidental custom-they fraternize in their own 

Masonic gatherings. But the municipal center project is fast becoming a reality, and already the Chinese have moved from this section of Pennsylvania Avenue. Just where they will permanently relocate has not been determined.

Second and Third Streets, is an old landmark familiar to many old timers-Cornwall's Book 

Store. Collectors still go there to make purchases and to browse among records of the past, but the average Washingtonian seldom goes to this neighborhood to do his shopping.

CHAPTER III. 

 AND NEARBY DEPARTMENTAL BUILDINGS The Executive Mansion, or White House, as it is better known, was the first government building to be completed in Washington. President

Washington is said to have selected the site, which is about a mile west of the Capitol. When first built, the White House had very spacious grounds, extending from Fifteenth to Seventeenth Streets and including the ground now called Lafayette Square. This last-named park was divorced from the White House Grounds when Pennsylvania Avenue was cut through, more than a century ago, and the squares to the east and west of the Executive Mansion early became the sites for the Treasury, War, State, and Navy Departments.

In 1792 the Three Commissioners of the District advertised for plans for the Capitol and President's House. Dr. Thornton, it will be remember- 55

ed, won the prize of five hundred dollars and a building lot for the best plans for the Capitol. 

The successful competitor for the President's 

House drawings was James Hoban, an Irishman by birth, who had lived in South Carolina, where he designed the State Capitol. Hoban's plans for the White House were dignified and pleasing, and he well earned the five hundred dollars cash and building lot awarded him for his plans. 

Hoban liked this city, and made it his home for the last forty years of his life; he died in 

1833. He was active in all sorts of affairs besides architectural work. "Captain Hoban", for he was the captain of a local artillery company, was the first Master of Federal Lodge, and was also for many years its Treasurer.

Architect James Hoban was appointed to rebuild the Mansion after the British had burned it, in 1814. Contrary to popular opinion, the 

Executive Mansion was known as the White

House before the restoration; it has always been painted white, probably because of the soft texture of the sandstone of which the walls are constructed. Another structure designed by 

Hoban in Washington was Blodgett's Hotel, at 

Eighth and E Streets, where Congress met following Admiral Cockburn's destruction of their Halls.

The cornerstone of the Executive Mansion was laid with Masonic ceremonies on the three hundreth anniversary of the landing of Columbus,

October 13, 1792. George and Martha Washing ton are said to have inspected the White House in 1799, the year of the General's death, and a year before the United States Government officially moved to this city.

John Adams was the first President to occupy the White House, which has been the home of all of the Chief Executives since 1800. The building was by no means ready for occupancy when the 

Adams family moved from Philadelphia, as attested by the fact that Abigail Adams used the huge "audience room" for drying clothes.

The general outline of the Executive Mansion remains today as designed by Hoban, but it has not always looked as it does. The rectangular north portico was not erected until 1823, and the more beautiful south one, until 1829, although both of them had been planned by Hoban. The

White House formerly appeared taller than it does now because the front yard was quite a few feet lower than the floor of the front portico, which today is just a little above the driveway. 

Originally there were terraces on the east and west sides, but these were covered up years ago, only to be rediscovered during the restoration of the White House in 1902. At that time President 

Roosevelt had the wooden interior framing of the building replaced with steel, and caused to be added the Executive Offices that now form the west wing, and the visitor's entrance that forms the east wing. More recently, while President

Coolidge was in office, a new fireproof roof was constructed, and the old pine rafters cut up and sold for souvenirs.

The front lawn of the White House is beautifully landscaped, and is dotted with historic trees, many of which have been planted by Presidents and their wives. A bronze statue of President 

Jefferson, which was presented to the United

States by Lieutenant Uriah P. Levy, U. S. N., a member of the Jefferson family, formerly stood on the front lawn, near the corner of Pennsyl vania Avenue and East Executive Avenue. Be fore being erected on the White House lawn, the Jefferson statue was displayed in the Rotunda of the Capitol. Some fifty years ago, however, it was sent back to the Capitol Building, where for a while it kept company with the silent throng of heroes in Statuary Hall, but now it again stands in the Rotunda. Let us hope that it stays there.

The rear of the White House Grounds is not usually open to the general public. One notable exception to this rule is Easter Monday, when the First Lady of the Land plays hostess to thousands of little children, who bring their brightly colored eggs to roll. At most public functions the rule is that children are not admitted unless accompanied by adults, but at the picturesque Easter Monday event at the White

House, the old rule is reversed; adults are not admitted unless they come with children. And

the police see that only one adult comes in with each child! So anxious are many grown-ups to witness this gala event that they stand outside the gates and wait for unattached children to take them in. A favorite trick is for little boys to charge an adult twenty-five cents or half a dollar for the privilege of going in with them. The boys then leave the happy man or woman and return for the next customer. 

Of recent years many unsightly outbuildings, mostly decrepit greenhouses, have been removed from the rear of the White House, and now the grounds contain excellent tennis courts and a magnificent fountain, not to mention small formal gardens, which are usually looked after by the ladies. Some of the Presidents and their sons have been great lovers of pets, so back of the 

Executive Mansion there are a number of kennels. 

Generous citizens have presented Presidents with all sorts of queer pets, from cats to giant lions. 

President Jackson received a huge Numidian lion from one of the minor rulers of northern Africa, but Jackson was not anxious to keep so wild a pet, and he auctioned the animal off. About two thousand dollars was paid by the owner of a circus for this king of beasts, and President Jackson promptly turned the money over to a home for children. "Old Hickory" was not so hardboiled, after all.

The extreme eastern end of the main floor is occupied by the largest room in the White House, the East Room, which measures forty by eighty feet. This is the one room still open to the general public. It is the State Reception Room, although it was used for a quarter of a century as the State Banquet Hall. Most people have hobbies that absorb part of their spare time, but the world learns little of individual hobbies unless they are very unique, or of great men. President Jackson, who was an inveterate smoker, collected pipes, and his assortment was said to be the largest private one of his day. Most of Old Hickory's pipes were made by

him from corncobs, but not a few were clay ones, with long reed stems. When Jackson was President, the White House is said to have "stunk with old pipe and smoke", and of Bourbon whiskey, too. Jackson was extremely democratic, always called his friends by their first or nicknames, and always ate with a steel fork, even after he moved into the most honored house of the land, where silver was, of course, used on the table at all times. But Jackson was not to be weaned from his old habits, and he had the White House table set with two forks at each plate, one of steel and one of silver. The guests could choose whichever one they wanted, but Jackson always used his old steel one.

If the walls of the White House could speak, perhaps they would tell many interesting stories about the people who have lived at 1600 Pennsylvania Avenue. Modern Americans are familiar with the fact that President and Mrs. Coolidge often entertained at breakfast, serving hot cakes and sausage most of the time, and that President 

Hoover likes to have his intimate friends come around for a little heavy exercise-medicine ball practice-but how many Americans know that President Hayes's administration was the only period before the Volstead Act when intoxicating liqours were not on hand? That was because 

Mrs. Hayes was a member of the W. C. T. U.

Buchanan was extremely interested in details, and it is said that he personally read every letter

addressed to him. Andrew Johnson was a tailor before he entered politics, and we are told that he found relaxation in making his own clothes, that is, in tailoring them. Roosevelt, who was very fond of vigorous sports, found time to box and fence, and lost one of his eyes, or the sight of one, while engaged in a friendly bout. Even his opponent was not apprized of the fact for many years.

The White House is now in excellent condition, and it is probable that for many more years it will continue to house the Chief Executive of our land. As recently as thirty years ago a movement was started to secure a new President's House, but the present building is so symbolic of all that rings true in American tradition that even the Presidents themselves do not want to give it up.

CHAPTER V. 

AREA BOUNDED BY THE UNION STATION- CAPITOL PLAZA, MASSACHUSETTS AVENUE, SIXTH STREET, AND PENNSYLVANIA AVENUE 

The broad expanse of land between the United States Capitol and the Union Station is rapidly becoming one of the city's most beautiful downtown parks, but not for over one hundred thirty years has this section been entirely clear of buildings. When the Union Station was erected, more than a score of years ago, this park was "planned", but the World War unfortunately upset the admirable plans of the experts who had been set to work by President Roosevelt, and only today is the park becoming a reality. 

After the Federal Government had made definite plans to build a city on the banks of the Potomac, after surveys were duly made, and after the eminent city-planner, Major L'Enfant, had laid out the streets and avenues on paper, a real estate boom of importance made its appearance. Lots were sold to people from all parts of the United States, and many men made heavy 89

investments of capital. One of the investors to get in "on the ground floor" was President

George Washington. He chose two building sites just north of the Capitol Building, on the west side of North Capitol Street, between B and C 

Streets, and had his friend, Dr. Thornton, who was the designer of the Capitol itself, draw the plans for two adjoining brick structures. Washington's friend, Lafayette, sent over several white marble mantels for the new houses, and these carved masterpieces are still preserved in the present George Washington Inn. Washington erected two buildings next to each other for the sake of economy, and no doubt intended to realize some profit on his investment, but he died in 1799, the year in which the houses were probably erected. Nevertheless, the houses were rented, and until their demolition just before the war, were used for hotel purposes.

Opposite the George Washington Houses, for as such they were always known, stood the home of Truck "A" of the local Fire Department. This fire house was likewise demolished about the time of the war, to make way for the Plaza development. Many small dwellings, most of them cheap boarding houses in their last years, were removed from the square immediately north of the Capitol.

Now let us ramble over to Judiciary Square, immediately to the north, to see some very genuine landmarks, and hear about the history of this old park and its buildings. Oldest and most beautiful of the buildings situated in Judiciary Square is the District Court House, formerly the City Hall.

We must remember that only since 1871 has this ancient edifice been exclusively a court house, for before that time we had the usual form of municipal government. In 1820 the city was large enough to need a permanent home for its local government, so the eminent architect, George Hadfield, who had been appointed many years before to do work on the Capitol, was commissioned to design an appropriate City Hall. This was done, and the cornerstone was laid with impressive Masonic ceremonies under the auspices of the Grand Lodge of the District of Columbia. 

Mr. Hadfield's was a fine design, and the building remains today as the most excellent tribute to his architectural skill, for it is the only important building designed wholly by young Hadfield. The city fathers were reluctant to tax the people enough to pay for such a big enterprise, so a lottery was provided. This form of raising money was often resorted to in those days, although it was not always successful. The building, started in 1820, was first occupied in 1822, but the east wing was not completed for another four years, and the west wing, which completed the symmetry of the structure, was not wholly completed until 1849. 

At is stands, the entire building is two hundred fifty feet wide, and the wings, which project beyond the front line of the center portico, are a little more than one hundred fifty feet deep.

Lincoln, who was enjoying a night at the theater just after the close of the War between the States, was shot through the head by John Wilkes Booth.

of the intended victims killed, but one other, Secretary of State, Seward, was seriously stabbed. 

In later years, after the Government had purchased Ford's Theater, and turned it into an office building, another tragedy was enacted within the same walls-the interior of the building collapsed, carrying many innocent people to their deaths. Criminal negligence on the part of the contractors who skimped while remaking the old theater was responsible for this horrible event.

CHAPTER VIII. 

THE MALL AND FEDERAL TRIANGLE 

The City of Washington, as designed by Major L'Enfant, was divided into four sections-Northwest, Northeast, Southwest, and Southeast. The

Capitol was the focal point, and from this building radiated North, East, and South Capitol

Streets. There was no West Capitol Street; instead, a long park to be called The Mall. This park now stretches all the way from the Capitol to the Potomac River, and will be eventually the center of much of our national administration. 

The Federal Triangle, between the Mall, Pennsylvania Avenue, and Fifteenth Street, will be the largest and most attractive group of governmental buildings in the world. More about them later.

For many years the Mall was no more than a commons, and only today is it becoming what its designer intended it to be a broad, green avenue lined with monumental but useful structures. 151

Let us start our trip through the Mall at the

Zero Milestone, the point from which all distances on the national highways passing through Washington are measured. L'Enfant had originally planned just such a marker to be placed in what is now Lincoln Park. The stone itself is on the northern edge of the Ellipse, a large tree-circled park immediately south of the White House grounds. The Ellipse has seen many important events in our city's history, and has been the host to all sorts of athletic teams, from football to real rugby.

In a secluded little grove of trees a few yards west of the Zero Milestone is the Millet-Butt

Memorial Fountain, a charming little monument. 

F. D. Millet, a noted author and artist, was a drummer boy in the Civil War, and Captain 

Archibald W. Butt, U. S. A., was military aide to two Presidents, Roosevelt and Taft. Both Millet and Butt lost their lives in the S. S. Titanic disaster in 1912.

South of the Ellipse is one of the newest thoroughfares in the city, Constitution Avenue, formerly called B Street, and before that, Washington Canal. This early waterway was planned as far back as 1792, when it was proposed to cut a canal from tidewater in the Tiber to tidewater in the James Creek, a distance of a little more than a mile. Tiber Creek, of which we have spoken before, wended its ways past Capitol Hill through the Mall to a large mouth about where Seven-

153 teenth and B Streets now intersect. The Tiber ran along where Constitution Avenue now is, between Seventh and Seventeenth Streets. The canal went south from near Seventh and B to the center of the Mall, and from there went eastward to Third Street, where it again turned south for a block or two. From this point the route is still marked for some distance, for the roadway over the old canal is called Canal Street. Near South

Work on the Washington Canal must have started soon after the city was surveyed, for in November, 1795, the Maryland Legislature passed an act opening and completing the canal already begun. The proposed sum, over $50,000, was to be raised by a lottery. This was undoubtedly insufficient, for in 1802 a company was organized to complete the canal. Still more funds had to be raised, however, and in 1809 yet another company was formed, this time with a capital stock of $100,000. Before this date, in 1804, wharves had been constructed at Seventeenth and Twelfth Streets. Boats first passed through the Washington Canal in 1816. This short canal joined the Wahington-Georgetown Canal at Seventeenth

Street, and the latter connected at Rock Creek with the Chesapeake and Ohio Canal, which tapped the rich and productive region in the vicinity of Cumberland, Maryland. It would seem

that such a wonderful series of waterways would be very beneficial to the city, and so the early civic leaders thought; but alas, the canal never was a commercial success, and it always was a great expense. By 1871 it had become such a menace to the health of Washington that it was arched over and converted into a huge sewer. 

One lasting memory of the old canal system still stands at Seventeenth Street and Constitution Avenue-the Lockhouse-but we will hear about that in another chapter.

Before we go any farther, let us stop for a minute and look at the quaint little stone building on the northeast corner of Seventeenth and Constitution Avenue, diagonally opposite the Lockhouse. This little place resembles a tomb, but it is only a storehouse for tools. Over a century ago it was one of the gatehouses of the Capitol Ground, having been designed by Charles Bulfinch, Architect of the Capitol during the reconstruction of that edifice. About 1874, when the arrangement of the Capitol Grounds was somewhat altered, the gatehouse was removed. So fine a gem of architecture was it, however, that rather than destroy it the authorities had it re-erected on the Mall, and there it stands today, always an object of curiosity.

South of Constitution Avenue, on a grass covered hill, stands the most prominent structure in our capital, the Washington Monument. The complete history of this famous shaft fills a large

WASHINGTON MONUMENT 

155 volume, so we cannot here repeat it; anyhow, the reader probably will not be interested in hearing about the many trials and tribulations of those who sought to perpetuate the memory of the Father of His Country in this form. Congress tried in vain several times to get the consent of the Washington family to permit the remains of President and Mrs. Washington to be removed from Mount Vernon and placed in a memorial to be erected in the Mall, but in 1833 a society was formed to plan and erect a memorial on this well chosen site, without the remains of George Washington. During the next fifteen years plans were drawn by Robert Mills for a very elaborate and ornate structure, and money was raised by popular subscription. The original plans called for a circular colonnade two hundred fifty feet in diameter and one hundred feet high, from the center of which was to rise a shaft six hundred feet tall. Many extra features were intended to be incorporated in the memorial, but only the obelisk-like shaft was ever built. As it was, the society had enough trouble getting that part erected. 

On the Fourth of July, 1848, the cornerstone was laid with elaborate Masonic ceremonies, which included the ancient custom of pouring corn, wine, and oil on the first block. During the next six years the shaft was raised to a height of one hundred fifty-four feet, and there it stopped, because the funds of the society were exhausted.

The line around the shaft today indicates how far the work had progressed. For a generation the incomplete monument stood before Congress came to the rescue and supplied the necessary funds to finish it. In 1879 the base was strengthened, and a year later the work on the shaft was resumed. In 1884 the Monument, as it is known throughout the land, was dedicated. 

This building, for many years the tallest structure in America and still the tallest in Washington, towers a fraction over five hundred fiftyfive feet above the gently sloping hill on which it stands. The base is fifty-five feet square, and the sides are perfectly plain. At the base a simple doorway admits the visitor, who can reach the top either by a very long climb up the stairway, of by a ride on the commodious elevator. Many prefer to walk, usually down, in order to view the one hundred seventy-seven memorial stones on the inside wall. These are quite interesting, and include gifts by Masonic Lodges, states, cities, and many organizations. The Pope sent a stone for the shaft, too, but some rascals are supposed to have carted it off to the river one night before it could be placed in position. 

The top of the Monument is pierced by eight small windows, two on each side, which offer excellent views of the city and surrounding territory. On a clear day even the foothills of the Blue 

Ridge Mountains may be seen.

The United States Navy was born during the Revolution, and has had many capable leaders ever since its inception. Probably the best known of these is John Paul Jones, a native of Scotland, who, as commander of one of our sturdy little fighting vessels, was the "first to compel a foreign man-of-war to strike colors to the Stars and Stripes". Commodore Jones led a very colorful life, which ended in Paris in 1792. 

In 1905 his body was discovered, disinterred, and borne in state back to the United States, where is was placed in the beautiful marble sargophagus in the crypt of the Chapel at the Naval Academy in Annapolis.

The country was slow to appreciate and properly honor the memory of this great man, but today there stands at the foot of Seventeenth Street, near the Washington Monument, an impressive memorial to this great fighter. The memorial consists of a heroic size bronze statue of John Paul Jones, in back of which there is a fifteen foot pylon containing a bas relief and inscription relating to his deeds. The monument was unveiled in 1912.

CHAPTER IX. 

WEST WASHINGTON AND POTOMAC PARK 

West of the government buildings in the vicinity of the White House and east of Rock Creek is an old section of our capital that still shows traces of the early development of Washington, and also shows evidence of the present trend to rebuild downtown near the business center.

In order to picture the growth and development of this neighborhood we must go back to a few years before the American Revolution when, in 

1771, a German by the name of Funk conceived the idea of founding a city on the Potomac River below Georgetown. Georgetown was a generation old at that time; yet it was still a small settlement compared with Alexandria, then a flourishing tobacco shipping port. Daniel Carroll, one of the local landowners, about that time started another community at Turkey Buzzard Point, on the Anacostia River, and called it Carrollsburg. The lastnamed town has totally disappeared, and the only 169

tangible memento of it is a little-known street named after the vanished Carrollsburg. 

Jacob Funk called his development Hamburg, after the famous city in his native land, but for many years the residents affectionately called it 

Funkstown. Today it is colloquially called Foggy 

Bottom. Roughly, it extended from Eighteenth to Twenty-third Streets, and from C to H. At least one old residence located in this now extinct town still remains, although it shares none of the glory showered upon other landmarks that have passed the century mark. I refer to the small, red brick, two and one half story house located at 2029 E Street. Occasionally occupied by families of little means, this old mansion, nestled among non-descript shacks and cheap buildings, still retains stumps of its old shade trees, and leading up to the doorway there are still fragments of the old paving stones. Historians have been unable to determine exactly when this little home was erected, or by whom, but it is known to have existed as far back as 1789, when the 

City of Washington had not even been planned.

We know very little more about the vanished town of Hamburg, but, although we are told that it was not much of a success, and that its land was incorporated into the District of Columbia in 

1790, we note with interest that Washington's first brewery was erected in this vicinity in 1796. 

It was appropriately called The Washington Brewery, and was a stone building, located in the

Wares of this factory, which soon became well known, were first advertised in 1809. Some of the structures of the old glass factory stood for more than a century.

Those who like to know about still older places will learn with interest that farther west, near

Part of this rock was later blasted and cut for foundation stones for the White House and Capitol. Today only a small boulder remains.

Most of the fame now attached to the Octagon House arises from the fact that President James Madison and his beautiful and charming wife Dolly lived there for about a year after the White House had been burned by the British during the War of 1812.

GEORGETOWN 

Georgetown is a much talked of section of the District of Columbia that is older than the City of Washington. Next to Alexandria, it is the oldest settlement on this part of the Potomac. 

Casual visitors are apt to think that Georgetown was named after George Washington or King George of England, but such is not the case; it was probably named after two other Georges. 

These were George Beall and George Gordon, owners of the land before the town was laid out. 

That was back in 1751, when a tract of sixtythree acres was made into a town by the authority of the General Assembly of Maryland. Thirty years later sixty-one more acres were added, and shortly after, yet more. The little town rapidly grew in size, and in 1789, one year before the District was decided upon, Georgetown was incorporated. Robert Peter, whose son Thomas later married Martha Washington's grand- 191

daughter, was the first mayor. For four score years Georgetown had a government separate and distinct from that of the City of Washington, but in 1871 Congress repealed the Charter of old Georgetown and placed that locality under the municipal government of the District, allowing, however, the retention of the name City of Georgetown. That too was taken away by Congress in 

1895, when even the old street names were abolished and replaced with ones conforming to the nomenclature adopted for the entire District. 

Since then, proud residents have done their best to preserve the traditions of old Georgetown, but only lately have they had any notable success. 

During the World War, when houses were selling and renting at premium prices in the Capital, Georgetown heard the knock of opportunity, and promptly opened wide her door. Ancient buildings that lacked even the most simple sanitary convenience were grabbed up and renovated. 

Palatial old homes were dusted and sold or rented to officials and members of society. Carriage lamps were resurrected from forgotten stables and from junk shops, to be placed beside newly painted colonial doorways. Plumbers, plasters, bricklayers, electricians, gardeners, and others were engaged to restore dozens of old houses. This movement was started during the 

War, but since then it has gained in popularity.

Today Fort Stevens is only a small open space with a flagpole, marker, and a few of the once numerous ramparts. But during the Civil War this old fort was a busy and important place, for it marked the Union line of defence that checked 

General Jubal Early's Confederate Cavalry on its almost successful attempt to raid Washington.

In the summer of 1864 General Early and his men worked their way toward Washington from the Monocacy, near Frederick, Maryland. Only a few regular troops were stationed in the capital, so news of the sudden Confederate move in this direction caused much alarm. Civilian employees of the government and soldiers who were in the city on leave or recuperating from illness or minor wounds were hastily pressed into service. 

Mr. Francis P. Blair's wine cellar, in Silver

Springs, was so tempting to the invaders that it temporarily halted their march. This gave General Wright time to organize the defence, which proved successful but none too strong. Several skirmishes took place in the woods between Fort 

De Russey and Stevens, and also between the latter and Fort Slocum, farther to the east, but the main action was along the Seventh Street 

Pike, protected by Fort Stevens. The Confederate

Line actually got as far as the present grounds of Walter Reed Hospital, where they used a tall tree for a sharpshooter's post. This old tree was long pointed out as a relic of the battle, but it blew down in a severe storm in 1922. 

So alarming was the attack on Fort Stevens, first called Fort Massachusetts, that President 

Lincoln himself drove out to the battle. It is said that only with force was he made to get off the rampart, where he was needlessly exposing his tall form to a shower of "minnie balls". A marker on the old rampart of the fort today tells

about Lincoln's visit to the scene.

One account tells that Union forces lost some three hundred killed and wounded, but the actual number killed on both sides probably did not exceed one hundred. A tiny national cemetery on the east side of Georgia Avenue, near Van Buren Street, protects the remains of some of the brave men who defended Fort Stevens. One of the young officers on the Union side was Oliver 

Wendell Holmes, now past ninety years of age, and until his resignation in 1932 a very active member of the Supreme Court of the United States.

Eleventh to Thirteenth. The twelve foot bronze statue of Lincoln holding the Proclamation of

Emancipation in one hand and resting his other hand on the shoulder of a crouching negro was erected with funds contributed solely by emancipated slaves.

New Eastern High School, a modern structure complete in every detail, is four squares east of

Lincoln Park on East Capitol Street. Two blocks beyond New Eastern is another well filled but unpopular building, the City Jail. This building is but one of several municipal institutions located in this vicinity, which until World War days was a vacant common, except for the Jail and surrounding buildings. Near the Jail used to stand the old workhouse, whose place is now taken by a large penal farm in Virginia. To the north and east of the Jail is the crematorium, where bodies may be burned, and east of this on Eastern Branch is the shack once used to confine the notorious leper, Early. 

Southeast of the Jail are the modern buildings of Gallinger Municipal Hospital, a model civic institution. During the Civil War, when thousands of soldiers were stationed in Washington, the commons in front of and near the Jail were the site of a large military hospital. 

There are no more buildings between the Jail and the Anacostia River, or Eastern Branch, as it is more often called, but the vicinity is nevertheless historic. Many years ago a toll bridge crossed Eastern Branch on a line with East Capitol Street. On the far bank of Eastern Branch opposite the Jail, was situated the ancient village of Nacotchtanke, the home of some eightly Indian warriors of the Powhatan Confederacy, a branch of the Algonquin group. When the Jesuits came

to Maryland with the first settlers sent over by 

Lord Baltimore, some three hundred years ago, these priests learned what the Indians of this vicinity called their tribe, and translated it to the Latin name Anacostia. 

Before we leave this section we must stop to see Congressional Cemetery, not from any morbid curiosity, but because of the many noted people buried here. William Young, one of the original proprietors, owned this land before it became the property of the Vestry of Christ Church, in 1807. 

Because of the many congressmen buried here in the early days, and because of the unique brown stone monuments erected by Congress, this burying ground early took the name Congressional

Cemetery, which it still retains, although it has always been owned by Christ Church. The graves of many government officials and prominent citizens may be seen in Congressional Cemetery; they include those of Elbridge Gerry; Pushmataha, Indian Chief; George Hadfield, William 

Elliott, and William Thornton, early architects of the Capitol; General Jacob Brown; and Mayor Maury.

Across the street from this, on the west side of Ninth Street and south of Pennsylvania Avenue, is the site of Tunnicliff's Tavern, an old hostelry that was doing business previous to 1795. Once called the Eastern Branch Hotel, it was the stopping place of President Adams when he visited this new city before the Government moved here from Philadelphia. Mr. Tunnicliff, the proprietor, later built a better hotel near the Capitol. In 1821 Tunnicliff's Tavern was acquired by Captain William Easby, a shipbuilder, whose family lived in the old place for about thirty-five years. During the latter part of the last century the old tavern was run by a Mr. Hoffmann, who erected over the door a large sign reading, "Washington Beer". The yard was enclosed by a high board fence and made into a beer garden. During its last years this old tavern was used as a winter storehouse for a collection of sideshow snakes, et cetera. When finally torn down, in 1931, the walls and timbers were in excellent condition. Near the site of Tunnicliff's 

Tavern, at 630 South Carolina Avenue is an old landmark, The Maples, built by Captain William 

Mayne Duncanson in 1796. Captain Duncanson was an English adventurer who made a large fortune in India and then came to America. In

New York City he met Greenleaf and Law, and with them came to Washington, where he invested $133,333 in real estate. But his speculations were not wise, and in 1799 he sold The Maples and moved to a small house near the Potomac River, where he died in poverty in 1812.

South of The Maples, on the north side of G

Presidents Jefferson and Madison.

The Marine Barracks, Washington headquar ters of the Soldiers of the Sea, are located in the block bounded by Eighth, Ninth, G, and Eye 

Streets, Southeast, and date back to 1802. Aaron 

Burr was imprisoned in the old Barracks in 1804 after he had killed Alexander Hamilton in a duel.

Another historic fact associated with the Marine Barracks is the founding of the Knights of Pythias there in 1864. The Marine Band, known the world over for its fine music, is stationed at these Barracks.

A few blocks east of the Marine Barracks, at Twelfth and G Streets, is the Cranch School, another old landmark which has served the community for many years. South of the Cranch 

School and east of Eleventh Street is a section that used to be dubbed Piketown, when the village of Anacostia was officially known as Uniontown.

The Navy Yard was established in 1799, when some thirty acres of ground were purchased and the construction of a seventy-four gun ship-of-the line was authorized. In 1814 the Yard was set on fire to prevent its capture by the British, but after the war more buildings were erected, and the Navy Yard grew in size and importance. Today the Navy Yard employs but one third of the men it did during the World War, and many of the shops are idle, but the facilities are great, as can be seen on Navy Day, when thousands of visitors flock there to see the gun shops, diving school, model test basin, radio test shop, et cetera, and the ships that are at dock. Around the Yard countless relics of naval ordinance are exhibited, all of them very interesting, including guns from ancient vessels, and all types of projectiles.

Anacostia dates from 1854 and has many fine homes, there are no interesting landmarks within its boundaries. To the southwest of this town we see Bolling Field, Army Airport, and farther on, the Naval Air Station.

The high ground behind Anacostia is called 

Congress Heights. During the Civil War several earthworks were erected on the crest of the hill, and today the ruins of them may still be seen. 

St. Elizabeth's Hospital, national institution for the insane, occupies much ground in Congress

Mention might be made, however, of the Government Experimental Laboratory at Bellevue, on the border of the District, on the Potomac. At this station various agencies of the Government make scientific tests of much value. 

If we want a nice drive, we can skirt the suburbs in the vicinity of Congress Heights and return to Washington proper by way of Pennsylvania Avenue extended. From the end of Pennsylvania Avenue we get an excellent view of the 

Capitol, Library of Congress, and Capitol Hill.

259 On our way across Southeast Washington to The Island, as the Southwest section used to be called, let us stop at 324-6 Virginia Avenue, Southeast, and look at the fine old buildings that were once the home of a Mayor of Washington, Captain Thomas Carberry. His old residence is now the Friendship House, an institution that does much good for the young people, especially those whose home conditions are not what they should be.

Not far from Carberry's house was the Duddington Manor House, pretentious mansion of the wealthy Daniel Carroll, one of the score of original proprietors of the District. This building was erected in 1791 in the square bounded by E, F, and Second Streets, and New Jersey Avenue.

Major L'Enfant, enraged because Carroll's house was built where an avenue was proposed for the new-born city, had the mansion torn down. Daniel 

Carroll, a former member of the Continental Congress, a delegate to the Constitutional Convention, and a member of the first State Senate of Maryland, was not to be treated in such a high-handed manner, especially when he himself was one of the original proprietors of the city. He therefore wrote directly to President Washington, who, in his characteristic manner, weighed both sides of the question and then ordered Major L'Enfant to rebuild Mr. Carroll's house, but not where the proposed avenue was going to be cut through.

Daniel Carroll was a prominent member of the 

Catholic Church-Archbishop John Carroll, the founder of Georgetown College, was his brotherand Daniel gave to the Church the land where 

St. Peter's, the second oldest Catholic Church in Washington, is now located, at Second and C 

Streets, Southeast. 

South of the site of Duddington Manor House, on the east side of New Jersey Avenue, is a row of brick houses that was built by Captain James 

Barry in the early 1800's. Although these houses look rather decrepit today, they still attest to the excellent skill of their builder, and to the fine taste of their architect.

The last section in our itinerary will be the Southwest section of Washington, formerly cut off from the rest of the city by James Creek, Tiber Creek, and the Washington Canal, which connected them; hence the logical name for the 

Southwest, The Island. 

Although for the past fifty years the chief development in Washington has been in a northwesterly direction, the Southeast and Southwest sections were at first considered the most desirable ones for residences, because they were near the Capitol and the River. Building near the waterfront in Southwest began in the 1790's, but there was already a small town on the shore of 

Eastern Branch at Buzzard's Point. This town was Carrollsburg, sponsored and subdivided by 

Daniel Carroll in 1770, a year before Jacob Funk

Today Carrollsburg has vanished-its buildings are no more-but the name of the town may still be seen on the lampposts of a short street in that section. There is at least one existing relic of vanished Carrollsburg, however, the cornerstone of St. Mary's or Barry's Chapel, which was erected in 1806 on the northwest corner of 

Half and P Streets, Southwest. Captain James

Barry was the donor of this little church, whose cornerstone is now preserved by the officials of St. Dominick's Church. The western boundary of 

Carrollsburg was James Creek, now covered over except for a short distance at its mouth, where it runs beside the Army War College Grounds. 

When the City of Washington became a reality, and the Commissioners sold lots, a syndicate was organized for the purpose of speculating in 

District of Columbia real estate. James Greenleaf, wealthy son of a Boston patriot, Robert 

Morris, financier of the American Revolution, and several others made up the syndicate, which was formed in 1792. In 1794 another wealthy man, Thomas Law, came to Washington and joined them. Captain Duncanson joined the party in 

1795. These few men, together with Daniel Carroll, who already lived here, erected most of the buildings put up for the Government and for speculation previous to 1800. The building activities of the syndicate commenced in the Southwest, where the tip of land between the Potomac

and Anacostia Rivers soon became known as Greenleaf's Point. The syndicate erected the first row of houses in Washington, in 1793, on 

Four and One Half Street, and called them

Wheat Row. The buildings are numbered 13131321, and are still in use, and in fine condition. 

Unlike so many of the row houses built today, those of Wheat Row had style, and they still reveal the fine taste used by their designer and builder.

Another building erected by the syndicate in the 1790's still stands at 470 N Street, Southwest. 

It is now the Neighborhood House, a social service center. Thomas Law, one of the investors, planned and later built several houses on New Jersey Avenue, south of the Capitol, but before that he erected a fine mansion, also still standing, at 1252 Sixth Street, near Greenleaf's Point. Mr.

Law was the son of the Lord Bishop of Carlisle, so he needed no other recommendation as far as society was concerned. In 1795, the year after he came to the United States, Thomas Law married one of the most "eligible" young ladies in America, Elizabeth Parke Custis, granddaughter of Martha Washington. It was for his bride that Law built the handsome residence on 

Sixth Street. 

Fate played sad tricks on the members of the old syndicate-Law's marriage was unhappy and soon broke up; Duncanson spent the balance of his life in poverty; Robert Morris became a

Greenleaf was swamped with indebtedness. 

While we are in the lower part of The Island, let us pay a visit to the Washington Barracks and Army War College, located on Greenleaf's Point. Until 1881 the U. S. Arsenal was located on these grounds, and until 1869 there was also there a penitentiary, the one in which were confined and later hanged the conspirators who assisted Booth in his plot to assassinate President Lincoln. During the War of 1812 the few soldiers stationed at the Arsenal hid some of their powder in a well before fleeing in front of the superior forces of Redcoats. For some reason or other the British, it is said, fired a shot into the well, and lost about a dozen soldiers in the violent explosion that followed.

In 1903 the large brick and stone building of the Army War College was built on the tip of Greenleaf's Point, commanding a fine view of the Potomac River. A bronze equestrian statue of 

Frederick the Great, renowned military leader, was erected at the War College, only to be put in storage a few years later when we went to war with Germany. The Washington Barracks and 

War College Grounds today are very beautiful, the spacious parade ground being faced on two sides by officers' quarters of colonial design and barracks for the enlisted men.

Water Street past the Naval Militia Armory, the

City Morgue, numerous wharves, and the Munic ipal Fish Market. Many residents of Washington can look back with delight to those bygone days when the riverfront was a busy place, lined with large and small boats. To be sure, there are still steamship lines from Washington to Norfolk and Baltimore, but there are very few excursion boats, and the glamor of the waterfront has dwindled. The Washington Channel, between the city and Potomac Park, is deep enough for most river steamers, but there seems to be a lack of good wharfage, and that prevents the outside interests from coming to Washington with their shipping facilities.
