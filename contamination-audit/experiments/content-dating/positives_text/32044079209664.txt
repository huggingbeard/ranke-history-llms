OLD AGE SECURITY INTRODUCTORY 

It is the purpose of this booklet to outline a plan for financing, extending, and dignifying public old age assistance in Minnesota. It ought to be of interest to the members of the Minnesota legislature, to students of political science, and to citizens generally whether over or under 

65 years of age. Public assistance is no longer a side issue in government. It is a major factor in both state and local administrations. In the writer's county just one-half of the county tax levy goes for relief. This is in addition to state and federal subsidies. Old age assistance is the largest permanent load in the field of relief. 

Unemployment relief overtops it at present but that condition ought not to continue.

IS A SPECIAL ADDITIONAL TAX ON MIDDLE CLASS INCOMES JUSTIFIED? 

In Douglas County, Neb. (Omaha), a careful study of the legally responsible relatives of 800 cases receiving old age assistance showed that there were 1271 such persons. The list of legally responsible relatives is the same in Nebraska as in Minnesota, and the social and economic conditions are much the same. We can safely assume that the 66,000 old people whom we plan to add to the roll have 100,000 legally responsible relatives. At present there are 90,000 individuals paying income tax and this figure will undoubtedly soon reach 100,000. The special additional tax on them for the benefit of the aged would average $50 a year or if you count the present income tax levy on them also, $80. These income tax payers will of course include relatives of the aged in the highest third of the population from the standpoint of income, as well as relatives of the aged in the middle class.

Personally we should approve of making the additional income tax for the aged an offset to the total of income tax paid in the higher brackets, but here we run up against the legal mind which would not approve of two rates of taxation on the same stratum of income. It is altogether probable that the aged who have relatives in the higher brackets are the aged who have adequate private resources, though of course the two groups do not exactly correspond. The wealthy

who would pay the additional old age tax must regard it as a contribution to the general welfare.

The tax situation in America may be sunmed up in a word. The rich pay heavy taxes in both amount and percentage. The poor pay heavy indirect taxes, i. e., in proportion to their ability to pay them. The middle class is the least taxed if we consider ability to pay. Men with incomes over $10,000 are the principal source of capital under our economic system (note a.) so that it is not quite fair to compare spending incomes with investment incomes. The federal income taxes paid on incomes under $5000 probably do not pay for the cost of collection. They might be called 'token payments." Until 1937 Our state income taxes on middle class incomes were also nominal. The 1937 legislature substantially increased them. Nevertheless they are still low. 

An Englishman with a $5000 income pays around $1000 to the government (since outbreak of hostilities more). A Minnesotan pays $120. He must have a $16,000 income before he pays $1000 under our state income tax law. A comparison that may be made is one with the sales tax. In Illinois this tax is three per cent, but three per cent on the entire spending income, so that the Minnesotan who pays three per cent on the third thousand of a three thousand doilar income and nothing on the first two thousand is really paying only one per cent on the entire income and he must have an income of $6000 before he pays three per cent on his entire income. The easing of taxes on the middle class is undoubtedly due to the fact that as Wm. 

Allen White says, the middle class has almost everywhere in America political power disproportionate to its numbers. These are facts, however unpalatable they are to some people. THE AGING VERSUS THE AGED 

Under modern conditions old age security is a social problem of the first rank. It should be distinguished from the problem of the aging. Men between 45 or 50 and 65 have not lost their economic value. It is the business of industry to care for them. Theodore Roosevelt attempted, rather unsuccessfully, to distinguish between "good" and "bad" corporations, but there is a line of distinction today which should hold. A good corporation may be defined as one, the age distribution of whose employes corresponds with the age distribuion of the working population. 

How this may be done without lowering the standard of efficiency is something for modern industry to work out. If this were done we should not be hearing demands for pensions at 50. 

CONCLUSION-The residual unrest of our old people is both understandable and justified. They want independence, and in the modern world independence means cash in one's pocket to be spent without restrictions. They do not want their awards determined by the "budget deficiency" method but rather in accordance with a standard of living set by the legislature. It is not so much the amount of the award at present that is objectionable, but the conditions under which it is granted. To extend the "budget deficiency" system to the aged or other permanently incapacitated persons as the Social Security Board is now attempting to do, is to assume that the agitation of the last 25 years for pensions is meaningless. Ten cents a day for the twenty million adults and children now on relief would use up only three quarters of a billion leaving a quarter of a billion for other necessaries. We can, if we want to, condemn oneseventh of our population to live on one-seventieth of our national income, but it is hard to understand how anyone who is interested in the future of his country, can sit down calmly and imagine that our free institutions are safe with twenty million of our population regimented as no despotic government has ever regimented the mass of its people in peace time. We have been shocked lately by the new clothing rationing card in Germany but the Germans did not invent rationing clothing. Any one who has spent a day in the office of the old style private relief agency or the new style county welfare board has seen clothing rationing in full operation from socks to neckties (if any).

Summing up the arguments for the plan above outlined: ONE-The plan is financially feasible. 

TWO-The obligations of relatives are defined in a practical and enforceable way, and it is bet lieved that, considering both the direct personal obligation to furnish housing and the group obligation contained in the special tax on middle class incomes, the maximum obtainable from relatives is secured. THREE-The obligations of local governments are definite and financially feasible and sufficient to secure the 20

active interest of the local community. FOUR- 

The allowance of a certain amount of private income in addition to the grant makes a distinction between the thrifty and the thriftless which a great many relief authorities here and in Europe approve. FIVE-The stigma now attaching to public relief of the aged will disappear when two-thirds of them receive it. The only objectors will be those who think of relief as punishment-that grisly hold-over from a barbaric past. The plan ideally combines the resources of the state, nation, and locality. The pension may be regarded as composed of three parts a ten dollar food budget contributed by the state, directly for the poor and indirectly thru a special tax on middle class incomes, for others; housing, contributed by the relatives and the locality; and a liberal personal budget of $10, contributed by the nation out of its pooled abundance. The doubling of this personal budget (it is now usually five dollars) will mean that newspapers and postage stamps will no longer be regarded as luxuries, nor a monthly hair trim as an unattainable ideal. All three of these budgets may be added to by personal income within limits. This presentation tries to look at the problem of old age security realistically; and to steer a middle course between those who would provide "ham and eggs" and chocolate eclairs for all over fifty, and those who would sink the dependent aged into the undifferentiated mass of direct relief clients. We shall be pleased to receive any comment you may wish to make.
