THE MATANUSKA VALLEY 

The Matanuska Valley comprises a very small part of the vast region of the Territory of Alaska, its area being approximately that of the State of Rhode Island. The valley reaches to tide water, at the extreme northeastern end of Cook Inlet, 125 miles north of the Gulf of Alaska. Its approximate latitude is 61 degrees 34 minutes north, and the Seward Meridian at 149 degrees 22 minutes west longitude runs through it north and south. From the Talkeetna 

Mountains on the north to the Chugach Mountains on the south the average width of the valley is 20 miles, and its length is approximately 60 miles. The valley floor varies from 36 to 400 feet above sea level.

The Matanuska River flows from the northeast, turns south near the town of Palmer, and at the south side of the valley joins the westward flowing Knik River to form the Knik Arm of Cook Inlet. Both of these streams are fed by extensive glaciers and their waters are heavily laden with silt. The name of the valley is appropriate, for it is derived from two Indian words meaning muddy water. Along the foot of the Talkeetna Mountains flows the little Susitna 

River which empties in Cook Inlet.

Numerous lakes and streams occur in the valley, serving as excellent habitats for trout and salmon. Moose, bear, mountain goats and mountain sheep are some of the more prominent game animals in the vicinity. 

The only kind of mouse is a small species of field mouse. 

Carnivorous animals as coyotes, foxes, and wolves are rare. 

The black bear occasionally causes some losses to pigs and cattle. There are no snakes of any kind.

The freedom from many pests that plague farmers in most agricultural regions is a noteworthy asset of the 

Matanuska Valley. 

FARMING AREA. The approximate area of the Valley be low Moose Creek is about 1,000 square miles. About 28,000 acres or almost 44 square miles are now in farms, a number of which were abandoned by homesteaders prior to 1935.

suitable for new farms. In the nearby Talkeetna and Chugach Mountains there are 200,000 acres, or more, which is excellent for sheep and cattle; now used almost exclusively by mountain sheep, mountain goats, and moose. 

The Valley is about as near the Arctic Circle as Oslo in Norway and Leningrad in Russia. Practically all of Finland and most of Norway and Sweden are farther north. Climate of the Valley is tempered considerably by the Japanese Current, which, after crossing the Pacific Ocean to the shores of North America, flows north and west in a broad arc through the Gulf of Alaska and thence southwest along the Aleutian Islands. Winters are not much longer than those of the Northern Intermountain States, nor are their temperatures so severe. 

CLIMATE. Only once in the past 10 years has the thermometer registered 32 degrees F below zero, and that for but one day. The average January temperature is 13 degrees F above zero (in Minneapolis, Minnesota 13 degrees F), February 18.9, March 24.3, April 36.7, May 47.0, June 55.4, July 

58.3, August 55.7, September 47.1, October 36.4, November 

21.9, December 12.2. In January 1942, the lowest temperature was 23 degrees F (below zero), and the highest 35 degrees F (above zero). In August, 1942, the lowest was 39 degrees F, and the highest 78 degrees F. Summer temperatures are never oppressive and seldom does the thermometer register 10 degrees below zero or lower during the winter. 

The average date of the last frost in the spring is May 24, and the average date of the first frost in the fall is September 10, making an average growing season of 108 days.

The duration of daylight during December is reduced to about 7 hours, but the days lengthen so rapidly after January 1st, that by the middle of April there are 14 possible hours of sunshine and by the middle of May 17 hours. From mid June to July there is practically no darkness and since temperatures are favorable, farmers and growing crops work double and triple shifts.

There are considerable variations in temperature, precipitation, wind direction, and velocity within relatively short distances in the valley. Normally the prevailing winds are

from the northeast and east-southeast. The composite influences of the air currents from the cold Bering Sea on the west, the Japanese Current on the south, and the dry winds from the northern interior of the continent blend to produce a climate that is without extremes at any season. 

In the winter the east winds, called "Knik" winds, are always warm, and they correspond to the "Chinooks" in the western Intermountain States. Occasionally winds from the northeast reach a considerable velocity, especially during the winter season. Since snowfall is light and the soil is then comparatively dry, considerable soil erosion may result. 

When winds of high velocity occur, either from the east or the northeast, temperatures rise sharply and the atmospheric humidity drops in proportion. It is not unusual for the hygrothermograph to record a rise in temperature of 20 degrees within two hours with a drop in atmospheric humidity of from 40 to 50 per cent in the same period.

The average annual precipitation, recorded over a 10year period, is 15 inches, most of which occurs during July and August, and during the winter months when the snowfall averages about 40 inches. May and June are normally the dry months of the year. But droughts have never been sufficiently severe to cause crop failure. Rain usually occurs in light showers or as drizzles so there is usually no run-off. 

Long continued cloudy and rainy weather during August and September may make it necessary to use special methods for drying hay and grain, such as placing it on poles, but usually both hay and grain will dry when the bundles are set up in shocks which permit ready circulation of air. Less than half of the precipitation comes during the winter in the form of snow and rain. Warm spells during the winter may be accompanied by rain and followed by the formation of ice sheets on roads and fields. This ice appears to be the cause of the death of some of the perennial plants, especially alfalfa and clover. Snowfall is rarely heavy enough to interfere with highway travel except occasionally when the winds blows it into drifts.

Thunder storms rarely occur in the valley, and in a period of 10 years only one severe hail storm has been reported and that was local. Only one small tornado has been observed, and that was at a considerable altitude, with its funnel never reaching down to the valley floor. 

There are often brilliant displays of the aurora borealis whose movements at times are very erratic. It is commonly believed that these disturbances in the high upper atmosphere have a measurable effect on the weather. However that may be, observers have noted that a strong northeast wind is often preceded by a peculiar auroral display, consisting of orange or yellow mist-like festoons apparently suspended from the upper atmosphere and covering the whole sky and moving rapidly from the north to the south or to the southwest. Occasionally these disturbances are so severe that radio communication between distant stations is disrupted by static.

The Matanuska and Knik Glaciers, which have now receded into the higher mountain elevations at the east end of the valley, once joined to cover the entire area between the Talkeetna and Chugach Mountains. This tremendous mass of ice was several thousand feet in thickness. Deposits of morainic material at successive elevations on the slopes of the moutains between the 2,000-and 3,000-foot levels support the opinion of some geologists that ice masses have occupied the valley floor at several different periods. 

SOIL. Almost the entire valley floor, with the exception. of a few outcropping of stratified rocks, is covered to an unknown depth by gravel, rocks, boulders, and outwash material dropped by receding glaciers. Large rock hills, or buttes, composed mostly of fused graywacke schist rise to considerable elevations in an intermittent chain across the valley of the Knik River. There are numerous lakes, some of which are landlocked, while others are connected by streams which eventually empty their waters into the

Knik Arm of Cook Inlet. The extensive tide flats are composed of deposits of silt from glacial streams. The high-level benches were formed by glacial outwash. 

Deposits of fine silt, mostly rock flour ground by the glaciers, are left on the Matanuska and Knik River bars during flood periods. After the waters have receded and the rock flour has dried to dust it is carried by the prevailing northeast and east-southeast winds and deposited on the valley floor as a top soil cover over the morainic material. The loess formation is deep and coarse near the river banks in the east, but in the western part of the valley it becomes progressively shallower and of finer particles. Approximately 65 per cent of the total valley area, of 1,200 square miles, can be cleared for cultivated crops or for permanent pastures.

The soils of the entire Matanuska Valley-Cook Inlet area are classed as "Knik" fine sandy loam. However, there are many gradations of soil types in the valley, running from muck through all the soil classes to very coarse gravel. 

Since the deposits of morainic material on the valley floor are quite recent, there are sections that are low and marshy, without established drainage systems. In some of these areas extensive deposits of chara marl are to be found, while others contain peat beds of considerable depth. 

Soils in continuous cultivation need and respond well to applications of barnyard manure or commercial fertilizers, especially nitrate, potash, and phosphate. Farmers have learned much by experience and the Matanuska Agricultural Experiment Station has furnished information from experiments on the suitability of different types of soils for various kinds of crops as well as on the kinds and amounts of fertilizers to apply, but yields can be greatly improved by more knowledge. The maintenance of soil fertility and the prevention of soil blowing also require more investigation.

Streams, springs, and lakes provide some farms with water, but usually excellent water is secured from wells varying from about 15 feet to over 100 feet in depth. Electric pumps and water-pressure systems are now coming into general use on farms.

There are large variations in elevation within relatively short distances in the valley. From tide water, at the head of Knik Arm, the benches rise progressively higher toward the north and east, reaching their maximum elevation at the foot of the Talkeetna Mountains. 

NATIVE VEGETATION. Before clearing operations for agriculture began, the whole floor of the valley was heavily forested-birch, spruce, cottonwood, with alder and willow predominating. Very few of the trees are more than eighteen inches in diameter, but considerable spruce lumber for local use has been sawed and is of fair quality. 

Grass grows luxuriantly in open parks in the forest and above timberline on the south slopes of the mountains. 

Twenty-one varieties and species of grasses and sedges are known to be indigenous to the valley and adjacent mountain slopes. Where fires have destroyed the forest growth, fireweed, Epilobium angustifoliam (L.), and wild false redtop, mostly Calamagrostis canadensis (Michx.) Beauv. and C. inexpansa (Gray), predominate. On the tide flats and low wet areas aquatic plants abound sedges, reed, several varieties of marsh grass, wild peas, vetches, and plantain. 

Harvested when the sedge seeds are ripe, the hay of these growths makes fair winter feed for livestock. 

Most of the soils are fairly productive, because the top is composed of fine rock flour, ground by glaciers, that is rich in minerals. But the organic material is limited to a comparatively thin layer and becomes exhausted after several years of cropping and must be replenished. Organic fertilizers produced on the livestock farms are supplemented by various commercial fertilizers in order to attain maximum crop production.

LAND CLEARING. Two methods are used in clearing the forest growth from the land. The trees are cut down with axe and saw. The best time for this is during the winter months. Most of the spruce is sawed into suitable lengths to be used as logs for lumber or as props and timbers for the mines. The birch and spruce not suitable for props or lumber -8-

are used for fuel. The tops, branches, bushes and all other waste are piled and burned as the slashing proceeds. 

The next step is to get the shallow-rooted stumps out of the ground and off the land. The quickest way is to push them out of the ground into piles with a bulldozer, following slashing in the spring, but if the "dozing" is done so soon after slashing, considerable surface soil is pushed out along with the "green" stumps. It is advisable to burn around the stumps as much as possible. Then at the end of a year or two, the bulldozer can push the stumps into piles very economically and with little loss of valuable surface soil. A bulldozer of about 80-horsepower can average dozing an acre of such stump land in one to two hours, but on green stump land three to five hours per acre are required. The 

Alaska Rural Rehabilitation Corporation operates several bulldozers and charges for work on an hourly basis. 

The final step in getting the land under cultivation is breaking. This is done with a heavy breaker plow or disc followed by a heavy disc. The ARRC is also equipped to do this work for farmers, charging for work on an hourly basis. 

FIELD CROPS. Mixtures of oats and peas or of oats and vetch, yielding up to eight tons of ensilage or three tons of dry hay per acre, are relished by all classes of livestock. 

Dairymen prefer hay of this mixture to the baled or chopped alfalfa hay imported from the States. Annual vetches and 

Canadian field peas, either for livestock feed or for seed purposes, make heavy growths of vine, and the yields of threshed seed average up to 30 bushels per acre. To date neither specific insect pests, such as weevils or aphis, nor fungus or virus disease, which are so troublesome in many pea growing areas, have been reported in either the pea or vetch crops. At times, however, cutworms do extensive damage to the young plants, both of forage and garden crops. 

A wide variety of field crops, pasture plants, vegetables, berries and ornamentals are grown in the Matanuska Valley and as further tests are made, the list will undoubtedly be increased. Due to the coolness of the summer, the hot weather crops such as corn, squash, cucumbers and tomatoes -9-

are not grown out of doors, but the last two are profitably grown in greenhouses. 

Soil and environmental conditions in the valley are exceptionally well suited to the growing of canning peas. The quality of the canned product is excellent, and the yields compare favorably with those in the best pea canning districts in the States. It is necessary to make but one planting of peas in the garden each season, for if the pods are picked regularly the vines continue to bear profusely until killed by freezing weather in the fall. Vines of the tall varieties such as the "Tall Telephone," often grow to a length of 10 to 12 feet, with pods and peas correspondingly large. 

VEGETABLES. Many kinds of vegetables grow luxuriantly. The excellent quality and flavor are due to the soil and atmospheric conditions, such as ample moisture, cool temperatures, long days and the fertility and suitable texture of the soil. These vegetables include radishes, leaf and head lettuce, early and late cabbage, cauliflower, parsnips, celery, rutabagas, turnips, carrots, beets, chard, string beans, rhubarb, peas, onions, kohlrabi and spinach. The Petrosky variety of turnip is notably high in quality. The head lettuce is unusually crisp, cabbage is crisp and sweet. In 1941 about 

7 acres were planted to lettuce, in 1943 about 23 acres; cabbage in 1941, about 10 acres, in 1943, 28 acres. Carrots grow rapidly and keep well in storage. About 8 acres were planted in 1941, and about 17 acres in 1943. Celery is crisp and delicious in flavor. The area devoted to this crop is expanding rapidly. About 17,000 bunches were grown in 1943 and sold at good prices. The onions are mild. High yields per acre of all vegetables are secured. A total of about 90 acres was planted to these vegetables in 1943. 

FRUITS AND ORNAMENTALS. Bush fruits, such as cur rants, gooseberries and red raspberries, produce large yields of excellent quality. Strawberries are large and of good flavor. Wild berries found in the woods and mountains include currants, raspberries, high-bush cranberry, low-bush cranberry, and blueberries. These wild fruits are picked by most residents and are much prized for jelly, preserves —10—

and juice. Spiced low-bush cranberries with moose or caribou meat and home grown fresh and cooked vegetables is typically Alaskan.

Many ornamental shrubs have been introduced, such as the bush honeysuckle with its gorgeous flowers and fruits, roses, and a Siberian Pea shrub. Native plants, as the mountain ash, elderberry, birch, and Spirea are used. Numerous herbaceous perennials do well. Many people have splendid gardens and flower beds.

GRASSES, LEGUMES, AND GRAINS. It is interesting to observe the effects of soil and climatic conditions on plants. brought in from various environmental conditions. Many of the perennial grasses and legumes, quite hardy and vigorous under severe winter conditions in the Northern States and Canada, will make excellent growth the first season. but succumb the first winter. There are many cultivated grasses, however, which are well adapted and produce heavy yields. The best cultivated grasses so far found are Russian wild rye, smooth brome, Kentucky bluegrass, creeping red fescue, slender wheatgrass, and meadow foxtail.

None of the heavy producing, tap-rooted legumes has been found hardy for more than from one to three years. 

While winter temperatures are comparatively moderate they are not constant. Soil heaving occurs from alternate freezing and thawing and the tap roots of many legumes are broken. Heavy winds drift the snow, leaving the surface soil exposed. Occasionally warm rains in winter may cause ice sheets over the entire soil surface, resulting in suffocation of plants. The fibrous rooted, Siberian alfalfa, Medicago falcata (L.), perennial vetches, and the small-leaved types of white Dutch clover have been found quite winter hardy. 

They produce best when seeded in mixtures with grasses, or on established grass meadows. Under judicious management the yields of hay and pasture from such mixtures are fairly high.

A considerable number of varieties of seed and fiber flax have been grown experimentally during the past six years. 

Yields have been uniformly high, and there has been no evidence of rust, wilt, or pasmo on any of the plantings. The -11-

oil content of the seed varies from about 30 to 43 per cent, and the iodine number, for the varieties grown, ranges from 197 to 217. No extensive field plantings of the crop have been made.

Soil and climatic conditions are not suitable for the growing of winter wheat. Fall rye is injured very little in winter and is one of the surest cereal crops which can be grown. 

Oats and barleys of any variety mature readily, produce good yields, and the grain is plump and well filled. Climax, Victory, and Swedish Select oats are the varieties largely grown for grain. Of the barleys, Olli, Trebi, Hannchen, and 19B, the latter a hulless, hooded variety, developed by the Alaska Experimental Stations, are grown extensively in the valley. Chogot wheat, a Siberian variety, is the standard spring variety grown. The average yield of oats is 45 bushels per acre; hulless barley, 23 bushels; awned barley, 30 bushels;

Chogot wheat, 22 bushels; and winter rye, 20 bushels. 

Corn and members of the sorghum family cannot be successfully grown because of the low mean summer temperatures and the short seasons. Buckwheat matures readily, but is not often grown, Rape and kale are occasionally seeded as pasture crops for swine and sheep. Sugar beets and mangels make indifferent growth and the yields are low. Soybeans have been tried repeatedly, but even the earliest varieties have seldom matured to the pod stage. 

EXPERIMENT STATION. The University of Alaska Agri cultural Experiment Station was established in the Matanuska Valley in 1917. Since that date approximately 150 varieties and crosses of potatoes have been grown experimentally. Red varieties, except for early use, are in small demand on the Alaska market. White varieties are grown extensively and find a ready market through civilian and commercial channels and at the army bases. 

Considerable research information on potato growing is available at the Matanuska Agricultural Experiment Station, and a potato improvement program is under way. In 1943 about 570 acres and from 2500 to 3000 tons of potatoes were raised in the Valley, while in 1941 the yield was about 322 tons on 70 acres. -12-

POTATOES. Most farmers have a root cellar for storage of potatoes and other vegetables and the Co-op constructed a large potato storage warehouse in the summer of 1943. 

Many varieties of potatoes (White Bliss, Arctic Seedling, White Gold, Early Ohio, Green Mountain, Katahdin, and others) grow well. The varieties recommended at the present time for the main crop are the White Bliss, Arctic Seedling and Green Mountain. From 500 to 600 pounds of tubers are planted per acre. Commercial fertilizer including nitrate, phosphate, and most heavily, potash, is applied at the rate of 400 to 500 pounds or more per acre. Average yield per acre of marketable tubers is approximately 5 tons, but yields as high as 18 tons per acre have been verified. 

PLANT DISEASE & INSECTS. Several diseases, such as blackleg, rhizoctonia, scab, and late blight occur but can be held to a low minimum by selection and disinfection of seed tubers and by crop rotation. Invariably seed potatoes from certified varieties imported from the States show a high percentage of disease the first season, in some instances as many as 55 per cent. If the seed tubers are carefully selected and disinfected before planting, and if the diseased plants are rogued out each season for three years, practically diseasefree tubers can be produced. There are no potato beetles and the insects that transmit virus disease are also lacking.

LIVESTOCK. Since the Government colony was estab lished in the valley in 1935, the livestock population has gradually increased. The poorer animals have been eliminated and the quality of individual animals and the production per animal unit, especially in the dairy industry, have risen greatly. All classes of poultry, meat, and dairy animals are represented, as well as approximately 140 work horses. 

In general the climate is healthful, and the percentage losses of livestock and poultry from disease are relatively small.

A few farmers raise milk goats and rabbits. There has been a good market for rabbit meat. Only a very few farmers have been raising a colt or two. The number of horses in the Valley has declined greatly during the past five years, being replaced by tractors for farm power. —13—

DAIRYING. The production of grade "A" milk, according to Alaska regulations, is one of the chief types of farming in the Matanuska Valley. The milk is delivered every morning to the Co-op Creamery in Palmer. Several routes have been established by farmers so that one truck is used to haul the milk for several farmers. The milk is shipped by truck from 

Palmer, by the Co-op, to its pasteurizing and bottling plant in Anchorage and sold to the consumer. Prices received in 1943 by the farmer were $6.20 per 100 pounds of milk with 

4 per cent butterfat content. The retail price for milk in Anchorage in 1943 was 30 cents per quart and the supply was inadequate to meet the demand. In the summer of 1943 there were about 700 dairy cows in the Valley, and one-third as many head of young dairy stock. The total amount of milk purchased from farmers by the Co-op in June 1943 was 204, 643 pounds.

The Guernsey is the most common breed at the present time, but Holsteins are increasing in numbers. There are also a few Brown Swiss and a number of crossbred animals. 

There is a considerable demand in Anchorage and in other parts of Alaska for various dairy products such as cottage cheese, buttermilk, and ice-cream mix. BEEF ANIMALS. A few farmers raise a small number of cattle of the beef type. The Hereford and the predominant 

Shorthorn are most common. There is excellent summer range in the Talkeetna Mountains, north of Palmer, and the fall grazing period may be lengthened considerably by pasturing the extensive tide flats bordering the Knik Arm of Cook Inlet. But even so the winter feeding season is long. 

In 1939 the reported value of meat and meat products shipped to Alaska from the States amounted to $2,190,000.00. It should be possible, by good management, to produce a considerable amount of this in the Valley. —14—

SHEEP. At present there are a number of flocks of sheep on farms in the Valley. Most of these are crossbred Romney- 

Marsh, with a few flocks of Hamshires. Dressed lamb and mutton find a ready market locally, but the wool must be exported to the States for sale.

HOGS. As of May, 1944, there were approximately 900 hogs of various ages on farms in the valley. The demand for pork and pork products is active, and prior to 1942 considerable fresh pork was imported from the States. For the past two years the number of swine on farms has been increased until the production is sufficient not only to supply the local demand, but shipments of pork and pork products are also made from the Valley to other sections of Alaska. 

Hogs grow rapidly and produce excellent quality pork, bacon and ham. A number of farms have their own curing houses, and many farmers utilize the Co-op slaughter-house for butchering hogs as well as other kinds of livestock. Peas, oats and rape can be planted to provide pasture for a long season; and wheat, barley and oats are grown for grain. A number of farmers have been feeding garbage. The Territorial Veterinarian has his headquarters in Palmer and is available most of the time for treating livestock.

The fact that corn cannot be produced in the Valley is not a serious handicap, because the Hulles barley which is grown is almost equal to No. 2 yellow corn as feed for livestock and poultry. The use of barley for finishing poultry and meat produces a firm, well-marbled product that compares favorably with the quality of the dressed poultry, beef, pork, and mutton imported from the States. 

POULTRY. Of the total poultry population in the Mata nuska Valley in 1939, approximately 27,000 were chickens. 

Low prices for eggs and dressed poultry discouraged production. During the past two years the demand has increased tremendously, prices to the farmer have risen to 75 cents and even $1.10 per pound for dressed poultry and from $1.00 to $1.10 per dozen for fresh eggs. The White Leghorn and 

Hampshire Red are the breeds in popular favor. During the -15-

spring and summer of 1945 several thousand chicks were shipped in by airplane to be raised and sold as fryers. 

FARMER'S CO-OPERATIVE. Most of the farm products are marketed through the Matanuska Valley Farmers Cooperating Association under their trade mark "Matanuska Maid." The organization is owned and controlled by bona fide farmers. Management of the business is in charge of a 

Board of Directors, elected by the membership. A general manager is employed by, and is directly responsible to, the 

Board of Directors. 

The Co-operative was organized and incorporated by the 

Alaska Rural Rehabilitation Corporation in 1937, to assist in marketing the products of the farmers of the Government Colony. In 1939 the management of the Co-operative was transferred to the farmers. The equipment and all facilities for handling the business were purchased from the Alaska Rural Rehabilitation Corporation by the Co operative in the spring of 1940. Since that time a new creamery building for pasteurizing and distributing whole milk has been constructed in Anchorage. A new gravity watersupply system for the town of Palmer has been completed and a vegetable storage cellar 40 feet by 200 feet has been constructed.

Electric power is supplied to the towns of Palmer, Wasilla, Matanuska, and to the valley farms by the Matanuska Electric Association, Inc. This is a co-operative, organized under the Rural Electrification Administration. It purchases electric energy from a hydroelectric plant located about eight miles from the valley proper. Steam is used in heating all buildings owned by the Marketing Co-operative in Palmer, and is also available for sterilizing and for operations in the various processing units. 

The Co-operative operates a garage, machine shop, blacksmith shop, power plant, wood working shop, slaughter house, warehouse, creamery, trading post, dormitory, restaurant, cold storage, vegetable storage, hospital, dental office, barber shop, and beauty shop. Groceries, meats, dairy products, vegetables, fruits, clothing, hardware, household furniture and equipment, building material, machinery, feed, -16-

field and garden seeds, fertilizers, and automobile accessories may be purchased in the various departments of the trading post, warehouse, and garage. The increase in products marketed from farms in the Matanuska Valley during the past four years reflects the steady progress being made. 

The figures presented show only the value of products marketed through the Co-operative Marketing Association. A number of farmers market their products independently. 

The increase in production is due largely to the fact that in 1935 only about 650 acres of land were cleared and under cultivation in the entire valley. Now approximately 7,000 acres are cleared and either in cultivation or seeded to permanent pasture.

The total sales of all commodities by the Co-operative for the fiscal year ended December 31, 1940 were $481,057. For the fiscal year 1943 direct sales had mounted to a total of $1,008,000, including sales of farm products, and sales of goods by the trading post, creamery, warehouse, garage and other units owned and operated by the Co-operative. 

During 1940 the farmers sold through the Co-operative $68,- 993 worth of farm products, including vegetables, meats, dairy and poultry products. Payments by the Co-operative to the valley farmers for products delivered during 1943 totaled $302,781, an increase of 337 per cent, in four years.

MARKETS. A number of markets absorb the farm products from the Matanuska Valley. Most of the grade "A" milk, ice cream, and other dairy products are sold to the Army and through commercial channels at Anchorage. The supply is inadequate to meet the demand. The Army and business houses purchase fresh vegetables and root crops, such as carrots and rutabagas, as rapidly as they are available. Seldom are there any surpluses of these products to be placed in cold storage for postseason sale. The production of potatoes has about kept pace with market demands. 

There are other market outlets that normally use a considerable volume of farm products. The Willow Creek gold producing area, largest gold quartz mining district in Alaska, is located along the Talkeetna Mountain Slopes at the northeast end of the Matanuska Valley and on nearby Wil- -17-

low Creek. During normal times the eight producing mines employ several hundred men and purchase farm products regularly.

Fresh vegetables and other products from the Farmers Cooperative are purchased by the Alaska Railroad and by the 

Civil Aeronautics Authority. Outlying mining camps and trading posts supply their needs for fresh farm products as often as air transportation is available. 

FUEL. Birch and spruce for fuel are available on practically every farm. A few farmers burn coal, secured from nearby mines, or some use stove oil or electricity. Some of the birch is suitable for the making of flooring or furniture. 

Vegetable crates are made from local timber. Several sawmills have been sawing spruce into rough lumber and the coal and gold mines have used large quantities of spruce for mine timbers. Spruce is also used for fence posts. 

The Matanuska coal mining district is located in the Talkeetna Mountains, on the north side of the Matanuska River, northeast of Palmer. Coal from the three producing mines is used in considerable volume by military posts, the Alaska Railroad and the civilian population. Considerable quantities of farm products from the valley are used by these mines.

WEEDS. The only serious perennial weed that has invaded the valley so far is quack grass, and this weed has some special values because it furnishes nutritious pasturage or hay and makes a good soil binder. The worst noxious perennial weeds such as field bindweed, leafy spurge, Russian Knapweed and white top have not been introduced yet. Some of the annual weeds, as lambs quarters (locally called "Pigweed"), chickweed, and horsetail are often troublesome.

Many plant diseases, as wheat rust, and many injurious insects, notably the potato beetle and pea weevil, are absent.

Some damage to crops has been caused by cut-worms, but methods to control the depredations of this insect are being put into effect. -18-

ROADS AND TRANSPORTATION. Nearly every farm has access within a quarter of a mile, or less, to a gravelled road. 

These roads form a network in the valley and connect with the highways to Anchorage (about 50 miles from Palmer), to the Richardson Highway and the Alaska Highway to the

States, and various mountain roads. Railroad transportation is available at Palmer, Matanuska and Wasilla. Small airplanes land at Palmer, larger ones at Wasilla, and Anchorage is one of the major Alaskan airports with several airlines serving it.

MISCELLANOUS ITEMS. The climatic and soil assets have been described. Gravel and sand are abundant for the construction of roads, silos, and buildings. Peat is abundant and marl, very rich in calcium, is found in some localities.

The nearby mountains, rising to heights of over 7,000 feet, the glaciers, the rivers and creeks, and the lakes with their wildlife, both animal and plant, and scenery furnish a great variety of attractions for recreation during summer and winter. On nearly every Sunday and holiday throughout the year, people may be seen picnicking, fishing, boating, hiking, taking pictures, hunting, berrypicking, mountain climbing, skiing or skating.

There must be no misconception, however, concerning farming in the Matanuska Valley. Although soils are still fertile and average crop yields high, and while markets and prices are good, there are disadvantages such as short growing seasons, heavy rains at harvest time, high winter winds, long winters and the danger of over-production and low prices if agriculture is developed too rapidly and beyond market requirements. Here, just as in any farming area in the States, success depends on good judgment and hard work. 

Although the present supply of farm products is not sufficient to meet market demands, yet the farmers are not overexpanding. Tractors and modern machinery and methods are used in production, but only such equipment as is necessary for operations is being purchased. Obligations are being liquidated and surplus funds are being wisely invested as -19-

a reserve against possible low prices and market fluctuations during the adjustment period ahead. 

Of the 250 farm families in the valley, many are well established in specialized lines of production. There are 24 producers of grade “A” milk and 43 of grade "B" milk-a total of 67 dairy farmers. A considerable number of farmers are devoting their entire effort to the production of vegetables. 

Others are specializing on poultry and poultry products. A few are extensive producers of hogs. However, most farmers have several sources of income from general farming. 

At present there are 45 or more farm tractors in the valley and there is much exchanging of work and machinery. Most of the farmers prefer to have their own equipment; particularly a tractor (or horses), truck (1½ ton capacity is a good farm size), plow, cultipacker, grain drill, grain binder, and for potatoes a planter and a digger. There are three or four threshing machines in the valley, the ARRC has a good seed cleaner (fanning mill), the M. V. F. C. A. has a feed mill, a potato grader, and a few farmers have ensilage cutters. 

It is best for settlers to bring with them the kind of livestock they are interested in, preferably of breeds now in the valley, but only good quality stock because at the present time they are almost impossible to purchase in the valley. 

Furniture, tools, and household equipment should be brought along, because they are scarce and expensive there. 

Lower transportation costs may be secured by obtaining settler's rates. Full information may be secured about these rates from the Alaska Steamship Company, Seattle, Washington.

It is best to arrive in the Matanuska Valley in late winter or early spring, not later than March 1st. Usually hay can be purchased locally. It is most advisable, however, to make all arrangements for a farm to occupy, and for all farming needs, such as seed, fertilizer, machinery and livestock before the end of January. It is not advisable to move to the 

Matanuska Valley with family and equipment until one has made definite arrangements for a farm. The prospective farmer must make sure he has a place to go when he arrives. 

If possible it is best to visit the valley and make a thorough -20-

inspection before choosing a farm and making final farming plans.

The actual cash needed to get started depends upon the individual and upon the equipment he brings with him. If a farmer has security and proves that he is dependable and honest, a short-term loan for farming and land clearing operations may be secured from the M. V. F. C. A. and the 

ARRC at 5 per cent interest per year.

Various kinds of work are usually available in stores, garages, restaurants, etc., in Palmer and Anchorage; in sawmills, in the timber clearing land and getting out logs, props and wood; in the coal mines; with the Alaska Railroad; trucking coal, wood, timber, farm produce, etc.; with the 

Alaska Road Commission on road maintenance, repair and construction; teaching school; office work; trapping, salmon fishing in Cook Inlet, Bristol Bay, etc; and construction work.

HOW FARMS MAY BE OBTAINED. Farms or land can be obtained from the Alaska Rural Rehabilitation Corpora tion. When a farm becomes vacant the ARRC will sell or rent to desirable families who plan to make a home and to farm in the Matanuska Valley. A farm of 80 to 160 acres with, in some cases, Government land adjacent or nearby, can be purchased at prices ranging from about $4,500.00 to $6,000.00. 

Cash payment may be made for entire amount or terms may be arranged for about one-fifth down payment, the balance to be paid within 10 to 15 years at 3 per cent annual interest. These farms may be leased the first year at a monthly rental of $25.00 to $35.00 per month. In cases where purchase occurs at the end of the year, two-fifths of the rental paid applies on the down payment. The other purchase terms stated above apply on this rental purchase plan also. The prices of farms vary in accordance with the number of acres of cleared land, kind and condition of buildings, and other factors such as topography, distance from town, etc. 

Most farms have from 20 to 40 acres of cleared land, the buildings include large barn, 5 to 7 room house, chicken house, well and wellhouse and frequently additional small buildings. -21-

Farms or land may also be acquired from private owners, usually farmers. These farms may have been developed originally either by the ARRC or by homesteaders. The sale is negotiated directly with the present holder of the farm. 

Undeveloped Government land may also be purchased at prices ranging from $1.25 to $5.00 per acre. This land is usually covered with birch-spruce forest and must be cleared before it can be farmed. 

Many organizations have been started in the valley, such as Extension Service Homemaker's Clubs, 4-H Clubs, Red Cross Chapter, Health Council, Parent-Teachers' Association, the Matanuska Valley Farmers Cooperating Associa tion, the American Legion and Masons. The churches represented in Palmer include the United Protestant, Lutheran, Catholic, Seventh-day Adventist and Matanuska Valley Mission.

The Alaska Rural Rehabilitation Corporation has its main office in Palmer, Alaska. One of its main functions is to aid settlers in establishing farms and homes. It co-operates with the General Land Office of the Department of the Interior in the sale of Government lands. If additional information is desired about the Matanuska Valley, inquiries should be directed to the Alaskan Rural Rehabilitation Corporation in 

Palmer, Alaska, or the Commissioner of Agriculture for 

Alaska, Fairbanks, Alaska. 

SUMMARY. Because Alaska is so much in the lime-light at the present time and is receiving a great deal of enthusiastic publicity in the States, a few conclusions are in order, to aid and perhaps guide those interested in coming to the 

Matanuska Valley to farm.

Only about 44 square miles are now in farms. Perhaps an equal area can go into new farms within the next few. years. About 200,000 acres are suitable for sheep and cattle grazing, but this industry is still in its primary stage with problems of winter feeding, processing and marketing yet to be completely solved.

The winter temperatures in the valley are comparable to northern Minnesota. The last frost is usually out of the ground by May 24th. The average growing season is about -22-

days. Due to cool summers corn, squash, cucumbers and tomatoes cannot be grown. The latter two are usually profitable greenhouse crops.

The unfarmed areas of the valley are heavily forested and land clearing costs are higher than in the States. Care must be exercised in bulldozing stumps so as not to remove too much of the top soil. Organic matter in the soil is limited and becomes exhausted in a few years, requiring the use of barn yard matter and commercial fertilizers. Wind erosion is a problem in some parts of the valley. 

Field crops of oats and peas, oats and vetch do well. But summer rains require hay curing by dryers or by special handling. Crops of wheat, oats, barley and rye have always matured. Dairying and all varieties of garden truck farming are at present the most profitable, with the market saturation point not yet in sight. Potatoes have been a standard crop and have been a constant source of cash income. 

Processing, storage and marketing facilities are available through the Matanuska Valley Farmers Cooperating Asso ciation and this organiation has grown in ten years to a business with gross sales in excess of $1,300,000 annually. 

The psychological factors of rural life in Alaska must be seriously considered by prospective settlers. Fortunately in the Matanuska Valley there is a good network of dirt roads radiating from the town of Palmer. School busses, rural electricity, numerous social, recreational and religious organizations and complete shopping facilities take care of the needs of the valley. However, the long winters with occasional high winds and the usual sleet, ice and snow have been discouraging to many persons.

Alaska is a new country. It has all the problems of frontier settlement and development. The Commissioner of Agriculture recommends that persons interested in farming in the Matanuska Valley might profitably make an inspection trip to the region and decide for themselves the advisability of moving to this new country. If such an inspection trip were made first, many persons would be spared the possibility of discouragement and failure.

All interested persons should bear in mind that the Matanuska Valley is about seven days travel by steamer and train -23-

from the closest city in the United States, that transportation costs, living costs and farming costs are higher than in the States. Higher prices for farm crops and produce somewhat compensate for these higher costs, but there are definite limitations to any get-rich-quick schemes in agriculture in Alaska. The success record of farmers here is just about the same as in the States. It is most advisable to have adequate cash available before making the move to Alaska. 

The Alaska Rural Rehabilitation Corporation recommends 

$5,000 cash as a minimum for new comers seriously interested in making a career of farming in the valley. 

Those who have made good successes of general farming, dairying and truck gardening have done so because they were successful in their fields before coming to Alaska and have been willing to work long and hard up here. 

The success a family can make of farming and rural life in the Matanuska Valley is directly commensurate with experience, financial status and determination to make a go of it. There are opportunities here for a considerable number of farm families to "pitch-in," put down roots and grow with a new country. -24-
