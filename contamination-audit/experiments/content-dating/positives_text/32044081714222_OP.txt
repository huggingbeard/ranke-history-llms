Then as a rustling arose in the grass on the hillside they rushed up the hill and away. Francis walked very slowly in the opposite direction with the rabbit safe under his robe, and so continued until the shouts of the hunters were stilled. 

A nest under a log

At length the rabbit seemed to sense that danger was over and on passing a fallen tree that lay full length on the ground it intimated plainly that it wanted to be set down

just there. Francis complied with its desire. And then, as the old chronicler naively tells us, that rabbit jumped across the log and disappeared beneath it on the other side; but as Francis was about to turn away, it re-appeared, stood up on its hind legs, and with mute appeal seemed to invite him to come and look under the log. He did so, and there at play in the nest, frolicsome at their mother's return-were three little white rabbits. Francis bent down and talked to them. Then he went out and gathered tender grass and birch leaves and olive tips and took them to the nest so that the mother rabbit might stay with her little ones and not leave them in search of food that night.

Francis then went his way, but as he reached the bend of the road he looked back to the fallen tree thinking of the fearsome beauty that it sheltered, when, behold, the mother rabbit came out and jumped on top of the log, she raised herself on her hind legs, her red gleaming eyes were fixed on him, her forepaws were together as if in prayer, and her glad mother heart said psychically to him that understood,- "We thank you." Francis smiled and blessed them and then went on in peace and in love with all God's world. 

He had found a flower of mystic fragrance in the valley of humiliation.

Return At the secret call of his mother, Francis returned to his home in Assisi. But he was changed; silent and proud-he no longer sought his former companions; their pleasures had grown meaningless to him; he felt, too, their misunderstanding, their secret ill will, their half scorn. 

Francis had never made known in detail that vision of blood, that mirage-battle that had appeared to him when on the way to war. It seemed too sacredly personal to be submitted into the blaze of military noon and to the scorn-laugh of soldiers who could not understand. My secret is to me is a saying well understood by mystics who commune with God. Francis simply said that when at Spoleto, on the way to battle he had distinctly heard a voice saying that his hand should not shed blood and commanding him to return to Assisi and there await what would come to him. He had obeyed that Voice.

A Successful Merchant 

Francis was very successful as a merchant. His father's vast supplies of goods were open to him as salesman and he

made huge profits on them in the markets. (Did he here again recognize Human Greed?)

Dreams of Pietro Bernardone 

Pietro Bernardone was once more looking kindly on his son, and when Francis asked for money for a banquet for his friends, old and new, Bernardone answered that request with lavish prodigality. Doubtless he thought that now his plans for Francis would be fulfilled; he would succeed his father in business; the boy, so beautiful and so financially successful, would marry the daughter of a nobleman. Francis would become a knight, a Prince, perhaps, and so add luster rather than disgrace to the proud name his father gave him.

Francis had apparently returned to his former way of living and to his old time companions. He lavished money riotously on banquets and on night carousals, but most of all he liked to dress as a knight, in imitation of Gautier de Brienne, then the hero of youth, and lead his companions singing songs of liberty and hero-worship of Crusaders.

Wounded Ideals 

After excesses of any kind come satiety and recoil. Some wounded ideal hastens to hide itself away from our regretful gaze. Something capacious in us and unfilled ever cries out, "More, more; give me pleasure, honor, beauty, love, gold-gold; fill me to acquiescence and full satisfactionor make me forget so that I may no longer see that reproachful ideal."

The ruins of San Damiano 

Francis went to Mass one day in the church of San Damiano. He noticed its poverty, its sore need of repairs; speaking later to the priest on the condition of his church and learning of his need, Francis at once offered to have the church repaired. The priest, knowing his father's wealth, and the position he held in the father's store, agreed to accept the money needed for repairs.

Francis in the market place 

Francis went at once to the market place and sold there very advantageously his horse; unfortunately he sold also several bales of his father's goods, and putting all the money made on that market day into a little black cloth bag he gave it to the priest.

Again there was dissension in the home of Pietro Bernardone, and a violent quarrel took place between him and his son. Francis was now not only a coward-in his father's eyes he was also a thief. In vain did Pica, the distressed mother, plead for her son. Pietro demanded a refund of his money and threatened to take the case to the court of the Bishop. Francis accepted the challenge. 

Trial of Francis at the Court of the Bishop of Assisi The priest of San Damiano hearing of the quarrel and the trial, brought the little black bag back to Francis with the money untouched. The trial was held in presence of the 

Bishop of Assisi. Francis acknowledged all the charges that his father brought against him. The Bishop remonstrated kindly, but at last advised that the money for the goodsnot for the horse-be returned to Pietro Bernardone. Francis advanced with dignity and put into his father's hands the bag containing all the money he had received that day in the market. Then he took off his courtly mantle, his shoes, even his tunic and laid them at his father's feet saying, "I am no longer the son of Pietro Bernardone, God alone is my father now."

The Bishop hastily put his own robe over Francis' naked form and led him away, while Pietro, looking with satisfied greed into the bag, left the court rejoicing. 

The Crucifix that understood

Francis remained at San Damiano. He spent hours in prayer before the Crucifix-the pathetically weary face of the sufferer seemed to understand. 

Francis' world had collapsed. His own father was a slave of Human Greed. Complete disillusionment-of home, of society, of so-called liberty, of chivalry, of the Crusades, even of human love-stood gaunt before him. All things of earth were unrealities; all were as Platonic Shadow-pictures passing through the cave. 10

Panorama 

Francis prayed, his head bowed low at the feet of the tortured Christ. And then there passed before him the panorama of his day. He saw the lordly feudal castles with their incessant feuds and fights. He saw the aimless wasting wars of Guelph and Ghibelline, and the misery of the common people because of this rival enmity between the Pope and the Emperor. He saw Benedictine monasterieshigh, aloof, wealthy, self-sanctified-but they heard not the cry of the children in the valley. And blazing in regal magnificence, he saw the luxurious papal Court and that imperious potentate, Innocent III, Pope and King. 

And again came upon him that sense of the unreality of it all-the shadows passing in the cave. Francis groped in hypnotic gloom.

A Message 

Then suddenly he heard a Voice-it seemed to come from the Crucifix. It said sadly, "Francis, go out and repair my church; do you not see that it is tottering and about to fall?" 

What church? literally the church of San Damiano so sadly in need of repairs? Or symbolically the Church of 

Rome then grown top-heavy with temporal power, and pulled out of place by wealthy feudal Bishops and Abbots, and by a worldly papal Court-that Church now so sadly in need of spiritual repairs? What church, Lord?

Hours passed. Francis struggled in prayer. The old life was ending, the new was not yet born. In disillusionment, in utter unreality-all earthly values were passing away, but the painfully nascent spiritual values now in ecstatic pain were not yet born. It was the hour of Conversion; it was the process of passing into the higher life. And when at last Francis came out of that ecstatic communion he was twice-born. Henceforth he walked kindly and cognizantly among unrealities for he had found the Real. 11

CHAPTER III Conversion 

The twentieth century man smiles indulgently at the visions, voices, dreams so potent over men of the thirteenth century. Just the functioning of some as yet unknown physical or psychical laws; there is explanation; nothing of the supernatural in these brain-mists-so says today. But admit that these phenomena are still unexplained, these laws still scientifically uncontrolled; admit honestly that man, today even as yesterday, is still the unknown.

These visions have come to men who were tortured by the mystery of life; and at hours, too, when this torture had mounted to madness and all their being cried for answer and relief. And the answers came. They were startling, selfpacifying, world-revolutionary, divine not human, and eternally good.

Thus came the answer to the Hebrew poet-prophets, to Buddha, to Socrates, to Mohammed, to the medieval mystics, and to Francis of Assisi. And the world still treasures as its highest and best the messages received and made known by these mystics. Their writings are best understood by the heart of man when at its loftiest and purest; and certain men of all ages and times have heard and understood these messages.

A man is never the same after Conversion. He is turned around; he sees the things of time as shadows of things eternal. He sees the wrongs and the injustices of man to man, clan to clan, creed to creed-in a great pitying light that shines the same for all. 

Francis repairs the ruins of San Damiano

Francis went out from that second solitude a changed man. Dressed in the brown tunic of a peasant with a white cord holding it in place, he went boldly into the market place of his own town. He met there old friends and one time boon companions; they noted with smiling pity his emaciated form, his strangely sparkling eyes, his beggar-like appearance. All knew his story-and when he stopped old friends and asked them courteously for the love of God to help him to repair the ruins of San Damiano, they gave him what they had. Some gave money; one man, a stone-cutter, laughingly offered him stones. Francis accepted, and gathering 12

them into a bag, went to the Church where with his own hands, working as a mason, he labored until the rent in the wall had been repaired. 

First follower of Francis

The Chaplain of San Damiano was deeply moved by this devotion to an ideal on the part of Francis. He watched him closely while at work; he saw a man whose hands worked indeed at a hard task, but whose mind was at the same time in touch with God. This Chaplain followed Francis one night as he retired into the woods, and whilst sitting on the porch of the Portiuncula-far in the Umbrian forestthere ensued between the Chaplain and Francis a heart to heart talk as result of which the Chaplain offered to care for Francis in his own house and to become his follower in the fulfillment of that great mission which Francis declared had been given him by the voice of God. That voice had distinctly said to him when in a trance or cataleptic state, "Francis, go out and repair my Church." What Church?

Among the first followers that gathered around Francis were the Chaplain of Saint Damiano, Bernard of Quintavalle, Pietro dei Cattani, and Giles. Later came Rufino, Masseo, Juniper, John the Simple, and Leo. The twelfth disciple was the priest, Silvester.

Origin of the Franciscan Order 

And thus originated the Franciscan Order, branching out later into the First Order-the original Franciscans; the 

Second Order (Poor Clares), the Third Order (for people in the world); and going forth as an influence for good into every country of the globe, into every state of life from pontiff to peasant, from monarch to serf, and everywhere and at all times acting as a force for the uplifting of civilization. From so humble a beginning-such sublime results, from a Voice calling to a young man in the wilderness more than seven hundred years ago to a spiritual force girdling the world for seven centuries, vitally effective today, vocal in me this hour. Leprosy CHAPTER IV Self-Conquest 

In Francis' time the dread plague of leprosy was fast spreading over Europe. Soldiers and Crusaders living in insanitary conditions in the Orient and coming into personal contact with the Arabs, contracted this disease and not knowing the deadly nature of their malady came back to their homes chiefly in Italy and France and in this way leprosy was brought into Europe. The lepers were indeed segregated; rude hospitals were hastily built for them in isolated places. They were dressed in special robes indicative of their condition and forbidden to enter the towns. 

One day as Francis was riding over the wooded hills near Gubbio his delicate nostrils detected a faint sickening smell; he paused, looked down from the hill, and seeing there a number of rudely scattered huts- he knew at once that the breeze had brought him a fetid breath from the Leprosium. 

Instantly spurring his horse, he rushed away, far up the hill, past the woods, out under the clear blue sky and the sunshine. There he paused.

Conflict 

Francis had passed through the throes of Conversion, but here was a voice calling to complete self-conquest. He waited irresolutely, looking down toward the place of the dread Leprosium. His heart reproached him, called him delicate weakling, afraid of suffering, afraid of the Cross. 

And only the night before when in ecstatic prayer, he had distinctly heard a voice saying, "Francis, come! Follow me!" And he had answered, "Yes, Lord, I come."

Yet at that sickening smell of leprosy, he had grown faint, hastened away-far up to the hill top. There he paused, there he prayed. An hour passed in the struggle for selfconquest, in that bitter fight to make the Will triumph over desire to escape, over human weakness, over fears, and nauseating repulsions.

As the sun was setting Francis, pale from conflict and but half conqueror over self, began the descent homeward. 

Stark heroism called upon him to go to the Leprosium and there give sympathy and alms. But he could not; compromise suggested that he would send money-all that he had. Yes, he would gladly do that. 14

A leper in the path

With these thoughts in his heart Francis regained his usual happy calm, and came out on the open plain riding at leisure. He noticed a movement in the grass on the path before him. He reined in his horse. Then as from nowhere a leper suddenly stood before him. He was clad in the horrible yellow robe, his face was almost hidden, his hand was extended mutely asking alms. Brother

This was a crisis. Francis got off his horse, he approached the leper, filled his hands with money-all that he hadthen paused irresolute. The leper stood motionless, his face concealed, then putting the coin in his bosom, he knelt in silent gratitude. Francis bent over him, whispered, "Brother"; and then deliberately pushing up the face-cloth, he kissed the leper's brow. A wonderful light shot into the leper's eyes as Francis kissed him.

Peace 

Francis turned away, almost overcome with horror and repulsion, secretly fearful, too, that he had contracted leprosy. He mounted his horse and rode madly away, leaving the leper still kneeling in the path. He had not gone far however until moved by a strange impulse he looked back. 

There was no leper in sight. Francis turned his horse around, he was filled with wonder. Any figure on the plain could be distinctly seen from where he stood, and the leper had not time to reach the woods. Francis recalled too, how instantaneously the leper had arisen in his path—and now as strangely he had disappeared. He remembered, too, the mystic light that had shot into his eyes, as in the hour of self-conquest, Francis kissed him.

Francis sought him for some time, all over the plain, calling him again "Brother," but no leper made answer. As 

Francis pondered over the strange incident, he grew happily calm, his fears left him he had not contracted leprosy; and a sweetness altogether indescribable filled his heart. A new meaning came into the words, “In as much as you did it unto the least of my little ones you did it unto Me." He laughed in joy at the thought-had he given alms to Christ? had he kissed the brow whereon had pressed the crown of thorns?

Thus had the hour of self-conquest ended in a great peace. 15

CHAPTER V 

Francis Goes to Rome

Francis' band of followers now numbered twelve-others were desirous of joining his band. The priests of the regular clergy, at first tolerant of these enthusiasts, were now seriously asking by what authority Francis and his followers should go around preaching the gospel.

Francis determined to take the matter to the Court of Rome, tell his story simply to the Pope-then Innocent III, get his blessing on the work, and abide by his decision. So 

Francis and his followers set out for Rome, begging on the way, preaching in all the towns and villages through which they passed, and resting at night in some rude shelter or in the friendly woods. 

Lateran Palace

On reaching Rome, Francis went boldly to the door of the Lateran Palace and asked to see the Pope. The astonished doorkeeper answered evasively-yet truly-that no one at that hour could see the Pope-he was taking his walk through the grounds, and then, an hour later, he must meet ambassadors from France. Francis humbly withdrew; the attendant smiled on closing the door, thinking doubtless in his heart, that this fool was more tractable than many others who had come, unannounced, to meet the Pope. 

Papal Gardens 

Francis was at peace, his time would come; if not today well, then, tomorrow. He was about to turn toward the crowded street, when instinctively his eye was caught by the sylvan charm of the papal gardens and whether with the recklessness of a fool, the irresistible impulse of a poet, or direct guidance of God-he, a beggar in peasant-tunic and sandals, strolled happily in the palatial sanctum almost singing aloud the Laudes (Hymns of Praise) ever surging in his heart—and thus on turning the second terrace Francis came face to face with the greatest potentate then on earth, Pope Innocent III. 

Never was contrast more complete-wealth, poverty; pride of place, no place; cares of state, care of birds; King- 

Pope, peasant-fool. 16

Francis meets the Pope

Francis bowed courteously. The Pope paused at sight of this strange figure; never had the privacy of his morning walk been so rudely violated. Francis spoke and acted with courteous dignity. He told his story simply-his vision when on the way to war, his father's anger, his call to repair the ruins of San Damiano, his Conversion; his great desire to preach God's love to all the world. The Pope listened with proudly amused indifference. He rather enjoyed this play of contrast; wondered dimly just what kind of mind and heart-so different from his own-might be functioning in that brown robed beggar, now in petition for some favorhe scarce knew what-and prostrate at his feet. 

Man finds pleasure in the homage of his fellow man. The low bow, the lips touching the hand, the oriental kotow, prostration, and kissing of the feet are human-all too human-expressions of class distinction, pride of place, and superiority of man over man. On what is this superiority based? Surely not on the reality of things,-that which endures through the ages. Pope Innocent III is today vaguely remembered as a proud King-Pope, but the beggar at his feet is a spiritual force effective through seven hundred years and powerful today. In the big realities the relative positions of King and beggar are reversed. 

The Pope gently dismissed Francis, continued his walk, forgot the beggar and his absurd request; and, at the next turn of the terrace, went back to the palace.

Francis arose somewhat bewildered, he had been treated indulgently as a fool, perhaps he was one; but perhaps, too, God needed a fool as purge for all this palatial proud wisdom that he saw around him. At heart he liked the Pope and he pitied him amid all this pride and pomp of circumstance, for he saw the good in his heart; and his faith recognized in him the Head of that Church which Francis ever held in reverence. So committing his cause to God and saying a special prayer for the Pope, he returned to his companions. 

A Dream 

And that night Pope Innocent had a strange dream. He was in the Lateran Palace, in the room known as speculum because of the glorious view stretching out beyond and best seen from its windows.

The majestic Lateran church, basilica of Constantine, known throughout the world as "the head and mother of all 17

churches" stood prominent in the vista. But as the Pope gazed this night the mighty edifice did suddenly seem to totter, the towers swung forward, huge cracks appeared in the wall, a crash seemed imminent. The Pope lay rigid with horror, unable to move even to lift his hands in prayer. 

Then a strange figure arose on the Lateran piazza right in front of the Church, a man in brown robe and with bare feet rushed right up to the falling façade and spread out his arms as if to keep it in place. But already the swaying front was bending over him, about to fall and crush him. 

The Pope turned his head away from the horror that must follow. But hearing no crash and looking again he saw that beggar in brown robe suddenly grown as tall as the church wall, and, as a caryatid--he swung the huge façade back into position, and the old basilica stood once more firm and erect.

Then the man-again a beggar in peasant garb on the piazza-looked up at the figure now standing at the window of the room speculum. Their eyes met and the Pope recognized the beggar with whom he had conversed that morning -a gentle fool from Assisi who wanted to form an Order in which men should literally live according to the gospel. 

The Pope gave a great sigh of relief as the dream happily ended. Then he gave orders that the poor men from Assisi should be sought for wherever they might be and brought into his presence.

Pope Innocent was troubled. So many sects tinged with politics were arising. In the beginning they seemed altogether good-just a healthy return of men to primitive 

Christianity, to basal righteousness. So had Peter Waldo started. So had his followers, the Poor Men of Lyons, continued for some time. But fanaticism, as a florid weed, appeared amongst them, and thirteenth century Puritanism, more acrid than that of the seventeenth, was threatening the catholicity of the Catholic Church-which condones human frailties and takes back repentant sinners into the fold. A crustal self-righteousness gathered over the sect--and in the end crushed it.

David of Dinant was preaching some wildly beautiful doctrine about the oneness of God with the world. God is everything and everything is God. Nature-stones, rocks, 18

gems, flowers, trees-holds God sleeping and peacefully dreaming. Sentient life, from insect to man, holds God awakening and first pleasurably alive. Man holds God grown awake and aware: whilst steadily evolving and advancing into god-fulfillment in the All. And so all things are touched with God, all live and breathe and move in God, all ultimately return to God. All things are by him and of him and in him-and God is all in All.

This mystic lover of nature and of life-fore-runner of modern Pantheism, held a message for the few, not the many. His theories were negligible, moreover they were, in the main, adjustable to a monistic theology. Pope Innocent felt a tang of their beauty, their lofty charm, and he smiled benignantly.

But the Albigensians and that most rigid sect of the Albigensian sect-the Cathari-were really subversive of Catholic doctrine. They knowingly or unknowingly, advocated a return to Zoroastrian dualism. They looked into the problem of evil and solved it on the lines laid down by 

Zoroastrian Mani. God (Ahura-Mazda) made all good things of earth; Satan (Ahriman) made all evil things. 

Spirit is good and of God; matter is evil and of Satan. Man is spirit and matter, soul and body, good and evil. Earth is the scene of conflict. The good shall ultimately prevail. All those who by the spirit put to death the deeds of the flesh shall live: and the victory of each soul over self hastens the hour of the final triumph of good when Ahura-Mazda (God) shall reign over all forever.

The sect known as Cathari-the Purified, carried this Neo-Manicheism to extremes. They abjured marriage; they scourged themselves and, as Flagellantes, they ran naked and bleeding through the streets proclaiming their doctrine of spirit mastery over flesh. They approved of suicide as a way of escape from matter.

As a result of this unnatural restraint upon nature many of the Purified broke mentally as well as physically. Their revolt took the form of a violent return to human natureeven to brutality and bestiality. An orgy of sensualism took place among some German fanatics. They became known as Luciferians and they furtively celebrated the Black Mass even in Rome.

And so the Pope was troubled and doubly cautious as to any new Order arising in the Church and asking his blessing. 19

His dream of the tottering Lateran basilica-so vivid in the night, was slowly fading.

Interview A rustle of the entrance curtain aroused him from reverie. 

Cardinal John, advocate of Francis, stood before the Pope and announced that Francis and his followers had been found and brought to the palace. They waited without. A tired yet coldly courteous King-Pope said, "Let them come in."

Again contrast and again cleavage of personalities, and again the piercing power of Francis-reader of hearts! His real power lay in his admiration, reverence, and even love for this haughty handsome Pope-King who heard his story with apparent indifference and easy weariness. So old the new, and so new even today is that impossible old. "My son," said the Pope, as Francis brokenly ended, "what you propose is not workable, for most men it is impossible. Live your own life, observe your own ideal of poverty, but do not strive to bind men who shall come after you, by your bonds. They are for you, and for the few who now share your enthusiasm, they are not for the many, not for men of a second warmth, men nurtured in a stiller flame. Theirs is for them, yours for you. Go, my son, in peace with God's blessing. 99 But Francis did not rise. The spirit was speaking within him and he knew its power. "Then, Lord Pope," he said gently, "it is not possible for men to be really Christian-that is Christ-like. And our 

Lord was in error when he said, 'Follow me,' and his words were meaningless when he said, 'Preach the gospel: live the gospel: take with you neither scrip nor staff, nor money in your purse as you go--be like me.' Lord Pope, we want to be Christians, we want to be like Christ. We ask only your blessing; we ask to work with you, with the Church, with the spirit of Love that lives and breathes in the gospel. We would love all God's world in God. We are Christians; let us give out to others that which has been given to us. Only grant us permission to preach with your blessing. God will do the rest." 

Pope Innocent was perplexed. The heart of the priest warmed under the robes of the King. He remembered when 20

he, too, had thought it possible to live literally in accordance with the gospel. That was long ago.

Blessing 

The Pope arose and gently bidding Francis to come near, he placed his hands on his head in blessing. He then told him to go and in the name of the Church, preach the gospel and live the life of Christ. And he added graciously that as his followers increased and when he had formulated a Rule of life somewhat more intelligible he should return to Rome for full papal approval.

Tonsure The Pope then ordered that tonsure should be given to Francis and his followers, so that with this official recognition of the Church he might preach in the towns and villages through which he passed, and, with approval of the Bishop -in churches and Cathedrals.

Francis looked into the eyes of the world-weary Pontiff and thanked him for the favor conferred. Then a great wave of living love arose in Francis and passed from him to the Pope. All were held for a time in a holy charm. Francis would gladly have died then and there for the man in regal robes before him-Pope, King, Father, Friend. Innocent was moved by Francis' unabashed affection, he felt its sincerity. Something of a re-birth of trust in man and belief in human dignity awoke within him. His smile was truly fatherly and lit with divine light as he said, "Go with God, Brother, and announce salvation for all as the Lord reveals it unto you."

And thus in love and in filial reverence for the Pope as Head of the Church the great Franciscan Order had its origin. 221

CHAPTER VI 

Clara, Founder of the Poor Clares

The Scifi family of Assisi were eminent for nobility, respectability, and piety. Donna Ortolana ruled her household in most approved mediaeval manner and her children were models of beauty and goodness. 

Childhood 

Clara, the eldest daughter, was fair; her hair a golden brown. Perhaps the blood of some barbarian of Lombardy ran in her veins. Her younger sister, Agnes, was dark, of smaller stature, and typically Italian, while baby Beatrice was as yet of no pronounced type though her eyes were blue. 

The children were at play in the grounds of the old feudal manor, but their talk and their play were strangely unlike that of children of today.

Children's Crusade 

Lover

It was the early thirteenth century. Mysticism was in the air. Dreams, visions, spirit-commands, immediacy of the unseen, trance-escape from realities were the everyday experience of men and women who lived in the glamour of the Crusades. It was the age that made possible that strangest of all historic happenings-the Children's Crusade. A wave of longing for martyrdom spread over Europe-martyrdom for the cause of Christ in the Holy Land. It affected all, but its call to the children was irresistible. They went, they died; and with God be the rest.

So Clara was talking to her sister Agnes of the joy of martyrdom, of the quick way to the visible presence of the Lord whose love even now flooded her soul. Clara's eyes were fixed on the vision of child-Heaven. 

Donna Ortolana's entrance into the garden was a breezy call back to earth. San Georgio

Just what takes place in the hearts of nuns when they fall prostrate in the sanctuary before the high altar thereby publicly ratifying the vows of their Religious Profession? 

Poverty and with it go wealth and all the pleasures and privileges that money can bring: Chastity-and there pass away wraith-like human love, home, motherhood, and possible children; Obedience-hardest of all. What positive good makes possible the acceptance of so much negation? 

It is Vocation, the call, the spirit-promise, the transvaluation of worldly values, the touch of God shattering the timesanctities, showing the eternal. 

Does this glamour last through the years? Not for all, for many it dies down in gray disillusion, in cynic regret. In this case only heroic will-power can force the observance of vows. There are many unhappy nuns. 

For others this hour of consecration glows yet brighter with the years. They are the twice born, the purified, the mystics, the saints, the Claras of the cloister. 

For the greater number, however, there is neither cynic disillusion nor sanctity. They grow old perplexed, perhaps, but peaceful. They keep an open window on the world; they look out kindly and bless them that pass, them that sleep in the lighted cities, them that rest in well-remembered graves.

They feel the mystery of life. They know the fierce fires of temptation, and they fall at times into sin. But they do not stay down, they pray, struggle, arise, triumph-and go their way sadder and wiser and kinder.

They that know good and evil are greater than they that know only good. They that stand abashed before the mystery of their own hearts do not try to understand nor explain the actions of others, neither do they condemn. They that have touched the deepest depths are more tolerant when on the height. They that have truly known God as at the hour of consecration are never again quite fitted for the world. They remain in the convent. They wait perplexed and pitiful, they die in the peace of God. 27

Social Reformer CHAPTER VII The Third Order 

Francis of Assisi was a social reformer, a revolutionarybut he advocated "revolution by consent." The Feudal system was then strongly entrenched in Italy and in all Europe. 

Francis saw its original need, even its necessity; and he recognized, too, its power for good when the feudal lord was good. Chivalry, Arthurian idealism, and the true Christian knight were born of Feudalism.

But there were evils-chief among them were the rivalry and the frequent clash of proud Houses-the Montagues and Capulets of that day;-the ease and the indifference with which petty factional wars were started and that raged interminably.

The Third Order, established by Francis, was designed to meet the needs of people leading every day lives in a troubled world. They were to keep the Commandments with exactitude; restore any ill-gotten goods; pay honest debts; respect those in authority and obey them in all that is not sinful; but they were forbidden to carry arms or to shed human blood. This latter requirement clashed with their oath of military service to their feudal lords. Hence they were to solicit this privilege from their masters in the name of the Christ who died for all and who said, "Thou shalt not kill."

There were men of all states of life in the Third Order; masters and lords and even poets and kings as well as serfs and retainers and vassals. So in many cases there was effected this "revolution by consent," but not in all. But in order to have full papal opinion and blessing on this revolutionary provision of the Third Order, an appeal was made to the Pope, then Honorius III. The subject was made the matter of a conclave, as the Pontiff perceived more plainly than Francis, the far reaching implications of this provision. 

Protests against it were pouring in from military men, lords, feudatory bishops, and kings; pleadings in behalf of it came in from members of the lower classes and the devout members of the Third Order. 28 28

Papal Decision 

At length a decision was made and it decreed that—those members now fully initiated into the Third Order and who were in daily life fulfilling all the other requirements of that state, and who sincerely in their hearts felt that they ought to obey their conscience rather than the commands of authority-were exempt from their oath of military service to their feudal lords.

Rome had spoken. There was consternation for a time, the whole feudal system seemed toppling; factional clashes ceased, as the petty Ruler knew not now on whom he might depend so many men had joined the Third Order. At length there was compromise. Meetings were held in the town halls, and representatives of all classes assembled to discuss their problems. In most cases there was amicable adjustment; and a "revolution by consent" between the 

Majores and the Minores was effected. 

Opposition 

But the Mayor of Faenza, a strong adherent of the Ghibelline party and a friend of the Emperor, saw truly how utterly disruptive of the feudal system and even of town patriotism this Third Order might become. There was tense rivalry among the little Republics of Italy, and their respective claims were settled by war. What would happen if rulers could not depend upon their subjects to bear arms? He learned to his dismay that almost a third of the men of Faenza had joined this new Order and would not fight at command of the Mayor.

In all other respects these people were desirable. They lived in peace with their neighbors; paid their debts; they were honest in giving tithes; they were helpful in all civic needs-but they would not bear arms, would not shed human blood, would not obey rulers who bade them fight for the Republic. And because of reverence for Francis and for the religious sensibilities of the members of the Order and their marked sincerity; perhaps, too, because of a sense of human justice deeper than factional patriotism-the soldiery who remained faithful to the Mayor of Faenza would not take up arms against their unarmed fellow townsmen.

The Mayor was in wrath; he would appeal to the Emperor; he would arouse all Italy against this lamb-like force more effective than that of wolves. 29

Cardinal Hugolin becomes Protector of the Third Order 

The controversy was again taken to the papal court. 

Cardinal Hugolin-soon to become Pope Gregory IX-always a friend to Francis and now with power to prove that friendship, caused all the scattered branches of the Third Order to be united in one Brotherhood. This was put under the special protection of the Cardinal himself with headquarters at Rome. The lambs had won; for in that age and time no power on earth could snatch its victims from the sheltering arms of Rome. Vision 

Francis was reverently glad. That night in vision he saw all men and women of the world as members of his Third Order. And no man bore weapons of war, and no hand shed human blood, and no man hated his fellow man. The lambs played with the wolves and enjoyed their protection; and his friends, the rabbits, were joyfully at play on the backs of adders; and hawks and vultures and all vicious birds of prey flew down to eat grain with the pigeons. All earth was at peace in millennial love.

We need a Francis of Assisi today. We need a power that can do for the "cannon fodder" of today what Francis did for the serfs of his day. We need a man who will challenge the rights of Nationalism over citizens as he challenged the rights of Feudalism over its vassals. We need a spiritual 

Power that can lift Humanity high over all nations of the earth, and proclaim aloud and with effective authority the commandment of Almighty God-THOU SHALT NOT KILL. 30 30

Francis in the Village Streets

Francis was troubled at the joyous acclaim and adulation of the throngs that gathered around him in every town and village which he passed. Iste est sanctus homo! "There goes the holy man. Behold the saint!" passed rapidly from lip to lip and from heart to heart as he appeared. Francis felt deeply his own emptiness and unworthiness as through him passed God's message of peace and goodwill and love to those near him in the crowded streets; to those who on the 

Umbrian hills sang songs of joy because of his coming. No man was more deeply loved and honored in his own day and amid his own people than Francis of Assisi. His coming to a town meant a holiday; his presence brought peace. 

Francis knew that God was the strength of his apparent strength; the light in him that the people loved; the voice in his heart singing of peace.

But while Francis himself was not at all deceived as to his apparent power, he felt that the people were deceived. And so one day, in carnival time, the day before Ash Wednesday, he disappeared from their sight. Alone with God

Francis was never a better friend of man than when he walked alone with God. There went out from him radiations of love and gentle pity and understanding and infinite compassion for mortals-near and far, known and unknown, sinners and sinless; for all life that suffers and dies todayhuman and sub-human; for all life new-born, for mothers in dread, mothers in child-birth, mothers in release and blessedness, for babes in cradles and all little children; for the Pope on his gilded throne and for Clara in her cloister. The saints have found a door of life that opens on the eternal. That is the secret of their patient peace. The light from that open door plays in their enigmatic eyes as they watch the pageant of the passing hour-the joys and sorrows of the little day of time. Nothing is final that is not eternal. 

And One Dark Night with Stars 

A hut of rushes

Francis left the shore and prepared his retreat nearer to the center of the island. His wants were few; he had wine and bread and water from the rills. At the base of a low mound he built a hut of rushes; just outside lay the trunk of a fallen tree. He fashioned a seat in the log, made it comfortable with leaves and grasses. He walked on the moundwith God; he meditated on the log, with God; he rested in the hut of wattels in the peace of God. 

Francis stood on the top of the mound under a night sky studded with stars. What are the stars? Are they the 

Islands of the Blessed? Are they the gradient homes of souls on their way to God? Should he himself pass on successively from star to brighter star, to ever larger, better, brighter star-thro' infinity, thro' eternity, unto the Beatific

Vision, into the glory-light of God?-yet on the way, even as now, with God? 

Were the stars toys that the Lord delights to roll and control? Order is writ large across the night sky studded with stars. That lordly retinue of constellations is passing in 32

controlled motion-slowly, ceaselessly. A thing moving presupposes a Mover. No object moves itself; no star ever started to move without initial outside of itself. That Mover, that Power from without is-God. 

These constellations move in law-bound sequence; the same yesterday, today, and tomorrow. Orion, glorious group of stars, still following the Pleiades; still attended by his faithful dogs, Canis Minor, Canis Major, and ever more defended by first magnitude Sirius, the Dog Star. Even thus they rose and passed over the builders of the pyramids; over "golden Mycenae," old when Homer was young; over 

Babylon the Great; over the night watches of shepherd astrologers on Chaldean plains;-and tonight that retinue passes in undimmed brightness and in the same law-bound sequence over an island in Lake Trasimeno and a lone thinker who watches a night sky studded with stars.

Law-physical, psychical, and moral-pre-supposes a Law Maker, a Law Controller, a Power having in itself in superabundance that which it infuses into other objects. That 

Law-Maker, that Controller, that Intelligence, that Morality is-God.

Hour of Temptation: Dark Night of the Soul 

And one night in mid-Lent the charmed solitude became a longing loneliness. Francis was human, all too human; he wanted to be a man with men. He longed to see kindly human faces, to hear the voice of friends speaking familiarly and with love. Then, too, was it wise to leave his young 

Order alone so long? Perhaps his Brothers, particularly the young men of his Order, were suffering in temptation even as he. He would go back tomorrow. It was all a mistakethis elusive pursuit of God thro' ever ascending planes of solitude. 

And then the future saint entered into the dark night of the soul. Demons of doubt assailed him-doubt of God, of man, of his own life as lived so far-of his Order. The 

Pope was right-men can not live according to the literal Franciscan Rule. All was futile; he had followed some willo-the-wisp and thought it God. Now it had sunk in the marsh and the night lay in darkness unlit by a gleam.

Despair is an ally of lust; it secretly undermines the barriers of the will so that the demons of Desire more readily rush into the heart and reign there unrestrained. Francis would be a lover, a husband, a father. He wanted a home, a wife, and little children. He would know relief andrelease. Sheltering darkness covered him as he sank to the depths in the dark night of the soul.

Penance 

Francis slept in exhaustion far into the following day.

Francis had reached that stage of hunger when the gnawing ceases and a contented unexpectancy sets in that just waits for the long sleep. He shrank from the thought of foodtomorrow, perhaps, but not tonight. One long night of penance in expiation of his fall; and then, tomorrow, perhaps he would go down to the beach; he would hail a passing vessel; he would give up this mad impossible. The night was chill; there was snow on the distant mountains. A beautiful flurry of flakes played over the island then hurried away to the distant hills. CHAPTER IX Francis Goes on a Crusade 

Francis was a child of his time as well as of all time. It was right to save the holy places of Jerusalem from profanation at the hands of the Saracens. It was right to keep these sacred shrines open and free so that devout pilgrims and penitents in expiation for their sins, and Christians of all countries might go there and pray and be pacified and purified.

Francis could not understand how any Ruler could refuse this privilege, and he felt that if he could only get into the presence of the Sultan and tell him the truth about these holy places and the story of the great Lover of mankind honored there-he would give orders that Christians should go thro' Jerusalem unmolested; and so the object of the 

Crusade would be effected without the shedding of blood. 

Francis was both naïvely wrong and eternally right. 

So Francis went on the Crusade. The expedition reached 

Egypt. Here the siege of Damietta was in progress. Francis went among the Christian camp striving to dissuade the soldiers from fighting. Many did, indeed, join the Third 

Order and desert from the army.

Sultan Malek-el-Kamel Francis succeeded in some way in getting into the city, and he went boldly up to the palace of the Sultan. Now it chanced that Sultan Malek-el-Kamel had heard of Francis and when, to the surprise of all, Francis stood at the door the Sultan ordered that he should be brought in. 

Again the subtle charm of goodness won over all Court formalities. Francis and Sultan Malek-el-Kamel met as man to man and each liked the other. Francis saw neither malice nor ill will in the heart of the man whom the Christian world held as all evil.

The Sultan listened indulgently as Francis told the story of the great Lover of all mankind-Saracens, Jews, Buddhists, as well as Christians. They talked far into the night. 

This was a new view of Christianity. Sultan Malek readily agreed that Francis and his followers should go unmolested to the holy places in Jerusalem. "And," he said, "whilst there pray for me that I may know which religion is the better.' "9 40

Ordeal by Fire 

As thus encouraged Francis asked that on the morrow he might go through the Ordeal by Fire to prove that his religion was right. He begged that a bonfire be made in the courtyard and that any caliph or devout Mohammedan should run with him thro' the flames and that he who should come out unhurt by the fire should be considered champion of the better Cause.

The Sultan stood in wonder at Francis' devout sincerity; he said that he doubted if any man in his kingdom would take that risk or pay that price for his religion. Suddenly a messenger arrived saying that the besiegers were making an attack on the walls of Damietta and that battle was imminent. The Sultan suddenly ceased to be a man happily at ease with a loving fellow man-he was a Leader prepared for battle. Before going out, however, he gave orders that

Francis should be guarded while in the city and that, if possible, he should be escorted far out on the way to the 

Holy Land. 

Fall of Damietta

The city of Damietta fell before the besiegers the next day. 

Confusion and horror and hell rioted with Death. Francis was in the midst of the wild soldiery sacking a city in flames. "Wrong, wrong, all wrong! There is no God here. This is not the way to the sepulchre of Him who died praying for his murderers. How long, O Lord, how long shall man take evil for good? O God, forgive us all, we know not what we do!"-so lamented Francis in the streets of fallen Damietta.

Francis was thoroughly disillusioned in regard to war and the glamor of Crusaders. He felt futile; utterly out of place; and as helpless as had been the white rabbit that, when pursued by the hunters, had taken refuge under his cloak, even in his arms. Yet he had sheltered that trembling creature and thrilled to its charm as later he had put it down safe in the nest.

So would God take Francis under his care and set him down somewhere safe from the savage soldiery then shrieking thro' the streets of Damietta. 

Francis is rescued 

And suddenly he found himself, he knew not how, no more than the rabbit-at peace on a wooded terrace in the garden of Sultan Malek. 41

Prayer in the Sultan's Garden 

Fighting raged around the royal fortress but it did not fall; and as Francis prayed for all the combatants, friend and foe, Christian and Saracen, mothers, wives, and little children-there was a lull, a cessation of savagery, a truce, a resurgence of humanity into the hearts of men. 

The demons of war high over the strife, fled away and there was peace in the tortured city. Return It was rumored throughout Italy that Francis was dead. 

He had last been seen in the market place of Damietta the day of its fall. He wandered bewildered through the streets, begging the soldiers to cease looting and killing; imploring them to put out the flames.

Then he was surrounded by a troop of Musselman guards, and just what happened then no one knew. Francis was no doubt killed as he knelt in prayer, or taken prisoner. And so it was rumored in the Christian camp and quickly spread throughout Italy that Francis had died in the Crusade. 

Time passed; and with it came many changes to the infant Order as established by Francis. With no longer the appeasing personality of the Founder among them, there were dissensions among the brethren. Some would have the Rule observed in the letter just as Francis had formed it. Others contended that strict observance of the Rule as given by the Founder was-impossible. Here was the beginning of that breach in Franciscan ranks that widened in the coming years and split up into innumerable sub-communities with the passing centuries.

Francis had perhaps acted unwisely in leaving his Order at that formative period. But there are forces more compelling than worldly wisdom. Then, too, Francis had admitted all applicants that came into his Order without any period of probation. The only requirement was "Go sell all that you have and give to the poor; and come, follow the 

Christ of the Cross." 

In the infancy of the Order and with the burning charity of Francis melting down all protuberances-this was workable. But in the dying down of that flame these crude protuberant personalities grew dominant. One Brother would have them appeal to the Pope for a general Protector; another would appeal to the Pope for permission to hold property; and, for the honor of God-to solicit funds to 42

erect a magnificent church; another, looking after the interests of the Poor Clares, and hearing that his charges had been rudely treated by some rioters in a masquerade parade, went to the Papal court and loudly demanded a Bull of Excommunication against any one who in the future would dare to insult his charges-the Poor Clares. Still others, touched by the rise of the Universities and seeing the growing prestige of the Dominicans because of their learning, openly advocated that the Franciscans, too, should go to study in the University of Bologna, Paris, or Rome. 

Now all this was perhaps good in itself, and good for special interests-but it was not the Order of Friars Minor as established by Francis. Humanly speaking it would have been better had Francis really been put to death by the Musselman guards that surrounded him in Damietta-instead of being spirited away to the Court gardens as commanded by the Sultan. But here again the divine way was bigger and better than the human; and Francis because of the death of self in the valley of humiliation which, at his return, awaited him, lives today-more humanly, divinely, abundantly.

There is no confusion known to mortals more hopeless than that of finding a life's work vaporized. Francis at his return to Assisi found himself in this confusion. There was no great joy at his return, safe from the Saracens. His best friends had gone to live as hermits or waited irresolute and perplexed. Something primal and strong and beautiful had departed from the Order.

Francis blamed himself for this state of affairs. He should not have gone away from the young Brotherhood; should not have attempted that impossible conversion of the Saracens; should not so completely have trusted those new men of the Order—they were young, they were untrained, undisciplined-perhaps unenlightened by God-hence they had not been able to ascend to the heights of sanctity on which his Order had been founded. It was all his fault-he had built the palace of the Impossible and it had collapsed. He stood in the ruins.

In this confusion he seemed to be again at the Papal court, where he had been treated indulgently as a fool. And again he heard the fatherly voice of Pope Innocent III saying, "This Rule of yours cannot be observed by men. It is too hard. It is for you and your immediate followers-lit by your love. It is not for others." 43

Alone

The Pope was right and-wrong. Francis drained the cup of suffering to its dregs in this second awful disillusionment and defeat in the valley of humiliation. 

After due consideration; after recognizing his inability even to comprehend what had happened while he was away; his utter helplessness to control the strong human elements surging around him-Francis solemnly announced his resignation as Head of the Order. It was accepted. 

Elias of Cortona was made Superior; and Cardinal Hugolin, later Pope Gregory IX, was appointed Protector General of the Brotherhood.

This was again a weak and human way out, according to worldly wisdom. But in some divine Plan it is decreed that a man shall go alone up to the heights of sanctity. The clinging of hands—even the holiest and dearest-can but retard.

And on the height it is the soul alone with God-the spiritflame fused with Divinity: sanctity, ecstacy-solitude.

Francis again sought strength in the calm of the mountains. Their call was irresistible. In this hour of grief and apparent frustration Francis fled to their strengthening solitude. He chose a cave under a wooded peak from which he had a far-flung outlook over the Umbrian panorama; and Assisi, the beloved, lay well in view. 

Hour of Decision 

Francis was about to make a momentous decision. Should he seek sanctification as a hermit in mountainous solitudes, in intimate touch with God-and thence pray efficaciously for his fellow men?-or did his way to sanctity lead through the valleys, the busy marts of men, the troubled cities, the weary ways of fellow mortals? Choice said, "God calls you by way of the mountain solitudes." But he feared that human choice. Had not Christ walked with men? 

So Francis sent a messenger to Clara, then Abbess of

San Damiano, and to Brother Sylvester telling them of his soul's conflict and asking each one to importune God to make known his will through them. And Clara prayed to know God's will for Francis and Sylvester prayed likewise.

Three weeks later Masseo, the messenger, returned to learn their answer. And Clara, in cloistered San Damiano said, "The Lord made known to me that Francis is to save his soul in labors with his fellow men." And Sylvester, 44

preaching in the village, said, "The Lord made known to me that Francis is to save his soul by labors for his fellow men."

Choice Francis heard the decision in silence, then half reluctantly turning from his own choice, he and Masseo slowly descended the mountain. 

Return to the Village

A strange peace came into his heart as he went down the slope, even an ecstatic joy; some new power struggled for birth within him. He touched tenderly the young birch saplings as he passed, he blessed the fur-folk as they scurried by.

The Birds Rejoice 

Suddenly, on a field from which grain had recently been garnered, Francis saw hundreds of birds feeding on the wheat-grains that had been left by the reapers. He stood entranced at their grace, their beauty, their varied forms and colors; and all at once he knew them as his little brothers and sisters-bright bits of God. He looked on them with new born love. The birds felt this psychic power; they gathered around him and fed unafraid as he walked among them. He stooped and touched them, he stroked tenderly their glossy heads and necks, their fluttering wings-and they extended them yet more to meet his touch-for Francis and the birds were all in the charmed circle of Love. 

Francis Preaches to the Birds

Then Francis preached to the birds. He told them of their beauty and of the God who had made them thus beautiful. He reminded them of the Father who provides them with food even as now in abundance, and gives them trees in which to sing and to build their nests, and urges them to mating bliss, and wakes to quivering life and joy and song the little eggs that they lay in their nests.

The Birds Depart in Song 

And the birds were profoundly impressed by this discourse. They opened their beaks, and craned their heads far forward, and raised their wings and looked up adoringly at 

Francis their big brother, their little god. Then Francis blessed them with the sign of the Cross and bade them rise 45

now and go their ways as the sun was going down over the hills and perhaps some bird duties were calling them. And then all the birds-having made obeisance with their beaks on the ground and their adoring eyes uplifted to him—arose from the field and separated into various groups-each in its kind and flew high into the air. Then simultaneously they burst into song-wonderful, concordant, heavenly.

Francis listened enraptured and understood; and he recognized the new power that was born in him that houras love of living things; tenderness for beauty-in flower and tree and bird and beast, in sunset on the hills, and evening thrilling with the song of birds-love of all God's world-just love.

And so Francis went down from the solitude of the mountain, and as the will of God-made known to him by Clara and Sylvester-went back to his mission among men. 46

CHAPTER X Demons Over Assisi 

The news soon spread that Francis had left the mountains and was returning to Assisi. Crowds gathered around him as he passed through villages and towns, many of his Brothers came to attend him and welcome him back amongst them. 

Francis and his followers slept that night in a field overlooking Assisi. He was weary; he would just rest tonight and finish the journey tomorrow.

A Vision 

And he, while sleeping, had a vision. He saw horrible demons pouring down vials of wrathful fumes and flames over the city. And wherever they fell there was strife. Men rose against men and brother against brother, and contention, ill will, fierce hatreds-tormented the city. Francis knew that this was the hour of the dominance of evil. He arose in his dream and prayed for his beloved Assisi. And he called to the demons visible and invisible and bade them depart. He held up the Crucifix and blessed Assisi and again commanded her enemies to leave her in peace. He cried with a loud voice saying, "In the name of Almighty 

God I command you evil spirits to depart."

At the sound of his own voice in loud imprecation Francis awoke. A few hours later, still early morning, and though troubled in mind by the vision, Francis went on to Assisi. 

As he entered the town he saw preparations for battlesoldiers hurrying hither and thither and quickly mounting their horses and joining companions on the way. What had happened?

Feudal War 

Now it chanced that contention and a great quarrel had broken out between the Bishop of Assisi and the podesta (mayor). The matter was indeed trifling but neither would yield. It seemed that they hated one another without cause. 

Yet the quarrel flashed into hot anger, so that each called on his feudal forces and prepared to settle the matter by contest.

Francis heard of this quarrel, saw the preparation for battle and suddenly recalled his vision. Not the Bishop, not the podesta was in fault; they were victims of the 47

demons that had poured down vials of wrath and ill will and hatred over the city.

News spread that Francis had returned and was even then entering the city. The soldiers stopped and stared as he passed.

Francis sent messengers to the Bishop and to the mayor. 

He pleaded with them that they cease preparations for fighting until they would hear what he had to tell them. 

But with the entrance of Francis into the city, a great change had come into the hearts of the people. They relaxed, the momentous cause about which they were quarreling did not seem so momentous. Peace was returning and sanity. 

The city shuddered and snapped tension as if suddenly released from a snarl. What were they fighting about? Nobody knew. Why should they hate one another-why? Feelings changed, the Bishop recalled many courtesies on the part of the podesta, and the young and somewhat arrogant podesta recalled his old reverence for the good Bishop. "We are in error," they said, "let us be friends. Why should our followers hurt one another."

And so when Francis appeared there was already reconciliation in the air.

Laudes Francis requested that the two friendly enemies should meet together in the Bishop's courtyard. Then he sent hist singing brothers there, telling them to sing to the assembled multitude in presence of the Bishop and the podesta his well known Laudes (Hymns of praise). And at the end of the canticle these singing brothers should say that in honor of this reconciliation Francis had just written another strophe to his Laudes-which they would gladly sing. And clearly and sweetly they sang: "And blessed be my Lord for those who for thy sake can pardon give; and bear-the malice and mistakes of men.”

Fiesta The old Bishop and the proud podesta clearly understood what Francis meant by these lines. Their hearts were touched, they clasped hands in presence of the astonished people who had gathered in the courtyard. 

Then the Bishop asked that in honor of the return of Francis to Assisi, a free day should be given to all the people. 

The podesta graciously assented, adding that he would make 48

it a fiesta also, and that all-children and toilers, old and young, all feudal retainers of the city-should have this day free for a great holiday. 

And so instead of meeting in battle array, the soldiers dismounted, and joined in the general gaiety; and there was that day reconciliation and peace and good will in all hearts and a great fiesta for every man and woman and child in 

Assisi. What wrought the change? 

Naïve? Well, yes; credulous, carefree, quite mediaeval.

The demons were pouring down hatred into the hearts of men. At Francis' prayer and exorcism they fled. Then hatreds ceased and Assisi held a fiesta.

No question as to whence the demons came; what they were: why they happened to quarrel over Assisi; whither they went as in sulphurous fumes they departed; why they delighted in strife and in evil; what was their nature? their origin?

Men of every age have sought to find the forces that work through phenomena-physical and psychical. To each age its own explanation.

But the facts remain that hate and blind unreason were in the hearts of men urging them to war; and then suddenly these feelings changed, and these same men were filled with peace and good will. What wrought the change? What substituted the gay Assisian festival in the place of feudal war? 

And today men's minds are filled with anger and ill will urging the world to war. What can change these feelings?

What can bring peace and good will into the hearts of men and substitute a world festival for a world war? What does science say? What does psychology say?

Philosophy according to Kant, posits noumena as the substance of phenomena. Spencer calls this force manifesting itself through phenomena-the Unknowable. This is mere word jugglery. Let science, psychology, philosophy say honestly that they have no answer to the questions-What works behind phenomena-physical and psychical? What motivates or actuates mass emotion? What makes the madness that in turn makes war?

We all know of hatreds that suddenly die down; and of pity, remorse, and tears taking the place of satiated rage. 

Why? what brought about the change? 

What is man? O scientists, let your white mice and guinea 49

pigs alone, and turn to the study of man-man as a whole, body and mind, man the mysterious, the many-faceted, the 

Unknown. O psychologists, search for the psyche in man, search till you find it. O philosophers, lovers of wisdom, seek truth; look for it through all phenomena, physical and psychical; through noumena, through the Unknowablefollow the glint boldly and unashamed even though it lead you to demons pouring down wrath over a sleeping city and to a holy man whose prayer has power to drive these evil influences away. 

Prayer-that radial wave flashing between man and God -perhaps truth lies hidden there; seek it anywhere, everywhere, O Lovers of wisdom, but seek till you find Truth.

Comets 

In ancient times, in the middle ages and later, the appearance of a comet was considered as a portent of evil; war, pestilence, assassination of rulers, and civil uprisings followed in its wake. A comet appeared, visible even by day, in the streets of Rome-just before the assassination of 

Julius Caesar. What can be the connection between a flashing light hurrying through the heavens and war, pestilence, assassination of rulers upon earth? Is it mere coincidence? are these tales legendary-based upon old myths and churchsuperstition? Or is it possible that virulent vapors from incandescent masses of matter hurtling through the stratosphere may seep down into our atmosphere and prove an irritant to the minds and bodies of men? We all know that excessive heat arouses irritation in children. They cry, they seem to suffer-perhaps being younger their skins are more sensitive to the rays streaming down upon them from a comet. In adolescent minds and in full grown men this invisible shower of radiations falling upon the earth may arouse anger, extreme irritability leading to uprisings, assassination of rulers or of any other power claiming to rule over them and being in reality so impotent to avert everyday distress.

There are many names for the same thing, many unexplanatory explanations for the same occurrence. That new light, longer than a star, now scurrying through the skywhat is it? A comet, harbinger of evil; a sign of war; a hot breath of anger of the gods because of iniquity in the hearts of men; flaming demons pouring down hatred and ill will over the earth as they pass;—or irritant exhalations of 50

incandescent matter exploding in upper air and seen as shooting stars? 

Comets are so says today-parts of our solar system swinging in ellipsoidal orbit around our sun-predictable as to time of return. Halley's comet is visible to earth every seventy years. Reckoning backward-it was in all probability, Halley's comet that appeared at the time of the assassination of Julius Caesar. But as to the real nature of comets, their possible influence upon men in passing, the effects of their fumes, radiations, atmospheric disturbances on the regions over which they pass-there is no explanation. 

We are not believers in legends-whether they be the myths of Greece or the saint-heroes of the Middle Ages. We are not even skeptical with the frank dignity of doubt; nor are we cynics discerning guile or ignorant superstition as the actuating force under all symbolism. We are honestly agnostic-we do not know. We ask science, philosophy, Religion to unite; to focus all their powers on the old, old, never explained, yet ever new-problem of Evil. We reject age-old dualism, Manicheism in all its forms and phases old and new. We are monists, theologic and scientific. Credo in unum Deum. We wait perplexed; we ask study, clear thought, and unbiased investigation of the bases of legend and myth. 51

CHAPTER XI Count Otto-Crusader Castle on the Height 

One day as Francis reached the little mountain village of Gubbio he found great rejoicing among the people. For old 

Count Otto, lord of the castle on the height, rode smiling in the procession that wended its way to the church. What had happened? Count Otto had been hitherto inaccessible. He dwelt alone in his lordly castle on the height save for a few retainers who had remained faithful to his interests while he was away in the Holy Land.

Count Otto had returned from the Crusade a disillusioned and embittered man. He had been at the seige of Acre, he had taken part in the bloody triumph at Damietta. He had seen there the horrors that Francis had seen-but he was participant in blood. The reaction in him was hate and scorn even of his fellow comrades in the Crusade; in Francis reaction took the form of a pitying love for both victor and vanquished.

Bad Wolf of Gubbio 

On returning home after a long absence and silence, the Count found that his heirs had taken possession of his estate; he had to fight for re-possession of his rights. He won brutally and, having put to death his favorite nephew who was in the revolt, he lived henceforth in lonely bitterness. His exactions on the villagers for overdue tithes were almost unbearable. Especially did he demand in tribute all their young and tender lambs-and so the people called him the bad wolf of Gubbio.

Effect of the Coming of Francis 

But on the rumor that Francis was coming, Count Otto descended from the hill and rode into the village. Strange to say the people greeted him kindly; some new spirit was awake in the village. And when the crowd, formed to welcome Francis, gathered around the Count, he grew human, genial, generous. 

Count Otto remembered Francis. He recalled Francis' frantic efforts to stop the butchery at Damietta. He still 52

saw that ecstatic face as it passed thro' the ranks of rude soldiers exhorting them to lay down their arms, to try to convert the Saracens rather than take their town and kill them for resisting. "It is wrong, all wrong," he cried, "This is not the meaning of the Crusade. Stop fighting-be Christians thou shalt not kill." So Francis had exhorted, so prayed, so passed-unheeded and apparently ineffective. 

But now that same spirit was upon the village; none knew it better, none felt it more keenly than bitter old Count Otto. 

He smiled at Francis, fraternized with the crowd, grew kindly human. Later he ceased to demand the harsh tribute of lambs; became a true friend of the village; and at the end of his life he was known familiarly far and near as the good wolf of Gubbio. 

This story as given in the concrete picturization of the time is now known simply as a legend showing Francis' strange power over animals. In the legend a fierce wolf, under kindly understanding and fair treatment, does actually undergo metamorphosis into a lamb.

Good Wolf of Gubbio 

Once upon a time there was a fierce wolf that disturbed the kindly village of Gubbio. Its depredations upon the farmers' barnyards were becoming unbearable: chickens, lambs, sheep were carried off nightly by this wolf; and now even Nicky, the black cat, had been caught and carried off and her shrieks whilst being devoured alive had aroused the children from their sleep and filled them with terror. And so the farmers and the youth of the village came together and decided that this very night they would lie in wait for that wolf and shortly end his victoriously vicious career. 

Francis on the Mountain in Prayer

In the meantime Francis of Assisi, the holy man, had ascended the mountain to spend the night in prayer and commune with God. He fell into an ecstacy-so the story runsand heard not nor heeded the wild animals of the mountain that gathered around him. They drew near, their red eyes lit the encircling darkness, they watched and waited, yet they did no harm; perhaps they sensed the psychic power of sanctity. Some lay down in deep repose under the spell, some stood erect visibly trembling, whilst others slunk away into the woods. 53

Recognition 

And early that next morning as Francis was descending from the mountain he paused upon a low hill overlooking the village of Gubbio. He heard angry voices, the sound of blows, and the cries of a hated creature in torment. As he drew near, the farmers paused somewhat confused-all knew the holy man and his presence brought peace. The shouting ceased, the tugging at the rope that held the wolf, the blows; the wolf stopped snarling, he stood still, his gaze was fixed with fierce recognition upon the saint. It had doubtless been one of the beasts that had watched Francis in ecstacy a few hours ago; perhaps he had been one of those that had slunk away. "What is that you do, my comrades?" said Francis.

The tale was soon told-the attacks upon the barnyard, unbearable conditions, the killing of Nicky, the cat, and the terror of the children. "And even now," said a rustic, gaining more courage as he spoke, "even this night he killed and ate a calf. Well, he got a good supper but it was his last one. We are going to put that wolf to death."

The wolf pleads Guilty 

Francis approached the wolf. "You're guilty," he said.

And then to the dismay of all, that wolf shook his head gravely in affirmation of the charge. He acknowledged he was guilty. Francis bent down and listened as the wolf seemed to be speaking in a language that saints understand. "He tells me," said Francis, "that he was starving last night and that is why"-here the wolf shook his head quite understandingly as if in full corroboration of what was said. "And moreover," continued Francis, "he promises me that if a little food be left for him at the edge of the wood, he will never again molest your"--but here the wolf shook his head in decisive negation, saying mutely "never again.

Pity works a Miracle 

A change was slowly coming over the hearts of the simple farmers. They felt the soft persuasive voice of goodness and kinship with all life that suffers. And when Francis turned with winning smile to the crowd and said, "Will you trust him?"-a sturdy youth stepped forth and unfastened the rope that held the savage beast. All expected it to snarl, even to bite the hand that set it free, and then to dash madly 54

away into the woods. But to the utter amazement of all, that wolf stood upright, shook the blood from out its eyes, and then came forward and lay at Francis' feet. The saint stretched forth his hand in pity and rested it upon the bowed and bloody head, and then-took place the miracle. For, says the chronicler, right then and there, before the eyes of all-that wolf did change into a lamb.

This wolf, continues the narrative, did never again do harm to any living creature. It followed Francis thro' the streets of Gubbio as docile as a dog. The people grew accustomed to its presence; they called it "Brother Francis' brother."

All knew the story of its metamorphosis-and yet some doubted. For this strange creature did at times appear to be a wolf, and then again a huge misshapen sheep, and then a lamb-according to the mind of the man who looked at it; but the little children always saw it as a lamb.

Little Brother of Saint Francis 

The people fed it faithfully according to promise and it lived to an old age. And one day the wolf wandered into the market-place and there lay down very quietly and died. The people carried him out to the hill overlooking the village and buried him on the spot of his transformation. They erected over his grave a crude headstone and they wrote thereon these words: "Here lies the good wolf of Gubbiolittle brother of Saint Francis." 55

CHAPTER XII Chivalry Ceremony of Knighthood 

On one occasion as Francis and his followers approached an ancient feudal castle, the estate of Duke Orlando, lord of Montefeltro, it chanced that the ceremony of knighthood was in process. All the neighboring nobility had been invited to the celebration, and amid flying banners, blare of trumpets, pomp of circumstance-lords and ladies were thronging the courtyard and hastening to the chapel where with all mediaeval pomp and solemnity a young man was kneeling in presence of the priest and going through the formula from which he should arise a knight. 

Chivalry has been well called the flower of Feudalism, and the ideal Christian knight was the most exquisite bloom of chivalry. 

At sight of so great magnificence and lavish expenditure of wealth some of Francis' followers protested at this waste while so many men were in want; they even thought that such men must be evil; but Francis, knowing their hearts, reproved them. He said the chapel ceremony of knighthood was high and noble, calling men to Christ-like ideals, moreover, loveliness and splendor and wealth and pomp are not in themselves evil. And looking kindly at the gorgeously attired lords and ladies Francis added: "God is also their master, He can call them when He will and make them good and holy. Judge no man." 

Francis Speaks to the Courtiers

After the Mass was ended and the young knight, weak from his long fast and the night watch, was taken into the privacy of the castle for rest and refreshment-the throngs again gathered in the courtyard. Francis looked on them with love, and climbing to a ledge overhanging the court, he began to speak.

There was a hush of amazed wonder in the crowd as the well known "little poor man" appeared on the ledge above and blessed them.

Francis felt the force of contrast-a beggar, wealth; lowly estate, pride of place; mendicancy, regal power. He felt too, his own dignity and inherent nobility, so partly in explanation, partly in justification of himself his first words were: 56

"Tanto è il bene ch'io aspetto 

Ch'ogni pena m'è diletto" "So great is the good to which I look forward that present pain delights me.' He then spoke with great sweetness and concern on the vanity of all things that pass with the little day of time. He begged them to gain for themselves eternal values. 

Francis' Sermon

He spoke of his Third Order with its loud call to righteousness, to sincerity in the unseen, to observance of all the Commandments of God, more especially that commandment which forbids a man to shed the blood of a fellow man. 

They listened, they knew well the controversy then raging to which he referred when he said that a man should not bear arms nor shed human blood. Some agreed with him, some disagreed, but all listened, for Love was speaking and it was as manna to the hearts of them that heard-for each heard just what he needed.

At the end of the sermon the Brothers sang the verses that Francis had composed-well known hymns of peace and praises of God's Love. Then bidding the revellers a gentle farewell, Francis turned to leave the place.

La Verna 

But he had not gone far when Duke Orlando overtook him. The young nobleman had been mightily moved by the words of Francis, he too would gladly win for himself eternal values. And so in gratitude, in a spirit of almsgiving, in reverence for penance and prayer, and in vicarious sacrifice-Orlando offered Francis a tract of land on the wooded height of his estate La Verna-an ideal place for retirement, contemplation, and prayer. Little did Francis or Duke Orlando know then what miraculous secret lay hid in La Verna-for that wooded peak, Monte Alvernia, later became the scene of Francis' stigmatization.

Stigmatization 

In a cavern on the western height facing the setting sunthere appeared a seraphic vision, a wild glory of light holding a cross, and there was stretched upon it a tortured form just like the Christ which had spoken to Francis so long ago in the church of San Damiano. Then there took place that 57

mystical projection of self into another whereby Francis in the pangs of ecstacy became the Christ on the cross. 

And when the vision faded away and when Francis' spirit came out from trance, it found his body lying almost lifeless on the ground, and as the eyes of the mystic slowly awakened they beheld in his own hands and feet and side the marks of the wounds of the Man on the cross." * 

Francis was never the same physically or mentally after his experience on Mount Alvernia. He was partially blinded by the light of the seraphic vision and never fully recovered his sight. He was more than ever the recluse, hermit, poet, mystic-man of God. Earth shadows were receding; more and more he played in intimacy with the eternal Ideas which are the only realities.

As thus inspired he wrote his Canticle of the Creatures, better known as the Sun Song. In it he calls upon all creatures of the earth to help him praise the Lord. 

Loudest of all should be the voice of Brother Sun; and next in sweet accord should rise the praises of our sister, the moon. Mother Earth with all her teeming life should praise the Lord. And brother Fire, so bright and beautiful; and sister Water ever chaste; and winds and rains and rainbows and night with all her stars should join Creation's chorus singing Laudes to the Lord.

And all the wildlings of the woods, more especially his little brothers and sisters, the birds, and all the singing larks should unite their melodies in the great nature symphonies and sing their loudest and best in Laudes to the Lord.

And as he wrote the wildlings of the wood gathered around him. Butterflies displayed their gaudy wings; beetles their shining armor; wood pigeons fluttered to his feet; squirrels gamboled near; rabbits reared on hind legs and looked. at him; and the air was filled with the voices of birds of all kinds striving to vocalize the mute praises of the creatures that could not sing-but they understood and signified their grateful acquiescence in the birds' songs of praise.

And so creation's Te Deum rose loudest and best around the man who best loved all God's world in God. *Deny, deride, doubt honestly-but determine to know. There is explanation; it awaits that Columbus of mind who shall first traverse the vast uncharted expanse of psychic seas. 58

CHAPTER XIII Visit to Clara: Death of Francis 

As Francis felt the end was drawing near he had a great longing to speak with Clara once more and to visit San Damiano-the little church of the Crucifix. That sublime 

Sorrow had spoken to him in his youthful hour of conversion; it had looked at him as Light of the light that flooded him in the hour of stigmatization. He wanted to see it again. 

It was guarding Clara now; he would speak with her once more and then leave her in peace in its understanding Sorrow. So Francis with a few followers left La Verna and advanced by slow stages to Assisi. Wherever they went crowds came to see the saint, to touch the hem of his robe, and so a little poor man almost blind was honored in his own time and town.

Guest of the Bishop of Assisi 

On returning to Assisi Francis found that the Bishop had provided for his coming. Much as he shrank from acceptance of the offer and to living in the stately palace with the Bishop yet his gentle nature would hurt no one, high or low or sub-human-so he complied. 

Now it happened that there was a celebrated Doctor from Paris temporarily making his home at the Bishop's court, and seeing Francis and noting his serious eye condition he offered to give medical and, if necessary, surgical assistance. 

The Bishop and many friends urged Francis to accept this kind offer and undergo the operation as later suggested by the great Doctor. Francis reluctantly complied.

Kind Brother Fire 

Tradition tells us that as Francis watched the preparation for some rude form of cauterization, he grew faint at heart as he noticed the instrument in the fire grow hotter and hotter; and as the Doctor drew near with the sizzling whitehot iron, Francis cried out, "O fierce Brother Fire, I pray you deal with me as gently as you can." He then lay silent and very still under the Doctor's manipulation and the cauterization. The Doctor marvelled at the will power which could keep Francis apparently quiescent under the slow painful process. But Francis later declared that he had felt no pain at all and that brother Fire had dealt very gently with him. 59

Francis' eyesight was somewhat improved after the operation; but he grew restless under care and luxury. He felt that he was offending his Lady Poverty, and in spite of all protestations he was again a beggar on the way to San Damiano. Clara had heard that Francis was under the care of the Bishop; she rejoiced too, at the great kindness he had received and at his general improvement in health. It was therefore something of a surprise to her when late one night she learned that Francis was at the convent door and desired to see her.

Now by a strict rule of the Order it was forbidden for a man to enter the cloister and particularly after night prayers. This was Francis' own rule for the Poor Clares. So 

Clara as Abbess must observe that rule. 

A hut in the Garden

A hut woven of boughs and rushes was hastily constructed in the garden for Francis. Every comfort possible at that late hour was given to their unexpected guest, but Clara would not see him that night. Francis was secretly glad to learn of Clara's strict observance of rule. He helped to build his hut of rushes, and being very tired he soon entered and lay down in the fragrant grasses and very soon his sister, Sleep, peaceful and pitiful, wrapped him in kind unconsciousness. 

Clara watched thro' the night. Was it right, was it kind to refuse him admittance? Perhaps he needed hot water for his eyes, needed-her? She would go out secretly-No.

She would call Agnes and go with her to the garden-No. 

Agnes knew not of his coming, she was asleep.

Clara at the Grating 

Clara watched the garden and its hut of wattels so deathly still in the moonlight. She knelt by the grating, her face close to the iron bars; she wept, she loved, she prayed. Then a great peace filled her soul-the peace that follows selfconquest and triumph of the higher over the lower. She knew that Francis lay in the arms of gentle sister Sleep, and tenderly she blessed him as anticipatively she said: "So let him wait God's instant men call years," Browning.

Leaving the grating and turning resolutely from view of the garden, Clara slept. 60

Interview 

And on the morning an interview took place between

Francis and Clara. They spoke together as travellers who see the end of their journey, and secretly are glad; for they see also the beginning of a better way wherein they wander as comrades and lovers after the "instant men call years." Request

In the course of the conversation Clara expressed a desire to see again the church of Santa Maria degli Angeli and to kneel once more in the hallowed place where in the long ago she had made her religious Consecration. They spoke together freely of that momentous night and the crude ceremony vibrant with love whereby Lady Clara Scifi, a maiden in silken garments and high head-dress and embroidered shoes-had suddenly become a Poor Clare in brown woven robe, black veil, and wooden sandals. The night, the adventure, the wild uncertainty, the steady pull of destiny, the love divine and human-all human, all divine-that made them one-let them live it all over once more-now in the sunset hours, now in the twilight lit by Sister Death. So 

Clara pleaded. 

Francis complied. He would go to Portiuncula, he would prepare for her coming; she should see once more the scene of her girlhood consecration and pray before the image of the Virgin in the Church of the Angels. That image had been to her what the Crucifix of San Damiano had been to him the symbol through which God had spoken. That evening Francis said farewell to San Damiano and to his spiritual daughters. He would never see them in their cloister home again.

Francis had added a final strophe to his Laudes Creatura rum (Praises of the Creatures): 

"Blessed be my Lord for our sister, the death of the body from which no man escapeth. Blessed are they who are found walking by thy most holy will for the second death shall have no power to do them harm.”* *Sabatier says that Francis should have listed in his Laudes a special strophe of praise to God for the friendship of Sister Clara. "The

Canticle of the Creatures is very noble"; he said; "it lacks, however, one strophe; it was not upon Francis' lips, it was surely in his heart:- "Be praised, Lord, for Sister Clara:

Thou hast made her silent, active, and sagacious, And by her Thy light shines in our hearts." 61

A few days later Francis sent some Brothers to conduct 

Clara and Agnes to Portiuncula. They should spend a day and a night at the little convent adjoining Saint Mary of the 

Angels. 

It was the first time Clara had left the enclosure of San Damiano since she went there many years ago. All outside seemed dearly familiar yet sadly strange. It had been a mistake to come, perhaps—she would hasten back to enclosure; the world was loud and harsh, it would not understand. 

Perhaps, too, the human was here rather than the divine. And custom, custom-the dead custom of the years cried- "Hurry back to enclosure."

Alone in the Church of her Girlhood Consecration 

Francis saw the struggle; he read the heart. So taking

Agnes aside he let Clara go alone into the church-then he closed the door. She was alone with God and memory in the sanctuary of Santa Maria-before the image of the Virgin.

And that evening Francis and the Brothers gave a repast in honor of Clara and Agnes. Seated on the ground in the garden as was the custom they ate and conversed, they spoke openly, they spoke heart to heart. They sang the Laudes- 

Francis leading.

Francis Sings Welcome to Sister Death 

The Brothers stopped at the usual ending, but Francis in a clear voice sang the strophe of welcome to Sister Death.

It was glorious, triumphant-a swan-song over the waterscalling. All understood.

Then Francis, lit with the love of God, spoke; and his words reached each heart saying just what it wanted to hear.

And the hours passed as moments, for all were enraptured, all were caught up in ecstacy, all were in touch with God. 

A Great Light 

Now it chanced that the people of Assisi and the neighboring hills saw a great light over the church and convent and garden of Santa Maria. They thought it was a great conflagration. So in all good will they hastened to help put out the fire. But on reaching the scene they found there was no material fire, but entering the garden and seeing Francis and Clara and the Brothers all wrapped up in light they 62

realized that what they had seen and taken for a huge conflagration was in reality the light of the love of God,—filling the convent, the garden, the valley, the world. 

Reverently they withdrew; and as they went their way they told their story to the villagers who were hastening to the fire; and they all watched from the surrounding hills until the great light faded and the quiet stars came out and watched over Assisi.

Death of Francis 

The smile, the light from within, that came into Francis' face the night of the spiritual conflagration never again went out.

Farewell 

Clara's departure the following day was surface-formal. 

Their farewell was the last; each knew it. But the song of the heart kept singing; each heard it. 

Telepathy has no language. What the eyes say when lit by spirit-love does not lend itself to verbal expression. 

Only Francis and Clara heard the soul-song vibrant in the silence. The parting was quiet, formal-final. 

Last Message 

A few days later Brother Elias, seeing Francis' weakened condition, had him carried on a litter down to Portiuncula. 

He was placed in a little hut in the garden at the rear of the church. There the Brothers gathered around him to hear his last message and receive his blessing. He spoke lovingly and cheerfully to each in turn, seeming with his old time sagacity to say just what each one wished to hear. Blessing Music

He blessed all the members of his Order, the dead, the living, and all "those who should enter till the end of time." 

He blessed them all-as he said "as much as I can and even more than I can."

He gave full forgiveness to those brethren who had wounded his heart by changing his Rule; and by failing to live up to his beloved and beautiful-if impossible-ideals. 

All now was in God's hands.

Francis seemed better and even gay at heart after this 

Blessing. His mind went back to its earliest love-Music. 63

He asked the Brothers to sing for him-sing the Troubadour songs of gay Provençe; sing his Laudes, his Sun-Song; sing his favorite Davidian psalms.

Song of the Larks 

Dawn

As the singing in the garden died down, the song of birds took up the strain. Two larks, high on the church tower, sang their loudest and best. Francis heard them. He smiled, he murmured, "My larks sing for me; and praised be my 

Lord for -"That sentence was never finished.

The Brothers knelt in prayer; the larks sang; morning dawned red in the sky. The hut, the garden, the church, the 

Portiuncula valley were suffused with crimson. The Brothers chanted the Penitential psalm. 

Sister Death 

Suddenly the dying eyes opened wide. What they saw, filled them with welcome and with glad surprise. Yes, he would come-gladly.

Francis strove to rise, to go, to answer, to comply-and fell back into the waiting arms of sister Death. 

The larks sang again more joyously than ever; sweet, sad, low, loud, voluminous, triumphant-they sang, flooding the morning with gladness. 64
