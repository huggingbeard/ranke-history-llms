II. 

THE CAPACITY OF THE WIFE TO CONTRACT 

AN UNMARRIED WOMAN at common law had full capacity to contract but pon marriage this capacity was lost. The development of the wife's right to ontract is interesting.2

THE FIRST STEP was the doctrine of "equity to a settlement." Under this octrine, when the husband or his assignee or creditor, for instance, sought quitable relief against the wife's property, the chancellor required that suitble provision be made out of the property for the support of the wife and hildren. This was simply an application of the equitable maxim that he who eeks equity must do equity.

THE SECOND STEP was the recognition by equity that property could be ettled upon the wife as her own separate estate. This estate she could convey r encumber. It was free from the claims of her husband or his creditors, but nce the wife had disposition over it, a contractual capacity developed. 

THE THIRD STEP was capacity to contract created by statute. These statutes re known as "Married Women's Property Acts," and vary greatly according jurisdiction. Under the more liberal statutes, a wife is given practically all capacity to contract, and to convey her real property, while in others she

THE COMMUNITY PROPERTY SYSTEM 

COMMUNITY PROPERTY was not part of the common law, and according to he consensus of opinion did not spring up as a definite system. There is evience that similar systems of marital communities existed in ancient Egypt, in Greece and in early Germanic tribes. The present system, however, developed ut of ideas, customs, and laws of Spain and France.5 

IN FRANCE it was known as the dotal system. The Napoleonic Code became he law of Louisiana, and the dotal system was adopted for its community. roperty laws.

IN SPAIN it was known as the ganancial system, and it is probably Spanish nfluence which accounts for adoption of the community property system in 

California and other western States.

CALIFORNIA has expressly abolished the common law marital forms of states, including estates by the entirety, dower and curtesy.7

PART II. 

SEPARATE PROPERTY 

IN GENERAL 

ALL PROPERTY acquired before the marriage; all acquired by gift, devise, bequest or descent; and all rents, issues and profits of separate property are declared by statute to be the separate property of the respective spouses. 

IN ACQUIRING OTHER PROPERTY the determination as to whether it is separate or community property depends upon the type of consideration given and the time when it is deemed acquired.2 

II. 

ACQUISITION OF SEPARATE PROPERTY 

ALL PROPERTY having its basis or origination from separate property is separate property; that is, accessions and accretions retain the status of the dominant property.3

VALIDITY OF A MARRIAGE 

THE GENERAL RULE is that a marriage valid where made is valid everywhere.¹ The basis for the rule is the desirability from a social standpoint of maintaining the married status no matter where the members of the relationship may move, and to insure the legitimacy of children. 

EXCEPTIONS may be listed as follows: 

1. Where the marriage is contrary to the Christian conception of that relationship. Although valid where made, it is not recognized everywhere. 

2. Where the marriage is contrary to positive local law it will not be recognized. This means that a statute affirmatively provides that a foreign marriage of a prohibited type shall not be recognized if the parties take up their domicile within the state enacting such a statute. It does not refer to marriages contracted within the state, but contains a direct reference to marriages contracted outside. .

California has no such evasion statute, and marriages are recognized in California, although the parties left this state in order to evade California statutory requirements. I. 

IN GENERAL¹ THE IMPORTANCE OF REGULATION of the use of waters is self-evident when consideration is given to the numerous industries dependent upon water resources. The ever increasing demands consequent upon increasing population have produced a demand for water which has taxed all sources to supply. In 

California, particularly the southern part of the state, the fullest possible application to beneficial uses is essential, due to climatic conditions which cause so-called "dry seasons of the year during which there is little or no rainfall." 

CALIFORNIA WAS THE FIRST STATE created out of the territory acquired from Mexico by the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in 1848, and its law of waters first became important enough to be the subject of judicial decision. In 1849 thousands of people emigrated to California to mine for gold. Placer mining, the first industry to spring up in this new country, required liberal use of water for washing the gold from foreign substances, and the right to take water from the streams became very valuable. Previous to the "gold rush" there were ample natural resources to supply the demand; consequently, there had been no need for legislation or judicial decision on the subject. During this critical period, however, the adjustment of a hundred thousand people to a new country, totally different climatically and topographically from their old homes, with no formulated rules for governing the existing situation, became a serious problem. Naturally, many disputes arose, and the newcomers applied, very informally, and as occasion demanded, what rules they could, based on such laws as they had been accustomed to, with the result that the regulations were widely varied in the several sections of the state.2

PART II. 

RIGHTS TO WATER 

I. 

RIPARIAN RIGHTS A RIPARIAN OWNER OR PROPRIETOR is one who owns land bounded by a watercourse, or through which a stream flows.¹ It is not essential that the watercourse be a river, or a creek, but it may also be a lake, a pool, or a navigable or non-navigable stream.2 In order to be riparian land, however, the land must be within the watershed, that is, within the region or area contributing to the supply of a river or lake; the drainage area. Where a large tract of land has a border on a river, but part of that tract is not within the watershed it has been held not to be riparian land as to the stream bordering on the main tract; that by the conveyance of a portion of the riparian tract, if the portion does not border on the river, no riparian rights pass with the conveyance unless expressly stated; and, if the portion conveyed is re-conveyed to the original grantor or his assigns, the land does not regain its riparian character. This point becomes very important in determining whether or not the use of the water is "reasonable," for the reasonable use of water is confined to riparian land.

THE RIPARIAN RIGHTS of a riparian owner consist in the right to have the water continue to flow through, or by his land, in its accustomed channel and volume, in its natural purity, and to make a beneficial use of it while passing by. RIPARIAN RIGHTS (EQUALITY) VS. RIGHTS BY APPROPRIATION (PRIORITY). 

"All riparian owners are equal in use at common law and none will be allowed unreasonably to impair the equal possible use of another. Equality and unreasonableness vary with the surrounding circumstances of extent of lands, seasons, volume of water, etc. On the other hand, appropriation gives an exclusive right measured by priority; it hence is a system of inequality and aims at certainty and to prevent variation. The law of appropriation is returning to the common law insofar as decisions are appearing, holding the rights of appropriators to be correlative; also insofar as statutes create administrative systems under which officials are given discretion to act for the general correla tive good of all users on a stream; also in regard to pro-rating statutes, and also insofar as, by the practice of rotation, appropriators are voluntarily pooling their exclusive rights for the common good. It is also returning to the common law characteristic of varying with the circumstances insofar as beneficial use, upon which the law of appropriation rests, must, of necessity, as regards irrigation, vary with the season, the year, the change of crops, the mode of use, and the number of neighboring irrigators. Especially is priority falling in regard to pollution of streams. where the Western courts are strongly tending to disregard priority as a justification. 

"THE LAW OF RIPARIAN RIGHT is one of the few instances where the com mon law, usually so individualistic, has accepted a communal system, and it is significant that in this it borrowed from the civil law, whose spirit is generally paternal. The law of riparian rights, being for and from older and settled communities, is restrictive upon each with a view to the correlative good of all. On new streams in the unsettled West, which require big projects before anyone can go there at all, (Ed. note this was written in 1911) it is an anomaly, although it contains basic principles of justice for small streams when growth has been accomplished. WATERCOURSES 

A NATURAL WATERCOURSE is water having a source independent of that fitful and occasional character that results from the falling of rain or melting of snow, flowing in a certain direction, through a definite channel, and hav ing a bed, sides or banks; it need not flow constantly, and may sometimes be dry. Watercourses include lakes and pools which have inlets and outlets. in which water flows. Riparian rights, as well as rights acquired by grant, condemnation, prescription and appropriation apply to watercourses.2

This supplement serves as an answer book to the bar questions contained in the Forrest Cool Law Review Volume on Community Property. The short form answers are intended as an analysis of the principal points involved in each question. They are not proposed to be model answers such as would be required in a bar examination. Since references are made to the volume on 

Community Property, it is believed the answers point the way to a sufficient discussion, thereby preventing repetition of material already presented. Answers number one and five are offered as model answers. 

Generally speaking, bar questions seek to test the applicant's knowledge of the essential points of law involved, his ability to apply the law to the facts and to reason logically. Seldom does a question ask for a mere conclusion; such an answer receives little or no credit. Many questions involve points on which there may exist a difference of opinion. Unless the problem specifically calls for the law of the particular state, an applicant is not usually penalized for reaching a conclusion contrary to the law of the particular state, or to the weight of authority, provided he demonstrates his abilities as mentioned above. It is not required that the answer be academic in character, nor that it set forth historical basis for the applicable rule. 

Since the period of time allotted in a bar examination is limited it is advised that the student prepare himself beforehand by writing answers to a number of bar questions.

ANSWERS TO BAR EXAMINATION QUESTIONS No. 1 (Model Answer) COMMUNITY PROPERTY 

When a right to property is obtained before marriage, as by contract to purchase, and the title is perfected after marriage, the property is separate property; the doctrine of equitable conversion and the fiction of relation back are applicable. If some payments toward the purchase price of the property are made with community funds, the community will be reimbursed or given a pro-rata share. That the land here involved was in another state is immaterial because the rights of a California spouse are not lost by taking them to another state and changing their form. Community property is all property acquired after marriage, except by way of gift, devise or bequest. The fact that here H took title to the Illinois land in his own name does not defeat the right of the community to its pro rata interest in the property. A change in the form of property does not change its status, and therefore, even though the land was sold and proceeds invested in stock, W is entitled to her share, which would here be a one-twelfth interest in the stock. 

There might be a difference of opinion regarding the character of the Cali fornia farm. A contractual right to the land was obtained during marriage, and this right is a community asset. The consideration for the contract, the time and effort of H, was in the nature of community earnings. Therefore it might well be argued that the property when finally obtained was community property. However, the Civil Code specifically provides that property acquired during marriage by gift, devise or bequest is separate property, and it has been held that this applies even though some consideration is given. If it is con ceded that the farm was H's separate property, it must also be held that the improvements made by the profits are separate property also, even though acquired as a result of the husband's skill and labor expended during marriage. 

The California court has held that it is impossible to determine what share of farm products was due to labor and what part to the land itself, and that therefore the profit derived from agricultural enterprises has the same status as does the land. It follows that W has no interest in the farm.. 

Formerly the rule in California was that a right of action for damages for personal injuries acquired during marriage was community property, even though the right was realized by judgment after a dissolution of the marriage, either by divorce or death. This is merely the converse of the rules involving rights acquired before marriage, i. e., that such rights are separate property, thus making the property itself separate though acquired after marriage. It is now held that a cause of action for a personal tort is not community property because it does not have two essential characteristics of such property, namely survivability and transferability. 1. The cause of action for personal injuries to the husband is in the husband alone. The general rule is that the husband alone has the right to sue for recovery of community property. Thus even assuming the automobile to belong to the community, John could sue without joining Mary as plaintiff, both for damages for his personal injury and for property damages. 

2. Although it has been held in California that a cause of action for personal injuries is not community property, the judgment or money recovered during the marriage belongs to the community. With regard to proceeds of the judgment for damages to the automobile, these would belong to the community if the car is a community asset, but not if it is the separate property of John. 

3. Under modern statutes, the wife may bring a separate action in her own name and recover "general" damages for personal injuries although the right to sue for "consequential damages"--including value of the wife's domestic services, expense of medical care, and loss of corsortium-is in the husband alone. If Mary's earnings are her separate property she may sue alone to recover for loss of earnings, but if these are deemed to be part of the com munity, the husband alone, as manager of the community, has the right to recover. If a necessary party plaintiff refuses to join in an action, he may be joined as a party defendant. 

4. Earnings of either spouse after marriage are community property in the absence of a contrary agreement. Such agreements between husband and wife may be very informal and do not require a writing. On the basis of the agreement shown here, a recovery by Mary for loss of earnings would be her separate property.

No. 10.

Under some statutes the community is liable for the wife's contracts for necessaries. The underlying theory is that the wife in so contracting was acting as the agent of the husband, and debts created for the benefit of the community by the husband or his agent are payable by him or the community. Of course, a husband who has supplied his wife with ample means to pay for food and clothing is not liable for purchases made on his credit, since such purchases are not necessaries within the legal connotation of the terms. 

In California, by statute, the property of the community is not liable for contracts of the wife made after marriage; nor is there any personal liability of the husband unless an agency relationship is established. 

No. 11. (a) Although there can be no community property unless there has been a valid marriage, when a person enters into a putative marriage in good faith, an agreement or partnership will be implied with respect to property acquired during the continuation of the marriage relationship, and such property will be divided as though it were community property. A change of form does not change the nature of property, nor does any presumption as to the nature of the property arise from the fact that title is taken in the name of a putative husband. Accordingly W is entitled to a one-half interest in Southacre. (b) When property is purchased by a husband and title taken in the name of the wife, it is presumed that a gift of the property was made to the wife. Further, all property acquired by a married woman by an instrument in writing is presumed to be her separate property. It follows that Northacre is W's separate property in the absence of any facts to controvert these presumptions.

Earnings due to skill and labor of either spouse during marriage are community property. When property is part separate and part community, as in the case of income from a business owned prior to marriage, the courts have evolved a formula for apportionment but this is not applied in cases of income from separately owned agricultural land, all of which is considered separate. 

It follows that H is entitled to the entire profits of the ranch, but that H and W should share equally in W's earnings. The ranch itself, and the hotel are, of course, H's separate property. (c) A husband is not liable for torts of the wife except where he would be jointly liable if the marriage did not exist. This rule which obtains in 

California, is contrary to that of the common law. 

A wife is liable for her own torts, and a judgment against her is payable from her separate property and her earnings, if these are not commingled with he community.

The community is not liable for torts of a wife committed after marriage.
