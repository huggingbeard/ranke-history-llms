INTRODUCTION 

N ACQUISITIVE GLEAM is inspired in the eye of the average male smoker by every new make of pipe, tobacco or cigar. In the course of surprisingly few years he buys sundry foul-tasting pipes that he can smoke only once, evil-smelling, medicinal-tasting tobaccos, and cigars that will always be recalled in the same Big Mistake category as the night he was introduced to poker and the time the wife returned from vacation ahead of time. 

When the cigarette smoker realizes that he spends around $100 a year on his pleasurable vice and when cigar smokers realize that they're not far behind the cigarette smoker in expense, they'll take time out to look at this smoking habit, to study it and use new-found knowledge to derive more real satisfaction from smoking.

It is the writer's hope that this book will serve as a guide to the bewildered smoker surrounded by products with brand names that tell nothing of their make, by tobaccos in disguise behind aromatic essences and perfumes and fantastic prices, by mechanisms categorically called "pipes" but needing few additions to look like products of Rube Goldberg's screwier side.

We've discussed tobacco-not a product advertised with seductive girls as the true solution to your sex problems or of a means of being nonchalant, satisfied, lifted, adjusted in weight, cured of cough, cooled, built up in social prestige or relaxed (depending on which type you buy). We'll stick to the story of tobacco, stripped of phoney adornment, the story of a plant that grows under certain conditions, that has certain characteristics derived from its seed and locale, the story of manufacturing operations brought into the light, the story of how YOU can vii

select your smoking equipment more intelligently, with sound, certain knowledge of what you're purchasing. 

We've dragged false, or fact-distorting, advertising up on the carpet and shown it for what it really is. We've plunged into the physiological and chemical sides of tobacco and arrived at as near an unbiased opinion as the author feels is humanly possible. If only the fluttery ladies and somber faced men who denounce tobacco will stoutly maintain that we represent ogre-like tobacco interests, and if only the big money-bags in tobacco will insist that we're trying to knife them in the back, we'll be happy. For then we'll have struck the happy medium of accuracy which we have sought to follow throughout the preparation of this little book. 

It's been fun digging information out of shells of superstition, checking facts with unimpeachable sources, browsing through the most accurate of literature on the subject, debunking, verifying, studying for the past several years so that the smoker may be better informed on smoking, smoking methods and equipment. We've sidestepped dry historical data so that we can concentrate on information that you can apply directly to your purchases and your smoking.

Our fondest hopes for this book are that the truths set forth in it will be spread widely among the smoking fellowship so that the tobacco world may have surer knowledge of the products it embodies. Chicago 

December, 1940. -S. P. R. viii

CHAPTER I TOBACCO AND THE SMOKER WHY HY DO WE SMOKE? How much may we smoke with safety? safety? Does smoking impair efficiency. Does it harm children? What are its advantages? What is smoking's effect on the body? We've sought to answer these questions in this chapter. Later we'll discuss tobacco from the economic, manufacturing and agricultural standpoints.

Debunking a few superstitions, that have never been proved: 

That smoking stunts human growth. 

That the pregnant mother's smoking in moderation harms her child. 

That tobacco affects the genitals in any way. 

That use of tobacco causes loss of weight. 

That so-called "denicotinized” tobaccos are free from nicotine. 

That smoking causes cancer, tuberculosis, heart trouble, and numerous other diseases (although there are solid grounds for belief that smoking causes a predisposition toward some of these ailments). 

That smoking facilitates creative work. 

That smoking serves consistently either as a stimulant or as a depressant.

WHY SMOKE? 

We find something like two-thirds of the people of this country smoking over a hundred billion cigarettes, and billions of cigars and packages of pipe tobacco. When [11]

we talk in billions we talk in tremendously large quantities, yet the industry in the United States grosses several billions of dollars a year in cigarettes alone. Why? 

Because people smoke.

Most of them don't know why they do, and many wish they didn't, but the fact remains that they do. A bunch of bogus theories have been advanced that smoking is an adult form of reverting back to the old thumbsucking days, that it makes people feel grown up, that it is because of the type of seasoned food we eat, but these either fall because of the sheer weight of their own ridiculousness or through being disproved scientifically.

The majority of smokers are started because of social or anti-social reasons. That is, they hid away and tried it because smoking was forbidden them-they're mimicking their elders, it makes them feel grown up or else they found others in groups smoking and take up the "filthy weed" either to hop onto the bandwagon or in defense against being overwhelmed in smoke-filled rooms. Most of them have their first smoke before they are 18 years old.

These are their reasons for starting. They continue to smoke because they attribute to tobacco an emotional outlet for unfortunate breaks and uncomfortable circumstances. It serves as a combination drug, and activity, giving exercise to fidgety person's senses of taste, touch, smell. It has a slight effect similar to rest at a time when the smoker can't take time off for rest without great inconvenience. Smoking gives people an occupation when they would not otherwise be occupied. The pipe smoker fills, tamps, lights his pipe, takes it from his mouth every now and then, polishes it on his nose, rolls the smoke around his tongue, studies the smoke, maybe blows a few rings. 

Similarly the cigarette smoker deftly takes the "fag” from a package, tamps it expertly, lights it, toys with the smoke, [12]

flicks off ashes and finally tosses the butt away. And the cigar smoker unwraps his cigar, unbands it, bites off the end, lights it, chews the butt, possibly sets a peeling wrapper in place with a lick. Frequently these sets of action hold the smoker to his habit more strongly than does the drug in tobacco. There is also some ground for the belief that smoking staves off hunger temporarily. 

Drug, habit, social reasons, fondness for certain flavors and aromas, activity-these are principal reasons for smoking.

Sometimes reluctance to becoming enslaved by a habit, or ill health, or desire to please someone who disapproves of smoking may cause a smoker to seek to drop his habit. Most of those who try to swear off are unsuccessful. Those who have succeeded found the best way was to drop it completely and find some other occupation to offset nervousness between smokes. Companionship in abstinence, forcing oneself to make a sacrifice for breaking the resolve and use of non-habit-forming substitutions for tobacco or nicotine are also sometimes helpful. 

The smoker knows when he's been overdoing his smoking. He doesn't need a doctor or a book to tell him he's been making a tobacco hog of himself. He'd derive marked moral satisfaction by applying brakes to his own foolishness rather than doing it on the suggestion of others. 

-TEMPERANCE-NOT ABSTINENCE- TOBACCO AND SMOKE CONTENT 

There are many physical ailments attributed to tobacco. Before we look at them, let's see what there is in tobacco that could cause this trouble. 

Apologists for it would have us believe that nicotine is the only seriously dangerous drug in tobacco and that it is almost entirely dissipated in the burning action. They say tobacco contains cellulose, albuninoid, malic, citric, [ 13 ]

pectic and oxalic acids, resinous fats, ash and, incidentally, nicotine. The ash? Oh, it just contains such essential minerals as potash, lime, magnesia, phosphoric acid and a little other stuff.

Tobacco unburned is of interest primarily to the chewer and to a lesser degree to the cigar-smoker who uses no holder. Friends of tobacco have found carbonic acid gas, water, potash and ammonia salts, oils and resinous substances to be products of combustion. 

Oils, resins and soot-laden water are the principal components of the goo left in pipe stems and filters or by blowing tobacco-smoke through a handkerchief. This is not nicotine as tobacco-haters sometimes assert. Nicotine is watery and transparent.

To take a few of the undertaker-callers found in tobacco and tobacco smoke by those who would have the weed wiped off earth's face we have: pyridine, picoline, lutidine, collidine, parvoline, viridine, rubidine, and coridine, prussic and carbolic acids, carbon monoxide, five forms of nicotine and a few other items the druggist won't sell without a prescription.

This school holds that there's nothing nice about tobacco or tobacco smoke and the only harmless things in it are carbon dioxide and water. They lump the other poisons (apart from nicotine) together as "tar derivatives" and shake their heads solemnly and sadly at the thought of what they'll do to the poor smoker. Dirty deposits of tar derivatives may contribute toward cancer's progress, they say. Tar derivatives are liberated by the smoking; they may cause throat irritation. And ammonia, too, is liberated to help the tar derivatives in their dastardly work. 

We've stuck to tobacco so far in this discussion, but remember that there are many other materials sometimes added to tobacco to give it flavor, aroma, uniformity or burning quality. Menthol is one; it doesn't necessarily [14]

protect the throat and it may help irritate it, but it does make the smoke taste cooler. Others are lead and arsenic, but these are also taken into the system, in harmless quantities from eating fruit that has been sprayed and not thoroughly washed.

That's a lot of poison for a person to have in his diet. 

Yet every year we hear of octogenarians or non-octogenarians who took on this poison throughout their lives and weren't apparently harmed by it. We have a hunch that those smokers were intelligently moderate in their smoking and because of this they didn't have to completely abstain as does the foolish smoker who overindulges for a few years and finds his health failing. 

Let's look more closely at nicotine. It's the really dangerous element in tobacco because it is there in appreciable quantities, both pro- and anti-tobacco people agree. Nicotine is the material that distinguishes tobacco from all other plants. It constitutes from one to nine per cent of the tobacco. Nicotine content varies from brand to brand as you can tell by consulting the following chart.

In general, European tobaccos have higher nicotine content, United States tobaccos have average amounts and Havana, Manila and Asiatic tobaccos contain least. The quantity of nicotine in tobacco bears no relationship to the quality of the tobacco-and this isn't a plug for “denicotinized” tobaccos; there has yet to be found a good denicotinizing agent that leaves tobacco so that it remains Tobacco. Natural West Indian tobacco contains less nicotine than so-called "denicotinized" ones.

More irritating material, including nicotine, escapes from the smoldering tobacco into the air than is drawn into the system of the smoker. This sometimes makes it harder for the non-smoker to be present in the smoke-filled room than for the smoker. The smoker at least has the [15]

filter action of the pipe, cigar or cigarette to strain the smoke before it reaches him.

Rapid smoking causes absorption of more nicotine by the body than does slow smoking with steady intermittent puffs. Smokers who puff rapidly also inhale more carbon monoxide and aldehyde and an amount of nicotine equal to that which escapes into air; those who smoke normally and more slowly inhale less carbon monoxide, nicotine, ammonia, aldehyde and furfural than escapes into the air. 

In line with this, paradoxically, if you find your throat being irritated by tobacco, switch from Virginia and other mild blends, to slower-burning ones with richer flavor, say Turkish. 

Thick cigars contain more nicotine than do thin ones.

The greater the moisture content of the tobacco, the more nicotine it contains. 

Special toasting processes do not appreciably lower nicotine content of cigarette tobaccos.

Stubs of cigars and cigarettes and heels of pipes contain higher percentages of nicotine than the beginnings of the smokes; these also generate higher temperatured gases which irritate mucous membranes more readily. 

Non-inhaling doesn't keep you entirely divorced from the nicotine in tobacco. The pipe and cigar smoker gets a little more than half as much nicotine per ounce of tobacco as the cigarette smoker takes into his system. 

The nicotine is dissolved into the saliva and swallowed. [16]

PHYSICAL EFFECTS OF SMOKING 

There's a not-too-unbalanced struggle going on among the pro- and anti-tobacco elements about whether or not smoking is seriously harmful to adults. No such question exists in the case of children. Growing children should not smoke tobacco. Their delicate nervous systems may be upset by it and investigations show a direct parallel between extensive smoking and low scholastic standing. Some teachers maintain that it isn't the smoking itself that harms the children-it won't stunt their growth-but that the connotations of smoking, its associations and distractions, spoil the young students concentration. Basically there is no difference in intelligence between the smokers and the non-smokers. Yet we find [18]

smoking to be an undesirable habit for children. They should be discouraged from smoking until later in life. 

The enemies of tobacco have many very effective arguments against its use, in reports of leading medical authorities about tobacco's effect on health. True, they say, tobacco may serve as a mild laxative, but aren't there others more practicable? And isn't tobacco also capable of causing diarrhoea if it is used in excessive amounts? 

When tobacco's friends say it helps keep cavities from forming in teeth, enemies leap to the attack with cases where smoking harmed gums, where teeth were broken or chipped on pipe stems and holders and where the dirty yellow deposit of tar derivatives on the teeth caused markedly unsanitary oral conditions. 

And that just starts them going. They drag in a list of diseases and physiological experiments that show tobacco to be second only to Hitler as The World's Worst

Evil. Many of their charges are accurate, many distorted and many false. Let's look at a few:

The Heart. Some believe that nicotine shrinks the diameter of blood vessels. This raises the blood pressure and may cause discomfort or pain in the heart. If used in normal amounts—and most smokers understand that normal amounts mean but a few cigars a day or less than a package of cigarettes or less than an ounce of pipe tobacco-it will cause a very slight increase in the pulse rate, possibly five to 10 beats; it may also raise the blood pressure slightly, around 20 mm. of mercury systolic and 8 mm. diastolic. This won't be enough to affect your health normally unless you're indulging in severe physical activity. The heart needs this last bit of push. It isn't so efficient when you overexert yourself. That's why coaches say lay off tobacco while in training.

If excessive amounts of tobacco are used the effect on the circulatory system isn't always so gentle. The heart [ 19 ]

starts palpitating noticeably and its rhythm may be disturbed. Pain after meals or at night is not a rarity. Arterial sclerosis may follow-although it never has been proved. 

Increased heart-beat, increased blood pressure, anemia, false angina, angina pectoris and tobacco myocarditis ("tobacco-heart") are other troubles that reformers threaten you with if you won't abandon the filthy weed.

Stick to moderate quantities of tobacco, though, and you'll have little to worry about in these quarters; some of the heart troubles really caused by tobacco fade away after tobacco has been dropped or excesses abandoned and they seldom leave permanent ill effects.

Tobacco affects women in the same way it affects men. Women may smoke in moderation during pregnancy with safety. No scientific proof exists that it exerts a harmful pre-natal influence and publications of The American Medical Association have branded tales of babies born with "tobacco-heart" and of women having all sorts of trouble in conception as a result of smoking, as mere atrocity stories with no foundation in fact. The question of women smoking is more one of somebody's conception of morality than a special matter of concern to the physiologist. 

Cancer. With the increase in smoking, there has been no increase in the prevalence of cancer of the mouth, so it is apparent that tobacco does not cause mouth cancer. 

However, it may predispose the smoker to such cancer by constantly irritating his mouth and throat through either excessive or improper smoking. The cigarette cough, catarrh of the throat is so common that one manufacturer employs the dubious advertising that states his cigarettes will not give the smokers a cough in a carload. The constant local irritation resulting from contact with the tobacco or the holder may further cancerous conditions also. 

Tuberculosis. There is no ground for the belief that [ 20 ]

smoking causes tuberculosis or has anything to do with it. 

A suspicion exists however that it may lower the body's ability to resist tuberculosis (and also other diseases) and that the throat troubles aggravated by smoking may afford easy access to T.B. eyes

Eyes. Aside from temporary irritation from smoke and occasional ashes in the eyes, the only real danger to the from smoking is the, fortunately, rare disease, tobacco emblyopis, a form of progressive blindness. Some doctors have also attributed this disease to excessive alcohol. Optic neuritis may possibly result from excessive smoking, a few doctors state. 

Digestive System. Nicotine and its fellow agents may slow the stomach movement and alter the proportions of digestive juices. It can throw the salivary glands off schedule and make it harder to take ether. It may retard hunger, this being the foundation for the belief that it causes loss of weight. It has been said to cause increased gastric acidity and intestinal movements to such an extent that certain physicians forbid tobacco as part of their treatment of stomach ulcers. 

Respiratory System. If you continue to over-smoke long enough you risk a chronic morning cough, hoarseness, chronic bronchitis, tonsilitis and sore tongue! Excessive smoking may even inflame the Eustacian tube and contribute toward catarrhal deafness. Deep inhaling is a contributing factor to respiratory troubles along with fast smoking and smoking of too large a quantity of rich tobacco.

Other Possible Difficulties. Tobacco allergy is occasionally found, but not often. Certain doctors believe the adrenal and thyroid glands may be affected by tobacco. 

The liver might become overexerted from ridding the stomach of nicotine and its fellow travelers. And you [21]

might try quitting tobacco for awhile to note the effect have kidney trouble. if you

Central Nervous System. This has been the subject for the greatest number of experiments with tobacco and has probably been found to be least affected of all parts of the body on which tobacco acts. The conclusion of numerous experiments is that the long run effect of tobacco is to depress brain activity on a mythical average person. High-strung people are calmed by smoking. Depressed people may be stimulated by it. Tobacco is more powerful as a depressing agent than as a stimulating one and is found to depress over 70 per cent of the people tested and to stimulate slightly under 30 per cent. 

Tobacco has been found to cause losses in accuracy of addition, memory span, rapidity of cancellation, steadiness of motor control, energy, rapidity of muscular control, facility of learning, accuracy of movement, and alertness.

It has been found to cause gains in rapidity of addition, reading, uniformity of movement, learning (other tests), and, through relaxing the smoker, acceptance of original creative thought. It retards muscular fatigue, accelerates automatized functions both physical and mental, and increases promptness of response in work needing sustained attention for hours. 

Length of Life. Statistics show that the less you smoke, the longer you live. Exceptions have existed, smokers who've lived for many decades longer than the average who smoke, but chances of any one person living that long, with or without smoking, are slim.

Conclusions. There are indubitably numerous detrimental effects that may arise from smoking. These generally are present in cases of oversmoking for a prolonged time. Moderate smoking, while not of appreciable benefit [22]

to the health at best, is not likely to cause the smoker serious trouble.

What's one man's meat is another man's poison, so no statements about the healthfulness or harmfulness of tobacco applies to everybody. And if you find tobacco is impairing your health, drop it immediately before the habit gets deeper rooted; save yourself the doctor bill of the man who went to the medico for advice. He couldn't drink coffee. Was there any medicine or anything the doctor would suggest as a solution to his difficulty? 

The doctor had the answer right at his fingertips. He advised the patient not to drink coffee.

Claims of advertisers that their cigarettes do not cut wind or impair the physical condition of the smoker or that their cigarettes will be a healthful substitute for fattening foods are branded as false and sometimes vicious by medical leaders. Tobacco is unhealthy. 

It may be best smoked in moderation. Then the smoker need not abstain completely from the pleasure he derives from the weed. Certainly moderate smoking is not really seriously harmful, otherwise the insurance companies, which stand to lose most from early deaths of clients, would have advised against it. So far, they advise only that we be moderate in our smoking, not that we stop completely.

Probably the attitude of the average smoker is summed up in G. L. Hemminger's little jingle: 

Tobacco is a dirty weed, I like it.

It satisfies no normal need, I like it. 

It makes you thin, it makes you lean, It takes the hair right off your bean;

It's the worst darn stuff I've ever seen, I like it. [ 23 ]

RICH The Pipe Smoker as he puffs at his mellow old briar. 

That pipe of his seems to be the key to his personality. 

Everything about him seems to spring from it. . . .

His pipes stand by, silent, loyal sentinels when he is a college boy, when the gang whoops into his room to sprawl on bed, floor and furniture, in a bull session wherein boys pool their information on sports and school, work and women, problems and pipes.. 

Then later on in life in his office, he puffs at his pipe as he works out those new sales and advertising campaigns that earn him his promotions. . . .

On those cool autumn week-ends spent hunting and fishing, when the air is crisp and the tried rod or gun in his hands feels good, a sturdy pipe is always his companion. . .

And at home, with dogs and books, with good liquor and old friends his pipe makes the picture complete. The time-blackened briar adjusts itself quickly to his moods, offers him solace in times of trial and refuge when the going is tough and helps him develop an immensely human philosophy.... 

You know The True Pipe Smoker. Watching him has probably been instrumental in making you want to smoke a pipe. But possibly something deters you. Possibly the pipe rears up and protests, or your jaw becomes tired, or you find your tongue gets too hot, or you habitually break pipes, or they don't break in well. [24]

That veteran pipe smoker probably had similar troubles when he first started to smoke a pipe. But he worked out the answer to his problems either alone or with the aid of an expert. You can do the same.

Let's go over the various difficulties pipe smokers encounter and work out the solutions together. If it makes you a member of the great pipe-smoking fraternity and leads to your eventual complete smoking satisfaction and enjoyment, rest assured that our gratification will be truly mutual. 

A typical complaint is:

TROUBLE IN SWITCHING FROM CIGARETTE SMOKING TO PIPES 

Anyone can learn to smoke a pipe if only he remembers that it's not a cigarette. You see, there's a world of difference between the two techniques.

A cigarette is a nervous man's smoke. Despite claims that it soothes the nerves like mother's hand on a fevered brow, that it lifts you, satisfies you, brushes you off, builds up your morale, your aplomb and your social prestige, it's still a nervous man's smoke. Everything about cigarette smoking is nervous, quick, jittery. You zip open a package, fish out a cigarette, flick a light onto the end, take long, quick drags, then toss the cigarette away. You inhale deeply, trying to snatch comfort from it like a dope fiend from his "powder". But it's like going to a sanatorium for a rest cure and finding yourself at a convention of high school cheer leaders.

Now a pipe's different. You fill it slowly and carefully, light it in a cautious, leisurely way, and smoke it almost languorously to smoke it properly. It gives you a true opportunity to relax, to let go. No nervous, deep inhalation of tobacco smoke for you! Just roll the smoke [ 25 ]

over in your mouth slowly, let it seep into your nostrils, give yourself completely over to a mouthful of luxurious and delicious tasting smoke. It makes a whale of a difference in your personality-and gives you a decided advantage of time in an argument!

But one of the most pathetic of smoking figures is the cigarette vet trying to smoke a pipe cigarette style.

He fills it quickly and sloppily, lights it in a hurry and misses much of the tobacco with the flame, smokes it at top speed. You can almost see the glow travel down the pipe like an elevator. Worse, he inhales! So he gets a gadgetladen pipe, blaming the pipe for his difficulties, and winds up just where he started—with cigarettes. 

That fellow should really learn to smoke a pipe, develop a taste for it and acquire the correct technique. 

Smoking a pipe is an art. If one wishes to derive real satisfaction from it he might as well start out correctly, taking added pains at the beginning so that correct smoking later becomes a well established habit. 

First he should pick good pipes. We'll discuss the manufacture and selection of pipes later. Then he should learn to break them in properly and light them correctly (which we cover in the Clinic). After that he's ready to learn to smoke a pipe. 

First lesson is: Smoke dry. Keep saliva out of the pipe. 

Practice, if necessary, on an unlighted, unfilled pipe. Keep the pipe stem out of the saliva trough just behind and below the lower teeth. And swallow frequently. A dry pipe smokes clean. It doesn't let the tar oils and condensation in a pipe run back into your mouth. It isn't spoiled by sogging saliva. It's not liable to go sour. We'll discuss additional ways of keeping the pipe dry later. 

Second lesson: Smoke slowly. You'll tend to revert to the cigarette-smoking habits, but try to keep your mind on the fact that you're smoking a pipe-check a tendency [26]

to forget until your tongue is burned-and soon you'll be merrily puffing away at your pipe just like all the fellows you've always envied. Test yourself by holding the pipe bowl every few puffs. If it is hot to your hand, let it go out and light it again after it cools (relighting won't affect the taste of the pipe after the first couple of puffs) and you'll be saving your tongue the discomforts of fire and brimstone. You know how to appreciate good liquor, don't you? You roll it over your tongue, test its fragrance, sip it slowly. Try appreciating your tobacco the same way.

Roll it over your tongue, let a little come out of your nostrils. Don't inhale-taste. It's lots cheaper, less potentand a powerful aid to correct pipe smoking!

Summarizing: 

1. Smoke dry. 

2. Smoke slowly.

THE PIPE THAT WON'T STAY LIT 

Not many smokers light pipes properly, and before we can take up their problem we have to cover the sister problem of how to fill it properly. A pipe properly filled is easily lit, and all the tobacco in it can be smoked if you desire. It's no crime to re-light a pipe four or five times during a pipefull. Just tamp down the tobacco, throw out the ash and light up.

Fill the pipe in layers, about a fourth at a time. Tamp it down after each layer is put in, but not too tightly. 

And draw through it as the first layer is tamped down to make sure that a flake doesn't jam the vent and you're not packing it too tightly. This fills the pipe evenly so that the fire may be carried over the entire surface and travel down to the bottom. 

If you use three matches to light the pipe at the beginning, you'll find you will have a perfect glowing coal. 

When the first match is applied, the light, quick-burning [27]

tobaccos burn first, spreading the fire unevenly. After a few draws, tamp the tobacco down again with a metal tamper and light it with the second match. A few puffs later repeat the process and by this time the light tobaccos have given up the fight so the third match lights the pipe evenly and uniformly, so that the entire surface of tobacco is a single glowing coal.

Be careful not to burn the rim of the bowl with the match.

Smoke slowly and leisurely and you'll find the average pipefull lasts between 30 and 45 minutes, will stay lit and will consume all the tobacco in it. However, don't be chagrined if a little soggy heel falls out when you empty your pipe. It's the least healthful part of the smoke anyway.

BREAKING-IN THE PIPE 

Probably more junk and bunk has been written about the breaking-in of a pipe than about any other aspect of pipe lore. Really it's quite simple.

If the pipe, unfortunately, has been varnished, remove the varnish with fine sandpaper to open the pores as best you can—although you should avoid varnished and stained pipes. Once a pipe has absorbed varnish and stain, it is practically impossible to get it all out of the pores. 

We'll discuss this more fully later. If the pipe has simply been waxed or oiled, you'll not need to take these preliminary precautions. But if it's virgin briar, you'll find the breaking-in process as easy as falling off a log—and much more sensible. 

1. Wipe out the interior of the bowl with a cloth to remove all dust, then moisten the bowl. Plain water is excellent, but if it's a virgin briar you may use half water and half either brandy or pure grain alcohol.* Dip the [28]

bowl of the pipe in the bath for a minute or two, then spill out the contents of the bowl and wipe the outside dry, leaving the inside damp. Don't wet the shank.** This procedure is only for the first time the pipe is smoked. 

Avoid honey or other sugary sweeteners at the start.

The sugar they contain burns and makes the pipe hot. It also gives a phoney, porous, cheesy cake that is liable to crumble away in places after you've built a satisfactory cake on it, making it necessary to ream out the whole cake and start all over again on a new one. 

2. For the first few times, fill the pipe only enough to start a nice cake at the heel. After this cake is started, slowly increase the amount of tobacco smoked at a time until the cake is built up along the entire pipe bowl interior. 

If you do this the pipe will be less bitter and you'll be sidestepping trouble from wet heels. Don't pack it too tightly. A free draft and a smooth-burning first pipefull is a long step toward proper breaking-in. By all means smoke slowly. If your pipe goes out a few times during the breaking-in period, you lessen chances of a spark causing a burn-out. 

3. Light the tobacco evenly over its entire surface (see preceding discussion in the Clinic). Don't hesitate to let it go out and relight it in case the pipe starts to overheat. Relighting won't interfere with the quality of the cake or the smoke. 

4. Smoke slowly, indoors, and keep away from drafts to prevent the pipe becoming overheated and cake forming unevenly or the pipe burning out. 5. Let the ashes remain undisturbed in the pipe until the pipe is cool when breaking-in the pipe. After the pipe is cool, empty it but don't scrape the interior. This helps cake the pipe. Remember, this applies only to while the pipe is being broken in, not smoking after the first dozen pipefulls. 

6. Let the pipe rest between smokes. Don't oversmoke it. Resist that powerful temptation to break-in the pipe all at once, and you'll certainly be better satisfied with the way it smokes in the future.

BURNT-OUT PIPES 

The trouble here isn't with the pipe. It's with the smoker. He overheats the pipe, causing the bowl to char slowly away and possibly to be penetrated eventually by the intense heat. You can tell that you're overheating a pipe if the pipe burns your tongue or if it feels very hot to the touch. These are your warning signs that mean you're to investigate the cause of the trouble. 

You'll pardon my talking to you like a "Dutch uncle," but it's necessary. Burning out of a pipe is a serious offense to all sensitive, pipe-loving smokers. Briar is wood, not asbestos. If you smoke it in a high wind you cause tremendously rapid oxidation of the tobacco and create a degree of intense heat that no wood can withstand. 

Similarly you get the same affect by being an "asthmatic" smoker and both inhaling and exhaling through the pipe. 

Don't blow back into your pipe. You're not smoking a blow-torch! There are even supposedly intelligent smokers who drive in open cars with lighted pipes in their mouths or hands and express surprise if those pipes burn out! [30]

Sometimes it's the pipe's fault, it's true. Occasionally you'll encounter one that has a soft spot that catches the fire and holds it. Any reputable manufacturer gives you a guarantee against the pipe burning out within a reasonable time just to protect you and the manufacturer's good name. If this pipe burns out, it may be exchanged. The bowl is sent to the factory and you get a new one.

But the factory marks the bowl so that if you attempt to exchange it again you're classed as a burner and are entitled to no future replacements. 

Let your pipe go out if it gets too hot and start smoking again after the pipe cools. Take it from your mouth after each puff. Follow these two precautions and you'll be out of the burn-out habit in no time. 

When smoking out of doors, pack the pipe tighter than normally and leave the ash in until the pipe is completely smoked out. These expedients tend to slow down the rate of combustion. 

And, incidentally, if you find the pipe is burning your tongue, even though you think you're smoking it slowly, try hard candy, such as lemon drops, between pipefulls, or switch your brand of tobacco to a slow burning mixture such as any good tobacconist can blend. If this does no good, quit smoking for awhile; you may be suffering from indigestion or a particularly sensitive tongue. 

THE PIPE THAT COLLECTS CONDENSATION 

Probably your first impulse when you find this musical, bad-tasting moisture in your pipe is to "break" the pipe, or remove the stem from the shank, and shake out goo or else to shake it out through the stem, leaving a bitter taste on the bit. Be careful to avoid this though; the pipe is warm and the briar is expanded. You'll chance cracking the shank if you remove the stem while the pipe's hot. Toss out the tobacco, absorb the tar oil and saliva with [31]

an absorbent cleaner and take the pipe to your favorite repair shop.

Ask the repairman to loosen the fit of the stem in the shank without risking your pipe shank's unity. Have an expert do it so that it won't be so loose the stem will fall out or still too tight after a little was shaved away. After the pipe has been loosened in this manner, you may safely "break" it while the tobacco is still burning, clean the stem by blowing through it, and clean the shank by centrifugal force. 

Remember: This advice is only to wet smokers, smokers who permit saliva to enter their pipes. Such a remedy is really a stop-gap while the wet smoker learns to smoke a pipe dry (see first discussion in the Clinic).

The wet smoker is wise to get an extraordinarily large pipe collection too so pipes will get a chance to dry out between smokes so he will be able to smoke each pipe only once when its turn in the rotation comes around, giving it a better chance to dry out quickly and completely, eliminating tampering with the fit of the stem. 

PROPER PIPE-CLEANING PROCEDURE 

Use pipe cleaners lavishly. They're inexpensive and probably the smoker's most helpful tool. A straightstemmed pipe, made without obstructing gadgets, may be cleaned without detaching the stem and can be kept much more sanitary than the more complicated types, since it's no trouble to run a cleaner or two through the pipe after smoking it. 

And then when you're through with the pipe for the day you'll want to clean it more thoroughly. A pipe is hot, expanded, immediately after it has been smoked. 

That's no time to remove the stem from the shank (called "opening" or "breaking" a pipe). The shank might crack.

So tap the pipe gently in the palm of the hand to remove [32]

ashes and any wet heel present, blow through the stem, push a pipe cleaner through the stem so that its tip enters the bowl, and rest the pipe vertically, bowl down. Let it remain in this position until it is completely cooled and dry. 

The cool, dry pipe may be "broken" or "opened" with complete safety. Pull the cleaner out through the tenon end of the stem to prevent getting goo on the mouthpiece. Then fold the stem double and swab out the shank. The pipe should then be ready for storing for future use.

Don't retire with a dirty pipe in the house. Leave a dry pipe cleaner in each one you've smoked during the day to absorb moisture. Remember to put it away in the vertical position so moisture can gravitate to the bowl from which it is most easily evaporated. 

Put your pipes away for a few days rest while they are still sweet. A pipe requires a longer vacation after it has started to go bad.

PIPES THAT GO SOUR 

When a pipe has been oversmoked and carelessly cleaned for a long time, it protests and tastes foul. The tars and acids in condensation have done their work and the pipe ceases to be a source of pleasure. Then is the time for a good pipe root cleaner and sweetener. Pick one with a pure grain alcohol base and a chemically pure preservative or disinfectant such as oil of wintergreen or oil of clove. Give a wide berth to supposed cleaners with acetone, ether, acetic acid, vinegar or other too-strong or toxic bases. 

Use a little swab or stiff brush for cleaning out the shank. One usually comes with the pipe root cleaner. 

Don't be afraid to scrub. Rinse the brush in hot water after it has been in the pipe to avoid contaminating the [33]

fluid in the bottle and repeat the process until the pipe is satisfactorily clean. Watch against discoloring the stem by the action of hot water or alcohol on it. If you do so discolor the stem, clean off the color with Bon Ami. 

Use a dull reamer to bring the cake down to around a sixteenth of an inch or the traditional "thick as a nickel". 

Don't use a knife. You're not whittling. This helps both eliminate the area of sogginess and protects you against the bowl becoming temperature cracked. Briar and carbon expand at different rates when they're heated and if the cake is too thick the difference in their expansions may be enough to crack the bowl.

A clean cloth dipped in the solution is excellent for rubbing off wax and dirt on the outside surface of the bowl. This opens the pores from the outside (if it's a virgin briar) and lets the pipe breathe. If it has been varnished, shellacked or lacquered, remove the finish. 

If you carry a "smoker's companion" with you at all times you'll find the prevention of the pipe becoming soggy to be greatly simplified. The "companion" is a little metal gadget with three arms. One, like a large nail-head is a tamper, helpful in filling and lighting the pipe properly. Another is a pick that is often handy in removing clogs in stem and shank. The third is a spoon for cleaning out wet heels that hold the pipe-spoiling moisture in the pipe and delay its evaporation. Don't leave wet heels in pipes. 

Put the "sweetened" pipe away for awhile. Air it for a few cold, dry, wintry nights. In a few days—or weeks, or months-it'll forget you've mistreated it and will return to your service. Of course this type of cleaning will work perfectly only on the natural virgin briar, not on the pipe that has been stained and varnished. The pores of these pipes wouldn't open properly in a Turkish bath! [34]

THE PROBLEM OF SWITCHING PIPE TOBACCO BRANDS 

Remember the carbon and briar in the pipe absorb tar oils from the tobacco. After you've stuck to one brand of tobacco for awhile your pipe gets used to that brand.

Now if you switch brands and change to a tobacco of widely different mixture, you're sticking your neck out. 

Sometimes the tobacco to which the pipe is accustomed won't go with the new brand and the result may be something you'll regret. It will probably take over a score of pipefulls before the new brand has completely supplanted the flavor of the old one in any pipe that has been smoked much. Ream and thoroughly clean the pipe and air it for several days before trying the new blend, to give the merits of the new brand an even break.

LOOSE PIPE STEMS 

A new plug inserted by an expert pipe repairer is the only permanent cure for a loose stem. However, you can affect a temporary correction of the trouble by heating the tenon of the bit, then expanding it by pressing on its nose while it is still heat-softened. Be sure to press it straight downward to avoid bending it out of shape. 

Don't widen it too much. It's better to increase the bit little by little to be sure not to overdo it. Remember that this is only a temporary cure. As the pipe becomes heated by smoking the pressure of the shank on the bit forces the bit back into its former shape. 

THE PIPE BREAKER 

Few men who respect their pipes and give them the care to which they are entitled ever have cause to make this complaint. If you like your pipes, take care of them [35]

and they'll last for many years more than most other small personal possessions.

The hip pocket is no place in which to carry a pipe. 

A vest, breast or side pocket is much better. And if these are inconvenient, leave your pipes in your overcoat, automobile pocket, brief case, lunch box or wherever else you may be able to keep it out of harm's way. 

And when emptying pipes, don't hold them by the tip of the stem to tap out the tobacco. This causes tremendous leverage, gives you a weak grip on the pipe from which it may easily slip and is dangerous to the pipe. 

Rather, cup the hand gripping the pipe around the base of the bowl or use your spoon in the "smoker's companion" to empty it. 

Cracked bowls result from banging the pipe against hard objects in emptying them or from letting the cake in the pipe get too thick, then smoking the pipe out of doors in cold weather. The effect of the former cause is obvious. That of the latter results from the carbon expanding more than does the briar under certain temperature conditions. If it cannot expand within the pipe, it breaks its way through the pipe walls. 

Cracked shanks come from wetting the shanks or heating them (which expands them) and then attempting to force the bit into the shanks. Make certain that the pipe is absolutely cool and dry before extracting or inserting a stem, and then don't force it. It may need refitting. 

Sometimes repeated immersions in water and drying out of the shank will crack it. Grasp the stem in the palm of your hand, not by the tip, and pull and turn it at the same time when extracting stem.

Broken stems come from dropping the pipe, biting through it, or improper emptying of the pipe. We've covered the proper methods of handling the former and [36]

the latter causes. But the stem-biter is a new problem. 

Use a thin stem and you'll not be compelled to clamp down on it like a bulldog. Use a softer bit than the molded type and you'll find the stem holds out longer. Sometimes you can file a new mouthpiece onto the old bitten-through stem. More often you've no recourse but to get a new stem.

A three-cornered file is used to extract the tip of stem usually left in a broken pipe. Push the file in as far as it will go and then twist and pull at the same time. If this doesn't work, try a gimlet to remove the obstruction. If the shank has a metal band, there's no danger of breakage from this. However, heating the metal band to soften the bit stub may help in the extraction of the stub. If the plug is held in place by dried tar oils, use pipe root sweetener in bowl and shank, let it soak thoroughly, then try the file again.

If the stem is too large, place it on a sheet of unmounted sandpaper of the finest grade held in your other hand and twirl the stem until it is sanded down to the proper size. 

If pliers, reamers, pipe cleaners and the pick on your "smoker's companion" fail to remove an obstruction from your pipe's air chamber, try pure grain alcohol held in the pipe (with the mouthpiece and bowl plugged up) for several hours. This may disintegrate the obstruction. If this fails it may be necessary to have your repairman redrill the pipe opening. 

Replace a broken molded vulcanite bit with a new one. It's cheaper than having the old one repaired. Bits cut from the block find their most vulnerable spot where the strong-jawed smoker bit down too long and too hard. 

To repair these, file down the mouthpiece to where the stem is solid again and cut down the edge to get a place where the teeth can grip it. This looks rather crude, but [37]

it works. To give it a better finish, sand it with fine sandpaper and burnish it with a hard, smooth metal object. 

RETAINING THE PIPE FINISH 

Only time-mellowed and aged antique furniture can compare with the soft lustre, the richly glowing finish of the properly polished pipe. The pipe is polished not with varnishes or lacquers, but by prolonged service and the affection of its owner. 

Toss your stains and varnishes into the trash can, sand off those cheapening polishes from your pipes, and let the polishes that are the pipe's privileges alone go to work on it. The natural oil from the hand of the smoker and from the tobacco will, in time, put an unmatchable polish on your pipe and bring out markings and grain.

It is a finish that no manufacturer, with commercial stains, varnishes and secret finishing process, can duplicate. 

And the more the pipe is smoked, the more its color deepens, darkens, richens. To observe this progress, use a chamois cloth and rub the pipe before and after each time it is smoked.

Occasionally the pipe will be service-soiled, possibly a little battle-scarred. At these times send it to a professional pipe renovator or put it in the hands of anyone who respects pipes and has a high-speed linen buffing wheel. 

The wheel is used to apply very hard wax to the pipe.

Many veteran smokers see that their pipes are given complete overhaulings three times a year, at the same time they're machine polished. This overhauling includes cleaning, reaming, sweetening shank and polishing the stem back to its original black velvety, lustrous finish. Use 

Bon Ami for a stem polish and to remove discoloration; or, better still, use glycerine and rotten stone on a wheel. 

Your renovator will probably overhaul and polish the pipe for about 35 cents. [38]

Then to retain this polish, use the chamois cloth frequently, occasionally rub the pipe along the oily pores just behind the nostrils, don't rest the pipe in an ash tray where ashes and coals may mar its finish, and handle the pipe carefully—as you would a valued piece of antique furniture. [39]

CHAPTER III BRIAR PIPES O EPHEMERAL THING IS YOUR PIPE. For 

No years it should serve your moods, calm you, blend into your thoughts. So pick it carefully, smoker, that your companionship with it may be of the fullest. 

And how to choose a pipe?

Let's travel its length seeking points of quality. Then let's review its history from the freeing of the briar burl from its hard-won place in earth, through its formation in the hands of craftsmen until it comes into your hands, a thing of beauty, a source of pleasure. Soon your familiarity with pipes and briar will be far superior to that of most veteran pipesters.

OF WHAT THE PIPE IS MADE 

First, the briar. Get an all briar pipe, not a patchwork of clay or putty fills, or a disguised piece of kindling hiding behind a false-face of flaw-hiding stain and cheap, shiny varnish. Putty in a pipe that is stained light stays light long after the rest of the pipe has darkened. These pipes are then of remarkable value-if you're gunning for the first prize at a Hard Time party! Choose a lightfinished pipe, a virgin briar, so that, like any other friend, you may be well aware of its faults and merits. Be sure it has no stain, varnish, or plugs of putty or clay and that it has not been waxed. The true virgin briar is aged, cured, sweet-smoking from the first puff. It breaks in quickly, colors well, and gives the utmost in pipe smoking pleasure. [ 40 ]

Insist that your dealer guarantee your pipe to have no putty or clay fills, no stain, no varnish or other artificial, pore-clogging finish of any kind. Buy only a clear, pure briar that has simply been sanded smooth. This has its pores open, so that tar oils may be absorbed by the briar and exuded by its pores. It darkens in its service to you. and the grain becomes increasingly evident, constantly more beautiful, ever more valued by you.

Of course we're not implying that every rusticfinished, or stained pipe is sold by a charlatan and manufactured of disguised materials. The reputable manufacturer of an established, high-priced pipe, we can assume, colors and rustic-finishes his pipes to appeal to certain classes of pipe buyers and uses first quality materials. With all other brands of dark-finished pipes, however, you're taking a long-shot gamble and while you may get a good smoke from one of these "treated" pipes, you won't get the best. Coloring matter must necessarily interfere somewhat with the porousness of the briar—and it's the porousness of the briar that, with its toughness, makes it the finest material in the world for smoking pipes. Once a pipe has been stained, some of the coloring material must always remain in its pores and interfere with its taste. 

SELECTING THE BOWL 

The bowl, be it barely larger than a thimble or approaching the size of a teacup, should be chosen in accordance with your own wishes and yours alone. So long as it is made of good briar almost any size will do, just so the walls are not egg-shell thin. The middle and lower portions of the bowl should have walls around threeeighths of an inch thick or thicker. The upper part of the bowl may be thinner if you wish. A pipe including much briar has greater absorption capacity of tar oils than small, [ 41 ]

thin-walled ones, but don't get a pipe too big and heavy for your jaws and teeth. The badly balanced young treestumps some pipesters try to smoke are exhaustingly heavy!

Be sure the bowl is pure briar, not lined with patented materials or coloring matter. These substances, on which are based the old malarkey about a pipe not needing to be broken-in, are either neutral (in which case they're useless) or are positively a deterrent to proper breaking-in of the pipe. No pipe is already broken in for you. It must become accustomed to your brand of tobacco, to the type of cake you like, to you, your smoking habits. The simple virgin briar, free from stain and varnish, breaks in best. 

Pipes are as individual as finger-prints and may only be fitted to the smoker by his smoking them!

SELECTING THE SHANK 

The shank may be long or short; it makes little difference. A long pipe doesn't smoke any cooler than a short one and is usually more poorly balanced. A fast smoker could burn his tongue were the smoke to travel the length of a garden hose! Smoke in your eyes is a different matter. First try increasing the angle to the side at which you hold your pipe. Then try smoking less vigorously, so that only a thin wisp of smoke is kept curling from your pipe. When these two recourses have failed, a longer or a bent stem is in order.

We naturally assume that the shank of the pipe is of briar. Occasionally some other material enters the field, but it doesn't last. For example, the duraluminum-shanked pipe was first introduced to the market for from $17.50 down to $10, depending on the size purchased. It held a small, exclusive market for awhile despite the abnormal amount of condensation such a pipe caused and the [42]

ridiculous price. Then came imitators. First $3.50, then $2.50, $1.50, $1.25, $1.25 with an extra bowl, $1, finally 

49 cents, the price of the imitations toboganned! Down too came the price of the original metal-shanked pipe to around half its former amount.

Then the money-losing newcomers to the field were modified. Plastic stems were tried with plastic shanks and the bowls, like those of the metal-shanked pipes, still sat on top the stems like a too-small hat. One by one they dropped from the field. A few stubborn ones stayed on and were rewarded with a little market of freak-seekers. 

But the briar shank remained supreme since it and only it had the combination of beauty and utility, of porosity. and line that makes a pipe look and smoke like a pipe.

THE PROPER BORE 

The bore of a pipe, unlike its unwelcome social polynym, is probably the most essential and crucial point in its manufacture, in its smoking quality and, consequently, in its appeal to you as a purchaser. If the bore is straight and true, entering the bowl in the center at exactly the bottom of the tobacco chamber, you're saved the annoyance of a moisture pit at the heel of the pipe or of a deposit of unburned tobacco after every pipeful smoked. A perfect bore means a perfect draft. If it's too large it smokes too hot and the tobacco comes through the shank. If it's too small the smoker is forced to puff too hard. But if it's perfect the smoke is cool and clean. 

The bore should be on a straight line with the opening in the stem and just large enough to admit a pipe cleaner. 

The pipe cleaner should run straight through shank and stem without obstruction, greatly simplifying the cleaning process. [ 43 ]

FILTERS AND TRAPS 

Filters, moisture condensation chambers, slop traps and other such fittings are unwelcome nuisances that have probably done more to detour smokers from pipes than anything except wifely protests. Some of the paper filters are terribly foul messes! And metal "filters", instead of allowing the tar oils to oxidize, compel the smoker to draw his smoke through the "soups" to the harm of the smoker and the pipe. Metal also causes excessive condensation and conducts heat its length. 

An even more unwelcome after-effect of these contraptions is that too often pipes are advertised and sold for the intricate filter devices instead of the briar, because a better, though less to-the-point sales story can be built around a dramatic-looking gadget.

To get an idea of the ridiculous lengths to which these filth-catchers have been carried, write to the government patent office, enclosing a self-addressed stamped envelope for information on patents held for the would-be "betterment" of pipes.

Pipe manufacturers and their distributors are fully aware of the unnecessary complication of a pipe by gadgets. A leading distributor of imported pipes sent out a report stating that pipes shouldn't be purchased for "the gadget, tube or internal mechanism as only quality and properly aged briar attributes to the smoking quality and inveterate smokers almost invariably destroy whatever tube may be furnished them in a new pipe."

The sincerity here is admirable especially when we learn that this distributor's leading imported brand has one of the largest and most outlandish pieces of mechanism employed in the bowels of a pipe! Somehow we can't help being reminded, by the tricky chamois or leather googetter, of a plunger used in cleaning out a toilet bowl! And [44]

their troubles pyramid. The new models of a brand of pipe using leather plungers are now made with a metal ring inside the shank. It seems the leather stuck to the shank and cracked it when the stem was inserted!

I have never seen a gadget that is any good-and we've searched for it for years!

Filters often leak and manufacturers lie to each other, the public and themselves in order to obtain a get-richquick priority on some Buck Rogers style contraption.

It's a sordid mess aroused by an unworthy object. 

Like the metal-shanked pipe, many of these gadget chamber-cloggers are "seven-day wonders" that live for a short time until the smoking public has them properly appraised and then they die a slow, cut-price death. Think of the pipe that would have its owner insert a couple of cigarettes in the shank as filters before it is smoked! How many pipe smokers are willing to lug a package of cigarettes around in order to smoke their pipes without having huge empty chambers to speed up the combustion and make the pipe smoke terrifically hot? It's a swell idea from the standpoint of cigarette manufacturers and patent seekers-but its real merit ends there. There are also pipes that "clean like a shotgun," have wells for collecting moisture (which stains and fouls the hands when you attempt to remove it), contain patented gadget after screwball ideas, all for the sake of a new sales argument and a complicated pipe.

A wad of cotton, a folded pipe cleaner, carburetors, stokers, these are just a few of the contraptions we find offered as pipes. In fact the gadget-charged pipes have taken over such a large part of the market that it is often hard to find pipes free from such nuisances. If you have difficulty obtaining pipes without unwelcome adornments (or disfigurements) and meeting the other requirements set forth in this section, drop a penny post card to [ 45 ]

the author, 59 West Monroe Street, Chicago, and he will gladly let you know where such pipes may be obtained. 

SELECTING THE STEM 

Amber, ivory and other exotic substances are used for pipe stems and even hard and soft plastic stems exist now, but nothing has been tried as long and proven as satisfactory as a good old para rubber or vulcanite bit. If you bite through your pipe, choose a vulcanite stem "cut from the block" or carved out of a solid piece of vulcanite. 

These are softer and more resilient than the molded vulcanite stems which are made by heat and compression or injection molding and are hard and brittle. Also the mouthpiece should not be too thick because if the bit is thick, the smoker must clamp down on it harder to keep it in his mouth. A wide, thin mouthpiece is usually best since this permits several teeth to grip the stem and distributes the pressure. Get a short stem and shank if the pipe jiggles as you smoke it and is hard to hold steady. 

The stem should fit snugly into the shank but it should not require a trip hammer to be set in place nor a vise and lever to be extracted. 

SOURCES AND BRANDS 

Now it's still possible that a pipe which has apparently met all of the requirements we've outlined will not smoke well. Something has been-accidentally or deliberately-put over on you somewhere along the line. Your best defense against this is to buy from a reliable source on a money back guarantee. You simply can't go wrong this way. Or, if you prefer and are willing to pay the premium price required for the protection, buy a brand which has been proven good through years of carefully built up reputation. But with neither varnish, putty nor stain on the pipe! [ 46 ]

And pick an American make. There's a duty on imported pipes that is sometimes in excess of 40 per cent of their selling price! You can't possibly be getting all you pay for in such a pipe. The briar in American made pipes comes from the same sources as that in foreign made ones. 

American craftsmanship is unsurpassed anywhere in the world. It's only a silly type of bootlicking that makes us kowtow to the trade name of imported pipes and pay a premium of well over half the purchase price to do so. 

Smokers are often "taken for a ride" as a result of this weakness. One mail order pipe house tacked "Ltd." on the tail of its name, used the word "London" freely in advertising and on packages, employed British references and terms, naming pipes after various stock English characters, showing a near-replica of the Royal Coat of Arms and the Official Seal of the King of England and things of that sort and built up a beautiful business among American suckers until the Federal Trade Commission slapped a rein on them. 

Another used the name of an English city in its business name, advertised pipes as being "The Best British Brand" and again turned tail and ran on the suggestion of the Federal Trade Commission. 

There are lots more. If you're interested ask the 

Federal Trade Commission, Washington, D.C., about them. No punches are pulled in naming names there. 

Beware of this ambiguous advertising and sky-high promising. You pay for what you get usually or else you're overcharged. Dealers take advantage of the neophyte pipester's awe of foreign made pipes. And the legitimate manufacturers of British and other foreign-make pipes similarly keep their incomes sizable as a result of this widemouthed admiration new pipe-buyers have for antiquated names and foreign countries' products. [ 47 ]

No country has craftsmen that surpass those of America in making quality pipes. All use the same quality materials. And no import duties burden the price of 

American made pipes. 

So-Buy American.

PIPE STYLES AND COLLECTIONS 

No sophisticated pipe smoker has "a pipe." He has "pipes." Possibly only seven possibly (if he's a wet smoker) many times this number. But there's a pipe for every purpose and even a pipe has to have a few days' rest once in awhile. 

Do you spend much time out of doors? Then it's the sturdy, thick-shanked, tough, short, heavy pipe for you. 

Do you grab quick smokes at the office or during intermissions? Then pick the flat little pipe that fits the vest pocket without being too hard on the lining. 

Do you smoke around the house, after meals and while reading? Then you'll go for the big-bowled pipe with the curved stem. 

Do you hunt and fish? Then pack a big "pot" or "bulldog" pipe into your kit. 

Do you golf? A short, small pipe will not interfere with your swing and will console you after those 300 yard hooks or slices off the fairway. 

Do you smoke while driving? Then the straightstemmed medium bowled pipe with a dime gadget fitted over the top, to prevent ashes and coals going berserk, belongs in your collection. Give a wide berth to the pipe with a cap made of briar. If the briar is first quality you pay a stiff price. If you pay the same price for a pipe with a briar cap as for one without it, you're taking a chance of getting inferior quality in the former. [ 48 ]

There should be at least seven pipes in your collection, one for every day in the week. You're the best judge of the styles you like best. And stick to conservative shapes in your collection of pipes for smoking. Later we'll discuss the other materials of which pipes are made and the freak shapes and styles, exotic models, that you may want as collectors' items. But these briars are your smoking pipes. The dry smoker will find seven pipes enough. A good briar smoked several times a day and alternated and not smoked for three or four days is pretty sure to keep on smoking nicely unless you're a wet smoker who can only smoke one pipe once a day. In that case you'll need a much larger collection.

THE SOURCE OF BRIAR 

Before we can discuss what you should pay for a good briar pipe, we'll have to cover the background of briar. 

Where it comes from, how it reaches the manufacturer, manufacturing processes through which it passes, selection and grading of bowls and further details about the stem. 

High in the mountainous Mediterranean countries, where the soil is barren and rocky and where growing conditions are among the most adverse in the world, grows a tough little shrub, a member of the heather family. Botanists call it Erica Arborea. We call it briar.

Strong mountain winds tear at the hardy little plant. 

The soil resists its efforts to grow. But the briar drives its roots into the tiniest of crevices and forces them wider little by little. Fighting tenaciously, first for a foothold, then for growth, the little shrub develops a tightness of grain, a hard solidity where it is in contact with the earth that makes it unique among plants of the world. Just above the roots, immediately beneath the juncture of the plant with the ground, is the toughest portion of the plant, [ 49 ]

a burl. This burl, found in most mature briar plants, is neither stem nor root although it is penetrated by the trunk of the briar and the roots seem to grow from it. It is an advance guard for the shrub forcing an opening in the dirt and rock above the roots and anchoring the trunk firmly against the elements that would break or bend it. 

Only this burl (often erroneously termed the "root") is used for pipes although in Italy the young shoots are cut annually and sold to street-broom makers in bundles since the shoots are long and tough. On tended briar-growing areas the "roots" are cut every three or four years and enough of the plant is left for further propagation for the next cutting, three or four years later. These young burls, however, are very small, making it almost essential that the entire burl be shaped into the pipe. It takes at least 

40 years for the burl to be big enough to be of real consistent value in making genuine briar pipes and not require the incorporation of the trunk portion in the burl into the pipe bowl. Therefore the choicest burls are the older ones since age makes for sturdiness and size, beauty and close grain, as well as the right degree of porousness. The trunk traveling on through the center of the burl differs little from ordinary lumber. The four-year or even other twelve-year exploited territories produce pipes generally inferior to those from the newly opened fields or the very old ones-ones which turn out briar from 40 to several hundred of age. years

Too, briar produced in the softer low-lying countries has less flaws and is softer than that which comes from the hard-soiled mountain regions. Therefore, since hardness is a virtue in briar, a perfect piece of briar from the mountain sections is more valuable than one from valley sections, even though the valley briar may be the more flawless of the two types. There's a difference in hardness and closeness of grain that gives the edge to the mountain briar. [50]

Phillips A. Hayward, chief of the Forest Products

Division of the Department of Commerce, writes us: "Briarwood is obtained from the tree Erica Arborea which does not grow commercially in this country and is native to the countries of Western and Southern Europe particularly Italy and France, and also Algeria. Briarwood is also obtained from Spain, Corsica, Sardinia, Sicily, Greece, Asia Minor and the northern coast of Africa. Erica Arborea is a dwarfed tree usually not more than 16 or 17 feet high. The roots (burls) furnish a hardwood of deep red color while that part of the shrub outside of the ground is used to a large extent in making brooms. "The wood of the roots (burl) is a fine, easily worked wood and is not easily charred. There are few steps in the process of manufacture. The extraction of the raw material is done by hand. The fibrous mass is then covered with straw and earth and dampened with water in order to protect it from the heat. The wood is then rough hewn, squared and reduced in size into various shapes suitable to the ultimate production of the finished pipe at the factory. 

The pieces are then boiled in water for about 12 hours and then left to cool in the boiler for an additional 12 hours so that the wood may be more easily worked. The rough hewn pipes are then for the most part exported to other countries where the final steps in the process of manufacture take place."

This is a concise, cursory summary of the early steps through which briar must pass, devoid of embellishments and giving you the story without romanticizing. From a report from Vice Consul Joseph I. Touchette, Algiers, (issued by the Department of Commerce) we learn that in recent years production of Algerian briar has been about 44 million pounds. "When production assumed commercial importance, circular saws were introduced into Algeria so that blocks could be cut for exports, thereby [51]

saving considerable freight on waste material. It is estimated that 70 per cent or more of a briar stump is wasted when converted into blocks of suitable size and quality for pipes." "the Government prohibits cutting during summer months, owing to climatic conditions, which cause considerable checking of the roots. Cutting begins with the first rain in November. Following sawing, the blocks are boiled in water for 12 hours to extract the sap and tannin. 

The blocks are then dried, graded and bundled in burlap bags for shipment, each bag containing from 60 to 85 dozen blocks." Cheapest of these bags sells for around $16.50 apiece. The higher quality briar blocks bring around $100 a bag. 

The burying of the briar under a blanket of moisture retaining material may require additional explanation. 

This facilitates the death of the briar without its cracking as it would if it were left where the hot air could get to it. 

The simmering or boiling of the blocks of briar serves to remove sap and tannin and causes the blocks to take on a yellow-brown color. The drying of the boiled blocks is one process manufacturers find particularly important. If it's done improperly an abnormally large percentage of the wood is wasted. The more slowly the blocks are dried on racks, the less likely it is that warping or cracks will mar the perfection of the briar. Sudden elimination of moisture causes warping or cracking. Also the briar retains greater strength if the moisture is driven out from it slowly and naturally.

Some factories pride themselves on taking three or more years to cure the briar going into their pipes. The minimum time that is allowed pipes to dry before they are baked to remove the last bits of moisture is three months. 

For these three months (or several years in certain cases) the blocks are kept in a dry room at normal temperature, [52]

protected from the elements but not subject to artificial heat. After this period they are placed in special driers to allow air to reach as much of the surface of each block as possible from all sides. The heat in these drying rooms is slowly raised from room temperature over a period of several weeks to a couple of months and the drying operation is finally pronounced complete, when the wood is bone dry. This entire drying process is referred to as "curing the blocks."

WHY BRIAR? 

But why all this trouble? Why travel to another country, go to its more inaccessible regions and dig a hardto-get chunk of wood that has to be transported to seaports by man or mule pack and then has to be babied this way? The reason is simply that there is no substitute for briar that can match it in quality! Don't take just our word for it. Let's consult the experts:

Quoting Tariff Information Surveys of the Tariff Act of 1913, "Briar root or briar wood is an imported wood derived from the white or tree heath (Erica Arborea)....

The wood is obtained from the burl where the trunk expands, at the base and is the only part of the tree used. The ivy and laurel woods are of domestic growth, found chiefly in North Carolina. They are somewhat similar in appearance and quality to the briar root, but are much softer and impractical for the manufacture of pipes. "Any substance used in making pipes, such as clay or meerschaum, may be regarded as a substitute. Attempts have been made to use the domestic ivy and laurel woods in place of the imported briar wood but these substitutes have not been found satisfactory by manufacturers. Other domestic woods, which are used as substitutes, are apple (for cheaper grades), manzanita, wild lilac, red gum, and birch. Ebony, an imported wood, is also used." [53]

Says Schedule 14, "Sundries," of the "Summary of

Tariff Information, 1929, series" compiled by the U. S. 

Tariff Commission: "Most of the tobacco pipes marketed in the United States have bowls made of briarwood and mouthpieces of hard rubber. . . . Briarwood is the most suitable of all woods used for pipe bowls. It is tough and durable and not easily split by smoking; it does not burn readily, and has a sweetness not found in other woods." 

Alexander L. Howard in "Timber of the World," writes: "The name is a corruption of the French bruyere (heath). This heath provides one more of the remarkable illustrations of how Nature has provided us with one wood, which surpasses all others, for a particular purpose for which we require it, because, in spite of world-wide efforts to find a suitable wood from which to make tobacco pipes, there is nothing comparable with the wood of Erica Arborea."

BRIAR PIPE MANUFACTURE 

And so we find briar the king of pipe-making materials. From the manufacturer's standpoint, after it has been cut into blocks and cured, the brittleness of the briar is all-important. If it is brittle it will tend to crumble when the cutting tool works on it and will be less inclined to split (which would be disastrous). It cuts the same in all directions, resulting in a smooth surface. Were it to have rings of annual growth well defined between soft, porous pulpy layers the surface would be rough on the finished pipe; the porous area offering less resistance to the cutting tools. If the hole is drilled through stem and into the bowl in these ringed woods the drill is likely to follow the soft band in the wood and ruin the block of briar. 

Therefore, the manufacturers of quality pipes stick to briar -it's almost totally free from these annual rings. [54]

The early shaping of these pipes is almost as exciting as the opening of oysters on a pearl hunting vessel! There is always a chance that the block of briar that is in the hands of a craftsman this very minute will turn out to be one of those extremely rare, very straight, close, beautifully grained pieces of briar without a flaw that will command almost any price the owner wishes. It won't smoke any better than any other flawless pipe of like briar, but its merit lies in its rarity and beauty. 

This is the exceptional pipe, so rare that it is not listed in any of the general commercial classifications of briar pipes. We'll discuss these various classifications in the next section, under "Briar Pipe Selection."

First step in manufacture after the blocks are cured and thoroughly dried is the trundling of the blocks to the benches of skilled inspectors who judge the blocks as to size and apparent flaws. It's difficult to ascertain the depth of some flaws until after the pipe has been cut. Some surface flaws on the block seem to be dangerously deep and some deep flaws are not noticeable on the surface. 

Therefore the most skilled of selectors, usually men who have worked with briar for many long years, are employed in this inspection for they save the company the expense of processing blocks of briar that will later have to be discarded as inferior anyway. These men also divide the briar according to size.

The various sizes are cut down to standard size blocks -that is to the size of block necessary for turning out a certain shape and style of pipe-by use of regular woodworking machinery equipped with extremely hard metal cutting surfaces in order that each cut will be a clean one, that the instrument won't be easily dulled by the hard briar and carry too much "bite" into the wood, and that the operation may be as efficient as possible. Briar is no ordinary wood. [ss]

The block is then placed on a machine similar to a router, each block being held in a jig very rigidly. Then the cutting tools go to work on the block. One tool similar to the old-fashioned type of lemon squeezer or to the fourfinned reamer bores out the inside of the bowl and another, an enlarged intaglio of the tool just described shapes the upper portion of the outside of the bowl, down to the level of the shank.

A frazing machine forms the lower portion of the bowl and outlines the stem of the pipe. This machine is a modified lathe and the tools used in shaping the stem and the lower portion of the bowl are similar to lathe tools. 

Next comes the most delicate and most important of the operations in pipe making. It is started mechanically all right, with an upright borer starting the air chamber in the center of the shank, but this can only penetrate a short distance with safety, usually no more than an inch. 

After that comes a precise hand operation that can be done properly only by the most skilled of pipe makers: the boring of the shank. Even briar has hard and softer spots, straight grain and then perhaps a sudden quirk in the grain's direction, caused by the young root having to fight for its existence with hard, rocky soil. Briar isn't homogenous like plastics or steel. Only the most sensitive of hands detect when the bore is penetrating the shank too rapidly (a soft spot) or when it is working under difficulties (a hard spot or small particle of sand imbedded into the briar). They must be almost instinctive in their reaction to check the deflecting action of a twist in grain direction which might take ordinary woodworkers unaware and cause the bore to enter the pipe off-center. The bore must enter the bottom of the bowl interior at exactly the point where it terminates in order for the boring operation to be considered a success and the shaped pipe, even though flawless, to be considered of first grade. [56]

And next, the most debated department in the pipe manufacturing industry—the finishing department. The pipes are sanded down to a smooth, even finish by using progressively finer grades of sandpaper on belts that conform to the shapes of the pipes. Up to this point there is no debate. If the pipe manufacturing processes terminated here save for stamping trade names, fitting with bit and other absolutely necessary mechanical operations, all pipes would be virgin briar. If any of these pipes contained flaws, the customer could see them and not be fooled, by special processing, into believing that the flawed pipe is of the same quality as more valuable pipes. 

Even pipes that are devoid of flaws or have only minor ones are subject to debate over the question of finishing. One school says leave them alone; further processing only jacks up the cost to the consumer and, while it may enhance the beauty of a pipe or its saleability to stain, varnish and wax, it's not increasing its good smoking properties and more often than not impairs the smoking quality of the pipe.

The rival school doesn't disprove these arguments but points out, with some logic, that the manufacturer is in the pipe making business to make money and if inferior pipes can be salvaged and loaded on the market by use of a little filler, coloring material and some glossing material, well then, that's the stuff they are going to use. They'd already spent craftsmen's time and some money on the block before they found the flaws and they want to salvage their investment. Or they hold that even though their pipes are first quality and they know that they have grains that would make their owners possessively proud, the demand of uninformed customers for dark finishes and even for outside varnish makes it necessary to manufacture pipes meeting these specifications.

One distributor of pipes both stained and unstained, [57]

states: "A virgin briar is usually the pipe lover's choice as the grain may be examined more readily and flaws may be more easily discerned. Then the coloring quality as the pipe ages makes them a thing of beauty as there is no dye used in their finish. "Oil finish is the most expensive and desirable as it outlasts the shellac and lacquer finishes and because it is more expensive to produce. It is practically confined to the finer imported pipes." 

Says a far-famed British manufacturer who uses all types of finishes on his various lines of pipes: "The color of a Bruyere pipe is distinctive. Nothing but oil need be used in the polishing and finishing process.'

This latter group, then, dips the pipes in the color of stain that is currently in fashion among the trade. 

Buffing follows on all types of pipes, both painted and virgin briars. The buffing wheels are covered with various types of cloths or leathers and are designed to bring out grain pattern, remove excess stain, drive color into the briar or polish the pipe, depending on the speed of the buffing wheel and the type of material used on it. Some manufacturers use such polishing materials as pumice, tripoli or even rouge on their pipes during the final sanding and buffing operations but others maintain that some of these materials cause oil to be deposited on the pipe and therefore affect the purity of coloring of virgin briar. 

The pipe bowls are then sent to the mounting section of the plant where a pocket is made in the shank for the reception of the tenon of the bit and the necessary leveling off of the bit and shank takes place. We'll discuss stems in more detail later.

Those pipes that are not to be smooth finished are caused to undergo additional processes. These are termed "thorn," "rustic," "relief," or "shell" finish by various manufacturers. This process is invariably used on dark [58]

pipes and is occasionally another step toward concealment of flaws in the pipes. Sand blasting is the accepted method of causing the pipe to take on this finish, the sand cutting out depressions in the soft parts of the bowl between the hard portions, causing the former to stand out like the shell of a walnut. Experiments are now being conducted by certain pipe manufacturers to bring out a natural finished pipe with this type of graining. If successful, it should embody the advantages of the natural virgin briar (they're numerous) and also incorporate the beauty and comfortable feel of the shell briar. 

Another type of special processing of briar bowls is the boiling in oil or oiling them at intervals of a few days for several weeks to give them a dark color without the use of pore-clogging stain or varnish. This is less harmful to the pipe than staining or varnishing, it increases its cost considerably and it is a process that time would otherwise accomplish more attractively for the smoker. The pipe would take on its natural oils from the hands of the smoker (or his nose if he rubs his pipe bowl on the oily pores behind his nostrils) and from the tar oil of the tobacco. 

BRIAR PIPE SELECTION 

Now in the course of manufacturing any lot of pipes, the maker is bound to discover a high percentage of bowls turn out to have flaws in them. Others without flaws have imperfect graining, soft spots, sand spots or other features that prevent their being classified as first grade. 

These bowls are then assorted into sixteen different classifications by experts who inspect them inside and out with the most careful of precision. Since no two briars are alike, it is necessary that this be an important, and expensive, hand operation.

First quality blocks have no flaws such as cracks that might otherwise be filled and are properly bored, the bore [59]

running down the center of the shank and entering the interior of the bowl at the bottom center of the tobacco compartment. Occasional pipes of first quality, because of their flawless quality and their outstanding grainwhich is parallel, straight up and down, close and sharply defined command a price far beyond that of ordinary pipes. First quality briars invariably color nicely when they're smoked. A nicely colored, even-shaded pipe indicates a consistent and uniform texture of briar. 

From the purchaser's standpoint, in this pipe selection, we must remember that age and grain are characteristics by which briar is judged. However, once the pipe has been cut from the burl, age is undetectable by the average purchaser. Grain, then, and flawlessness are the two features by which the layman may judge a pipe.

In the sixteen classes of briar, top grade is usually less than 5 per cent of the pipes, unflawed specimens of excellent grain. In some lots the percentage is pronouncedly smaller. These usually bring the higher prices. Seconds in any manufacturer's line are usually sold under a separate trade name at slightly lower prices. Some unscrupulous dealers would have you believe that some of the classifications well down the line, possibly even in the thirteenth or fourteenth bracket are "seconds." Theirs is a false use of the term as we learn from the following letter from 

George A. Buchanan, secretary of the British Briar Pipe 

Importers Association, Inc.: "There is only one definite meaning of the word 'Second' as applied to a briar pipe and this is the quality immediately lower than the pipe referred to. For example, in the case of the Sasieni Pipe, their next pipe in price range would be the H.R.H. In the case of the GBD Pipe, the next pipe in price range would be the City de Luxe. "A great many retailers have been in the habit of taking a cheap pipe, which is perhaps five or six times removed [60]

in quality from the first grade made by the same manufacturer, and referring to it as the second of the first grade pipe. This is false advertising and such a statement made by a retailer cannot be reconciled with the actual facts."

Beneath the true seconds is a third group which contains flawless pipes of inferior grain. Then there are slightly flawed pipes of good and excellent grain and cheaper pipes ranging down to heavily filled and colored ones on the market for anything from a dime up. The number of pipes in the classifications increases with the decreasing quality. The lower the quality, the more pipes in it. 

Manufacturers of high quality pipes bootleg out to the regular channels of trade their inferior grades of pipes, although they customarily record their first, second and sometimes third quality pipes under widely publicized trade names. In these lesser grades of pipes, the quarter, 50 cent and dollar ones, there are none that will give perfect smoking satisfaction to the customer and most are simply a foolish investment. The purchaser does well to buy from only the most reliable of sources or else stick to a trade name that has won his confidence through past performances.

Much of the difficulty smokers have in getting used to pipes is a tendency to try to get used to these cheaper grades, atrocious quality pipes. They "plan to get a good one after I've learned how to smoke a pipe." Some pipes are worthless. Their value is as fleeting as that of the flashy junk you buy at carnivals or the prizes you win at street fairs. Pass by the gaudy, goofy styles and the shiny cheap ones and put the quarters and halves and dollars you'd have spent for them into a kitty with which to purchase good pipes. You'll be happier about your pipe purchases that way.

These cheaper flawed pipes that are sent to be filled [61]

before being stained and loaded on the unsuspecting customer, or "bargain" hunter are termed first, second, third-line putty, etc. These are invariably made with the bowls stained dark and varnished over. Often these cheaper grades and even pressed briar (briar ground into dust and held together by a bonding agent while shaped in a mold) are mistaken by the purchaser as an honest-to-goodness pipe value. Avoidance of dark stain and varnish of all types is the best possible safeguard against this mishap. It is also well to bear in mind that prices are regulated more or less by importers of briar pipes who insist on a substantial profit for distributing these pipes and set a retail price to the customer to allow the retailer a handsome profit also.

To bear this out the writer has personally negotiated for the importing of first quality flawless pipes from both France and England and this is the picture. 

The cost of a quality briar equivalent and comparable to the ten dollar standard set by several imported pipe lines, can be purchased F. O. B. England for approximately seven dollars a dozen American money' at the prevailing rate of exchange.

It is also of interest to know that practically all pipes imported from England selling for under five dollars are invariably puttied, and in some rare instances putty has been found in imported pipes selling in higher price brackets.

BRIAR PIPE PRICES 

On the ordinary market we find imported pipes of first quality selling for around $10. Seconds in these same lines are in the $7.50 neighborhood and thirds are usually $5. Fourth and fifth qualities of imported pipes are ordinarily $2.50 to $4 and about the cheapest imported pipes available are between $1 and $2.50. [ 62 ]

Equivalent qualities of pipes made in America and free of import duty sell for far lower prices BUT AT NO 

LOWER QUALITY. Of course any prices we discuss must be modified since we cannot allow for abnormal war conditions. During 1918 there simply weren't cheap pipes to be had and manufacturers attempted to conserve briar by making mostly smaller shapes of shorter-shanked pipes.

It so happens that at the time of the writing of this book, there have been no satisfactory imports of briar from Europe due to the war, and the result has been a lowering of quality in all pipes, imported and domestic. 

There has also been a sharp increase in the price of all pipes-in some instances as high as three dollars each. 

All large shapes have been taken out of pipe manufacturers' lines and the cheaper ones are being cut from North Carolina laurel and other substitute wood (see 

Briar Source).

In considering prices it is also important that we remember that first quality briar sells for about the same amount the world over and that the manufacturing operations on most pipes are essentially the same. Those manufacturers who loudly proclaim that their pipes undergo a fantastic amount of operations before they are delivered to the consumer seldom have pipes pass through more actual pipe-making processes than the pipes of quieter manufacturers. The surplus is usually in the special marking of the pipe or the inserting of gadgets, collars, ornaments or other patented modifications from standard in their pipes, virtually none of which improve the smoking quality of the pipes. And nationally advertised pipes must bring more money than the brands which are sold privately or direct. Somebody has to pay for the advertising and it's a cinch that the manufacturer has no intentions of doing it. [63]

Import duties are 40% and two and a half cents a pipe. Brokers' fees-inland freight-insurance and other incidentals bring the cost up to approximately fifteen dollars a dozen. After grading these pipes on a five dollar top quality basis in four grades to $1.75 low, a handsome profit as will be seen, was realized on an average of 4 price brackets. If you are still sceptical, let me assure you that the average ten dollar pipe delivers to the retailer at a range from $5 to $6.25. The exporter and distributor of this pipe deducts his profit from this figure. The government then deducts its 40% and two and one half cents per pipe.

With these considerations, let's look at pipes at their true value, free from the cost of very expensive advertising, trick finishes, cost-increasing gadgets, import duties and other burdens which are unnecessary.

A pipe of rare beauty of grain, first quality, devoid of flaws-a real collector's item-can be placed in the hands of the consumer at a price not in excess of $5! 

Some extremely rare ones may be worth more money, it is true, but they're extremely rare as we mentioned before-and they smoke no better than other first quality briars.

An excellent first quality briar pipe with good character and beautiful markings is worth around $3.50no more! 

First quality grades with good marking and fine sand spots should sell for around $2.50. And pipes of high quality, containing no putty or clay fills and with very slight surface sand spots (black specks), which won't interfere with the smoking quality, should be around $1.75 to $2. Anything inferior to these should be avoided by the smoker who respects himself and good pipes. 

Remember the best quality pipes have gone through the vat process, have been aired and seasoned and contain [64]

perfect wood, good workmanship and finest quality block Para rubber stems. A moderately high price is not too much to spend on such a pipe. Remember the cost per pipeful when evaluating a pipe and you'll find the cost over the period it will serve you is amazingly low. Compare it with the cost of cigarette smoking! 

A good pipe, like a fine violin, improves with age.

A cheap pipe, like anything else cheap, has a short and none too sweet life.

Many so-called exclusive tobacco shops which advertise pipes under their own name will take pipes made to sell regularly for $1.50 or $2 and, under the private label of the store, will bring somewhere in the neighborhood of $3. If you're still a novice in judging pipe values, insist on buying a registered brand under a specific label whenever a dealer insists that his private label is made by a reputable manufacturer.

In this way the buyer stands an even chance to get at least what he is paying for, not of giving a dealer in whom he has less than implicit confidence a chance to extort added profit from him. If you are an expert and able to recognize shapes and styles that indicate certain known brands, you will be able to recognize these imposters among private brand pipes.

Another wrinkle is for a tobacconist who is handy with a lathe and wheel to select a few clean and goodlooking pipes in a $1 or $1.50 price group, sand them down to take off the original shiny finish, trade marks and other identifying marks, and then alter the bowls of the pipes just a trifle. A bone screw is fitted into the stem for a drinkless effect, a brass nail is cut and inserted into the stem with the head showing for a gold dot and the stem is buffed with rottenstone and glycerine and finished with 

Diamond wax to give the effect of a hard vulcanite stem instead of simply a molded one. [65]

The sanded bowl is finished with pumice stone and oil. The pumice smooths out the scratches and the oil with a touch of stain brings out the grain. A felt buff with hard Carnaubas wax and the pipe really looks expensive.

The finished product brings anywhere from $5 to $15, depending on the gullibility of the prospect.

The story of course is "hand made at our own bench" or "made by myself from selected blocks." Look at the dealer's equipment. It takes drill presses, frazing wheels, lathes and a host of other equipment to manufacture pipes. 

Does he have equipment for pipe manufacture or only for pipe repair-and alteration? Does he have a few blocks of briar laying around the place or enough from which to make a real selection of the best for his customers?

Insist on seeing the pipe made from the rough block to the finished pipe. If he is truly all he claims to be he will be only too happy to permit you to witness the entire operation. It is of priceless advertising value to him and all true craftsmen take delight in showing off their handiwork. So again we advise you to pass up tempting offers of which you're not absolutely certain and buy from reputable, established stores, and choose recognized brands until you become connoisseur enough to judge quality. 

Similarly pipes advertised at $1 "worth $5" by various mail-order houses are seldom out of the $1 class. Study the ad carefully enough and you'll probably find in infinitesimally small letters "seconds." They're often "not at liberty to reveal the name of the internationally known manufacturer," or the pipes are "rejected for slight surface flaws so small the naked eye can't see them; the smoking quality is not impaired." Don't let these ads stampede you! The pipes are usually made to sell for $1, almost invariably in fact. Find out the exact nature of the imperfections. Often they're more serious than the ads lead you to believe. [66]

CHAPTER IV PIPES OF OTHER MATERIALS KING BRIAR STANDS UNSURPASSED at the head of the many pipe smoking materials. And these others materials are legion!

Taking some of them alphabetically we find that pipes have been or are being made of: amber, animal teeth, bamboo, basketry, bird's legs, bone (including human), bronze, cane, Chinaware, clay, copper, corncobs, earth, glass, gourds (sometimes with briar or meerschaum bowls), horn, iron, ivory (walrus, mammoth and elephant), leather, leaves, meerschaum, nut shells, porcelain, pressed briar, reeds, silver, steel, stones (precious and semi-precious as well as slate and the softer types such as catlinite, soapstone and steatite), and various types of woods! 

Of course for your regular pipes, the ones with which you do your "serious" smoking, you'll choose nothing but briar. However, occasionally you'll want something novel that will still give a fairly satisfactory smoke; or you may have left the house without a pipe and want something that could give a satisfactory smoke for a short time; or you'd like to add variety and color to your pipe collection.

Therefore we'll discuss a few of the more popular pipe materials other than briar. We'll leave the dissertations on pipes smoked by small groups of people in remote sections of the world and on those that were used by the aborigines to others who cover that subject more completely.* 

*For example: DUNHILL, ALFRED, The Pipe Book, The Macmillan Company, 

New York, 1924. [67]

MEERSCHAUM 

One of the beautiful old pipe-smoking traditions, meerschaum (which means "sea-foam"), is slipping into history at a rapid pace. This soft, white clay is often seen carved into intricate patterns, as a liner for calabash or briar bowls, in ordinary pipe shapes or as blocks with an opening hollowed out for the tobacco. 

This material is mined principally in Asia Minor from deposits formed, much like those of the coral island, from the shells of myriad tiny crustaceans. Each chunk of meerschaum is embedded in an outer layer of reddish clay-like material. It is soft when it is found in the earth, but it hardens upon prolonged exposure to air and forms the material with which we are familiar. The lumps must be hardened before they can be made into pipes. They are cleaned and polished to determine their quality, graded as to size and shipped to manufacturers. Good meerschaum is free from sand and highly porous.

Almost all meerschaum manufacturing is done by hand or with simple lathes. The block is soaked for about half an hour and then roughed into shape with coarse cutting tools. Then finer machine and hand tools are used to work the meerschaum. Foot lathes are employed to carve and semi-polish the outer part of the pipe. Drills cut the air chamber and tobacco container. The absorbed moisture is then dried out of the block, making it harder and more brittle. The stem is fitted, the pipe polished with special polishing materials used almost exclusively for polishing meerschaum, and the meerschaum is boiled in white beeswax before the final polish.

Screws are usually set into meerschaum bowls and their amber stems with cement. The cement may be softened enough to permit the removal of the screw by heating a wire and placing it in the opening of the screw. Screws may be reset in meerschaum or amber by mixing gum [68]

arabic with plaster of Paris and adding enough water to make a paste. It takes several hours for this mixture to harden. Any other breakage or difficulties to the meerschaum pipe should be repaired by an expert in meerschaum work. 

Meerschaum is often imitated. Cheapest of the imitations is unadulterated plaster of Paris! The trade name "Massa-Meerschaum" is applied to meerschaum dust and chips bonded together with adhesive or plaster of Paris and shaped into pipe bowls.

And now a few tips on the smoking of meerschaum pipes:

When you smoke tobacco directly in the meerschaum pipe, you create a baked portion of clay that deters the coloring from penetrating clear to the outside of the pipe save when it is very hot. Therefore use an upper, false bowl until your pipe is the exact color you want.

Don't hold the meerschaum pipe wrapped in a chamois unless you wish it to remain white. The chamois prevents coloring. 

Hold the meerschaum pipe by the stem when you smoke it, not by the bowl. This prevents blotches appearing on the bowl where the fingers held it.

The old superstition about boiling a meerschaum in milk is meaningless. The boiling does not particularly affect the color save that it may drive some of the wax from the bowl and the wax is the agent that holds the color in the pipe, but once it is colored it cannot be removed by boiling. And the heat of boiling may soften the screw in the stem.

Few smokers have success in coloring their meerschaums naturally by the smoking process. It is sometimes wise for them to have their meerschaums artificially colored or to do this themselves.

The artificial coloring process consists of scraping out [ 69 ]

the bowl interior and cleaning the inside with a pipe cleaner and alcohol. The stem is removed and the pipe is baked at 135 degrees F. for about an hour. Then a very fine abrasive is used to remove all scratches and dents. The bowl and stem hole are plugged and a paste, made of dyes of the correct colors and a carrying base such as linseed oil, is rubbed over the pipe evenly and smoothly. After this the color is baked into the meerschaum by holding the pipe over a lamp. Go over the whole pipe burning in the color lightly. 

Cool the pipe, then polish it. 

We suggest that you experiment with an old piece of meerschaum before trying to color a good pipe. 

The most beautiful pipes in existence are of handcarved meerschaum, the work of prolonged labor of master craftsmen. The fragility and high price of this material prevents it from being recommended as a pipe to be used regularly by the ordinary smoker.

CALABASH 

Calabash pipes are made of a type of gourd commonly grown in South Africa. The crooked necks of the gourds are ideally tapered to receive a stem, usually of vulcanite but occasionally of amber. The bowl is lined with block meerschaum, if it is a truly fine calabash. Others, the cheaper varieties, are lined with massa-meerschaum, porcelain, plaster of Paris, clay or briar. These cheaper pipes may even be made of American-grown gourds. Don't expect the top quality at bargain prices. 

The gourds are usually artificially curved by use of special weights and "setting" equipment to bend the necks to exactly the desired shape. The grown gourds are harvested, their necks severed, pith is extracted and the fleshy layer inside the gourd is scraped away. The outside of the gourd is scraped and polished with a fine abrasive and [70]

the gourds are dried in fresh air and sunlight. Seasoned gourds are fitted with mouthpieces and bowls. The bowls are set in cork or some other mount, usually so that they may be removed to simplify cleaning. The space beneath the bowl in the calabash is used to capture juices and assure the smoker of a cool, dry smoke. 

Clean the pipe before smoking, not while it is hot. 

Use ordinary fluffy pipe cleaners on the inside of the gourd and in the hole at the bottom of the bowl. Remember the meerschaum lining is fragile so don't hack at it with any metal instrument. Pure grain alcohol may be used in cleaning the gourd.

The calabash is less fragile than the meerschaum and gives a cool smoke but it is so bulky that it cannot be conveniently carried about as can the briar pipe. It is used most often at home while reading or wherever it may be conveniently stored.

CLAY 

About the only redeeming feature of the clay pipe is that it is cheap. It smokes hot, breaks easily, is most uncomfortable to the teeth and is seldom attractive. 

Ordinary mined or water-edge clay is used for their manufacture. It is ground fine, dried, roughed out and given to the pipe maker who shapes a bowl and runs a long steel needle through the stem for an air chamber. 

A steel mold shapes the outer part of the pipe and the pipe is finished, dried and kiln-baked. 

CORNCOBS 

"Washington, Missouri"—ever hear of it? That's the home of the far-famed "Missouri meerschaum," the corncob pipe center of the world! [71]

Not just any old cob will do for a pipe. It must be thick and long and have a reasonable straight section in it. 

Special care is taken of the corn around Washington, Missouri, to be sure it is just the right sort for cob pipes. 

These farmers receive almost as much for their cobs as they do for their corn!

Between one and two pipes may be made from each satisfactory cob. Cobs are automatically cut to the proper length and they're carried on conveyor belts to machines that automatically cut the recess for the tobacco. Handturning follows and then loose outer fibre is removed in a machine that's much like a cement mixer. Loose openings in the cobs are filled with plaster of Paris or other material to prevent excess porosity. The cobs are then dried, sanded, varnished, redried and smoothed off. Stem holes are bored, pipes are labeled, revarnished and stem-fitted. All sorts of materials are used for the stems and shanks. The stem determines the value of the corncob pipe. Some of these are as high-priced as $1, and the bottom price is a nickel. 

A few smokers prefer the corncob to any material except the pure briar. Corncobs smoke hotter than briar and are not suited to the beginner but they make a good, sound, temporary pipe. Being far more absorbent than briar, don't expect the cob pipe to last long.

PORCELAIN 

Porcelain pipes are tongue-burners. The most attractive of these are the European-made ones that are handpainted and fitted with weichsel (cherry-wood) stems, metal caps and tassels. These are the ones you often find associated with beer steins and are strictly show pieces. 

Best smoking of the porcelain pipes are the double-bowled Baronite styles. Even these have the disadvantage of fragility and they are not nearly as cool a smoke as briar. [72]

OTHER TYPES OF WOOD 

More direct substitutes for briar are those other woods, largely of American make that we've attempted to use for smoking pipes. Some have been moderately satisfactory as we can see from the following quotations:

H. S. Betts of the Division of Forest Products, the United States Department of Agriculture, writes: "The principal substitute for briar root is the root of kalmia or mountain laurel, the supply of which comes chiefly from 

North Carolina." 

A Pennsylvania report on "Wood-Using Industries” reads: "Five woods supply the raw material for this line of manufacture (smoking pipes); two of them are foreign species. Applewood leads the list as to amount, and is the only wood reported cut in the State. The best grades of pipes are made from French briar, ebony and rosewood, but the last named was not reported in Pennsylvania. The sapwood of sweet or cherry birch and red gum is used for cheap pipes, the former to imitate calabash and the latter rosewood. Olivewood pipes resemble meerschaum when finished."

A government report quotes "Crow's Pacific Coast 

Lumber Digest" of December 5, 1932: "What American woods are the best substitute for French briar in the manufacture of pipes is a question recently asked the U. S. 

Forest Service. 

"Two shrubs found extensively in Southern Oregon and in California, manzanita and wild lilac, have burls which local foresters believe are a very fair substitute for French briar. Of the two, wild lilac appears to offer the greater commercial possibilities, owing to the larger size of its burls and their more frequent occurrence. Qualities sought for in woods for pipe bowls are high resistance to charring, freedom from warping or cracking, attractive [73]

figure, ability to take color and polish, and a 'sweet' taste after continued use." 

Royal S. Kellogg in his book "Lumber and Its Uses" (1931) is quoted by a government report as follows: "Laurel (kalmia latifolia) is a fine-grained hardwood, produced in small quantities in the Southern Mountains. 

It is nearly as hard as dogwood, and as heavy as white oak. 

It is not available in large sizes nor in great quantity, and is known as Kalmia in the manufacture of tobacco pipes.' 

Applewood is also used for pipes, its principal value being its attractive color, which is darkened by long steaming at low pressure. Old orchard trees which have ceased to bear fruit furnish the raw material. 

The sweeter woods of ordinary types such as cherry (which heads the list), apple, pear and maple are far to be preferred to pressed briars or the very poor grades of briar pipes. They are more absorbent than briar but they give a pleasant, sweet smoke while they last and don't smoke very warmly. Hickory is another wood that can be made into an "ersatz" pipe. The pipester's major objection to laurel is its strong resemblance to briar in appearance after it has been treated with chemicals and darkened. 

In fact this resemblance in a pipe so treated has caused many of the laurel pipes to be passed on to the public as non-virgin briar. Purchasing of a true virgin briar is the layman's best safeguard against this. [74]

CHAPTER V PIPE STEMS PRICE, DURABILITY, BEAUTY AND COMFORT, all contribute toward the popularity of the Para or vulcanite (hard rubber) pipe bit as being the most generally satisfactory. Second, with its fragility and high price, induced by its rarity, as principal disadvantages, comes amber. Certain of the plastics are used by some manufacturers for transparent bits or trick colors, but many of these are harmful to the teeth, being extremely hard, or are very brittle. Soft bits lose their shape readily and soon look in the same class as a hand-rolled cigarette because of their lack of symmetry. Wooden bits won't stand the gaff of pipe smoking and ivory and other exotic substances never attained wide-spread popularity. So hard rubber stands generally supreme as a pipe bit material. 

This material is usually purchased in the shape of rectangular blocks or in long black cylinders the width of the widest portion of the bit. These cylinders are often pre-bored, saving the manufacturer that job. The manufacturer then cuts it to the desired length and shapes it on properly surfaced grinding wheels, usually using several wheels for the manufacture of each bit in order to counteract the clogging action of the small rubber particles and to use increasingly smaller sized abrasives so that the bit will take on its own distinctive, lustrous, velvety polish. 

Those manufacturers who have orders for very unusual stem shapes invariably cut them from blocks. 

Curved stems are bent by softening the bits with [75]

heat, then bending the tubing to the desired shape and holding them in that shape for the few moments it takes for them to "set" after which the shape is permanent. 

Sometimes they are cut from the block in the curved shape and then a series of separate straight bores are made in the bit, that in the arc of the curve being cut through the outside and the openings thus made being plugged. This method of bit making is comparatively rare and is disappearing since it makes cleaning of the stems difficult. 

The very cheapest type of hard rubber bit is made in either compression or injection molds. Compression molds the powder with heat into a half-stem shape. The two halves (halved the length of the stem) are joined with heat and the rough joining mark is abraded off. 

Injection molds flow molten rubber into a chamber in a die. The die is cooled by immersion in water, after which it is opened and the molded bits ejected. Where production is heavy, these bits are made in quantities, radiating out from an entrance chamber like the spokes of a wheel. The molten rubber is forced in through the central chamber and it penetrates all the compartments. The "fins" or portions of rubber adhering to the sides of the bit are sanded off and the bits are broken from the central piece (that was in the chamber) called the "gate."

Most bakelite, plastic and ambroid bits are purchased ready-made by pipe manufacturers and are made in a manner similar to that employed in making molded rubber bits. 

Just as with bowls, there are standard shapes and styles for stems and bits. The styles available all serve special purposes. The saddle bit gives added strength where the stem and shank meet and still does not force the mouth to open very wide to accept the stem. The bent stem allows more comfort to the smoker than the straight one, since it requires less leverage to hold it in the teeth. The [76]

fishtail mouthpiece is to permit many teeth to clamp down on the stem. For complete smoking satisfaction, choose the stem that you like almost as carefully as you choose your bowl.

AMBER 

Amber is an exquisite pipe bit material but it is so very high in price that its popularity is declining save among collectors. Amber is a mineralized gum, petrified resin, solidified through hundreds of years, found along the coast of the Baltic sea. It is mined much as are precious and semi-precious stones. It burns. A common superstition among old pipe men is that the "magnetism" of amber preserves the teeth! Another is that it wards off sickness from the wearer; that's why many bead chains and amber beads on rosaries are so popular even today! 

Amber is imported in cakes of rectangular shape from which craftsmen make the precious glass-fragile stems. Its texture is pleasing to the hand and the teeth. 

It is cut to the correct shape of block with a band saw, sanded and polished into shape and drilled. The brittle stuff is so flamable it is necessary to take precautions against its becoming ignited both while it is undergoing manufacturing operations and while it is in the possession of the smoker. [77]

CHAPTER VI PIPE ACCESSORIES 

ITH MATERNAL SOLICITOUSNESS, dealers in tobacco products throughout the world offer to the pipe smoker accessories to help him nurse his pipes through the most difficult of periods, to ornament the pipe, increase its versatility, house it and fumigate it. 

In time, if we are patient, we may find some big-hearted Samaritan offering us a terribly essential radiator cap for our pipes! 

Which of these does the pipe smoker need? And how can he judge the best quality accessories? Let's look at the ones we might consider. 

Pipe Cleaners, lots of them, long and fluffy ones are the most essential of accessories. If we use them generously, and often, we'll do more to keep our pipes working with us, not against us. They're not expensive and you'll soon learn which fit your pipe best. Colored ones are pretty and sort of dress up your smoking stand or pipe rack, but be careful not to use them in your pipe sweeteners that have alcohol content lest the color run wild. Use only white cleaners in the sweetener. There are some on the market with sharp cutting wire woven into the cotton pile so that the wire reams the shank of the pipe and the stem.

We don't recommend these because these cleaners are not as absorbent as others and they sometimes enlarge the bore of the shank so that tobacco is more readily sucked into the mouth. Pipe cleaners may be obtained in a variety of lengths in almost any color imaginable and in an assort- [78]

ment of thicknesses. By purchasing from tobacconists with large stocks you can usually obtain far better bargains than in buying the advertised cleaners most widely distributed in almost all small tobacco outlets. 

Pipe Sweetener should be of a type that has a pure grain alcohol base or even a good brandy or other 100 per cent proof liquor. It should be applied with cleaners and with a stiff brush for swabbing the shank. Such materials as acetone or acids are not the best treatments for the briar and these are often found as bases for so-called sweeteners. 

Directions come with the packages.

Pouches may be of any sort that can carry tobacco. 

Count on the humidor to keep the tobacco moist; no pouch is absolutely airtight for long. Pick a pouch that is durable and free from unpleasant odor that may affect the tobacco. The old-style oil silk liners crack in a few months and sometimes smell badly. Latex or spongerubber inner linings have proven more satisfactory. At any rate purchase only pouches that carry guarantees to last a year or longer if you pay more than a half-dollar.

The English use an ordinary leather button pouch. They like their tobacco on the dry side (and it is healthier that way) and usually they carry only a day's supply, a habit that we might well emulate. Some of the dime or quarter pouches will serve the smoker's purpose admirably. A pouch with an opening large enough to accommodate the pipe and a couple of fingers makes filling easy. Too, when you purchase the pouch, ask the salesman for a little dime humidifying unit that will fit into the pouch to help keep the tobacco moist. This may be a tin box with holes in the sides, filled with an absorbent material, or it may be a water-absorbing clay pellet that slowly exudes its moisture into the tobacco.

Reamers should be adjustable so that they will fit all [79]

bowls. Otherwise an assortment of them may be necessary.

That's our principal objection to the type with rigid fins radiating out from a central shank. The reamer should not be too sharp; we don't want to cut into the briar. 

Humidors may be of glass, porcelain, crockery or wood and the lids should fit tightly. Copper and certain other metals are not as acceptable as the aforementioned materials because the metals sometimes react with chemicals in the tobacco and form salts or otherwise impair the smoking quality of the tobacco. In the lid, up high where it cannot get in direct contact with the tobacco should be a hollow section filled with clay, sponge, blotting paper or other absorbent material. This is loaded with water or rum and serves to keep the tobacco in condition. If the moisture comes in direct contact with the tobacco, mold may set in.

Pipe Racks may easily be home-made or you may purchase racks capable of holding from one to sixty pipes. 

The only restriction on these is that they must hold the pipes so that the stems and shanks are vertical and bowls are at the bottom of the vertical column. This lets the moisture in stem and shank run down into the bowl, from which it may be readily evaporated.

Buttons of meerschaum or metal are often offered smokers to put in the bottom of their pipes to correct for the bore entering the tobacco chamber above the floor. 

The small amount of tobacco wasted by not having a button is negligible, certainly not enough of a savings to justify caking the button into place. Buy a button if you want it, but it's not really necessary.

Retorts work satisfactorily, but we don't like them. 

The idea of a retort is that it clamps a rubber lip onto the stem of the pipe and the bowl is kept higher than the stem. 

Heat is applied under a retort filled with alcohol and soon the alcohol comes bubbling up the length of the stem into [80]

the bowl. Take the heat away and it all comes bubbling down again loaded with sediment that collected in the shank and stem. It's as much fun as similar coffee mixers -but it's all unnecessary. The stiff swabbing brush we suggested for use with the pipe sweetener does a more efficient job than the liquid alone can do; then, too, the alcohol affects the rubber stem, acting as a mild solvent and making the stem more prone to collect goo from condensation.

Wind Caps are helpful to the motorists and the outdoor smoker in keeping ashes and coals in a pipe. The dime kind that fits inside the top of the bowl with spring pressure is quite satisfactory, although more expensive ones are obtainable. The cap permits a pipe to have more style and versatility than the use of a pipe with a built-on cap. 

Ash Trays for pipe smokers should be made with a cork or rubber knocker in the center and enough container space to prevent the unburnt tobacco and ashes from flying outside the tray when the pipe is emptied against the knocker. A pipe smoker, being a man, classifies dinky ash trays along with guest towels and tea napkins as being entirely too small for any really practical use. [81]

CHAPTER VII CIGAR ACCESSORIES 

IGAR HUMIDORS are probably the only really important accessory the cigar smoker requires; and he needs this only if he purchases cigars in quantities of a box or more. The humidor may be amazingly expensive if you choose one of rare imported wood and lined with expensive insulating material, or its price may be quite reasonable; it may even be home-made. One type consists of a box in which a cigar box may fit in or only the cigars from the cigar box; on the top of this humidor box is a strip of blotting paper or other absorbent material, usually mounted in metal with a screening cover to make it easier to handle; and the box is air-tight. Ordinarily the cigars are not out long enough to be hurt by airmoisture, but in winter when we have steam heat that is particularly drying it is often wise to keep them in a humidor.

Some ingenious business men line one drawer of their desks with zinc and zinc-line the top so that the fit is tight. 

The cigars are kept in the front of the drawer and at the back, where it cannot touch the cigars, is a clay block to distribute moisture inside the drawer. Often small humidity-testing gadgets are kept in the drawers to make certain that the cigars are kept in best condition. Most smokers, however, can judge by feel whether or not the drawer is too moist.

After cigars have dried out they must be brought back to their original state of perfection by carefully increased exposure to moisture. The wrapper is the most [82]

easily penetrated part of the cigar and if it becomes too moist it may stretch and loosen, ruining the cigar before the filler can have become re-moistened. First expose the cigar to only a small amount of moisture and take several days (or even weeks, if the cigars are valuable) to build up the moisture volume to normal. 

Cigar Holders, like cigarette holders, are made of almost as many materials as are pipes. To mention but a few we have briar, hard and soft rubber, plastics including bakelite, metal, amber, meerschaum, weichselwood, and bamboo. These are not really popular and play their most important part in helping cure some smokers of an excessive nervous habit of chewing cigars, which is often unsightly. Most smokers prefer to taste the tobacco directly. 

Cigar Pouches are becoming a rarity. The stiff leather and metal ones, once carried by all cigar smokers are on the decline, due largely to their uncomfortable bulk and the metal or cellophane wrappers in which many cigars are usually encased. 

Cigar Ash Trays are too numerous and varied to discuss in anything like detail. About the only thing they have in common is that they're larger than cigarette trays and have depressions that accommodate cigars comfortably. A recent editorial in Collier's bemoaned the fact that there were no truly satisfactory cigar ash trays on the market. The editor was so deluged that it took days for the staff to extricate him from the pile of cigar trays the mailman brought.

Be chary about purchasing accessories, whether they be for pipe or cigar. If you've felt the lack of a certain bit of equipment and find just what you want, go to it and make the purchase. But be sure you sell it to yourself. [83]

CHAPTER VIII TOBACCO-FROM SEED to SALE 

FROM SEEDS SO TINY that an ounce will plant two acres of tobacco springs an industry of such tremendous proportions that it is difficult for men accustomed to "tens" and "hundreds" to envision its vastness!

Picture, if you can, more than a million and a half acres producing almost a billion and a half pounds of tobacco worth well over a quarter-billion dollars. This is production in the United States alone; world production hovers around seven billion pounds a year!

China grows far more than a billion and a half pounds a year! The U.S.S.R. produces nearly a halfbillion annually! Turkey, Greece and Japan produce around 150 million! Italy, France, Germany, Canada, Bulgaria, Chosen, Cuba, Yugoslavia, Hungary, Puerto Rico and Algeria also add generously to the total annual production!

In this country North Carolina is our leading tobacco state with an annual crop worth more than 100 million dollars! Kentucky's crop is nearly 50 million; South 

Carolina's almost 25 million. Virginia, Georgia and

Tennessee are next in line in order and 20 other states find their tobacco crops a sizable part of their agricultural reports. Tobacco taxes in 1939 totaled $580,159,206! 

What price smoking!

We export between 400 and 500 million pounds of tobacco a year and import more than 70 million pounds! 

That's some business! [84]

TOBACCO AGRICULTURE 

And the foundation of the whole mass enterprise is the farm, where the greatest of care must be taken in nurturing the delicate tobacco plants to maturity so that the leaves will be of the desired size, color, texture and quality. 

Three members of the family constitute smoking tobaccos. The daddy of them all is Nicotinia tabacum, first of the tobaccos to have found footholds all over the world. 

It takes on the characteristics of the soil wherever it is grown. Nicotinia rustica is smaller-sized, oval-leafed, the source of Turkish, Syrian and Latakia tobaccos. Smallest of the three is Nicotinia persica, which grows only in 

Persia and produces the famous Shiraz tobacco.

There are variations in the ways of growing tobaccos, depending on the type of tobacco, "after-treatment" it is to receive, and climate in which it is grown and cured. 

Let's skip briefly through a rough outline of the basic agricultural procedure in growing tobacco. 

First, selection of soil. It must be loose and rich, in a warm or temperate climate, and must have the correct amount of lime, magnesia, phosphoric acid, potash and nitrogen. The lighter the soil, the lighter the tobacco leaves it produces. Southern tobaccos are heavier, sweeter, more fragrant than Northern. Northern ones are more delicate, less odorous than Southern. The Phillipines and Cuba have the most nearly ideal climates for growing tobacco of all places in the world.

The seedbed is a protected area in which tobacco plants are grown before they are strong enough to be transplanted. Only a couple of square yards of seedbed are needed for each acre of tobacco to be grown. The seedbed is protected from cold and is located where the sun can reach it. Rich, loose black or sandy loam, over [85]

which wood has been burnt, and into which the ashes have been raked, is best. Seeds are sprinkled over the bed, pressed into the soil and the surface tamped down for greater firmness. Farmers guard these seedlings with all their resourcefulness against enemies: colds, insects, hail and heavy rains. Glass or canvas protect them at first. Then for the first few days after the plants are up the covers are removed during the sunny parts of the day. Later they're dispensed with entirely except in very inclement weather. Within a month from the time the seeds are first planted, the tobacco plant should be ready for transplanting providing it has at least 4 leaves and is around five inches high.

Damp weather is best for transplanting. The seed beds are wetted, plants gently removed so that as few roots as possible are broken and the plants are set in holes about five inches deep in the field and earth pressed around them. Transplanting machines are the most efficient instruments used in doing this work. The plants are set in little hills in parallel rows for better drainage, about 

1,600 hills to an acre.

During the cultivation a constant battle is kept up against insects and weeds. Much of this must be done by hand because of the limited working space between rows of tobacco in the precious tobacco-producing land. The soil must also be kept loose. At the age of two months plants are "topped" or flower buds are pinched off. Sometimes the very top leaves of the plant are also removed. 

As growths attempt to take the place of the portion removed, these too must be pinched off to force all the growth into the leaves. The decrepit-looking lower leaves, usually badly battered from contact with the ground, are overripe and coarse. They are also removed in the "priming" operation. Pipe tobaccos are primed high and topped low. The lower leaves are called "flyers.' [86]

After priming and topping, leaves grow rapidly. Usually there are a dozen or fewer leaves per plant. About the third month, when the leaves are yellow-green, thick, heavy, yellow-flecked near the edges and surfaces exude oil stuff it's time for harvest.

Harvesting may be of the individual leaves or of whole stalks. In individual leaf harvesting, lower leaves are gathered first to assure uniformity, then the middle and lastly the upper leaves. The lower leaves are called "lugs." These leaves are tied together in "hands" and are processed in essentially the same manner as those harvested by the stalk.

Stalk harvesting is done by splitting the stalk well down and cutting it off just below the lower leaves. 

Cloudy days are preferred for harvesting. Six or eight plants are hung on a stick and the sticks are then transported to the curing place. 

Curing may be one of three types, sun, fire or flue. 

Sun-dried or air-cured tobacco is hung on scaffolds in open air, protected from blowing about too vigorously in the wind and after about five days of sun the sticks are moved to a light, airy curing barn. The leaves yellow in this process and it's important that they don't cure too fast so that the color turns too dark. Ventilation is watched in the barn to help control curing. Doors and ventilators are kept open in the day and closed at night in normal weather. In very dry weather the reverse is done to keep plants from getting too dry. Once tobacco is cured, barns are kept closed.

For fire-cured tobaccos a wood-fire is built in the shed under the stalks on their scaffolds. The fire is kept burning for about four days, giving the leaf the strong flavor preferred by the export market. [87]

Flue-cured tobacco is processed in a barn which is warmed by pipes carrying hot air through the barn. This is the most expensive, most readily controlled method of curing tobacco. It is almost invariably used for lightcolored tobaccos because they require more exactness than darker types.

Curing is staged according to yellowing of tobacco, fixing of color, curing of leaf and curing of stem. Each needs a different degree of heat to be done exactly, hence the readily recognized superiority of this latter curing method over the others. Fire-cured and dark air-cured tobaccos in this country are used principally for snuff and chewing tobacco so their use is diminishing. Light aircured tobacco and flue-cured are the types on ascendancy. 

Following the curing the leaves are stripped from their stalks. This is done in damp weather when the leaves would be less likely to crumble. They're broken off as close as possible to the stalk and 10 or 15 leaves of a type are tied together near the stems into "hands," using a poorer leaf of similar color for the binder. The hands are either strung together on sticks, 18 or 20 hands to a stick, and hung in the barn for storage or they are "bulked" or piled together. This bulking causes the tobacco on the inside of the pile to sweat. Sweating sets or fixes the sap between the upper and lower membranes of the leaf which carries oils that distinguish the aroma and flavor of the different tobaccos.

The leaves are occasionally stirred so that those on the outside can be placed on the inside and to keep temperatures exact (90 degrees to 114 degrees F.). They are bulked on platforms so that air circulates freely about them and sometimes mats are used on the outsides of the "bulks" to lessen evaporation. Bulking causes fermentation of leaves and some change in color. 

Sometimes exotic substances are sprayed on the [88]

tobacco in this mellowing process. Rum, cider, grape wine and other such materials are used. The leaves are dried to about the texture of fine kid leather, packed and stored until sorting time.

Sorting may be done at the time of stripping or picking, but it is best to wait until the leaves have been more completely processed and more time can be given the sorting operation. For it is in this stage that the farmer can do most to build up his proceeds from tobacco-growing. He should sort them according to type so that each hand and group of hands is as nearly exactly alike as working conditions permit. Then, if the quantity is sufficiently large for each type of tobacco, the types are grouped together preparatory to marketing. If it is not sufficiently large it should be grouped along with the next higher or lower grade to lower the cost of marketing the tobacco. 

There are various ways of classifying the tobacco. 

One is as flyers (first two bottom leaves), common lugs (next two), good lugs, bright leaves, long red, short red and top leaves. The Agricultural Marketing Service of the United States Department of Agriculture publishes official standard grades for most types of tobaccos grown in this country. The farmer is urged by government experts to sort and arrange the tobacco into lowest quality lugs first and then in order of quality to the best lugs, thin leaf in order of quality from lowest to highest, heavy leaf in order of quality from lowest to highest and wrappers in order of quality from good to choice. There are symbols employed in designating the various types of tobacco in accordance with type, quality and color. 

The tobacco, all sorted and graded, is tied into hands of four to eight ounces with rafia or leaf and packed. 

Cheaper grades are packed in hogsheads under pressure; the hands are laid in layers, each at right angles to the one below and hydraulic pressure squeezes the tobacco in [89]

place. Care must be taken to keep the pressure from being too great lest it cause the tobacco to darken appreciably. 

TYPES OF PIPE TOBACCO 

Virginia. This tobacco is best known in its "Bright” form, but it also is grown as a dark type. Its name is rather confusing since both North and South Carolina produce more of it than does Virginia and it is also grown in Florida and Georgia. It is the lightest colored of domescic tobaccos, the "Bright" being lemon-yellow and best grades containing no green. The darker Virginia ranges from dark tan to brown, depending on the soil and processing. Light Virginia is mild, contains little oil, has a high sugar content which makes it taste sweet and has a woody taste that many smokers find a pleasant addition to a blend. This tobacco is flue-cured and extensively exported. It has high nicotine content. 

Too much Virginia in a mixture will make it bite because of the lack of essential oil in the tobacco. Not more than 15 per cent of the mixture should be Virginia. 

The light color makes it valuable in dressing up the appearance of a mixture. It burns very slowly, another reason for not having too much in a blend. 

Cavendish. This is any form of tobacco, treated by being dipped into sugar water, maple or rum, which gives it a dark mahogany color and a very sweet flavor. It can be used much more freely than the Bright in a blend, some few manufacturers using it for as much as 95 per cent of a mixture!

Burley. Second largest tobacco crop of the country is burley. It comes from Tennessee, Kentucky and Southern Ohio. The tobacco is yellow-green to yellow-brown in color. Its body is as light as its color. Burley has a [90]

capacity for absorbing much flavoring sauce. In itself it has a mild flavor that fits most blends well. Burley has a low nicotine content.

Burley is important in both pipe tobaccos and cigarettes. It is the basis of most advertised brands of tobaccos. Most cheap and popular priced smoking blends range from 75 per cent on up in burley content. If you find your tobacco is too highly flavored or too sweet, add as much as 50 per cent of uncased burley to the mixture. 

If your blend burns too fast, add 5 to 20 per cent of burley. 

Burley is ordinarily air-cured.

Maryland. Here we have a small crop, grown only in southern Maryland. The tobacco has the interesting characteristic of holding fire well no matter what quality it may be. It is dark reddish-brown to dark brown, with thin dry leaves. The aroma is neutral making it well suited for mixing with poor-burning burley or other tobaccos that are prone to lose their fire. 

Perique. The crop is small but very interesting. It is grown only in and near St. James Parish, Louisiana. It is of about the same appearance (and odor) as prune skins, the color being almost black.

Aroma and flavor of Perique is different from that of all other tobaccos largely because of its unique curing method. The tobacco is pressed, allowed to soak and ferment in its own juices and become strong. The pressing is repeated several times imparting to the tobacco a heavy fragrant quality. It is always kept very moist, often wrapped in oilskin, oilcloth or other air-tight material.

The harvested tobacco, with midribs removed, is put into a twist containing about 20 leaves. The twists are packed a dozen to a box and subjected to the pressure just described.

So interesting and useful to blends is this tobacco that some imitators started putting spurious Periques on [91]

the market in the form of dyed and fermented tobaccos of other types. Over 50 per cent of the tobacco sold as 

Perique is an imitation of the real thing!

Perique adds flavor and aroma to blends. It burns slowly and lowers the bite of fast-burning tobaccos. Too much Perique is hard on the smoker. More than 25 per cent should never be used in a blend and 10 per cent is safer.

Latakia. This is an oriental tobacco from Syria. The 

Island of Cyprus is also well known for it. The plants are small, usually being only three feet high (other tobacco plants grow as tall as 15 feet!); and leaves are thin and very dark brown.

The whole plant, including the flowers, is used in making tobacco and, unlike other tobaccos, stems and leaf ribs are the sweetest part of the tobacco. It is smoked in the curing process until it attains the well-known dark color by which it is detected. The tobacco has an oily appearance and a heavy flavor. Despite this outward appearance it has a low nicotine content. 

It is a fine tobacco used in most good smoking mixtures. Only a little is needed in a blend. One ounce in a pound noticeably affects the smoke and three or four ounces are the most any smoker could wish for. If you're not hardy go easy on smoking Latakia; 10 per cent is plenty. Keep it bone dry lest it mold quickly. 

Some domestic brands of tobacco, notably the second and third grade Virginias, are dyed to imitate this tobacco, too. The fake Latakias are often synthetically mixed, blended and flavored so that the deception is difficult to detect. More thoroughgoing medicine men take half real and half artificial Latakia and case, blend, bulk, and marry together the tobacco to get an almost perfect imitation of the real McCoy.

Turkish. It is hardly fair to describe Turkish tobacco as a single classification. There are probably well over a [92]

dozen expensive, fine quality, well known different types of Turkish tobacco and scores of minor types. Some leaves are very small, some large; some are oval-shaped, others are lance or tongue-shaped; some are stemless, others prominently stemmed; and colors range through light and dark yellows, reds, greens and browns. While most recognized qualities of Turkish tobaccos are good, there are many inferior ones. Turkish tobaccos are used more in cigarette manufacture than in pipe tobaccos. 

They're purchased either in their several districts or from expert Turkish blenders. Finest of Turkish tobaccos come from Macedonia. Here the best known is Xanthi district's production, egg-shaped, fine-textured leaves of orange to reddish-brown color. It has good burn, fresh, sweet taste and varying nicotine content. Xanthi tobacco is best known to the trade as Basma. Choicest of Basmas are called Dubex. Another fine Xanthi tobacco is Yacca. 

In the Kavalle district of Turkey, the outstanding tobacco is Mahalla. It has delicious aroma, excellent burn and small, light-colored leaves. Other prize districts of 

Turkey are Salonica and Monastio.

In Asia Minor the district of Samsoun grows long, pointed leaves of superb quality. Samsoun tobaccos generally have much nicotine, good burn, moderate aroma and orangish-to-red-brown coloring. Bafra is a popular exception. It is a very aromatic and tasty tobacco with deep red, fan-shaped leaves. Smyrna tobaccos, also from Asia 

Minor, are used extensively for blending. 

These Turkish tobaccos reach their peak in perfection when blended together by experts who devote their lives to the work. Egyptian cigarettes are usually made of 

Turkish tobacco. Sometimes Chinese tobacco is sold as "Turkish." Much "Turkish" tobacco comes from Greece.

Other tobaccos. There are other pipe tobaccos, but they're not commonly used nor of sufficient importance. [93]

CUTTING PIPE TOBACCO 

Cured pipe tobacco leaf is shipped to the factory in hogsheads packed so tightly that crowbars are needed to pry it out and wedges are used to slit the hands. The separated leaves are wetted and softened, usually stripped of the midrib and pressed into cakes before being cut. Often casing solutions are applied before the leaves are put into the cake or "cheese" form; at other times the casing is done after the cakes are cut to the desired size. 

Plug Cut tobacco usually has the stems (midribs) removed before it is cased by dipping it into the casing solution or spraying the solution over the tobacco. It is bulked for a day, piled into a square steel frame and inserted in a hydraulic press under two tons of pressure for a half-hour. Plates are then clamped on top and bottom of the frame to hold the tobacco in place, then stored in this way for a day or so to permit the casing solution to penetrate the tobacco.

The finished "cheese" is removed and cut in a slicing machine to the desired thickness (usually 12 to 2 mm.). 

The slices are either packed in flat tin boxes as Sliced Plug, broken up and sold as Crushed Plug or rubbed up as Ready- 

Rubbed or Coarse Cut. 

Fine Cut tobacco is prepared in almost exactly the same way except that it is cut into shreds about a half mm. wide, like cigarette tobacco, and dried to only around 13 or 14 per cent of moisture. 

Scrap Tobacco is cased more heavily than Plug Cut, run through wringers, partially dried out to small flat pieces in a scrap machine, run through a drier which eliminates dust and cuts down moisture to around 12 per cent, and packed for sale.

Granulated tobaccos are blended before the leaf is cut. They are then caked and cut to quarter-inch squares [100]

of varying depth, freed from dust, cased by spraying, stored for awhile, freed from all but about 10 per cent moisture, and packed for sale. 

These are the principal forms of tobacco used in this country. There are some communities that use the Black 

Fats, Shag, Water Balers and other rather unusual processes which involve special types of casing solutions or casing methods but they're hardly of interest to the ordinary smoker. Birds Eye tobacco, that is, the type that has crosssections of the stem mixed in with the rest, is practically unknown in this country save in the most formidable of smoking mixtures, the ones, that those of us, who confess to having a certain amount of physical delicacy, (the same degree of sensitivity that causes us to avoid being hit by automobiles) assiduously avoid!

CASING AND MOISTENING. 

How dry should tobacco be? The answer to this depends on whether you're talking from the standpoint of the consumer or the producer. The consumer gets more if he buys his tobacco dry and moistens it himself. The producer, naturally, prefers to wet the tobacco as heavily as possible. But in order for tobacco to smoke properly, 10 to 20 per cent of moisture by weight is considered safe. 

The tobacco that crumbles in your hand when you crush it into a ball is too dry. That which stays in a tightly compressed ball when you crush it together is too wet. If, however, the tobacco crushes into a ball without breaking and then starts to "creep" or slowly spring back toward its previous shape it's approximately correct in moisture content. The dryer the tobacco, the less nicotine it gives off when burnt and the more rapidly it burns. 

In manufacturing the cut tobacco is first baked dry and then remoistened to exactly the degree specified by the [101]

manufacturer so that all of it is fairly uniform. Then it is packaged in as near a moisture-tight container as is practicable for the price and quality of tobacco. Some smokers attempt to compensate for the lack of moisture-tightness in their pouches by using a fresh vanilla bean or a slice of orange peel in their tobacco containers. We don't recommend this. In time it'll cause mold in the tobacco. 

Purchasers of tobacco in large quantities may often receive it bone dry. Some of them put small quantities of it in a container that is air-tight along with a medium for exuding moisture, such as a clay block or a clean odorless blotter and let the moisture from this medium slowly penetrate the tobacco. The tobacco is occasionally stirred so that it absorbs the moisture fairly evenly! Somtimes rum or fine brandy is used instead of water in the moistening unit. Care is taken to keep the moisture unit from direct contact with the tobacco as this may cause mold. 

If a large amount of tobacco is being moistened at once, time is saved by spreading the tobacco out on a table covered with wax paper or other water-protecting material, the tobacco is sprayed with a fine atomizer and it is lifted, tumbled and dropped, this "tumbling" process doing much to spread the moisture evenly. 

If the tobacco is too moist, it may be best dried by spreading it out on a table and letting the desired amount of water evaporate from it or even by removing the lid from the humidor and occasionally stirring the tobacco so that it loses its moisture evenly. 

Sometimes the solutions sprayed on tobaccos, especially those employed by manufacturers, are to give the tobacco an unusual aroma or flavor rather than just to give it humidity. These are called "casing" solutions. Certain of them contain artificial flavorings that remain as a sticky mass in the bottom of your pipe. Others are entirely [102]

combustible and harmless to the smoker. Glycerine, licorice, molasses or sugared water, rum, maple, apple or peach extract and even perfume are used by various manufacturers. 

These are used in concentrate or extract form, not as diluted products. 

Tobacco producers keep the formulas for their casing solutions a secret. The casing solution depends on the kind of leaf being used, results sought, local legislation and value of finished product.

A popular casing solution that most smokers could try at home, if they wish to experiment, is a vanilla concentrate made by adding one part of vanilla extract to six or eight parts of water. Another is made by using one part vanilla to eight parts alcohol, brandy, whiskey or rum.

Essence of rum, one part, with eight parts of alcohol or other good liquor, is a fragrant, pleasant-tasting casing solution. Another is three parts caramel concentrate one part rum, one part vanilla and 10 parts alcohol. By "alcohol" here we mean pure grain alcohol, of course.

Experiment with these and other casing solutions using pure burley as the material to absorb it until you find one that suits your taste. After that find a few more because no one can please you indefinitely. You can purchase the solutions in the desired strength from any pharmaceutical house. 

Never use more than two ounces of liquid to a pound of tobacco. If the tobacco is very dry, don't tumble it until moisture has penetrated it enough to prevent danger of the tobacco powdering. Don't rub the tobacco when you're tumbling it; lift it high-tumble and drop it. 

After casing the tobacco, leave it piled up and covered for 12 hours, then after it has bulked in this manner tumble it again and let it dry out to exactly the desired consistency. [103]

TOBACCO BLENDS 

The pipe makes the tobacco and the tobacco makes the pipe. No one blend will satisfy the smoker for any great length of time. He tries many of them as his taste changes. There is often a different reaction in the body to different types of smoking material at different times. 

In the morning the smoke may taste heavy and strong, the very same smoke that in the evening after a good meal will taste light and mild. Therefore it is to the smoker's interest to mix his own tobaccos to enjoy smoking at its fullest or else to mix different brands to his taste. A steady diet of any one thing, including a single brand of tobacco, is not pleasant.

It costs about 10 per cent more to mix your own smoking tobaccos, although it's possible to mix a good blend for $1.50 to $2.00 that is far better than anything in its class you can buy for that price. You can't match the cheap burleys converted into smoking tobacco by manufacturers of popular-priced tobaccos-and if you could obtain it you wouldn't like it unless you had similar means of changing it from its original form. 

Beware of private or specially blended aromatic formulas offered at fancy prices ($3.00 to $5.00 a pound). 

Many so-called tobacconists or blenders have a habit of taking some cheap plug (burley) adding a half ounce of

Erinmore or other imported aromatic, sprinkling in Virginia to dress it up and charging two to three times the value of tobacco. Others have private brands under their own label and charge whatever the traffic will bear. 

Read the package carefully to see if the factory, district and state of the "manufacturer" dealer is accurate. 

His number, if he is in a factory, appears prominently in the store. You can easily detect outside manufacture. If he takes tobacco from tax-paid containers, there won't be [104]

the usual labels and statement of manufacture on the package.

Price, remember, is no criterion of smoking satisfaction. Individual taste is the only true criterion of this. If you like the dime tobacco, El Fumio, smoke it and enjoy it! 

Use your own taste, not the false standards of a smoking snob, in picking the tobaccos you like best. Some of the fancy prices charged by blenders for mysterious tobaccos are for the same brands you can buy across the counter for around a quarter a package with possibly a little flavoring thrown in. Count on the tobacco, not the casing solution, for your good smoke.

There are also special aromatic and treated tobaccos which sell at exhorbitant prices because, largely, of their overpowering casings. Often they're ordinary tobacco simply specially colored. Their real value is usually around 

15 to 20 cents and their selling price 30 to 35 cents.

The proper procedure in preparing a tobacco blend is to first prepare a basic mixture and then add other tobaccos to it to suit your varying taste. Burley, Cavendish, and Maryland are the best bases in blends. That is, they may serve as the greater part of a mixture if the mixture is to be a natural one with a true flavor. 

You may wish to add to your tobacco the flavoring material Deer Tongue (about a quarter-ounce to a pound [105]

of tobacco) or Tonka bean. These are either mixed in in fine form or macerated and the powdered emulsion sprayed on the tobacco.

For a mild, average, slow-burning mixture add an ounce each of Latakia, Perique and Virginia to 13 ounces of base plug (Burley) and a quarter-ounce of Deer Tongue. 

Very fast smokers will like a pound of tobacco blended of two ounces of Perique, one ounce of Latakia and 13 ounces of Burley.

A rich, heavy tobacco, heavily seasoned is made by blending an ounce of Havana (see cigar tobaccos), two ounces of Latakia, one ounce of Perique, one ounce of Virginia and 11 ounces of plug.

A natural English type of tobacco which serves as a steady diet for awhile, but which should be smoked slowly, is two ounces of Latakia, two ounces of Perique, three ounces of Virginia, one ounce of Turkish and eight ounces of plug (burley). This is best cased with rum. 

An expensive, truly fine tobacco is made of five ounces of Latakia, five ounces of Perique and six ounces of Turkish, respectively valued at about $1.25, $1.00 and $1.80 Total, $4.05. This is cased with brandy, whiskey or rum. 

Another fine, rich blend is three ounces of Latakia, three Perique, three of Turkish, two of Virginia and five of Plug, which is considerably less expensive. 

A heavy, rich blend is four ounces of Latakia, one ounce of Perique, three ounces of Turkish, one ounce of 

Virginia and seven ounces of Plug (Burley). 

A very rich blend is four ounces each of Latakia, Perique, Virginia and Turkish.

Aromatic tobaccos may be made of ordinary tobaccos at a considerable savings by using a quarter-ounce each of Forest Rose, Erinmore, Bulwark and St. Bruno to 16 [106]

ounces of tobacco. Or these aromatic materials may be used individually. Never use too much of them; they soon become obnoxious and sickening in quantities. 

Add these aromatics by pulverizing the tobaccos to a powder and mixing them carefully into the rest of the blend. If this isn't done and the smoker encounters a large piece of the aromatic all together-well, he'll wish he hadn't. Its taste is overpowering! Incidentally, these aromatic tobaccos taste better if they're allowed to blend or marry with the rest of the blend for several days or longer. This allows more complete and perfect blending, particularly with natural mixtures that require time to blend and mellow. 

Three aromatic tobaccos follow:

Very full, strong aromatic tobacco is made by mixing an ounce each of Erinmore, Bulwark, Forest Rose, Perique, and Virginia with 11 ounces of Plug (Burley).

A mild, medium aromatic is made by adding to 13 ounces of Plug, two ounces of Perique, one of Virginia and a fourth of an ounce each of Erinmore and Forest Rose. 

And a mild, light aromatic is 10 ounces of Plug, one and a half each of Latakia and Perique, two ounces of Virginia and one of Bulwark.

For an effect of a combination of maple and rum in your tobaccos use equal parts of rum essence, maple and vanilla, dilute sparingly with alcohol, spray on the tobacco and press the tobacco for 24 hours. Two ounces of this solution is used to a pound of tobacco.

Every smoker should have three mixtues of his own liking: (1) a rich and heavy after-dinner smoke, (2) a slow-burning outdoor smoke, and (3) a medium blend for office or everyday smoking. He should keep the tobaccos of these on the dry side, but not too dry. 

Turkish tobaccos are often low in nicotine content, [107]

cool smoking and have excellent natural aroma and flavor.

Were it not for their high cost they could readily supplant Virginia in most blends to the betterment of the blend. 

To much Virginia tends to bite, spin the head, and too much Perique or Latakia may upset your system as they are quite heavy.

In mixing blends, it is important that the smoker have a complete understanding of the difference between mild and strong tobacco. Stronger tobaccos are cooler in smoking and less prone to bite. They're heavier and often darker than the others. Mild tobacco will be hot and bity because of their low oil content.

To find the happy medium, a combination of both mildness and coolness is the tobacconist's art-the smoker's ultimate Utopia. Experiment with different blends until they suit your taste. Just as you may prefer your coffee stronger or weaker than your neighbor with more or less. sugar or none, or with cream or black, so does your taste in tobacco stand ruggedly, individually yours.

Bow to the dictates of no others in pleasing it. 

CIGAR TOBACCOS 

Principal of the foreign cigar tobaccos are:

Cuban (Havana) leaf, particularly that tobacco that has sent the most poker-faced and phlegmatic into roars of delight and paeans of praise,the tobacco from the Vuelta 

Abajo district, is the world's foremost general cigar tobacco. Soil and climate in this region combine to produce the most nearly perfect cigar raw material in the world. 

Of course, not all Cuban tobacco is this good, but certain of the cigars in the Vuelta Abajo district (there are only a few thousand of them produced each year) are preserved in special carved cedar chests for state occasions like rare old wines to be brought out as the highest mark of hospitality for the most esteemed of guests. [108]

Partidos produces a wrapper that is famous in Cuba for its lightness and gloss, both highly desirable characteristics of a wrapper. Remedios Cuban tobacco has a heavy body and rich flavor especially fine for blending with United States tobaccos. In general Cuban tobacco has a low nicotine content, small brown leaf, rich flavor. 

Sumatra tobaccos are used almost exclusively for wrappers. These wrappers are elastic, glossy, light, with good color and a neutral flavor that doesn't detract from that of other tobaccos used in a blend with them. 

Java tobaccos have a heavy body similar to Sumatra but only a small part of the crop is of a quality that we can use in this country. It's a cheap substitute wrapper for 

Sumatra but it can also be used as filler or binder. 

Puerto Rico and the Philippines produce mild cigar tobaccos, usually milder than Cuban ones. Some indication of the mildness may be derived from the fact that Philippine children are not permitted to smoke until they're 10 years old.

Domestic tobaccos come from Connecticut, Massachusetts, Northern Florida, Southern Georgia, parts of 

Pennsylvania, New York, Wisconsin, Minnesota and Ohio.

Lesser quantities come from other states. 

Connecticut tobacco is of two types, Broadleaf and Havana Seed. These are either grown in the sun or under the shade of a canopy of cheesecloth or laths. The shadegrown tobacco doesn't have the sun's growth-hastening influence acting on it and therefore the leaves grow larger, silkier and thinner with less gum and body than those into which the sun drew strong nutrition from the earth. 

Shadegrown tobacco is used for fine wrappers. Broadleaf tobacco is broad with silky leaves and the tip very elastic, small-veined, richly grained and excellently colored. Havana Seed leaves are smaller and narrower, thin, but with less elasticity, poorer grain and larger veins than Broad- [109]

leaf. Heavy or slightly damaged Havana Seed leaves are used for binder.

Pennsylvania produces long Broadleaf tobacco which, like Connecticut tobacco, is air cured. This state's tobacco is dark, heavy-bodied and used for filler and binder.

Ohio's best known tobacco is the Zimmer Spanish, small leaf which resembles the Cuban types and, like them, is cured by fermentation. Little Dutch is the next best known. It's a larger leaf. A small quantity of Gebhart is also grown for use in stogies. 

Wisconsin grows excellent binders, usually used with 

Connecticut wrappers and Pennsylvania or Ohio fillers. 

Northern Florida and Southern Georgia grow Havana seedlings and Sumatra seeds into tobacco used for wrappers. Havana seed retains its native characteristics of size and appearance for several crops before it reverts to the normal Florida type. Sumatra seed seldom produces a type resembling Sumatra tobacco after the second crop. These have good body and aroma similar to respectively Cuban and Sumatran tobaccos, but not quite the same degree of fineness. About 15 per cent of these thin, elastic leaves are used for wrappers. [110]

CIGAR MAKING HERE ARE THREE PARTS TO THE CIGAR, the filler or core, the binder or leaf surrounding the filler to hold it in the correct shape, and the wrapper or outer leaf which forms the cigar into an integral unit with an attractive outer appearance. The fine cigars made by the old Spanish hand-working method have no binder because their filler is a pad of flattened out leaves rolled together in one unit instead of a bundle of loose sprigs of tobacco. 

Fine Havana tobacco is brought into the Cuban factories and carefully stored in bales which are marked with the grade of tobacco. Storing is most important because the tobacco must be kept in perfect condition, cured to just the right degree. Fine factories blend the tobaccos of different years and different sources in Havana to develop choice combinations of tobacco smoking.

Leaves for fillers are shaken loose, separated from other leaves and smoothed out from the long, ropelike shape they assume in storage. The better tobaccos are then dampened with water (cased); inferior tobaccos are cased with solutions containing artificial flavoring. After the casing the tobacco is cured in piles, aired out.

In the process immediately before manufacturing, girls rip out the stems or large central rib of the leaves and stack the halves in low even piles. They are given a final curing in barrels, blended with other leaves to make a good combination of flavor, aroma, and burn (often nine to 11 different grades of tobacco go into one of these com- [111]

binations) and sent to the manufacturing (rolling) room to be made into cigars. 

Wrappers are managed directly by the foreman. He selects leaves, checks casing, and sees that they're delivered to the selecting department in time for the next day's work. Leaves are selected according to size, color, texture and quality. Wrappers are counted out in small stacks of 

25 or 50 and delivered to the cigar maker. Also sent to the cigar maker is filler corresponding to the size of cigar to be made.

With no tools but a square piece of board, a sharp knife and a pot of adhesive, such as gum tragacanth, the expert Cuban or Spanish craftsman shapes the filler in the palm of his hand to the shape of cigar his factory is making at the time. Then, if the cigar is being made by the Spanish method, he winds the wrapper, which he has previously cut to the proper shape, around the filler like a bandage. 

It starts at the tuck (or tip that is to be lit) and spirals on around to the head where it is held in place with a touch of the adhesive. Once the cigar is all shaped (and the precision of this hand work is amazing), the craftsman holds it against a gauge on his board, cuts away the surplus length from the tuck so that it's exactly the required length, and the cigar is put to one side. When 50 have been made into a bundle, they are collected, and stored in suitable containers. The scraps of tobacco, small leaves and other waste tobacco are brushed off the board into a burlap pocket fastened to the front of the working board. These are later used in the manufacture of short-filler cigars. 

These finished or dull-finished fine cigars are assorted according to their wrappers. Dry ones are put in one stack, and glossy or oily ones are in a second. Experts next subdivide each into sections in accordance with their colors, named by the Spanish words for yellowish, reddish, and greenish. These in turn are subdivided according to their [112]

shade into light-colored (claro), reddish colored (colorado), and deep brownish colored (maduro). There are also in-between classifications of colorado claro and colorado maduro. Careful assortment is next made so that almost identical cigars will be packed in a box and the smoker or retailer can tell approximately what color cigars he will unpack before he opens the box. Next government seals are affixed and the boxes are ready for storage or delivery. Some cigars are all set for smoking as soon as they're made, but fine cigars, like fine wines, improve with age, especially when they're stored in Spanish cedar containers, the essential oils of which intermingle with those of the cigars to produce a perfect blend that improves flavor and aroma.

This is the way the old-time craftsmen of Cuba make the cigars classified today by the trade and the government as Imported. This classification applies only to the 

Cuban cigars, those brought in from the Philippines or

Puerto Rico are given the names of their country of origin. 

In the United States the hand working is much different in many places, although some, such as Key West, or Tampa, Fla., follow the Cuban method of working almost exactly. In the U. S. A. team working is much more common. One worker makes the bunches, rolling the filler into cigar form with the aid of a binder. He then passes the bunch on to one of the two wrapper rollers working with him. The wrapper roller finishes off the bunch with the wrapper by hand. The disadvantage of this method of manufacture as compared to the single worker method is that the worker knows when a bunch was a little loosely packed in one place or too tight in another and he can compensate for this by tightening or loosening the wrapper accordingly. The American method, though, is much faster. 

Most low priced cigars are made with the aid of molds, [113]

of wood or metal. The bunch maker delivers his cigars to the mold instead of to the wrapper and by keeping the bunches under pressure they attain the identical shape of the mold. The wrapper roller then removes them and completes the operation.

The machine method of manufacture is amazingly efficient and is done with a large number of the American made cigars, particularly those made to sell for less than a dime. The wrapper leaves are held laid out flat on a perforated metal suction plate. The plate is exactly the size and shape of the wrapper. The machine automatically cuts around the plate, removing the surplus of the wrapper leaf. The tobacco to go into the filler and binder is also fed into the machine and cut and shaped to the right size automatically. Then as the bunch rolls diagonally over the suction plate it automatically picks up the wrapper. Machine manufacture has materially lowered manufacturing cost and has made it possible to give the smokers quality cigars at quite low prices. However, tobacco is a plant and is therefore not absolutely uniform, so the machine can't make allowance for the individual leaf like the hand worker does.

The machine method of working even removes the central ribs automatically from the leaves. 

The cigars of Havana tobacco are of two types. There is one group called Imported. These are the cigars made in Cuba and are considered by the trade as the finest in existence. Naturally they're higher priced than other types. 

Second of the Havana classifications is called Clear Havana. 

These are cigars made of Cuban (Havana) tobacco in American factories, sometimes by hand and more seldom by machines. Bonded cigars of this sort are made of exclusively Havana tobaccos and under close government supervision. 

Minor foreign makes of cigars are the Manila cigars, [114]

made in the Philippine Islands, and the Puerto Rico cigars, made on the Island of Puerto Rico. 

In blended cigars the cigars may be of very choice tobaccos and fine workmanship but the blending is done to cater to individual taste for mildness and other characteristics that could not be obtained from one type of tobacco alone. Among these are: 

Cigars with Havana or part Havana fillers which are called Seed and Havana cigars and include Broadleaf cigars (with Broadleaf wrappers) or shadegrown cigars (with Shadegrown wrappers). Other combinations are 

Sumatra or Connecticut Shade for wrapper, Havana for filler and binders of Connecticut or Wisconsin tobacco, etc.

The thing to remember, regardless of blend, is that your fine cigar embodies all long filler, choice tobaccos (regardless of type) and excellent workmanship. Taste and aroma, because of this, are purely for the individual smoker's taste. He may prefer an almost entirely domestic cigar to the fine Havana ones, or possibly the amount he can afford for cigars may affect his selection. 

The "nickel shooters" are cheap cigars made, for the most part, of domestic tobacco with Sumatra wrappers or the same combinations of tobaccos found in higher grades of tobacco or of cheaper grades, scrap tobacco or leftovers after the fine quality ones are made. 

Incidentally, just prior to the writing of this book, it was possible to obtain a satisfactory quality all-Havana cigar for five cents, if the smoker didn't chew his cigar to pieces. The taste and aroma were surprisingly good, but the filler was short rather than running the full length of the cigar as it does in the better-grade long-filler cigars. 

Stogies are machine made cigars of domestic tobacco.

They're closed at the head while cheroots are open at both ends. [115]

CHAPTER X CIGAR SMOKER'S CLINIC ORTIFIED WITH INFORMATION on the types 

FOR of tobaccos and their manufacture, the smoker walks to the tobacco counter all ready to make his purchase. 

Possibly the following will serve as a guide which will assist him to get his money's worth. 

Pay no attention to: 

1. Fancy packages or metal tubes. They must necessarily increase the price of the product, and you're paying that difference. Furthermore they often conceal the cigar so that you can't properly judge them. Take metal tubes for example. There's a current craze for them because they were once used only on the 30 to 50 cent cigars. They're now being used on cheap cigars to jack up their selling price. Gold foil paper did the same some time ago. The aluminum foil tube costs around 42 cents, each. Using one of these tubes for a cigar that sells at three for 50 cents would leave you just a cigar which has two for a quarter value. Why pay that extra premium? 

2. Spots. Green spots on tobacco are said to show the quality. Actually they simply represent a place where the rain splashed dirt upon the leaves and kept the sun from coloring them normally. This craze grew so great at one time that manufacturers used to spot tobacco artificially. 

It's meaningless. 

3. The current demand for light color. Light color doesn't represent mildness. In fact the Cuban authorities often laugh at the fad in America. They know the dark, [116]

rich colors represent complete curing and full flavor and not a lower nicotine content or a cooler smoke, as many of the uninformed believe. 

But pay attention to these three important points: 

1. Taste. If a brand has proven to be of a type of tobacco and a blend that appeals to you (don't worry about what the other guy likes), stick to it and choose other brands of similar tobaccos or nearly the same type of blend. 

2. Workmanship. A well-made cigar has no bellys in it when you feel it. It should be uniform and not too stiff or too yielding. Soft cigars get soft and soggy after a few puffs and they may fall apart in the course of being smoked. 

Stiff cigars are often too tightly packed and therefore may be difficult to draw through. Cigars that aren't evenly packed and have hard and soft places along their length have the filler unevenly distributed and therefore will most likely burn unevenly and draw poorly. 

3. Burn. The cigar should burn evenly and symmetrically so that the coal lies perpendicular to the sides of the cigar at all times. It should hold its ash for awhile (this represents long filler and good workmanship) and the ash should be white to steel gray. Ashes from short filler cigars often flake off during smoking like a dripping faucet.

Taste and burn are interdependent. Without good burn all is lost. But if a cigar tastes like the morning after, who wants it to burn anyway? The cigar should hold its fire for two or three minutes without being puffed and it should go out in about five minutes or less when it's laid aside.

The condition of the cigar is important. If it's too dry, especially if it's Spanish hand made, it'll probably have a wrapper that cracks easily and a cracked wrapper on the cigar without a binder makes the cigar worthless because the smoke leaks out the side and the draw is ruined completely. Cigars should be fresh. A fresh cigar is one [117]

that has been kept at a neutral temperature and at the proper humidity since its manufacture. This means the leaf retains its pliability without becoming too moist and being subject to mold, misshapeness, and poor burn. 

If the dealer has permitted the cigar to dry out and re-moistened it several times there's a chance of the wrapper having been loosened or permitted to stretch. You can tell when the cigar has been so treated by watching the cellophane jacket for fogginess. Similarly, if the cellophane has become yellowed, you'll know the cigar has been in stock too long and has lost some of its desirable and essential oils into or through the cellophane. 

If the cigar crackles when it's subjected to gentle pressure it may be too dry. If it powders in your fingers, you'll know it's too blamed dry! If you do happen to have a dry cigar on hand, you can often bring it back to smoking condition by cutting off the tip of the head as you do for smoking and then breathing gently for a minute or so into both ends. The warm moisture of the breath penetrates and remoistens the cigar giving it back its lost flexibility.

The proper way to derive full satisfaction from a cigar is to obey three simple rules: 

1. Smoke inside. If you puff away at a cigar while out-of-doors the wind and your walking will carry the aroma away from you and the taste will be lost to you because your mind will almost certainly be on other matters than the quality of the cigar. Indoors you can sit down, make yourself comfortable, retain the pleasure of each puff for a moment, and relax while you enjoy the cigar. 

2. Smoke slowly, let the smoke roll over your tongue for a few seconds. Taste each puff so that you relish the quality of the cigar. Make it last as long as possible and you'll be less likely to smoke it so fast it makes your mouth hot. [118]

3. Keep your cigars in good condition. When you carry them in your pocket, keep the tuck up because it hurts the smoking quality of the cigar less if this end breaks somewhat. When you leave them around the house, don't let them dry out. The cigar box is only a temporary container. Later you'll want to transfer the cigars to a comparatively air-tight or non-absorbent container such as glass, metal or vitrolite-lined one. A Mason jar will do.

If the cigars will be kept in the container for any length of time, some moistening device should be used, such as a blotter or sponge in the top where they are not in direct contact with the cigars. If the humidor is to be in regular use, an investment in a humidity gauge will be a sound one. 

Humidity should be around 60 to 65. And cigars should be kept away from radiators or other temperature extremes. 

The cigar that doesn't draw may not have a sufficiently opened head or else the filler may have been compressed when you bit off the tip. Try pressing the tuck and head of the cigar to work the filler around a little. It may improve the draw. A pin in the head of the cigar doesn't help noticeably. Try cutting off the tip with a cigar cutter rather than biting it. It's a neater job and you're less likely to tear the wrapper and start it unwinding or to take out part of the filler that way. 

If you chew cigars too much, bite down on them too hard or dislike the taste of tobacco directly in your mouth, you want a holder. It should be chosen to fit the size of cigar you smoke. Moisten the head end of the cigar before putting it into the holder and it will fit better, adhering to the sides of the holder. Don't take the cigar out, until you're all through with it, after it is put in the holder. 

The end would be too battered to put back into your mouth anyway. 

Don't condemn a brand because just one cigar dis- [119]

appointed you. If it was ever satisfactory to you, you've probably simply hit upon one of the cigars that wasn't up to the manufacturer's standards and the next one you try may be better.

Now a word about shapes. They're strictly a matter of personal preference. Big ones, ideal for after-dinner, often have finer quality tobaccos in them and they're also usually more expensive than others. Little ones, of course, are perfect "grab smokes" and medium size ones, say perfectos or panatellas, are quite good for everyday smoking. 

Every manufacturer has his own shape so it's better to call for them by simply pointing than by name, except when you're requesting one definitely associated with a certain brand.

Remember that price and quality are related. The giant size cigar at a low price is almost certainly a "rope". 

You get what you pay for.

Taste, burn and aroma of a cigar is based on its blend, skill of manufacture, selection of tobacco, and proper conditioning. Study something about these things whenever you enter a cigar store and you'll soon be an expert cigar smoker and a happier man. [120]

IMPORT CHARGES 

Just to get some idea of how much of the retail price we pay for pipes goes to import duties, let's examine some of the rates:

Briar pipes, finished or unfinished, valued at less than $1.20 per dozen are taxed a nickel apiece plus 60 per cent of their invoiced value. Pipes from the United Kingdom or France, the exceptions, are taxed 22 cents each plus 40 per cent of their invoiced value. Those valued between $1.20 and $5 per dozen are taxed the same save that those from the United Kingdom are taxed 5 cents each plus 50 per cent.

Briars valued at more than $5 per dozen are taxed the same as the lower valued pipes. 

Clay-bowled pipes (not including meerschaums) with mouthpieces of some other material are taxed the same as the lowest-priced briars with the exception in this case being the Netherland pipes. They're taxed 2½ cents each plus 30 per cent. Pipes made entirely of clay, valued at 

40 cents per gross or less, are taxed 15 cents per gross.

Those valued at more than 40 cents per gross are taxed 45 per cent of their value. Unfinished meerschaum is taxed 

20 per cent.

Other products, including pouches, holders and other smoker's accessories, are taxed about proportionately. [122]

Cigars of all kinds are taxed $3.60 per pound plus 

20 per cent of their value save when imported from Cuba; then the rate's $2.25 per pound plus 12½ per cent. 

Cigarettes are taxed $4.50 per pound plus 25 per cent except when brought in from the United Kingdom which is $2.25 per pound plus 122 per cent.

TAX-FREE IMPORTS 

Many traveling smokers miss an excellent opportunity to bring tax-free merchandise into this country by failure to keep up with the tariff regulations on smokers' items. 

The following information may be a guide to assist you in determining what you may bring into this country without paying import duty:

Under the provisions of paragraph 1798, Tariff Act of 1930, as amended, a resident of the United States returning from a trip to a foreign country, may import free of duty and internal-revenue tax, articles acquired by him merely as an incident of his trip to the value of $100, including an aggregate of one wine gallon of alcoholic beverages and not more than 100 cigars, if these articles are for personal or household use, for souvenirs or curios, and not for sale or bought on commission. You will note that the quantity of cigars which may be imported free of duty under the $100 exemption is limited to 100. There are no other limitations on tobacco, tobacco products, or smokers' articles which may be included under the $100 exemption, provided, of course, that the requirements of the exemption as set forth in the statute are met.

Nonresidents arriving in this country are permitted to import free of duty and internal-revenue tax 50 cigars or 300 cigarettes or 3 pounds of manufactured tobacco, if these articles are not intended for sale or other commercial use. Any pipes which are the property of a nonresident [123]

arriving in this country and which are owned by him and in his possession abroad at the time of or prior to his departure from a foreign country, and which are intended for his personal use and not for any other person nor for sale, may be entitled to free entry upon his arrival in the United States under the first provision of paragraph 1798 of the tariff act.* *Quoted from a letter received by the Author from the Department of Internal Revenue. The rag picker and the clothing manufacturer may smoke the same brand of cigarette. Call it a vice if you will, but no other vice is equally genial, equally conducive to good fellowship and understanding.

Wouldn't it be great if, before making any major decisions-whether they affect a few cents or millions of people-the persons on whom the responsibility rests could give themselves over to fifteen minutes of quiet retrospection over a glowing bowl of soul-satisfying, soothing tobacco?
