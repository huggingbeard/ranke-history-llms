No satisfying biography of HERBERT EDWIN LOMBARD can be written.* Any attempt even to sketch his life seems doomed to fail. Fond of society, he lived essentially alone and left no written record of his days. He gave himself freely in service to others; but no man had access to that which he did not give. 

Characterized by remarkable common sense in material affairs, he was very simply at home in a spiritual environment. Two worlds were equally real to him. Of his spiritual experience, which might seem to separate him from other men, he never spoke. With a recognized genius for human friendship, he was strangely reticent.

Even his most intimate friends had no key to certain doors. Those of his congregations who knew him best have told me that they gained the clearest insight into his private thoughts by listening intently to his prayers. With all the world shut out, yet not forgetful of time or place, *I am particularly indebted to Frank Alanson Lombard for constant help in writing this sketch of his brother. In fact, the record of Herbert Lombard's early life and his beginnings in the ministry could not have been written without his generous assistance. C.S.B. [5]

he talked simply with his God, creating an atmosphere in which unseen realities were convincingly revealed.

The American Lombards came from England in the seventeenth century bringing a name which probably originated in Northern Italy, or from connection with the Lombardy bankers. Nathan, fifth in descent from John, was born to Joseph, September 4, 1777, at Brimfield, Massachusetts. 

On March 14, 1802, Nathan married Delight 

Allen (b. Sept. 11, 1777), whose father, Caleb 

Allen of Sturbridge, served (1775-1777) as private and sergeant in the Revolutionary War. 

The young couple moved to Sutton, then one of the three most important towns in Worcester County, where Nathan as a cabinetmaker, a skilled worker in fine woods, could expect a more attractive future. Their eldest son, Alanson 

Allen (b. Jan. 25, 1803), learned his father's trade; and a fine example of the family work may be seen in the high mahogany pulpit of the 

First Congregational Church in Sutton. 

In 1830, Alanson Allen Lombard married 

Alexa Ann Hutchinson; and to them on January 

19, 1834, was born Henry Faulkner. Henry became a skilled mechanic and, after a journey to what was then the wild west, took work in the [6]

city of Springfield, Massachusetts. There, on 

December 31, 1862, he married Nellie Callahan, a petite girl of twenty-two whose parents had died, leaving her little except an heritage of pride and ineradicable horror of debt. 

Nellie Lombard was a member of the State Street Baptist Church of Springfield, and brought to her new home a strong, forward-looking faith; but she consistently refused in democratic America to recall or to hand down legends of Huguenot days in France. That subject was taboo. Proud and ambitious, she would be indebted to no man, not even to her fathers, for what she might be able to work out through her children. 

For her first confinement, Nellie went to her husband's home in Sutton, where, on November 

19th, 1863, Herbert Edwin was born. He was a "goodly child"; and many are the stories of the attention that the dark, curly-headed boy attracted upon the streets of Springfield during the next few years. Once his mother was questioned as to how she stuffed his stockings so smoothly! 

Springfield was not "home" for long. Under the strain of precise and exacting work, Henry Lombard's health, which had kept him from military service during the Civil War, failed in a nervous break during the late 60's. 

He was [7]

ordered to give up all city employment and to go into the country. The house and small farm, near the eastern shore of Lake Singletary, which had been bought by Nathan Lombard about 1830, was now owned and occupied by his son, Alanson; and there Henry and his family were made welcome upon terms which divided the house and the produce of the farm for the use of two families. 

Thus Herbert Edwin became a country boy, in 

Sutton where he had been born.

Life upon a New England farm has never been easy. None of the Lombards were successful farmers. Changed conditions left the shop of the cabinetmaker idle; and Henry's health forbade more than short intervals of return to the practice of his trade. No modern installation of electricity or running water lightened the drudgery of house or barn. Cash receipts were few. Expenditures had to be made with care. The Lombards were proud. They cared for appearances; but they would not go into debt. Still are remembered the personal privations that sustained family independence and made possible a goodly measure of hospitality. 

For nine years Herbert was an only child. His playmates were few. His mother was his most intimate companion; and of her character, am- [8]

bitions and trials, he came precociously to have appreciative understanding. She was the soul of hospitality, and made much of every cultural contact for her own enjoyment and for the sake of her small boy. 

After the birth of his younger brother, Frank, in 1872, Herbert became more than before his mother's helper; and influences from those years undoubtedly contributed to make him what he later became: a welcome ministrant to the ill, the aged and the dying, a great host, a genial entertainer, a man for whom discrepancies of age were no barrier to intimate association. Some of the best friends of his youth were among the aged; some of the most loyal friends of his later years were young. In personal matters he never reckoned in years and seldom recognized birthdays.

Upon graduation from the Sutton High School, personal desire, as well as the urgency of teachers and friends, pointed to further study; but family economy made further schooling at that time impossible. He studied by himself, however; and, from the unsatisfying labor of the farm, turned gladly to accept an appointment as teacher in an ungraded district school. That was a hard assignment for a mere youth: the teaching and discipline of boys and girls, some of whom were bigger if 1 [9]

not older than himself. The little children loved him, the older ones respected him; and his gracious tact carried him through the experience until, with a little money saved, he entered a business school in Worcester.

Fitted for office work, he then served as bookkeeper for Draper Bros. in Bloomingdale; and enjoyed new associations with men of business. 

During these years he was still a part of the home and church in Sutton, setting out many of the fine trees which grace the homestead and serving as superintendent of the Sunday school. He had not, however, found his proper place. By himself, with but little help from others, he continued to study until, having completed the equivalent of a college course, he entered Bangor Seminary in final preparation for the Christian ministry.

While in the Seminary and during summer vacations, he supplied regularly at Veazie, Orono, and Whiting. Upon graduation, in 1896, he accepted a call to a settled pastorate in Cherryfield, Maine, then an important town in Washington County. Among his papers was found the record of the Ecclesiastical Council which assembled at Cherryfield, June 23, 1896, to examine

Mr. Lombard with a view to his ordination. "The candidate presented ample testimonials of [10]

Congregational church standing, and of suitable training for the work on which he proposes to enter: and, on examination, was found to give good evidence of personal godliness, of intellectual and scholarly abilities, and of soundness of faith, as our Congregational Churches are accustomed to count soundness." After reading this conclusion, the Council duly approved the action of the Cherryfield Society in selecting their new pastor. 99 Mr. Lombard, as pastor of the Church in 

Cherryfield for over six years, greatly endeared himself to the town and became an influence far beyond the confines of his parish, being called, half in jest and half seriously, the young bishop of Washington County. A wealthy friend, not of his own town, offered him the deed of an island in Passamaquoddy Bay for a summer home to be built by other friends, if he would only marry and settle more permanently in Eastern Maine. 

Strong ties of personal affection might have kept him among a people whom he had come to love; but his brother Frank had gone to Japan in 

1900 as an educational missionary under the American Board, their mother was not strong, their father failing, and duty, together with devotion, led him to seek a pastorate nearer home. 

In October, 1902, the Byfield Church and Parish [11]

of Old Newbury invited him to become their pastor, and the bonds which united him officially with Cherryfield were broken.

Something of the esteem in which he was held in Cherryfield, and something of the regret with which his people released him for service in Byfield, may be gathered from the records of the Cherryfield Society. At a formal meeting held on 

October 21, 1902, the Church adopted the following resolutions: "It is with feelings of deepest regret, not unmixed with sorrow, that we have been called to consider the letter of resignation of our much loved and honored Pastor, and highly esteemed fellow-citizen. After carefully and prayerfully considering all the circumstances of its acceptance or rejection, and recognizing the duty that calls him from us, loyalty to him forces us to accept the terms of the letter, be it therefore "Resolved: That in the resignation of Herbert

E. Lombard, the First Congregational Church and 

Parish of Cherryfield loses a faithful, efficient, painstaking, and much loved Pastor, and affectionate friend, and the Sunday school a valuable member and co-worker. "Resolved: That while we regret the necessity of now parting with him, and deplore the cir- [12]

cumstances that cause him to leave us, we can but admire the spirit of filial devotion that dominates his life. "Resolved: That we as Church and Parish express our deep gratitude for the valuable and unselfish service he has rendered as Pastor and friend, and heartily commend him for his direct influence upon our boys, leading their minds in paths travelled by his own, and guiding them towards a lifelong course of thoughtfulness and self advancement. "Resolved: That while we accept his resignation, it is with heartfelt sorrow that we sever the ties binding Pastor and people, so strongly and tenderly attached.

As at Cherryfield, previously, an Ecclesiastical Council convened, this time in the Byfield Congregational Church in Newbury, December 11, 1902. 

With fifteen churches represented by ministerial and lay delegates the gathering listened to a statement by the pastor-elect of the main points of his religious belief. The Council was given an opportunity to question the candidate if the members desired, and then voted to go into private session. The Council "being by itself," as the record states, "voted unanimously that all the proceedings, papers, experience and statements of [13]

the Pastor-elect be deemed satisfactory, and to approve of the choice of the Church of Rev. 

Herbert Edwin Lombard, as its Pastor." 11 From 1902 until 1911, Mr. Lombard ministered to the Byfield Parish, and there, in the region made ever memorable by the pen of Whittier, developed an already strong antiquarian interest. Intimate connection with Governor 

Dummer Academy and association in the spiritual activities of Adelynrood gave variety and richness to his ministry.

The bicentennial celebration of Byfield had been held in June, preceding the coming of Mr. 

Lombard; and is duly recorded in John L. Ewell's 

Story of Byfield, which contains reference to Mr. Lombard and an excellent copy of a photograph of the pastor-elect. Six years later the 

Parish celebrated an Old Home Week; and upon that occasion Mr. Lombard gave the historical address. This is quoted in full in the Newburyport 

Daily News of July 27, 1908, and reveals the touch of a scholarly historian. With the purpose of chronicling the printed productions of the members of the parish, from the earliest days, he showed how these literary forces influenced the world of letters. Beginning with "The Prophecy of Samuel Sewall," Mr. Lombard carried his [14]

literary record down the years, mentioning Rev.

Moses Parsons and Elijah Parish of the eighteenth century, and then quoting from prose and poetry relating to Byfield up to recent years. His keen appreciation of the beauty of literary expression was shown in the many lines that he read. Most interesting was his concluding paragraph in which he referred to the American Antiquarian Society in his home city of Worcester and his use of its library to gain some of the material for his address. This same address, with considerable enlargement, he repeated before the Old Newbury 

Chapter of the Daughters of the Revolution, and the Newburyport Daily News of March 9, 1909, again faithfully printed it in full. 

From Byfield, Mr. Lombard devoted much time to the Historical Society of Old Newbury, of which he was elected president in 1906. Under his presidency the Society celebrated the Whittier 

Centenary in December, 1907; and the fine building which the Society calls home in Newburyport owes much to his promotion. The numbered edition of the Booklet recording the Exercises of the Whittier Anniversary contains several letters written to him as president of the Society revealing something of his wide and intimate associations. [15]

In December, 1909, Mr. Lombard allowed his collection of books, bindings and bookplates to be placed upon exhibition in the building of the Historical Society, where they filled ten cases. It was really a notable showing of New England literature, centering largely upon Newburyport and Essex County authors. Whittier's books, of course, had a leading place, with nearly all of his publications in first editions, many autographed or accompanied by autograph letters. Longfellow, Lowell, Harriet Prescott Spofford, Edward Everett 

Hale, Alice Brown, Bliss Perry, Florence Converse, and other writers, many of whom were personal friends of Mr. Lombard, were well represented. Eighteenth century Newburyport sermons and publications by officers and graduates of Governor Dummer Academy had been carefully collected. Bookplates, of which Mr. Lombard had one of the leading New England collections, rounded out the exhibit.

During the academic year 1910-1911, the younger brother from the Orient was on furlough fellowship at Clark University in Worcester, and supplied for several months at the Tatnuck Congregational Church. The need of that church for a settled pastor, and Herbert's desire to be still nearer his parents, led him to accept the call which [16]

was extended him from the Tatnuck Church in the spring of 1911.

It was not easy for him to leave Byfield with its personal and historical associations; but the move to Worcester brought compensations. His parents could make their home with him. During the next three years, the new church building so greatly desired by the Society was erected; but the strain of ministry, of placing the church upon a firm material foundation, and of the increasing attention needed by his father, proved too great; and in August, 1914, for personal reasons, Mr. 

Lombard felt obliged to resign.

The various documents connected with his leaving both Byfield and Tatnuck, all preserved in his papers, show the genuine sorrow and sincere regrets for the severance of the relations between pastor and church. As minister, teacher and friend, he left a lasting impression upon both of these congregations.

From 1914 to 1918, Mr. Lombard made his home with his parents in Sutton. During that time he supplied various churches and took an active part in all the Liberty Loan efforts which characterized those war years. In 1917, his father died at the age of eighty-two; and in the spring of 

1918 Mr. Lombard accepted a call to the Congre- ។ [17]

gational Church in Webster where he had supplied for an extended period in 1914. 

The years in Webster were among the happiest of his life. His mother, to whom he was increasingly devoted, occupied the parsonage with him. 

The old home in Sutton was sold as it could no longer be occupied. His mother, who had been confined to her home for several years, died 

November 15, 1923. When, after his mother's death, he resigned from the pastorate, the honors conferred upon him were fit recognition of the extent to which he had identified himself with church and community. In his letter of resignation, dated January 12, 1925, Mr. Lombard asked the committee to accept a gift from him of a sum to be devoted to the installation of new choir pews, this to form a memorial to his mother. 

The Church, knowing that their pastor desired to leave the active ministry to devote the remainder of his life to literary and antiquarian pursuits, chose him Pastor Emeritus and presented him with a testimonial illuminated on parchment in imitation of the lettering of the monks of the 

Middle Ages.

Realizing that no pastor emeritus should continue to live within the confines of his parish, Mr. Lombard moved to Worcester where he [18]

could be near the Library of the American Antiquarian Society and free for a wider service among the churches. In the Webster Church, however, he retained his membership and with its pastor cooperated in most cordial intimacy.

Long the Scribe of the Worcester South Association of Congregational Churches, he was in great demand for counsel and for special services, often supplying for extended periods when churches. were without settled pastors. From 1927 to 1929, he was acting pastor in Grafton, stated supply in Southbridge in 1929, and ad interim pastor in 

Whitinsville, 1937-1938. In these and other churches his ministry brought refreshment and was deeply appreciated.

Upon concluding his service in the Village Congregational Church, Whitinsville, he was tendered a memorial which he cherished as an expression of the heart of that people among whom he had come to feel particularly at home. Something of the power of his personal ministry was evidenced in all services of Communion; and of his last Communion Service in Whitinsville a visitor wrote to him saying: "I was glad to be in your congregation last Sunday. However, before the service was over, I had the feeling that I had boldly entered into the privacy of a special [19]

sacred family gathering where members sitting around a Table of Remembrance were being instructed and inspired gently, wisely and lovingly by the head of the family in the most important matter of reconsecrating themselves to the highest possible Christian life and service in their own homes and in their community, nation and world. "Intruder that I was, it did me good to be there. 

I write truthfully when I say I do not recall a

Communion Service, in which I was so gripped, uplifted and inspired as at that one Sunday. God's hand was surely upon you and his spirit within you all through that service and prompted you to say in a most impressive manner many things that each one in the congregation doubtless needed, as I did, to rehear, re-heed, and re-live. I want you to know that you were God's ministering angel to me that day."

As suggested in this letter, Mr. Lombard was a pleasing, conversational, and impressive speaker, whose appeal was always to the best, the most spiritual in man. He seldom startled his congregation by any new teaching, he was never sensational; but invariably he was practical and tenderly personal. Speaking after careful thought, he spoke entirely without notes in language that came extemporaneously. In consequence, none of his [20]

sermons remain, except in the lives of their hearers; and it is to be doubted that apart from his personality they would carry strong conviction.

Mr. Lombard's interest in the town and church of his childhood continued unbroken; and he was ever at their call, whether from Newbury, to give the historical address at Sutton's Bicentennial (1904) and to preside as toastmaster at the banquet, or from Worcester repeatedly to conduct the business meetings of the church when pastorless, and to counsel in times of perplexity. In recognition of such devotion, the First Congregational Church of Sutton in 1939 paid him the unique honor of election as Honorary Pastor. He was a prophet not without honor in his own country.

A life-long servant of the Church, Mr. Lombard held her doctrines with a liberal conservatism. He rejoiced in the richness of an "ordered" worship; he was at home in the pulpit, most happy in prayer, most effective in personal ministry. 

But in the footsteps of his Master he walked freely in the world of men. As a raconteur, he had few equals; and the world is poorer in that his stories of experience and personal incident were not recorded. He made sunshine for others; the shadows were for himself alone. 

He never [21]

married. He was never engaged. Gracious in society, he enjoyed the companionship of women, especially of the elderly; but to his mother he gave his deepest devotion.

Mr. Lombard was prominently identified with 

Masonry throughout his life. He became a member of Narraguagus Lodge in Cherryfield, Maine, in 1896, soon after his installation there, was Chaplain from 1897 to 1900, and was demitted in 1921. He entered the Royal Arch Chapter in 

1897. In 1921 he became affiliated with Webster 

Lodge, where he served as Chaplain from 1922 to 1926. He was a charter member of Webster 

Chapter, Royal Arch Masons, constantly called upon to help in work, and remained active on the rolls until his death. He was also made a member of the Newburyport Commandery of Knights

Templar in 1920. He was elected to honorary membership in Olive Branch Lodge of Millbury in 1928. He joined no Lodge in Worcester, but was made an honorary member of Eureka Chapter Royal Arch Masons in 1940, the only one thus honored in the Worcester Chapter. Mr. Lombard was always a welcome visitor at Masonic meetings; and when officiating, instead of reading the ritual, often spoke on Masonry-its obligations and its privileges. [22]

Having outlived his mother-which he once recorded as his only prayer-Herbert Edwin

Lombard died suddenly, as he wished, Saturday evening, July 13, 1940. He who had ministered to many in their last moments, went on alone. 

It seems probable that Mr. Lombard knew that his days were numbered. In the spring he had gone to Ellsworth, New Hampshire, to offer the prayer of reconsecration in a building which for twenty years had not known a Christian service; and he was to preach the first sermon in that reborn church on August 4th. He had only one other appointment before him. Strange! for he was a very busy man. The last week he had filled with social ministry; and, when asked as to how he was feeling, replied "we do not speak of such matters to those whom we love most. 

I have left until the last Mr. Lombard's connection with the American Antiquarian Society which began fully thirty years ago. I well remember the day, in the summer of 1911, soon after he assumed the Tatnuck pastorate, when he came to the Library and said that he would like to engage in some form of bibliographical research which would be of use to the Society. Knowing his familiarity with Newburyport literature, I told him we needed a bibliography of early Newbury- [23]

port printing, but that the most arduous part of the undertaking was to examine the long files of Newburyport newspapers for advertisements of imprints. He replied that all that he wanted were the files and a room to work in. So that very afternoon, in the heat of a summer's day, he took off his coat and started in. Later he obtained the loan of the longer newspaper files from the Newburyport Historical Society, and it was into the following winter before he finished his task. 

In 1913 he was elected a member of the Society. Thereafter, if possible, his interest increased. In 1914 he presented to the Library his collection of 2500 American bookplates, which at that time was one of the best in New England. It was especially strong in the finer examples of modern engraved plates by Spencely, French, Hopson, and Smith, but also included many rare early plates by Revere, Hurd, and other 18th century engravers. This was the beginning of our collection, for not until we yielded to his enthusiasm did we even attempt to secure bookplates. Rapidly the collection increased. 

Through his urging, we acquired the Terry and part of the Eno collection, and finally the great Frank E. Marshall collection, with its showing of over 20,000 plates. [24]

During all these years Mr. Lombard was constantly working to increase and exploit our collection. In 1918 he wrote an article for the "Bookplate Quarterly" on the bookplate collection of the American Antiquarian Society, so descriptive and concise that it was reprinted in the Society's Proceedings for October 1918. In 

1920 he induced Stanley Harrod of Toronto to donate his collection of about 600 Canadian plates. In 1921 he wrote for "The Bookplate 

Annual" an article on the Steven Day 1642 plate, which was reprinted in the Proceedings for October 1921. In 1930, largely through his financial assistance, we acquired the bookplate proofs and drawings of Sidney L. Smith. In 1934 he wrote for the American Society of Bookplate Collectors Year Book an article on the Bookplates of 

Frederick C. Blank. Also, to increase the collection, he must have written over a thousand personal letters to collectors and private owners. 

As a result of his activity and enthusiasm the bookplates in this Society today number over 50,000 plates, unquestionably the largest such collection in the country.

After he had started the Society collecting bookplates, Mr. Lombard next turned his attention to 

American literature. In 1923 he gave to the [25]

Library his collection of 600 presentation books, with autographed copies of the works of many nineteenth century writers, including a number of desirable volumes from the library of John Greenleaf Whittier. This started us on a new field of collecting, that of nineteenth century American literature. Strong in early American publications, and in political, economic, religious and social history, we lacked the original materials for the study of literary history. We first made up a list of over three hundred authors whose work had stood the test of time and whose contributions might be regarded as justifying their permanent place in American literature. Then we made lists of the eight thousand titles which in the earliest editions represented the work of these authors. 

During the past seventeen years this collection has been increased and completed, until today it has all but a small proportion of the books written by the authors selected. The forming of the collection has been in great measure due to the support and interest shown by Mr. Lombard. He gave us what we lacked from his own library in 1925 and he induced hundreds of book owners to donate volumes which we needed. 

I doubt if any other member of the Antiquarian Society was so constant a visitor to the Library. [26]

Not a week passed but we saw him enter the building, generally carrying under his arm some treasure or gift which he had acquired. His cheerful greeting, his optimism and his friendly attitude toward life were pervasive. He enjoyed social contacts. Unless sick or called by some necessary engagement, he faithfully attended the 

Society's meetings, renewing friendships with members, many of whom he had long known. 

This capacity for friendship was outstanding in his character. Everyone who knew him liked him, both for the sincerity of his nature and for his faith in people. He was proud of his connection with this Society, and we rejoiced in the fellowship. To those of us who were brought most frequently in contact with him, his death brings a feeling of sadness that words cannot express. We shall miss his loyal devotion, the stimulation of his enthusiasm, and his understanding kindliness.

He lived a useful life, always thinking of others more than of himself. At last, when his work was so largely finished and his time had come, he passed on-minister of grace-scholarly antiquarian-his body to rest in his native town, his soul to range in a wider ministry. [27]
