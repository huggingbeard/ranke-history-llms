READ THIS FIRST 

A column in the Birmingham, Alabama, News called "Light Spots in the Spotlight" is built around the idea that the headlines of any daily paper are depressing because they deal chiefly with crimes, casualties, calamities, and the general abnormalities of life. Along with these things there go, of course, many others of a brighter and more hopeful nature, but they attract less attention. 

The writer of this Light Spot column believes this brighter side of life is its normal side. In this column he calls attention to, and sometimes comments briefly on these more hopeful items appearing from time to time in the spotlight of the passing day.

This little volume is a mere collection of some of these detached paragraphs. They are apropos of nothing except the fleeting incidents which called them out. They were written under the impulse of the moment, after, or during, the reading of the daily paper. There was no attempt at literary excellence in their first off hand production, and they are reproduced with the same day by day flavor in which they were written without re-writing or effort to polish. Wise friends have advised that all localisms should be omitted, and that, perhaps, ought to be done, but even these are retained to preserve the original spirit in which the lines were written. The language is that of the street rather than of the study, and at times it is a most ordinary street at that. It is kept here with its original faults for the sake of the "homey" feeling the author always had when writing.

The writer comments on many subjects suggested by the events of the day; he discusses none, and there is no

logical connection between any two. Moreover, in reading the daily paper he occasionally found some quip or story over which he spent a merry moment. 

Some of them are placed here in the hope you may like, and over them smile a bit.

Running through the whole is a sort of life philosophy which has made this world a very bright place wherein the Light Spot man has worked. 

Maybe it will help you.

The normal is not news. The abnormal claims attention in the daily spotlight. Therefore the man from Mars would deem us a sorry lot if he drew his knowledge of human affairs entirely from the printed story of our daily doings. It is not the fault of the press—at least not entirely so. Folks do not care to hear from the thousands of homes whose peace was unbroken during the hours of last night. 

They want to hear about the one selected by the robber for his raid. The many homes whose fortunate roofs were kissed by the rising sun of this beautiful day are of no special interest. We are concerned about the one whose inmates were driven into the darkened streets by devouring flames. The man who dropped a nickel into the hand of poverty passes unnoted. The villain who snatched the pittance from the beggar is head lined on the front page. 

The multitudes of teachers who pour out their lives in the effort to stimulate, encourage and instruct our youth, striving to strengthen their faith in God and man, escape the spotlight. The fellow who propounds strange, bizarre and sensational theories fills the public eye. In our schools and colleges are thousands of worthy students whose quiet and manly, or womanly, bearing is so common as to evoke no note. A few vain fellows who think it funny to play the devil become the reporter's topic, and so bring into disrepute their comrades who deserve better friends. The homes where Heaven dwells make poor reading. We are thrilled by reports from those dens where hell breaks loose. The man will have a sorry story to tell who ten thousand years from now writes of our times, gathering his information first hand from the files of the daily papers published as the events occurred. He will describe our times as characterized by rape, murder, rapine, divorce, graft, and general lawlessness of all kinds and sorts. BUILDERS 

A recent writer, who attaches the title "reverend" to his name, has been eulogizing Robert G. Ingersoll. There is nothing surprising in that, for the brilliant pagan had many admirable traits about his magnetic personality. The scintillating mind, the impressive form, the musical voice, the witchery of words, the generous warmth of his whole personality made him a very attractive man. 

But just why the "reverend" should couple with the many good things to be said about the unbeliever a lot of harsh words about those who in that day did believe in the eternal verities of God's word is a mystery. Men like 

Spurgeon and Moody were contemporary with Ingersoll.

They built colleges, established orphanages, erected hospitals, made places of refuge for old men and women, founded asylums for the unfortunate, gave life and time to alle-

Mr. Ingersoll with all his great talents did nothing except to hinder those who would do such work. He laughed at them, ridiculed them, (his eulogist takes special joy in that ridicule); he sought to break the force of their appeal, and weaken the power of their plea by pouring contempt upon their work, and making sport of their deep convictions.

He, poor fellow, went out in the night, leaving nothing behind to comfort those friends who mourned his going, and nothing that would help a suffering world to bear its wounds.

And the men whom the "reverend" writer holds in contempt, along with his admiration of their ridiculerwhat of them? They, too, are gone, triumphing in the hour of death, and leaving behind institutions of grace, either founded by their efforts or strengthened by their toil.

Faith builds. Doubt enters the palace after faith is gone and says: "Ah-ha! let me teach you wisdom."

LIGHT SPOTS READING THIS AND THAT 

I owe much to Dr. Phelps, who, in my early life, called attention to the fact that no one has time to read everything. 

The volume of even worthwhile literature is too great for any one reader to do more than scratch the surface of its vast libraries. Each reader must therefore be carefully selective of his own reading matter. 

If he reads this, for sheer lack of time he cannot read that. It is of the utmost consequence that what we do select shall be of the highest value. 

The wise reader may therefore ignore most of "the passing show." The fellow who reads to "keep up" is not only advertising his ignorance, but also the fact that he has an uneasy dread of being deemed ignorant. Good books live. The other sort die soon. 

Dr. Phelps says that while there are many hundreds of good books which may be read with profit, there are really only a few which actually must be read. And he is right about that. Your list of the essential works would doubtless differ somewhat from his. Certainly mine does. But in any list the number of great books is small. Of course, if we are at all wise no part of our precious time will be given to chaff and dirt. I am here saying that with much time given even to the good, you will have little left for the best. 

If it is ever right to "sponge" on your friends this is the time. Use their time and eyes. In the course of a few

You cannot read both this and that. You must choose.

EARNING CAPACITY 

A recent magazine tells us that the college graduate earns on an average during his lifetime $150,000. The average high school graduate earns $78,000. Those whose education ended in the grammar grades make $45,000. The shop-trained boy who did not even finish the grades earns only $25,000.

This sort of appeal is quite right in trying to get the boy to complete his education, but the money argument can be easily overdone. The commercial value of an education is its least charm.

LIGHT SPOTS PRESENCE OF MIND 

Down in Tuskaloosa the other day I had occasion to say that if I had my life to live over again I would not change it by the breadth of one hair. 

Pelham Brown said: "Well, if I had mine to go over again, I would change everything in it." "Oh, you would, eh?" 

It was the good wife speaking. 

Now, Ladies and Gentlemen, that is what I call being in a close place. That fellow had to think-had to think mighty fast and straight. A false note here meant there would be no sugar in his coffee next morning—and other calamities were in the offing. 

But he was equal to the occasion. Without batting an eye or raising an eyebrow, he replied: "Yes, I would, and while I was changing, I would marry five years earlier than I did." 

And the sun shone once more! 

Can you beat it?

LITERATURE'S HIGHEST PEAK 

A friend sends me one hundred copies (vest pocket size) of the Gospel of John. This brief treatise is the highest peak of human literature. Judged by the number of readers, by its influence on the thought and actions of mankind, by the dignity and majesty of its diction, by the importance of its subject, or by all combined, nothing else written by man approaches its height.

And it was written by a man who was recognized by his fellows as "an unlearned and ignorant man." How do you account for this?

Simply by this: "They took knowledge of him that he had been with Jesus."

CAUSE FOR TEARS 

A comedian who has been converted and is now devoting himself to religious work told the organized Bible class conference the other day about finding a fellow weeping his heart out over the death of some one who evidently meant much to the mourner. Sympathetic inquiry developed the fact that it was not mother, father, brother, sister, nor even a friend. Pressed for an answer the unfortunate man finally let it out that he was weeping over the death of his wife's first husband!

It is a fine thing to have a religion that helps you to enjoy a good joke.

LIGHT SPOTS WONDERFUL TRANSMUTATION 

Every now and then some fellow announces that the transmutation of metals is an accomplished fact. This time two of them "confirm reports from Amsterdam" saying it is true. When that is really accomplished and men find a way to perform the miracle on a large scale, the very foundations of all our economic life will be moved out of place, and we will have to adjust ourselves to an entirely new order of existence.

But transmutation of a more important kind has been an everyday affair for centuries. The Nazarene said to 

His grief-stricken friends: "Your sorrow shall be changed into joy." And they found it to be literally true. And it has been true with multiplied multitudes of His people from that day to this.

The ability to change a baser metal into gold is a trifle in worth compared to this. The idea is not that time will dim our present grief; not that coming joy shall be so great as to make us forget the things which crush us now, but that the very thing breaking our hearts today shall thrill our souls tomorrow. The woe that weights us now shall be the bliss that wings us then. 

How can these things be? Bless your heart, I do not know, but I have seen it happen a thousand times. 

This transmutation business is a puzzle to our chemists. 

It is easy to God.

HOW BIG IS YOUR GOD 

The Sunday School lesson for today is about the Israelites crossing the Red Sea.

This is, of course, a clearly supernatural event when taken in its simple and evident meaning. Take any view of it, and the greatness and goodness of God dominate the whole picture.

The world has had much to say in late years (and none too much) of the goodness of God. It is vastly more than we have ever dreamed.

But if I were teaching this lesson today I would dwell much on the Greatness of God. 

On His Almighty Power.

We have discovered a wee bit about a few of God's laws. A very wee bit about a very few. The infinite fields of the undiscovered shrivel what we know, or think we know, into a mere dot. But inflated by our learning, we have just about robbed God of His power and freedom. 

We have said He cannot or will not violate His laws, and therefore the story of the Red Sea is an impossible thing. 

Some reject the story as false-others would explain it away, and so reduce the Almighty to human measures. 

We do this in the name of learning, and brand as ignorant, benighted, narrow, and superstitious, all who do not agree with us. Boasting of Wisdom, we become foolish. 

Glorying in tolerance, we become intolerant and oppressive. 

There is nothing new in the denial of the miraculous element of the Scriptures. And the denial always comes

LIGHT SPOTS 

from the same basic error. We would measure the Almighty with the tape-lines of men. We would dip out the ocean of Infinity in our little pint cups of tin, bought maybe from Woolworth or Kress.

As a matter of fact God never has violated any of His laws. It has seemed so to us, but the operation of other laws higher than the ones we know is entirely within the limit of even human reason, and what seems to us a miracle, is, after all, most natural.

Certain it is that God's Law of Love transcends all other laws, and at its mandate all others are held in abeyance and naturally so. 

I am not concerned about the particular interpretation. you put on the various events recorded in the Bible, but any construction placed upon them is false if it sets limits anywhere to the Greatness of God.

It is infinitely-note the word “infinitely”—it is infinitely easier for God to divide the Pacific Ocean, or dry it up altogether than it is for you to stop the flow of water in your bathroom when all your fixtures are in perfect order.

It is infinitely easier for God to individualize all mankind and count the hairs on the head of each tot than it is for you to distinguish between your own children.

All things that are, that have been, or will be, are the result of the effortless energy of the infinite God who feels no difference between the weight of the Universe and that of a feather. 

The Almighty God is almighty-all mighty. With Him there are no difficulties-no great or small-no hard or

TWO DAYS WAS I BURIED 

I said recently that there are more people who are today giving body, mind and soul to the cause of Christ than at any time in the history of the world. The next day after that was published I met a fellow who had been in a New Mexican snowdrift which covered him and his flivver completely and held him prisoner for two days and nights. He had neither food nor fire. But he says the same Lord who covered him up dug him out, and after thawing, he set out again seeking the lost in that sparsely settled country. 

Paul had some pretty lively experiences, but he was never buried alive in a snowdrift with a flivver for a coffin.

LIGHT SPOTS REAL RICHES 

We are never quite sure of ourselves, but I think I know what I am talking about when I say that few things could distress me more than to become the heir of Rockefeller's wealth. It would seriously interfere with my business and cause me to lose sleep, and I value sleep more highly than I value gold. 

But Charles Eliot is dead! Why couldn't he leave his vast knowledge and experience to me, or you? That would be a legacy we all might covet.

We may bequeath lands, moneys, titles, and other baubles to those we love-leave them to be sometimes blessing-oftener a curse. But wisdom, learning, character-these priceless things cannot be left to any. They die with us when we sink into the grave, and our children must begin where we beganat zero. Eliot is dead.

DEEP-ALSO WIDE 

If you have a sense of humor you cannot help smiling when you hear some good friend who has scarcely a speaking acquaintance with tolerance grow vehement in his denunciation of intolerance. In my experience the narrowest men with whom I have had to deal have been those who imagined they were extra broad. 

True it is there are some men whose convictions are so deep they are painfully narrow. But there are some who are so broad they are pitifully thin and shallow.

The right sort of man is one whose convictions are deep and controlling, but whose sympathies are so broad and fraternal that his love reaches out to all men of every type. The fellow who believes what he believes, and who for that very reason has the utmost respect for the faith and convictions of all his neighbors, is my ideal. 

We have lots of folks who are deep and narrow; even more who are broad and shallow. Heaven send us men who are both deep and wide.

LIGHT SPOTS THE COLLEGE BOY 

I have been to all the principal zoos and menageries in this country and Europe, including the leading colleges of the nation, and have seen most all the animals inhabiting the earth. To me the most interesting of them all is the 

College Boy. 

You rejoice to see him coming as night watchers rejoice to see the coming day. You are happy when he is gone as thunder-shocked people are glad in the silence that follows the passing of a storm. He has made me glad when the very heavens seemed to weep. He has kept old age from me when the snows of winter have whitened my locks, and the wife of my bosom has thinned them out.

He has his likes and dislikes, accumulating both the one and the other by instinct rather than reason. He cannot be made to assume responsibility, and it is as easy to keep him down as it is to keep a cork under water. 

He often has what men call brains, and uses them when he can find time, which is rarely ever, for he is a mighty busy man. He loves to sing, and usually sings bass, which is perhaps as well as not, for he is a base singer even when he is warbling tenor. His music is noise to most people, but his noise is music to me. 

He loves his books as a dog loves fleas, and loves his girl with an undying devotion which has been known to last two weeks "hand-running."

His most prized possessions are a college-colored sweater, his frat pin, and the pictures of five or six girls.

CHRISTMAS WOES 

What a happy time is Christmas! What a pity it can come only once a year! 

What a horrible time is Christmas! What a pity that it should come at all!

We hear much these days about a man's freedom and liberties, his personal rights, and prerogatives. Who shall say what a man is to eat and drink? Aren't we free men, and can't we do as we please? 

Exactly, no.

SEEING TWO SIDES 

I often envy the man who can see only one side of a given question. He can reach his decisions so quickly and so certainly. He can act so promptly and so vigorously. 

He is never hesitant for fear he may be wrong, or from the thought that the other fellow may have some measure of truth in his contentions. He can line up with his party, quit thinking and blindly follow where his bosses lead. He is often most effective, for while other men wonder what to do, he, right or wrong, is doing with all his might. He is generally known as a loyal friend and as an avowed opponent. His mind acts in a single groove and he is not worried by conflicting impulses. 

But after all, there are two sides to most questions. 

The lover of truth is driven by his devotion to truth to seek all the truth. He must move slowly. He will undoubtedly see the force of his antagonist's argument, and see the good in his opponent's character. He may be accused of disloyalty by his partisan friends, he may be derided as lacking in courage because he would "put himself in the other man's place," but it is such men who lead to ultimate victory. 

Such, after all, is the most effective fighter because he understands the other man's viewpoint. He learns to separate between men and measures, and loves the one while he attacks the other.

The one-sided man may win battles, but the man who sees all wins campaigns, and sets the policies of nations.

It is easy and comfortable to see only one side. It is a difficult task, a hard duty to see all sides, but it is a duty none the less. The truth shall make you free.

The great bandsaws rip those big logs as if they were paper, but every three hours and twenty minutes they are taken off and pass through the saw filer's hand again.

A young lady known to me has just celebrated her 90th anniversary in the vicinity of her childhood home, surrounded by adoring friends, and relatives. And she "lorded it like a lady" among them, receiving their homage with gratitude, but withal as a matter of course and as her due. 

I said "young lady," for age is not counted by years, but by interest in life.

And she is keenly alive to everything around her. She dresses like a belle and devotes as much time to her mirror as any flapper you know. She has the latest political dope and knows what this candidate is doing and what the other has said. Last year she read and recommended to her preacher son a theological treatise on the "Deity of Jesus." 

Occasional novels and romances she commends to the jelly beans among her grandsons and delivers lectures to the flappers among her great-granddaughters on how the descendants of Southern blue blood should deport themselves in modern society.

In the ante-bellum days numerous slaves awaited her pleasure, but she held them more by the law of love than by the law of the land. When the fortunes of war set them free, they refused to leave her home. She outlived them all, but to their dying days among their chief joys was the sound of her gentle voice.

She went through the storm of war, losing her all, chief among those losses being her faith in God, who allowed a favorite brother to be torn well nigh in two by a cannon ball while defending Port Hudson below Vicksburg.

She never recovered her property, but she did regain

LIGHT SPOTS 

her faith, which shone forth all the brighter because of what she suffered.

She fought her way through the darker days of reconstruction without yielding her high soul, or bowing her queenly head. 'Twas then she became a tower of strength to her husband and the inspiration of her four young sons, saving the one from despair, and filling the others with ambitions whose limit was the sky. 

The strain of those terrible days sent her husband to an untimely grave, to which she would have followed but for the tender care of the wonderful young women who came to share the lives of her oldest and youngest sons. 

In them she found real deaughters. With a tenderness born of Heaven and touched by its beauty, they made it possible for her boys to carry out the purpose formed at the father's grave "she shall never carry another burden."

Set at leisure by this love, more tender than wise, maybe, she became the connecting link of widely scattered kin, and none carrying a drop of her blood in their veins could wander beyond the reach of her thought and the visit of her wonderful letters. 

The evening twilight has fallen across her eyes and her hand is "shaky" after the passage of these 90 years, but the writing, once smooth as print, is still legible, and she is still the "central" through whom four generations keep in touch each with the other. 

And so, heart filled with love and lips wreathed in smiles, she awaits the Master's final call. It will not be for her "Twilight and evening bell

NOT YET 

I had a delightful night's sleep last Saturday night, and awoke to find these words running through my mind as if someone was speaking to me: "You are not yet fitted for that Land. You would be out of place there now. You would be awkward and self-conscious among those people. 

You must wait."

I have for a long time hoped, if I have not planned, for a serene old age. A time in which there would be no driving task, no care of others, when there would be light burdens, no financial worries, not because we would be rich, but because we would have learned to be happy on little. A time when we could enjoy our friends and loved ones without the urge of duty and the hurry call of business. 

But somehow that day seems slow in coming. Cares multiply, work increases, and even needless worry keeps knocking at the door. "You are not yet fitted for That Land”—perhaps that is the reason. The life we live is one of discipline for a fuller life, not a picnic to be ended at close of day. 

We may find much joy in life. Surely I have found it. 

All life has been to me just one long, happy day's work. 

But it has come to me that life is not for mere enjoyment. 

Pleasure has its place, but it's not the chief thing in life. 

There are deeper things involved. There are duties to be performed. Purposes to be fulfilled. Discipline to be borne. Burdens to be carried. Responsibilities to be met. 

And it is all to "fit us for That Land." When we are fully prepared for the place which has been prepared for us, then

151 we will leave these scenes whose brightest aspect is but dull compared to that which awaits us. 

After all, there is something better than "serene old age."

THE PROBLEM OF PAIN 

We have read much in the papers about the meanings. of the Miami storm. Some friends who seem to know all about it declare that it was sent as a punishment upon a fearfully wicked city. Others, with a dogmatism quite equal to this, pour contempt upon such an idea, and assure us that no such thing is, or can be, true. Each side appears to be on quite intimate terms with the Eternal, and do not hesitate to tell us exactly what is in His mind. 

This whole problem of Pain is one of the unsolved mysteries of the race. It has puzzled the soul for ages, and has been the rock upon which the faith of thousands has gone to wreck. Why should anyone suffer? If God is good and almighty, why does He not end it? Why did he ever permit it to exist? We are ushered into the world by pain and pain draws the curtain over life's last scene. Why?

It behooves us all to eschew pride of opinion, and walk softly here.

It would be foolish to deny that pain is sometimes punitive. No matter what your theory about sin may be, there is no fact more certain than the fact of sin. The thing itself is no theory. It is here. 

And equally certain it is that sin always brings painits own punishment-its final penalty being death. "The wages of sin is death."

But we are just as sure that pain is not always punitive. Calamity is not always misfortune, as paradoxical as that may seem. If the fingers of pain close our eyes on the beauties of earth, they likewise open them to the glories

THE WORKER'S SHIELD 

Last week I was in the home of an overdriven, Sabbathless worker. With something of hopeless bitterness in his voice he asked: "Why don't you people who have the ear of the public do something to help us fellows who have to work every day without chance to rest, or see a bit of our kiddies?”

He had a right to complain if no one had said anything about it. A Sunday job does not mean simply seven days work. It means a thousand, ten thousand, if the worker lasts so long-which he is not likely to do. 

To begin with, when you plead for a seventh day rest, the workers themselves often oppose you. I recall an effort to secure this rest for a certain set of toilers which they themselves defeated. They were new at their work, and their nerves had not yet broken. The extra pay made them think they could violate a simple law of nature—of Godand get by with it. At the same time it made them oblivious to the sufferings of those who had been worn out by endless toil.

People who believe in and seek to protect the Sabbath are met with accusations of "fanaticism," of "puritanism," of "seeking to force men to be good," or "compelling church attendance by statute,"—and, oh, well, you know the whole line. 

What shallow thought! If there were no churches in the world, no God in Heaven, no Hereafter, the necessity of a Sabbath would be no less, but rather more. If this world is to be the last of us, then we need to make the most of it, and surely no "all-time worker" can get very much out of

LIGHT SPOTS 

life. We are so made that we must unbend now and then. 

One rest day per week, rightly spent, will enable a normal man to work indefinitely. Lacking that day, it is only a question of time, and unusually of short time, when efficiency is lowered, interest in life is gone, nerves are broken, and health is weakened or lost.

There are some sorts of work which our modern civilization requires on Sunday. Perhaps life would be impossible, certainly very difficult without them. These things must be carried on, and somebody must do the work. But they who must needs do these Sunday tasks should rest on some other day-one day in seven. Men can do more in six days than they can in seven, and all parties concerned will be better off if the workers take the day for rest.

Friend of mine put aside $100,000 in cash for the evening of life. At 72 years of age the whole pile vanished in one night and he did not have enough to buy bread. Bonds were put up by another, and when needed they were not worth the paper upon which they were printed. Bank stock was the hope of another, and the bank went to the bad. Another placed his hopes in some fine children, but disaster came, and he had to take care of those whom he expected to care for him. Another put his surplus in solid railroad securities, but a change in the habits of folks took the value out of his securities, and he is now in the poor house.

The best security against old age is plenty of good life insurance, but that is expensive, and has its own elements of risk. "Trust in the Lord and do good. So shalt thou dwell in the land and verily thou shalt be fed."

That alone is certain, and it costs nothing.

You grouch around home folks. You are in a good humor when company comes. You are a boor with your own wife and children. You scold the whole outfit. Maybe you cuss in their presence. The Bible nowhere argues or enters into any sort of discussion with an atheist. It dismisses him with a single terse sentence: "The fool hath said in his heart, 'There is no God'."

When we see the astounding intelligence displayed in all nature from atom to constellation, we know the Bible is right. With abounding evidence of a supernatural intelligence existing everywhere, we must conclude that the man must be a fool who does not see and acknowledge God.
