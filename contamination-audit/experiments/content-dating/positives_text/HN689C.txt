To the Pupil 1. Try to do each exercise neatly and accurately, and as quickly as possible, but remember that accuracy is more important than speed. 

2. Do not be discouraged if you find that you do not work as fast as others, for the main purpose to keep in mind is mastery of as many ideas and topics as possible. It is better to understand a reasonable number of fundamental ideas than to go superficially over a larger number. 

3. Individual work is honest work; do your work by yourself in order that you may obtain real practice in doing the important things that are expected of the welleducated citizen. 

4. Do as much work as possible mentally. Where there is sufficient space, put any necessary written work on the page with the test so that you can locate the sources of any errors. In certain exercises, where the computations are long, you may find it necessary to do some work on separate sheets; these sheets should be kept in this book until you have checked your work. In other exercises you will put all of your work in a separate loose-leaf mathematics notebook. 

5. After you have checked your work and have verified the number of "rights" according to the plan used by the teacher, enter your score in the proper place in the record-blank on the inside of the back cover.

ATHEMATICS is represented in the picture below as the base upon which the tree of knowledge rests. It is shown as the source of life for the roots and branches which represent most of the great fields of knowledge. As we study mathematics, we find that it is indeed the servant of the natural sciences such as physics, chemistry, geology, and astronomy, and that through it these fields have been able to thrive and develop. In short, mathematics applied to one field after another forms the central core of a pupil's modern mathematical education. 

As we proceed with our study, we find that the value of mathematics is not only greater but more apparent in some branches of knowledge than it is in others. 

No one would question the value of mathematics in its applications through some of its branches to the pursuits of the merchant or the engineer. He might question its value to the student of English, but even here its use can be shown. 

There are, similarly, other uses of mathematics that are little known to most people. Many of these will become apparent to pupils as they continue their study.

Someone has said that "the forages, many important discoveries and inventions - now taken for granted been made only because some Newton or Einstein paved the way by the establishment of certain mathematical laws or principles. other basic sciences are indebted to mathematics. mula-language of chemistry is a kind of shorthand based on mathematical principles." 

And biology could not have reached its present high rank as a science if its students had not had measurements and numerical methods with which to express its laws. 

Besides those upper branches of the tree of knowledge which are shown in the picture, there are others not depicted which also depend upon mathematics. Architecture, for example, is closely related to it. We know that the ancients had a high regard for the circle, and that it dominated the Romanesque style of the tenth and

eleventh centuries. This is demonstrated by the prominence of circular windows and arches in the architecture of that period. In Gothic architecture, in which the equilateral triangle was featured, there is another clear demonstration of its use. We also find that Leonardo da Vinci, a famous Italian painter, sculptor, architect, and engineer, based much of his work upon an arithmetic basis. Modern scholars say that architecture is frozen music. 

In the study of music, we find that mathematics plays a prominent part. It enters into the construction of intervals of the musical scale, and plays a necessary and an important role in matters of harmony and melody. 

In nature we find demonstrations of geometric figures. For example, quartz crystallizes in the form of a regular hexagonal prism capped by a pyramid; salt takes the form of a cube; and other substances take on these or other geometric forms. 

From the geometric forms assumed by these substances we can observe that there seems to have been a mathematical design in nature itself. This is illustrated by the fact that all of them follow the formula that F+VE +2, where the letters. 

F, V, and E stand, respectively, for the number of faces, vertices, and edges of the geometric solid.

David Eugene Smith has said, "Mathematics makes contributions to civilization so evident that we need hardly consider them. If we imagine, for a moment, what would happen tomorrow if every trace of mathematics were banished tonight from the regions of commerce, of transportation, and of daily life in general, the picture would be that of a desert of starving humanity; no medium of exchange, no buying the needs of life, no measuring of time or of objects, no machinery for light or heat, no transportation, not even the simplest barter that requires counting. The picture is too impossible for us to conceive, save in some slight degree." 

Thus we find that whether everybody studies mathematics or not, there must always be a reasonable number of people who devote themselves to its advancement if the needs of today and of future generations are to be met. Upon that basis many pupils will find their study of the fundamentals of mathematics presented here will serve them after they have gone out into the world of work.

To the Teacher 

THE MATERIAL in this workbook may be used either as teaching or as testing material. If the pupils have not yet studied the content with which the exercises are concerned, the teacher may regard the various pages as teaching devices. If, however, the pupils have been studying similar material, then the exercises may be used as diagnostic (or achievement) tests to reveal the strength or weakness of each pupil with reference to the items tested. 

When the exercises are used for purposes of diagnosis, their main value is that the results obtained point with a fair degree of certainty to the causes of individual difficulties, and thus give the teacher valuable assistance in improving his instruction a much more valid aim of modern testing practice than the standardization of pupils' accomplishments for purposes of comparison with those of other school systems. 

The first part of the book is devoted to those parts of Algebra which the welleducated citizen should know and be able to apply in his daily life, whenever the need occurs. Obsolete processes such, for instance, as some cases of factoring- are omitted from this book; those processes that are included are kept as simple as possible.

The exercises and tests in algebra cover adequately the essential principles the pupil must master as a basis for all his later work in algebra. Moreover, they are presented in abundance and with much repetition, in order that the pupil may develop desirable habits in algebraic computation. 

The work in algebra comprises approximately the first two-thirds of the book. 

Following this, there is a study of Numerical Trigonometry from which the pupil acquires some idea of indirect measurement. 

The last part of the book is devoted to exercises on Demonstrative Geometry; thus, before the pupil leaves the ninth grade he is given an opportunity to learn what it means actually to develop a geometric proof. This work, however, like that in the study of algebra earlier in the book, is kept simple. Its grasp is made easier by relating it, as clearly as possible, to the informal geometry given in the two previous years.

In this book there is no section devoted to arithmetic as such; throughout the book, however, as the result of careful correlation of the various phases of the subject, drill in arithmetical processes is constantly afforded by the necessity for computation.
