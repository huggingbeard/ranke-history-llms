I. INTRODUCTION 

Pursuant to Senate Joint Resolution No. 21, a three-day hearing was held in Los Angeles during the constitutional recess of 1957 by the Senate Interim Committee on Collection Agencies, Private Detectives, and Debt Liquidators. Information of abuses previously uncovered was further investigated and suggestions for remedial legislation received. 

Prior Senate interim committees on the licensing of business and professions had delved into the problems concerning the establishment of a separate regulatory or advisory board to administer the Private Investigator and Adjuster Act. The present committee has focused its attention on violations of constitutional rights of private citizens and other unethical practices by private investigators, collection agencies, and the proraters.

While the hearing involved scandal-type magazines, this was actually a side issue growing out of the committee's major study, but it surely attracted much more attention. The problem of what can be done to get such publications out of interstate commerce, or out of the retail outlets within this State, is a big one. Their regulation is outside the scope of this committee's investigation, except insofar as private detectives are used either to verify or obtain information for them. 

The committee's recent investigation established that there are some definite abuses being practiced by a small but active minority of private investigators, collection agencies, and proraters. Our work has only scratched the surface of the tremendous task to be done. Yet it turned up many, many leads that should be followed through in order to do a good job for the people who have written the committee from all over California asking us to give them protection from the practices we were authorized to study.

We feel a great obligation to do a complete investigation for these many citizens who have asked for assistance. We are gratified by the great interest shown and by the fact that the attorney generals of various states have told us they are watching our work closely because they regard it as a pilot study in this field. There are many ramifications and much work to be done. The scope of the three-day hearing was quite obviously limited by factors of time and by the desire of this committee to insure that immunity from criminal prosecution by our examination would not interfere with or prevent any vigorous prosecution by law enforcement agencies. A satisfying result of these hearings has been the fact that in the months since this committee has been in existence and attention has been focused on the problem by many press releases, an unheralded number of proraters, collection agencies, and private detectives have closed their doors. II. PRIVATE INVESTIGATORS 

As with any state which has a large and rapidly growing population, there has been an influx of persons desirous to prey upon the innocent and the ignorant. The State of California is also the center of the motion picture industry and a sizable portion of the television industry. Noted personalities are, therefore, our citizens, and they furnish another source for extortion and invasion of the right of privacy. The 

Private Investigators and Adjusters Act has not had any major revision in the past five years. The times have changed, and so have the problems, abuses, and ethics of this profession. 

The major deficiency in the present law relates to the lack of mandatory licensing or registration of the employees of detective agencies. 

The investigation business is no longer a one-man operation. It is true that the present law indirectly regulates employees of detective agencies, but it is submitted that this is inadequate and unworkable. The licensee, under Sections 7551 and 7553 of the Business and Professions Code, is liable for the past and future conduct of his agents and employees. But, as to past conduct, the employer is only responsible if he "knowingly employed" a person convicted of a felony or any crime involving moral turpitude, or who has had a license denied, revoked, or suspended under the act. The licensee can always plead that he did not know. Since the employer has limited access to official fingerprint files and records he has no way of knowing the past history of a prospective employee except by word of mouth from other licensees. And the very difficult burden of proof in all such cases is on the Director of the Department of Professional and Vocational Standards. 

As to future or present conduct, the workability of the act is no less satisfactory. If a stupid, criminally inclined, or inadequately trained employee breaks open the "wrong door" or makes an illegal entry, license suspension for two or three months is not only insufficient punishment but also is locking the barn door after the horse has gone. Since the bureau has no record of those who may be working for a licensee, there is no adequate supervision and control. Furthermore, the necessity of a hearing makes the whole procedure rather burdensome and unrealistic. The obvious solution is some sort of screening before an employee is allowed to operate under a main license. More rigorous enforcement by allowing the director additional personnel would reduce the evil somewhat, but a more effective solution would appear to lie in the direction of a prior examination of persons whom a private investigator employs. This would include fingerprinting, photographs, and a statement as to prior experience and relevant information. Specific methods and administration are in need of further study. Added costs could be recovered by requiring a small license or registration fee from each such employee. An added benefit of this system of regulation would be that a prospective employer would have a list of qualified personnel on his desk from which to select a permanent or temporary ( 6 )

While the committee is aware of the good work being done by the vast majority of private investigators, those who operate on the fringe of respectability must be eliminated or controlled. Some of the agencies do need cleaning up without penalizing the reputable private detective agencies. Not only should the owner or operator of an agency be fingerprinted and his fingerprints cleared through the Bureau of Criminal Identification and Investigation prior to the issuance of a detective license, but also all persons connected with or employed by the detective agency, in any capacity, should be fingerprinted and the fingerprints cleared through the bureau. This would not seem to be too much to insist upon when, at the present time, every person who applies for a credential to teach in California must be fingerprinted and the fingerprints submitted to the Credential Division of the State Department of Education, which, in turn, submits them to the Bureau of Criminal Identification for searching and filing.

Another corrective measure to control the presence of undesirable persons within this profession would be to increase the amount of bond required for a licensee, and to require the bonding of all employees. 

Further safeguards could be secured by requiring a previous background or training in the law enforcement field of all employees of detective agencies.

The use of identification badges which are deliberately designed to resemble as closely as possible those of our public law enforcement agencies is another major problem in this area. These badges are used to coerce people into giving information which they would not do, if they knew the truth. Not only are badges used, but also identification cards which are so designed as to give the impression that the bearer is a member of the Federal Bureau of Investigation or a local law enforcement agent. Another aspect of illegal entry involves the use of electronic devices for the interception of conversations or messages by licensees or employees of licensees. Section 640 of the Penal Code defines wiretapping as a criminal offense and makes the licensee subject to disciplinary action and revocation or suspension of his license. But where plugs, blonders, or transmitters are used, if two persons are in the state of separating, or one is trying to obtain evidence, the premises entered are usually community property and the private investigator enters with the consent of the person in legal possession. Corrective legislation is presently before the Legislature. Senate Bill No. 929, Regan, would require that private investigators file yearly reports of all instances in which they have knowledge of the use of electronic devices for eavesdropping. Senate Bill No. 240, Richards, would provide for civil damages up to $500 against persons illegally recording conversations.

Much more remains to be done, however, in this problem of illegal entry. The committee vigorously investigated the famous "wrong door" raid on Marilyn Monroe and is appalled by the lack of competence and questionable tactics surrounding this incident. Civil, penal, and disciplinary penalties should be increased in order to deter this kind of activity.

The committee also investigated the question of whether Section 7538 of the Business and Professions Code is adequate from the standpoint of the limitation on the private investigator relative to the divulging of any information that he may acquire in his employment. The committee is satisfied that a definite tieup between some private detective agencies and the scandal and exposé magazines does exist. Since the magazines or their middlemen are clients of the detective agency, the furnishing or verifying of information does not come under the prohibitions of Section 7538. The committee believes that the privilege of holding a license in this business should carry with it the moral obligation to serve the public in an honest manner. To spy on Hollywood celebrities in an arbitrary manner for the express purpose of furnishing material and photographs for scandal magazines is an abuse of the privilege to hold an investigator's license. The Legislature should consider the passage of a new section making it unlawful for any licensee to accept employment for verifying, or the sale of, information of a scandalous nature to such magazines. The problems are tremendous. Many times the investigator is not told the nature or purpose of his investigation and isolated bits of information seem innocuous. An effective law should be drafted to protect the right of privacy of our citizens and not at the same time to hamper the freedom of the press.

III. COLLECTION AGENCIES 

While the committee was formed chiefly to investigate certain methods used by collection agencies which had come to our attention, as we progressed we found that these agencies employed so-called private detectives and others to harass people owing money and that they resorted to tactics which are very questionable. Certain collection agencies have been using strong-arm and other ruthless methods to force people into paying their bills. Also, it appeared that when the citizens tried to stand on their rights, additional excessive charges are placed on the bills so that it is almost impossible for them ever to get out of debt. During the hearings before the committee it was revealed that strong-arm bill collection methods were being used in Los Angeles. 

A private detective likened the collection system in Southern California to "Chicago at its worst".

Evidence was obtained by the committee which illustrated the dual connection between collection agencies and private detectives, especially in connection with the repossession of automobiles through nefarious means. Mr. Callahan was examined by Senator Dolwig on this point as follows:

IV. DEBT LIQUIDATORS 

Debt adjusters are individuals or companies engaged in the business of prorating the income of a debtor to his creditors for a fee or service charge. Ideally, the service they render to debt laden members of society would include setting up a budget on a workable basis. It would allocate a definite amount of income to debt retirement purposes, including payment of the adjuster's fee as defined by contract. The adjuster would then prepare a plan for distributing the available income periodically to the various creditors on a pro rata basis. This would usually require obtaining substantial concessions from some creditors. However, if satisfactory arrangements could be effected, the debtor would make regular payment of the total amount budgeted for debt retirement to the adjuster who would then disburse it to himself and the creditors in accordance with the agreed formula. The ultimate objective would be the final emancipation of the family from its debts and the re-establishment of its credit. 

While this type of business is not a new one, the number of those engaged in it has multiplied, and the geographic scope of their operations has enlarged at a prodigious rate. Those who have swarmed into the debt adjustment field recently have included a large proportion of unscrupulous or incompetent opportunists. In the Los Angeles area, however, the committee discovered a definite decrease due to mergers and consolidations and voluntary quitting of business because of adverse publicity which has attached to this industry in the past several years. The newspapers and better business bureaus have done a good job in educating the general public as to the dangerous methods of these operators. And the debt liquidators themselves seem to be setting their own house in order. Those who were on the fringe as far as their operations were concerned are going out of business, and those remaining seem to be pretty much on the up and up. Proper legislation will eliminate "racketeers and quacks" from the field and preserve a needed service.

The committees received considerable evidence of abuses within a minority of these organizations. They have used extravagant and deceptive advertising to claim far more than they were in a position to deliver. Many complaints have their inception in printed or broadcast advertising claims such as "Forget Your Debts," "Rid yourself of the worries and troubles of all your creditors, and similar representations calculated falsely to imply that, once the debt adjuster is employed, the debtor has no further responsibility or obligation to his creditors. Some advertise consolidations of all debts into only one payment, but true consolidations are rare if not nonexistent. Other advertising has misled debtors into believing that they can get a loan or credit which the prorater will use to pay off all their debts. 

Some have withheld their own fees from the debtors' payments but have failed promptly to make agreed payments to creditors or to obtain creditors' accession to the pro rata plan devised. Led to believe that ( 13 )

There is no uniformity in the amount of fees charged by debt adjusters or in the method of their exaction. The same company may charge different rates to different clients. The fees are often exorbitant and unregulated as well as considerably more than what the prospective client anticipated. Many times a husband and wife will call upon a debt liquidator merely to have the plan of business explained. There is no contract and no hiring: but they get statements for charges, for calling at the offices, when no service has been performed. Advertising would lead them to believe that the consultations were free. If the client fails to pay, the debt adjuster may institute legal proceedings to collect, or as is more common, he takes a note for his fees, which notes sometimes get into the hands of innocent third parties who assert them, even though the prorater has never accomplished any benefit for the debtor.

Another problem discovered by the committee emanates from the fact that there is an exemption in the "Check Seller's and Casher's Act" which permits in effect the operation in the prorating field under a collection agency license. This dual operation of debt liquidators and collection agencies was found under the same roof and with identical personnel. The prorater acquires a list of the client's creditors together with amounts which are due: this list eventually finds its way into the hands of the collection side of the business. The latter then call the creditor and solicit the account. While some of the proraters ask the debtor to give them written permission to turn the information over to a collection agency for solicitation, there is a definite conflict of interest of which the debtor is not aware. The prorater will give priority to those debts for which he is also the collection agent. Collection agencies cannot serve two masters; that is, the operators cannot represent the debtor public and, at the same time, represent the creditor public, thus handling the collection of claims against the same debtor for whom they are trying to prorate. It is the committee's belief that a person licensed as a collection agency should not practice as a prorater, as his relationship to the creditors whose accounts he is handling would not permit him to be fair to all of the other creditors and still perform his duties to his own clients. Likewise, proraters should not be licensed as collection agencies.

Another aspect brought out in the preceding testimony is the illegal practice of law by the prorating organizations. While they do not inquire into whether a claimed obligation is a just and proper one, such legal services are necessary to make any plan effective. The prorater must contact each and every creditor of the debtor and obtain from him a valid enforceable agreement to accept a prorated sum in order to make the plan workable. A verbal promise is not sufficient. It must be in writing to be effective and is known as a composition agreement among creditors. If any one creditor refuses to join, the plan is destroyed and the debtor receives no relief. If an attachment is run against the person, the debt adjuster must know and advise the debtor of his rights to obtain a release through the medium of an affidavit. 

This must be prepared and filed to obtain a release of the attachment, if the debt is not for necessities of life. 

While the State Bar of California believes that to be effective the debt adjuster must practice law, which he is forbidden to do, your committee believes that there is a legitimate field in which the prorater can operate under adequate regulation and supervision. The bad practices of a minority are not so overwhelming that legislation to outlaw the business of debt adjusting in California is necessary. Mr. Morris 

Rabinowitch, representing the Financial Counselors of San Francisco, had the following recommendations for corrective legislation : “I would like to recommend that no prorater be allowed to receive any fee until he can definitely substantiate the fact that he can get the arrangements with the creditors. Number two, that the prorater's fee not be allowed to be taken in advance or any part of it, and only on a pro rata basis that it is distributed to creditors. Third, that no notes or negotiable instruments be taken. 

Fourth, that a copy of any and all agreements be given to the individual at the time of signing, and one other thing is that once the man does sign, it should not become effective until after he has made a payment, because a great number of people will come in and sign the agreement at the interview, and will not return, because they have changed their mind."

(1) One of the most sanguine evils perpetrated by the unscrupulous and predatory operators in this industry is their false, misleading and "bait" advertising. Another provision which appears in all three of the bills is the compensation fee if a contract is terminated. No provision, however, is made where due to the fault of the prorater, a contract is canceled, and it would appear that even though the prorater is at fault, he might still collect his 7 percent of the unpaid obligations. This does not appear to be in accord with the usual principles of law, and might well lead to abuse in many instances. Another provision in the three bills being introduced by Assemblymen Weinberger, Wilson, and Senator Miller, is the provision as to the amount that must be paid out in a given period. We believe that some limits should be placed on accumulations; while the representatives of agencies argue that this provision is contained in the bill, in that the prorater can receive no compensation until the funds have been disbursed, it does leave the situation where creditors may not receive the funds that are due them within a reasonable time, and by accumulating 50 percent of the funds received within the 30-day period, a considerable amount might be in the reserve account at the end of a six months' period.

The provision in Assembly Bill No. 1398 and Assembly Bill No. 2156 

as to the percentage of creditors from whom consent must be obtained, in our estimation is too low, and that a more reasonable figure would be 663 percent. We give this as a thought for your committee to consider, in arriving at some legislation.

Also, may we point out that while the practice of law is defined or the prorater is forbidden to enter into the practice of law, we have a situation under which a great deal of abuse might arise, in that they may list claims that the debtor disputes, and handle them as an ordinary claim, and a situation will probably exist where it would be very difficult, or impossible, to prove whether or not the State Bar Act had been violated. Yet, by arranging for the payment of questionable obligations, the debtor has ratified the same and waived all defenses that he may have if the matter should come up in the future. 

As to Assembly Bill No. 253, I believe that while six states have somewhat similar laws, that the over-all problem is not particularly benefited; that debtors will lose jobs, employers will have a constant problem of harassment on attachments, and executions, changing personnel with the attendant expense; and more and more of the debtors will look to the bankruptcy courts for relief.

I should like to take a moment to discuss proceedings under Chapter 13 of the Bankruptcy Act, which is commonly known as the Wage Earners Plan. From the time of its enactment to the present time, we have seen few cases in which the act would actually have a beneficial effect. The proceedings are expensive, and since it appears that a vast majority of the bankruptcies that are filed would indicate that the obligations average approximately $1,500 the attendant expense of proceedings under Chapter 13 would add approximately another $500 to the obligation the debtor has to pay. 

The situation has not worked out too well, to my knowledge, in any area in which it has been tried over a period of time. This office has not been averse to using the proceeding, but we have found that with few exceptions we could work the problem out by agreement among the creditors and the debtor, with much less difficulty for both. My personal experience is that where Chapter 13 proceedings are considered, we can as readily work out the program on the telephone, without filing under the section.

As to Assembly Bill No. 3193, which appears to be a spot bill, we are not in a position to make much comment, except in a general manner. We also understand that Senate Bill No. 190 provides for a court supervision bill. This, of course, may be the answer to the problem, if the act has sufficient teeth and protections to both the creditor and the debtor, so that the secured creditors' security is protected and the debtor will be permitted to work without threat of loss of his job, and so that he can make the payments as provided for in the arrangement. 

We are not in a position to say at this time whether such an act would be economically sound and possible. However, if it were geared to the low-income family with obligations over $1,000 to $1,500 it would appear to us that there is a definite need for such an act. It takes care of the weaknesses under the debt adjuster or prorate plan, and yet it can provide a means of preventing bankruptcy.

V. CONCLUSION 

As stated previously, the committee feels that it has only scratched the surface in uncovering the unethical and unprincipled activities in these three related fields of private investigators, collection agencies, and debt liquidators. The response of the general public and of the 

Members of the Legislature has been of great encouragement to us. 

Such overwhelming support further establishes the necessity for additional investigation. Such a study will insure that the citizens of California will be protected in the future from unscrupulous operators.
