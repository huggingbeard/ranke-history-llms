You will be needing to spell more and more new words as you go on reading and writing. spell some of them wrong the first time. us spell words wrong now and then, but good spellers try not to miss the same word twice. 

Five steps will help you with words you spell wrong. Read them carefully. Here they are:

STEP ONE 

See just how the word looks.

Look at the word well so you will know just how it looks and just what letters are in it. 

Then, if you don't know what it means, look in your dictionary and find out. Do it now. iii

STEP TWO 

Say the word and hear just how it sounds. 

Say the word carefully without looking at it, and listen well. If it has more than one syllable, listen for them. Then look at the word again to be sure you are right.

STEP THREE 

Look away and try to remember just how the word looks. 

Say the word softly if you need to. Then say each letter in it. Then look at the word again to be sure you are right.

STEP FOUR 

Copy the word on paper just as it is spelled in your book. 

Use all the time you need to be sure it is right.

STEP FIVE 

Write the word on paper without looking at your book.

Try to write all the letters in their right order. 

Then look at the word again to be sure you are right. 

Always use these five steps to help you if you miss a word the first time. iv

Life in an early settlement in our country had to depend upon the strength of the community spirit. The rule was to divide the labor and work for the profit of the entire group. 8. INVENTIONS THAT HAVE CHANGED THE WORLD A. WRITING WORDS 1. This is your Study List. Say these new words with your teacher. Then find them in the story. forever forgotten forced following commence conversation drugs difference steel simply destroy suggest midnight telegraph arrange lock practice item 

For language practice today we invented a conversation game called WHAT IF? It's easy to arrange. You commence in the following way: 

One person thinks of a well-known invention. 

Then he simply asks such a question as, "What if all the world's printing presses were forced to stop forever?" The other players try to suggest the difference we should feel without the item just mentioned. 

Some of the best questions were: What if by next week we have forgotten how to make steel? 

What if all the telephone and telegraph wires were cut until midnight tonight? What if all stores in which drugs are sold should lock their doors every other day? What if something should destroy every clock on earth?

HOW TO USE THE DICTIONARY 

Important helps for using a dictionary are usually found in an introduction or front matter.

For example, the pages numbered vii-ix in Webster's A Dictionary for Boys and Girls explain things more fully than the following items. 

1. Vocabulary Entry. Each vocabulary entry of this dictionary is printed in large heavy-faced type. If it has more than one syllable, the division of syllables and the accent marks are shown, as in bra.va'do. 

2. Pronunciation. The pronunciation is shown by the respelling, in parentheses, following the vocabulary entry. Key words to show the meaning of the diacritical marks are given at the foot of the left-hand pages. 

3. Part of Speech. The abbreviation for the part of speech comes after the pronunciation. The meaning of each abbreviation is shown at the foot of the right-hand pages. 

4. Other Forms of the Vocabulary Entry. Words printed in small capital letters show other forms of a vocabulary entry; for example, of bravado and break. 

5. Definitions. When more than one definition of a vocabulary entry is given, they are numbered separately. 

How many do you find for the verb break, and how many for the noun break? 

6. Illustrative Examples. An example is sometimes given with a definition, to show how the word may be used in expressing an idea. For example, under brand find "as, a good brand of flour." 

7. Run-on Entries. When a vocabulary entry may be used as more than one part of speech, a heavy dash may be used instead of repeating the entry. These and other extra entries are given sometimes with and sometimes without definitions. They are called run-on entries.

We are learning to know the dictionary better and better. Some dictionary facts we know might be useful out of school as well as in it. Let's see how our dictionary helps may work for us outside of school.

TAKING THE DICTIONARY OUT OF SCHOOL 

1. Spelling. We learn words in reading and in hearing others talk, as well as in our spelling. Words aren't always spelled right in newspapers and signs. But the right spelling is always in the dictionary. 

2. Alphabetical Order. In the dictionary each word is put in its right alphabetical place among other words. 

To find words quickly, we must know alphabetical order.

We must know alphabetical order also if we want to use the telephone, because names are listed that way in the telephone book. 

3. Syllables and Accent Marks. The dictionary divides words and marks the syllables for writing or speaking.

When we write letters, we may need to divide words. 

Perhaps we read stories to other people. If we need help, it's in the dictionary. 

4. Word Meanings. The more we talk and listen and read and write, the more words we meet. Sometimes they are old, sometimes new. We know now that we can find them all in the dictionary.

We spend lots of time in school. But think of all the big and little vacations. We're always talking and listening, and we can't always stop a speaker to ask what he means. But we can always ask the dictionary.
