Why the Story Is Retold 

Almost fifteen years have passed since the 

Church Pension Fund was founded. A new gen eration has entered the service and activities of the 

Church. 

It is time therefore that the story of its beginnings, the statement of its principles and the record of its growth be told again. Hence this little book. Those who wish a fuller statement will find it in my Annual Report of 1930. 

The story is a happy one and I hope that I have made it clear in the telling. 

WILLIAM LAWRENCE

The Story of the Pension Fund Retold for a New Generation by 

Bishop Lawrence 

'IFTEEN YEARS ago when an aged clergyman without private means retired from active service he automatically became an applicant for charitable aid, really a beggar. He had to ask a charitable society supported by contributions in the churches to give him help, and he usually received a promise of from $50 to $400 a year: no more. The widows of clergymen were treated in the same way only with smaller figures. The Church papers had pathetic appeals in behalf of these societies; the stories of the poverty of those who had given their lives in service were often heart rending. Rectors in the prime of life with wives and children felt a chill as they looked forward to old age and their courage drooped. The dignity and efficiency of the clergy were impaired. DETERMINATION that the Church should do something to correct this evil resulted in the appointment by the General Convention of 1910 of a Commission to study the subject. The next Convention, 1913, having received a careful report based upon a wide study of pensions, resolved that "The Church should work towards the adoption of one pension system cover- Page 5

ing the whole Church" and outlined its principles, subject to the favourable action of the Dioceses. Supported by this, the Commission went ahead and formulated a definite system. After two years of study and discussion by the Diocesan Conventions the system was unanimously adopted, and after further exhaustive discussion was put into the Canons of the Church by the Conven tion of 1916. 

A charter was obtained from the State of New York authorizing a pension system for the clergy and their families. At its own request the Fund was made subject to inspection by the State Insurance Department of New York and is liable to have its slightest action inconsistent with the charter or sound actuarial principles censored and corrected.

No legislation of the Church has ever had more careful study on the part of the deputies to the General Convention, of the delegates to the Diocesan Conventions and of the clergy and laity of the Church than did the Pension Fund. Built upon the advice of leading actuaries and financiers this pension system stands by the unanimous approval of the Church; and is wrought into her canons as a part of her law. For over fourteen years it has had the support of the whole Church. 

HAT motives had the Church in adopting the W pension system? Many people would answer "Pity for the old parson"; "Sympathy with the lonely Page 6

widow". True. Others would say "The dignity of the

Church, for the old conditions were a disgrace". True again. Others, "The salaries were so small that no man could save up for old age, hence the Church by a Pension Fund should do the saving". Also true. Others, "The efficiency of the Church demanded it. Old and decrepit men were filling the pulpits and holding on tight because they could not resign and fall into abject poverty, while the young men were held back from promotion and from entering the larger fields of service. Moreover the certainty of some support in old age, some income for his widow in case of his early death, always gives hope, strength and enthusiasm to a man. in the stress of active work."

Fifteen or twenty years ago the Church people were thinking out the pension idea somewhat in this way. "If we wait for a man to get old and then collect the money to support him we are compelling the younger men and women of the Church to put out money in support of those who have finished their life work; we are paying for past service. The Board of Missions in its desire to support the retired missionaries through life is piling up obligations to support old missionaries and thus laying a heavy fixed charge upon the active missionary work, a 'dead hand' is being laid upon the offerings of the Church which are intended for living work. We must have a 'pay as you go' policy."

go' policy be carried out?" The answer was "Suppose we assume that the average clergyman after some forty years of service having retired on account of old age will live for five, ten, or twenty years longer, who is going to support him during that time? Who is going to support the widow of a rector who dies at thirty-five or forty? The charity method has failed to give them support and moreover we do not want to have our clergy and widows dependent on charity. What then? Why not let every parish, while its rector is young and vigorous do its part towards seeing him through to the end of his life; let each vestry when it pays its rector's monthly salary, set aside an amount which, put at compound interest, will see that rector through life to the grave? If all parishes will combine on this plan and will all send their share according to the salary to a central deposit place, to go on compound interest, then if the figures are worked out with the exactitude of actuarial science there will be enough to meet the pension demands. It will be a happy day when our aged pastors upon retirement receive not charity, but an allowance to which they have a claim for services rendered and paid in from month to month by their successive parishes through their years of active service."

This sounds so sensible now that we can hardly realize what a long and hard fight it cost to convince a minority of the Church of the advantages of the system. Page 8

THERE ARE TWO ESSENTIALS OF A PENSION SYSTEM 

I. Certainty. Whether the pension be small or large the young man who looks forward to it, plans for it, is promised it, has a right to that assurance throughout his active years and upon his retirement must receive it, not as a favour or a charity, but as a right. 

And to gain this certainty the construction of the system must be as firm and reliable as actuarial science can make it; it must be so planned that the money for the pension is assured before the payments begin to be made. System after system has failed because of a looseness of actuarial work or of kindly and apparently generous practices; and the failure of a Pension System is worse than that of a Savings Bank: it breaks faith with thousands and the evil effect goes down through the generations.

II. Hence it follows that the amount of outgo and of income must be balanced in advance. And this assumes that the administrators of the Fund will stand rigidly to the principles and actuarial terms of the Fund as adopted. When the Church has created a system its directors must without fear or favour administer the system. To give them the liberty to make exceptions in behalf of pathetic cases or special groups is to endanger the system. In the case of pensions the Church had the choice between charity with personal favour or Page 9

a system with definiteness of action: it selected the definite system: and although the hearts of the administrators may be moved by sympathy and a desire to make exceptions, their sense of responsibility to the Church holds them true to their duty. The administrators of the Church Pension Fund have been pressed again and again to yield to pathetic appeals and exceptional cases, but with all sympathy they have consistently stood by the orders given them by the Church in the adoption of the system.

In order that these conditions might be met in advance leading actuaries worked out from actuarial history and an exact record of the vital statistics of the clergy of the Church a sound system; and in all decisions bearing upon figures they are still consulted, and their decisions are final. 

WHAT IS THE SYSTEM? 

A parish, or Church organization when paying a clergyman's salary sends also to the Church Pension Fund an amount equal to 72 per cent. of the salary, including value of rectory, which at compound interest will secure him his appropriate pension: for instance, the clergyman may die young and his widow survive for half a century, or he may be one of the retired clergy who live to extreme old age. Each man's own parish will not necessarily supply his own pension, but taking all of the parishes and Church organizations together, Page 10

the per cent.

payments balance all of the pensions promised. 

The whole Church entering into this compact and continuing in it is assured by its actuarial work that the total of all these inflowing streams will balance the outflowing streams as these are demanded by the pension promises.

The system being accepted by the whole Church and dependent in its planning upon the active cooperation of the Church assumes by canon that every parish and clergyman is bound to support it. Upon this loyal and unanimous support the soundness of the system depends. The members of the Church are not individuals acting each for his own personal benefit, but they form a mutually depending body, clergy and laity, a great group, a Church. 

WHAT ARE THE PENSIONS? 

In planning for them the Church made a glorious venture of faith, and although fourteen years have passed we are still living in these years of faith. 

In the adoption of the system the Church resolved that she would have for her working ideal the following, roughly expressed:

All clergymen could retire at the age of sixty-eight. 

A man retiring would receive a pension equal to one half his average salary; and upon his death his widow would receive half that sum.

Page 11

A clergyman totally and permanently disabled would receive forty per cent. of his average salary for the last five years of service.

The widow of an active clergyman would receive half of the pension he had earned at the time of his death, with a separate sum for each minor orphan according to age.

If all the clergy had been twenty-eight years of age at the start of the system, these pensions could have been paid, but as the clergy were of all ages at the start and as no assessments had yet been made, or paid for them, there was not money enough in the treasury to start the system at that pace. Hence the Church had to be content with planning at first for very small pensions and build up a system which in time would reach its practical ideal. 

Ttem was in this form: he challenge to the Church by the pension sys- Inasmuch as the clergy are of all ages and as no assessment has been paid for any of them up to the starting of the system a sum large enough to meet these accrued liabilities must be raised before even a beginning of the system can be made: otherwise failure in the course of years is certain. That sum is five million and sixty-four thousand dollars. When that sum has been raised the Church can start its system in a modest way and gradually, with sacrifice and patience, work up to Page 12

its ideal. The pension promised for a full service will be but $600: the widow's $300. Then with firmness of administration, wisdom of action and the loyal support of the whole Church through the payment of the assessments of 72 per cent. the pensions may be gradually, very gradually increased. The $5,064,000 will be exhausted in time by the payment of accrued liabilities while the assessments pouring in will build up a far greater sum to meet the far greater payments of the coming pensions. 

This challenge was met.

No sooner had the majority of Dioceses confirmed the action of the General Convention than the administrators of the system entered upon a campaign for the five million and sixty-four thousand dollars. The fourteen months from January 1, 1916 to March 1, 1917 were given to the raising of the sum: they were months of intense education in an intricate subject, exhilarating enthusiasm, fine loyalty and glad sacrifice. These were the months just before the War: before the country had accustomed itself to large figures by the Liberty Loans; before modern campaign methods had been developed or modern propaganda invented: it was the largest sum ever undertaken to be raised at one time for any religious purpose.

The first note struck was the statement appearing one Monday morning in a great majority of the newspapers of the whole country that in the Episcopal Page 13

Church, perhaps the richest Church in the land, the average salary of its clergy was only $1,200: and that without pensions. Two subscriptions of $250,000 each were made by two generous women, nine of $100,000: then $50,000 and then the smaller figures: the interest deepened, the enthusiasm spread and as the weeks passed, Diocese after Diocese, parish after parish, men, women, and children claimed the privilege of giving: until on the last day set, March 1, 1917, one month before we entered the War, the goal of $5,064,000 had been reached: and within a few weeks the total gifts and subscriptions had reached $8,850,000. 

Meanwhile upon the same day, March 1st, the system began its operations and over 4,000 clergy, their wives and children were assured of pensions, which though small were certain. Distant missionaries as well as rectors near by felt a fresh thrill of devotion and the laymen and women of the Church, grateful for this beginning, set their hand towards the permanent support of pensions. It was a new day for the Church, for the dignity and efficiency of the clergy and for their leadership of the people.

With the system once started thousands of rivulets of assessments began to pour into the Church Pension Fund treasury and were taken in charge by the Trustees and invested.

The amount handled this past year is almost fifteen times that of the last year of the charity era. Under the Page 14

charity system the beneficiaries being dependent upon charity were unwilling to have their names made public: under the pension system the name of the first clergyman to retire, who happened to be a bishop, was placed at the head of the roll of honourable service: and since then the name of every pensioner has been published upon the roll of honour.

The gift of $8,850,000 was a great event, a token of the support of the Church. A far greater token, however, is that during the fourteen years over ninety-nine and a half per cent. of all the pension assessments which could have been received from the parishes and official organizations, have been received. Even in this troubled year 1931 the pension assessments ($1,175,000) exceed by $15,000 the receipts for any previous year. 

Until the creation of the Church Pension Fund no voluntary pension system had ever succeeded: the skill of Mr. Sayre in the invention and construction of the system gave a basis: then loyalty to the Church on the part of everyone and appreciation of the value of consecrated service have secured this one hundred per cent. result. 

We have had the sincere praise of being followed, in very close parallel, by the pension system of the Presbyterian Church, U. S. A., whose system began just ten years after our own. Other churches are taking action along the same lines.

TH HE TRUSTEES, elected by the General Convention, meeting once a year, place full power of administration in the hands of an executive committee who meet monthly except in summer. These men, accustomed to large responsibilities, take their duties for the Fund seriously; without their action the office makes no decisions.

The Executive Committee being in honour bound to carry out the contract of the Fund with the Church and beneficiaries stands firmly for consistent action, often against the personal sympathies of its members. For such action they are at times sharply criticised, but their duty is to keep before them the rights of the whole body of the clergy of the present and future even though apparent injustice is done to individuals. 

For instance: if a clergyman is helpless for months, and his physician says truly that he is totally disabled, the Fund is unable to give him a pension until a physician of the Fund (and our Medical Department is of the highest professional standing) advises also that the man's disability is of a permanent nature. The situation is pathetic, but the Fund's solvency depends upon standing by its actuarial basis which limited its pensions to "total and permanent disability". 

Or again. A clergyman who has retired has of course under his ordination vows the right to officiate as much as he wishes without emolument: or he may receive financial support for work done outside of his Page 16

clerical calling. The Church however decided after long discussion that when a clergyman has retired at sixtyeight, or after, and his pension has begun, he cannot receive further emolument from ecclesiastical sources, although he may return to active service and relinquish his pension. Hence the Fund's actuarial figures were based on this decision. Unless the Church is prepared to lay a staggering burden of annual charge upon its members it must accept the fact that when a clergyman retires for age, he has retired. The rigidity of this refusal was somewhat eased by the formal advice of the actuary, which has enabled the Fund to rule that occasional services for emolument, not exceeding one month in actual duration at any one place, would not interfere with the pension. To relax that principle further is in the judgment of the actuary courting financial danger. 

I quote from a letter of Mr. Henry Moir, whose international standing as an actuary makes any statement from him of the utmost weight: "One reason why pensions are used less than they should be is that they appear expensive. Yet it is a matter of balancing the accounts so that payments with accumulated interest may equal, or slightly exceed, the pensions paid. It goes without saying that if an annuity is paid at a fixed age, irrespective of whether the beneficiary continues to work or not, the cost of it will be much greater than the cost of paying a true pension commencing when the beneficiary ceases to work.

Page 17

"Pensions are not intended to increase income, but to relieve want and furnish a modest living to those who withdraw from active life. The whole theory of pensions falls down if a man can draw a pension and continue his work. The cost is tremendously increased. 

In the case of the Church Pension Fund the additional benefits that have been so wisely given, increasing the minimum pensions, etc., would fall to pieces if you were to depart from the principle of paying pensions only to those who have retired from active service." 

So long therefore as any clergyman receives a pension he is in honour bound to observe the rule of the Fund. The experience of these fourteen years has justified this sound policy.

AY I now summarize the happy growth during The receipt of $5,064,000 for accrued liabilities set up a sound but very limited pension system; the promise was made of a $600 pension upon retirement, and for a clergyman permanently and totally disabled, and $300 for a widow. That was all. And it was a great venture of faith on the part of the Church to place itself under such an assessment in the confidence of larger results in untold years to come. 

From the system too were excluded all aged clergymen who had retired while the Church was on the charity basis; and the widows of that regime also. It Page 18

seemed hard and to some critics unjust that these should be shut out; but the Pension Fund was looking forward and the financial burden was as heavy as the Church dared undertake. In spite therefore of criticism the 

Fund held strictly to its trust and the exclusion, hoping to correct these hard conditions as the years passed. 

The overflow of $3,790,000, the wise administration of the finances, the fortunate investments of principal, the loyal and unanimous support of all parishes and missions and the firm and consistent action of the Trustees, in standing by the principles of the Fund and its rules, have brought about the following result. 

Looking back through these fourteen years and supported by two fresh and complete re-studies of vital statistics and actuarial calculations the Fund has included the entire class of clergy which was at first excluded and every widow that can be discovered. To accomplish this the Fund has paid out $2,468,000 to these clergymen and widows and set up in reserve, to carry them through to the end of their lives, $1,267,000.

It has by the use of the income of one million dollars been able to send as an immediate gift a check for $1,000 to the widow of every clergyman dying in active service. 

Who knows the relief to her given by this check coming usually before the funeral, and followed by a letter stating the amount of pension that she and her several young children will receive each month? 

By the use of surplus income from various Page 19

sources, the Fund has been able to set up reserves according to the date of each clergyman's ordination and advance all of the pensions so that by July of this year the promised age pension of $600 has been raised to an average of $993: and for the widows, exclusive of grants for her minor children, to $387. There are now 5,314 active clergy and the number of pensioners, aged, disabled, and widows, is 1,233. The Pension Fund has paid out, in these fourteen years, more than eight million dollars: it is now paying out at the rate of a million dollars a year.

On March 9th the financial conditions were such as to justify the Trustees in promising that for the next few years at least the minimum amount of the age retirement pension be $1,000. Meanwhile the average salary of the clergy has increased from $1,200 to $2,800. 

The present assets are twenty-eight million dollars which are so held or pledged for reserves that the amount of every promised pension is assured, together with such steady increment as is leading the Church up to the prac tical ideal towards which it started fifteen years ago. It must however always be kept in mind that as the most generous pension system cannot do more than offer a very modest rate of living, thrift and habits of saving are essential to comfort.

With the continued loyalty and support of the laymen and women of the Church, the devotion of the clergy and the sound administration of the Fund, the Page 20

time for the completed system and adequate pensions will approach with each passing year.

WILLIAM LAWRENCE, President.
