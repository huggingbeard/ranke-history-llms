LETTER OF TRANSMITTAL TO THE CONGRESS OF THE UNITED STATES: 

I am pleased to transmit the Twelfth Annual Report on the Trade 

Agreements Program, as required by the Trade Expansion Act of 

1962. The report covers the year 1967. 

The remarkable postwar growth of international trade continued uninterrupted in 1967, but at a slower pace. World trade grew by almost 5%, to a new record of $191 billion. 

U.S. trade grew by a little more than 5%. Our trade surplus for the year reached $4. 1 billion, an improvement over the preceding year. The trade surplus fell off sharply, however, in the fourth quarter of 1967 and through the first half of 1968. We acted to reverse this trend through the Revenue and Expenditure Control Act of 1968 and by new export promotion measures. 

The year was highlighted by the successful completion of the Kennedy Round -- a milestone in the history of the bipartisan U. S. policy of trade expansion. Tariffs have already begun to come down. 

The U.S. made the first of five annual tariff reductions on January 1, 1968. 

The European Economic Community, the UK, Japan and some other major trading countries put into effect two-fifths of their scheduled tariff reductions on July 1, 1968. These tariff cuts will promote trade, higher employment, and higher income among all countries. 

America's economic well-being and the strength of the dollar are linked to our sharing fully in the future growth of world trade. I therefore directed the Special Representative for Trade Negotiations to conduct a long-range study of trade policy. This study will benefit from the views and recommendations of members of the Congress, the interested agencies of the government, and a distinguished group of private citizens serving on the Public Advisory Committee on Trade Policy. We must continue to seek ways in which foreign trade can help to keep our economy competitive and healthy and give to all our people the advantages of greater efficiency in production. 

The White House, October 8, 1968

I. WORLD TRADE IN 1967 

A. 

Developments in International Trade 

Free-world merchandise trade expanded to another record high in 1967, but its rate of gain slowed from the fast pace of recent years. Exports reached a level of $191 billion, $9 billion above 

1966. The annual increase plummeted to 4.8% from the 9.8% average growth of the previous four years.

Developed countries supplied nine-tenths of the rise in world exports, bringing total deliveries to $151 billion. The advance was attributable to the greater volume of goods moving abroad; on balance, price changes were minimal. Although the 5.5% rate of increase for these nations was much smaller than in 1966, the deceleration was even sharper for the developing areas. Shipments by the developing nations were adversely affected by reduced demand and lower prices for their products, stemming mainly from economic sluggishness in some major industrial markets. 

Imports of goods into world markets expanded at half the rate of 1966. Purchases of foreign products by the developing nations, which had provided a sixth of the increase in that year, accounted for only a tenth of the advance of 1967. 1. 

Europe improves export share.-Although its export growth moderated for the second successive year, Western Europe provided a larger proportion of world shipments than in 1966. The region's foreign sales expanded by 5.1% to a level of $91.4 billion, compared with a gain of 9.2% in the preceding year. The slowdown reflected trade developments in the area's two major market groups. 

Smaller rates of gain by all members except the Netherlands held the rise in exports by the European Communities (EC) to 6.6%, following an advance of a tenth in 1966. Higher shipments were reported by each of the Common Market countries, bringing the total for the Six to $56 billion. Sales among members were less buoyant than to other markets. Exports to the United States, valued at $4.4 billion, accounted for a larger share of EC shipments than in 1966. Deliveries to Communist Europe and China were a fourth higher in 1967 but represented only 4.5% of EC exports.

Foreign sales of the European Free Trade Association (EFTA) 

inched up by 2% to $29 billion, less than a third of the growth a year earlier. This deceleration was principally a consequence of the fall-off in shipments by the United Kingdom, whose trade is equal to the combined total of the other six members. All of the latter showed sizable gains in deliveries to foreign markets, with half achieving a faster rate of growth.

Intra-EFTA exports quickened in 1967, possibly due in part to 1

the elimination at the beginning of the year of the remaining tariff barriers on industrial goods from member nations. The up- surge of 10.5% in trade with EFTA partners was in contrast to small declines in sales to the United States and the EC. 

Arrivals of goods in West European countries showed a smaller rate of advance 3.2% compared with 8.2% in 1966. Common Market imports, valued at $55 billion, were slowed by the absolute decline in Germany's foreign purchases. Except for the leveling in 

Belgium-Luxembourg, arrivals were higher into the rest of the Community. Due to a substantial acceleration by the United Kingdom and Norway, the growth rate of EFTA's imports was somewhat greater than in 1966.

EC purchases from the United States were slightly lower, reducing our share of that market to 10.7% in 1967. This country's market position in EFTA, however, held at a tenth. 

Canadian exports reached a peak value of $11 billion. The rise of 10.4% fell short of the gain achieved in 1966, largely as a consequence of weakness in the U.S. market. This country absorbed an additional billion dollars of Canadian goods in 1967. 

Sales to the Soviet Union, on the other hand, fell by $178 million due to lower wheat shipments. Counterbalancing the decline was an almost equivalent growth in exports to Japan. 

The slowdown in the Canadian economy cut the growth in imports to 8%, compared with more than twice that rate in 1966. Nearly all of the increased arrivals into Canada came from the United States, boosting our share of that market to over 73%. U.S.- 

Canadian trade in both directions was stimulated primarily by greater movements of automotive products under the duty-free agreement of 1965.

As in 1966, Japan's exports were less expansive. Foreign sales of $10.4 billion were 6.8% higher, following a rise of 15.7% in the preceding year. On the other hand, the country's economic boom caused imports to accelerate to over 22%, the highest rate of expansion among the major industrial nations. With GNP rising by 

14% in constant prices and industrial production a fifth higher, demand was stimulated for foreign capital equipment and industrial supplies. The United States maintained its share of this strong market, again providing more than a fourth of Japan's foreign purchases.

In contrast to most other developed nations, Australia and the Republic of South Africa stepped up the pace of their trade. 

Exports of these countries grew by 10.2% and 14.2% respectively.

Australia benefited from heavier foreign shipments of wheat and iron ore; South Africa from improved sales of maize and refined copper. Imports of these two primary products climbed in 1967 after turning down in the previous year. New Zealand, however, 2

U.S. Foreign Trade 

U.S. foreign trade continued to grow in 1967, but both imports and exports were considerably less expansive than in the preceding year. As a result of a greater deceleration in imports than exports, our gross merchandise trade surplus increased to $4.1 billion from $3.8 billion in 1966. However, the surplus in the last quarter of 1967, less than $600 million, was considerably smaller than during the last quarters of the preceding several years.

U.S. exports, excluding military grant-aid shipments, climbed to $30.9 billion in 1967, a 5.3% gain over the previous year. Reduced shipments of agricultural commodities held the rate of increase in exports to somewhat below the 1966 rate of gain and slightly below the 6.9% average annual rise for the 1960-66 period. With sales of machinery and transport equipment strong, nonagricultural exports expanded almost as rapidly as in 1966. 

After three years of accelerating increases, U.S. imports advanced at a more modest rate in 1967, in line with the easing of demand and general business activity, particularly through the first nine months. At $26.8 billion, imports were 5% above the 

1966 level. This rise was only one-fourth of the sharp import gain in the previous year and a little more than half the 9.3% yearly rise in arrivals of foreign goods from 1960 to 1966. 1. 

Nonagricultural exports strong.-Shipments of machinery valued at a record $8.3 billion were 8% higher despite slowdowns in the economies of some of our most important trading partners. 

Contributing importantly to the advance were accelerated shipments of computers and parts and telecommunications equipment. 

Although not as buoyant as in 1966, exports of engines and parts, air-conditioning and refrigerating equipment, and scientific and controlling instruments also gained over the previous year. 

In contrast, exports of tractors declined. 

Foreign sales of metal-working and agricultural machinery registered only marginal gains.

The climb of one-fourth in exports of transport equipment to a peak of $4.3 billion was four times the average annual rate of growth in the first six years of the decade and accounted for 47% of the overall expansion in nonagricultural exports in 1967. 

Shipments of automobiles and trucks to Canada were stimulated further by the 1965 duty-free agreement. A strong recovery in aircraft and parts was sparked by stepped-up deliveries of commercial jetcraft.

Chemical shipments rose by 5% to $2.8 billion, about half the 

1966 advance. The slower pace was largely the result of a leveling in exports of plastics and resins to Western Europe and decreased deliveries of manufactured fertilizers to Korea, Pakistan, and Latin America. Exports of chemical elements and compounds and medicinals and pharmaceuticals advanced a bit faster. Sales of organic chemicals, mainly those used for making synthetic materials and insecticides, were especially strong. 

Deliveries of mineral fuels registered a 13% gain, four times the rate of increase in 1966. Impetus for this unusual strength was the rise in movements to the United Kingdom, Canada, and Germany. These followed the disruption of petroleum supplies from the Middle East and North Africa as a consequence of the Arab- 

Israeli war.

Exports of agricultural products fell 7% to $6.4 billion in 

1967 following a 10% gain in the preceding year. Sizable declines in shipments of wheat, corn, and grain sorghums were primarily responsible for the turnaround.

Wheat exports were one-fifth lower due in part to increased production in Western Europe, and in part to substantially reduced PL-480 shipments to India, Yugoslavia, and Egypt. Sales of corn likewise fell 20% as a result of larger harvests abroad, strong competition from other types of feedgrains, and limited supplies and higher prices in the United States. 

Among the crops showing export gains last year, rice was particularly buoyant. Accelerated PL-480 shipments to Viet-Nam accounted for half the gain and commercial sales for about twofifths. Sales of soybeans and unmanufactured tobacco registered only a moderate rise as larger supplies overseas limited the demand for these commodities from the United States. Our more competitively-priced cotton rebounded with a 7% increase in the value of foreign shipments as a result of smaller crops in some exporting countries and rising textile production in Asian countries. 2. 

Pace of imports slowed. -The 1967 slowdown in U.S. business activity was clearly reflected in the trend of imports during the year. The acceleration in imports which had occurred in the three preceding years, when business activity had risen steadily, came to a halt. Reduced gains in industrial production, business investment, and consumer spending limited the import rise to 5% over the 1966 total. Arrivals of industrial supplies and materials slipped to $11.8 billion, 44% of U.S. imports in 1967, as a result of the slackening in industrial production and inventory-building. Deliveries to the developing countries totaled $9.6 billion, representing a 2% gain. This was substantially less than the 

1966 increase of 11%. Shipments to Latin America were moderately lower while those to the Near East and the developing nations of Africa dropped sharply. Exports to East and South Asia continued to advance though at a slower rate. 

Imports from the developed countries totaled $18.9 billion, up 8% over 1966. Three-fourths of this gain represented increased arrivals from Canada. Reflecting weaker demand for industrial materials and foodstuffs, imports from the developing countries fell by 1% to $7.7 billion.

Exports to Canada gained strongly, but the 8% advance to $7.2 billion was less than half of the 1966 growth rate. An economic slowdown there was the principal cause of the deceleration in sales. Most of the advance was fueled by a $500 million or two-fifths rise in shipments of transport equipment. 

Deliveries of new cars, parts, and trucks as well as aircraft expanded markedly. Lesser increases were registered in exports of nonferrous metals and chemicals.

Greater arrivals of copper, nickel, crude petroleum and natural gas contributed most of the additional imports of industrial materials from that source.

II. 

THE CULMINATION OF THE KENNEDY ROUND¹ 

A.

Summary of Negotiations 

On June 30, 1967, the multilateral agreements negotiated in the sixth round of international trade negotiations under the auspices of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (the 

Kennedy Round) were signed in Geneva. On December 16, 1967, the

President issued a proclamation putting the first stage of the tariff concessions granted by the United States into effect, beginning January 1, 1968.

The success of the Kennedy Round, the most significant and comprehensive multilateral trade agreement in history, was the culmination of four years of complex and often difficult negotiations.

At the formal opening of the Kennedy Round in May 1964, the major participants agreed to proceed on the basis of a 50-percent linear (that is, across-the-board) cut in tariffs on nonagricultural products. Exceptions, being those items not to be subjected to the full cut, were to be limited to those necessitated by reasons of overriding national interest. Exceptions lists were exchanged on November 16, 1964. The United States, the European Communities (EC), the United Kingdom, Japan, and Finland submitted lists. Austria, Denmark, Norway, Sweden and 

Switzerland declared that, subject to receiving reciprocity, they would not claim any exceptions.

The participants had previously agreed that the linear basis of negotiation would not be appropriate for Canada, Australia, For a more complete record of the early phase of the Kennedy 

Round, see the 9th, 10th and 11th annual reports of the President on the trade agreements program. 12

New Zealand, and South Africa because of the special trade structures of these countries. These countries would, therefore, make specific offers on specific products. Canada submitted offers in November 1964, and the others followed during 1965. 

In early 1965 the countries which were negotiating on the basis of the linear cut met as a group to hear each others' detailed justifications of items on the exceptions lists and to examine their validity. Similar bilateral exchanges took place with the four countries participating on the basis of specific offers. 

Because it became apparent early in the negotiations that there were particularly difficult problems in certain industrial sectors, separate groups of limited membership were established to study these problems. The countries having particular interests in trade in chemicals, pulp and paper, aluminum, steel, and cotton textiles met to explore the means of assuring maximum inclusion of these product groups in the bargaining. 

In chemicals, other major participants insisted that reductions in U.S. chemical duties be accompanied by the abolition of the American Selling Price (ASP) system of customs valuationas a condition for any chemical concessions on their part. The 

United States responded that any conversion to the normal valuation system would require special counter-concessions, that any agreement involving ASP must be separate from the regular negotiations, and that Congress would have to approve it. 

In late 1965, the Special Representative for Trade Negotiations requested the Tariff Commission to undertake an examination of the ASP system. He asked it to determine rates of duty calculated upon the usual basis of valuation that would yield duties substantially equivalent to those paid under ASP. The Commission's report was published in July 1966 and a further confidential report concerning the impact of conversion was given to the Special 

Representative in October 1966. 

Discussions relating to other nontariff barriers proceeded in various ways. Negotiations towards an international code on anti-dumping regulations were conducted on a multilateral basis.

In order to determine the duty on any of these imported products, the ad valorem rate is applied to the wholesale price of the similar domestic product rather than the usual basis of valuation, which in most instances is the value of the imported product at the time of exportation from the foreign country. 13

Road taxes in several countries that discriminate against American types of automobiles were the subject of bilateral discussions, as were other nontariff barriers. 

The importance of the inclusion of agriculture in the Kennedy Round was emphasized by the United States. The European Communities, however, sought a more limited negotiation essentially aimed at the freezing of existing agricultural price-support levels on an item-by-item basis. 

Agricultural offers were not tabled at the same time as industrial offers, as originally intended. The EC took the position that it could not do so until its major common agricultural policy regulations were agreed upon, and this work was not yet completed. On grains, however, the GATT ministers had decided that the Cereals Group should undertake the negotiation of an international grains arrangement. Accordingly, in May 1966 major cereals-trading nations exchanged proposals for an international grains arrangement. Because of internal difficulties, the EC was unable to table its offers on agricultural products other than grains until August 1966. Agricultural negotiations thus were not fully engaged until September 1966, when the confrontation on agricultural offers began. 

The developed-country participants in the Kennedy Round committed themselves specifically to make use of the negotiations to contribute in a substantial way to the solution of the urgent trade and economic problems of developing countries. The first step toward this goal was the adoption of the principle that these countries would not be expected to grant reciprocity that is, to pay in equal measure for concessions received from wealthy countries- although they would contribute to the overall balance of the final agreement to the greatest extent compatible with their trade, financial, and development needs. A special committee was set up to deal with the participation of the developing countries. A tropical products group was established to deal with such products as coffee, cocoa, oilseeds, and vegetable oils. 

At the end of November 1966, major participants (with the exception of the EC) formally indicated what they would have to do, or expect others to do, to achieve overall balance in the negotiations. It was understood that reciprocity among the major participants would require that offers be modified through a process either of improvement or of withdrawal, depending on the relative quality of each country's concessions. Thus, the way was opened for the beginning of the intensive final bargaining phase.

Despite the quickening pace of the negotiations from then on, major differences among the principal participants remained unresolved through a four-day Ministerial meeting at the end of March 1967. It required difficult bargaining to settle them

Steel Sector.-Negotiations on steel resulted in closer harmonization of tariffs among the major steel-producing countries. 

Virtually all the peaks in these countries' tariffs were eliminated, leaving almost all rates no higher than 15 percent and most well below 10 percent. Except for U.S. rates, most steel tariffs had not heretofore been bound. In the final negotiating package, however, almost all rates of other countries were bound. 

The European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC) adopted a unified tariff for steel and agreed to reduce rates to an arithmetic average of 5.7 percent. The European Economic Community (EEC) agreed to reduce steel rates within its jurisdiction correspondingly so that a tariff relationship would be maintained between more-highly-fabricated EEC steel items and primary and less-fabricated ECSC items. 

The ECSC/EEC concessions were a 23 percent reduction from existing rates equivalent to a 10 percent reduction from the pre-February 1964 rates on 1964 imports from the United States. 

The United Kingdom reduced most if its rates by 20 percent and Japan by 50 percent except for a few alloy steel items. Sweden bound its rates at existing low levels. Austria harmonized its tariffs with the ECSC/EEC at a somewhat higher level. 

The reductions by the United States averaged 7 percent on 1964 imports. It generally harmonized its tariffs with the ECSC/EEC where they have been above those rates. United States rates higher than ECSC/EEC rates were reduced to ECSC/EEC levels, but no cuts were made where rates are now below ECSC/EEC concession levels.

2. Nontariff Barriers 

Anti-dumping Code.

-A major accomplishment in the field of 19

In nontariff barriers was the negotiation of an anti-dumping code. addition to the United States, the major participants in this negotiation were the United Kingdom, the European Communities, Japan, Canada, and the Scandinavian countries.

The anti-dumping code supplements the provisions of Article VI 

of the GATT with rules and procedures to be followed in anti-dumping actions.

United States administrative regulations contain detailed provisions relating to procedures for the determination of sales at less than fair value and of injury, but most countries' procedures lack such specificity. One of the principal advantages of the antidumping code to the United States will be the adoption by other countries of fair and open procedures along the lines of present United States practices. The code will provide both an opportunity and a basis for United States exporters to defend their interests in foreign anti-dumping actions. In particular, the new common anti-dumping regulations that are being developed by the European 

Communities will conform with the code.

Of special benefit to the United States will be the adoption by Canada of an injury requirement in its anti-dumping legislation. 

The lack of such a requirement has impeded United States exports for many years. 

Because the anti-dumping code is consistent with existing United States law, no legislative changes are required only the revision of the Treasury Department's regulations. The principal change in present procedures will concern limiting the time period during which appraisement is withheld to a maximum of 90 days in most cases. 

Other Nontariff Barriers. -Another nontariff accomplishment was the agreement to take action on the nontariff barriers included in the ASP package: the elimination of ASP by the United States; the elimination of the discriminatory aspects of automobile road-use taxes in France, Italy, and Belgium; the modification by Switzerland of regulations on canned fruit; and a reduction by the United Kingdom in the Commonwealth margin of preference on unmanufactured tobacco. 

Other nontariff achievements as a result of bilateral discussions included agreement that Austria would eliminate the discriminatory effect of automobile road-use taxes on larger-engined U.S. automobiles. 

Canada eliminated its restrictions prohibiting imports of fresh fruits and vegetables in three-quarter-bushel baskets. Canada also ceased applying the Canadian sales tax to the full value of aircraft engines repaired in the United States. The 11 percent sales tax is now applied only to the value of the repairs. In addition, Canada modified restrictive standards applying to aircraft engines repaired abroad.

Although not a subject for negotiation, quantitative restrictions were eliminated or modified by several countries. Of particular importance to the United States are the liberalization of import re- 20

strictions by the United Kingdom on fresh grapefruit and by Denmark and Finland on many agricultural products. Japan agreed to liberalize quota restrictions on some products. 

Several developing countries agreed to relax various nontariff measures as part of their contribution to the negotiations. These included the introduction of certain tariff reforms, the liberalization of licensing systems and foreign-exchange controls, and the elimination or reduction of prior-deposit requirements and tariff surcharges.

C. Conduct of Negotiations

The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 created the position of 

Special Representative for Trade Negotiations, to be responsible, under the President, for the conduct of trade negotiations and for advising the President on related trade matters. 

This post was held by Christian A. Herter until his death in December 1966, and thereafter by William M. Roth.

Several committees were created under the chairmanship of the Special Representative or members of his staff to develop and recommend policies and actions relating to the preparations for negotiations and for the negotiations themselves. Repre sentatives of the Department of Agriculture, Commerce, Defense, Interior, Labor, State, and Treasury served on these committees, with a representative of the Tariff Commission often serving as a technical adviser.

In deciding what products to except from linear offers, the U.S. not only withheld those products explicity excepted by the 

Trade Expansion Act of 1962, but took great care to avoid injury to domestic industries. For this purpose, extensive hearings were held by the Trade Information Committee and the 

Tariff Commission. The recommendations of the Special Repre sentative to the President were based upon the analysis of thousands of pages of this testimony, followed by lengthy and intensive deliberations by each of the inter-agency committees listed above. Studies in depth of critical products and industries were conducted both by the agencies with particular 22

responsibilities for them and by the Office of the Special Representative. Every available source of information was tapped in an effort to assemble the most comprehensive body of economic information possible before judgments of the possible effects of tariff reductions were made.

Even after the initial U.S. offers were tabled, the process continued, with the Trade Information Committee accepting from any interested party information concerning any matter relating to the negotiations as well as seeking it when the need arose. This included revision and updating of previous testimony on the economic conditions of industries, representations from industries not previously heard, and statements concerning foreign import restrictions. 

The Special Representative was assisted by the Public Advisory 

Committee for Trade Negotiations, a 45-member group appointed by the President and drawn from industry, labor, agriculture, education, and consumer groups. This group met from time to time with the Special Representative to review the progress of the negotiations and discuss problems arising in them. Many members visited the delegation in Geneva.

A roster of some 290 technical specialists in industry, agriculture, and labor provided technical information needed by the Special Representative. Additional industry task forces were created in each of the major sectors. 

Finally, six members of Congress, serving as Congressional delegates, were kept informed on all significant matters concerning the negotiations. They met regularly with the Special 

Representative to discuss such matters, and attended meetings in 

Geneva on occasion. Over the extended period of the negotiations, the delegation in Geneva and its backup team in Washington studied in depth the trade significance of other countries' offers. The delegation was divided into negotiating teams, each dealing with a group of countries. Thus members of the U.S. delegation developed a high degree of specialized knowledge concerning the strengths and weaknesses of other participants' offers and positions. 

Their day-to-day activity involved meetings with delegations of other countries in their particular group and intensive study of the information these meetings provided. 

The policy recommendations which the Special Representative made to the President therefore reflected careful analyses of every aspect of the negotiations. They were based upon the assembly and organization of the whole array of relevant facts and figures. They reflected the knowledge and experience of all Government agencies concerned with trade. They benefited from the counsel of the Congressional delegates and of qualified representatives of all sectors of the American economy 

23

business, agriculture, labor, and consumers. They were constantly reviewed and revised in the light of the most up-to-date information developed in the United States and the expert judgment of the U.S. delegation at Geneva concerning the problems and prospects of the negotiations. 

In the closing months even in the closing days when it became clear that some other countries were not able to match the tariff reductions the United States was prepared to make, the concessions it had initially offered were substantially reduced. 

Where the economic conditions of domestic industries had changed since the initial offers, the U.S. withdrew concessions in order to be sure that the intent of the Trade Expansion Act was fully carried out.

From the launching of the Kennedy Round in May 1964 to its conclusion in June 1967, no pains were spared to ensure that its outcome would be in the best interest both of the United States and of the world economy of which it is so significant a part. 

III. 

OTHER MAJOR TRADE DEVELOPMENTS A. Regional Trading Arrangements 

In terms of United States trade interests, the two most important regional arrangements are the European Communities (EC) -including the European Economic Community, European Coal and Steel Community and Euratom--and the European Free Trade Association (EFTA). Despite certain problems, particularly in the agricultural sector, our trade experience over the past ten years with these two regional groups suggests that closer regional economic integration can have desirable effects on the growth of world trade.

The United States has been one of the primary beneficiaries of the growth in European internal and global trade since the formation of the EC and EFTA. United States exports to the six member countries of the EC have increased at an average annual rate of 8.5% between 1958 and 1966, while our global exports showed an average gain of 6.8% a year. Over the same period our exports to the EFTA countries expanded at an average rate of 

8.2% a year.

As a result of economic slowdowns in certain EC countries, the export pace to the EC decelerated sharply in 1967, showing a gain of only 2.6%. U.S. exports to EFTA countries, however, recorded a 9.6% gain in that year, reflecting principally an increase in shipments to the United Kingdom.

The United States has consistently supported the political, economic, and military integration of Western Europe. We continue to support further steps towards an increasingly united but open Europe--with Great Britain a part of it and with close 24

S ties to America. Our support for European integration is based on the belief that it contributes to the growth and stability of the world economy, enhances the political stability and cohesion of the European area and enlarges the potential market for U.S. exports. 

We have consistently emphasized the need for the member states of the EC and EFTA to adopt liberal and open trade policies, in conformity with the rules of the GATT, if world trade is to continue to grow at a rate comparable with the growth of the past decade. The enactment of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, which provided the authority to negotiate reciprocal tariff concessions, was in part intended to reduce the discrimination against countries outside these regional groups inherent in the progressive reduction of duties among their member countries. As a result of the Kennedy Round of trade negotiations, completed in June 1967, the EC and EFTA countries granted tariff concessions which should contribute considerably to the continued growth of U.S. trade with 

Europe. The EC, for instance, gave concessions on 87% of its imports of U.S. goods (1964 figures) while the U.K., by far the largest market for the U.S. among the EFTA countries, gave concessions on 72% of its imports of U.S. goods. 

Although total U.S. exports of farm products to the EC have continued to rise, the EC's variable-import-levy system has adversely affected imports of certain U.S. agricultural products and is a subject of increasing concern to the United States. 

We are also concerned by selective preferential trading arrangements between the EC and certain other countries that are not designed to lead to full membership in the EC. These arrangements discriminate against nonparticipants and are inconsistent with the most-favored-nation principle, a basic feature of the GATT.

The EFTA countries completed on December 31, 1966, their movement toward the formation of a free trade area in industrial products by the removal of all duties on such goods traded within the EFTA.

In April 1967 the Presidents of the member countries of the Organization of American States met at Punta del Este, Uruguay, to give renewed impetus to the Alliance for Progress and to emphasize its multilateral character. The Latin American 

Presidents decided to create a Latin American Common Market and to lay a physical foundation for Latin American economic integration through multinational infrastructure projects. The 

President of the United States declared his firm support for these constructive Latin American initiatives and offered to recommend a substantial U.S. contribution to a fund to help ease the transition of Latin America into an integrated regional economy. He also announced that he would request substantial additional funds, to be provided through the Inter- American Development Bank, to assist multinational infrastructure projects. 25

All the Presidents at the Summit meeting agreed to join in efforts to increase substantially Latin American earnings from foreign trade. To this end, they pledged individual and joint efforts toward facilitating nondiscriminatory access of Latin American products to world markets, toward increasing Latin American earnings from traditional exports, toward avoiding frequent fluctuations in income from traditional export commodities, and finally toward adopting measures designed to stimulate exports of Latin 

American manufactured products.

To further this cooperative endeavor, President Johnson indicated that the United States would explore whether the developed countries could reach a consensus on a system of temporary preferential tariff advantages for the goods of all developing countries in the markets of the industrialized countries. (See 

Section VI.B). 

In the meantime, the countries of the Inter-American system are pledged to continue, within the framework of the Alliance for Progress, the cooperative endeavor to create a Latin American Common Market during the period from 1970 to 1985. A strong 

Latin American Common Market will be an increasingly good trading partner for the United States and will enable Latin America gradually to take its proper place in world affairs. 

The Latin American Common Market is to be formed through the steady development, improvement, and ultimate fusion of the two existing Latin American integration organizations: the Central 

American Common Market (CACM) and the Latin American Free Trade 

Association (LAFTA). The few Latin American countries which are not associated with either of these groups will be eligible to join the Latin American Common Market.

The two existing groups now include all of mainland Latin America from the Rio Grande to Cape Horn except the three 

Guyanas, Panama, and British Honduras. Some of the Caribbean islands, including several which have reached political independence as well as some which are still dependent on the United Kingdom, are in the process of forming a Caribbean Free Trade Area. Haiti and the Dominican Republic have not yet indicated whether they plan to join any of the existing groups. Panama has made moves towards joining the Central American Common Market, and 

British Honduras may join either the Caribbean Free Trade Area or the Central American Common Market.

The results of the efforts of LAFTA and the CACM from their formation in 1961 to the present have been limited but have served to show that potential economic gains from integration are large. From 1961 through 1967 the imports of the CACM countries from each other increased by 462%, while during the same period LAFTA countries' imports from other LAFTA countries increased by 135%. During this period, Latin America's trade 26

with other areas also climbed, but at a slower rate. From 1961 to 1967 the total exports of the Latin American countries (excluding Cuba) increased from $8 billion to $11 billion, or 38%. 

The increases in the Latin American countries' imports from each other have not led to decreases in their imports from the United States, their largest supplier. On the contrary, Ú.S. exports to Latin America increased from $3.3 billion to $4.1 billion between 1961 and 1967, an increase of 24%. The experience of other organizations for regional economic integration shows clearly that integration can promote increased trade and higher levels of prosperity. As integration and economic growth proceed in Latin 

America, higher incomes and the desire for higher living standards should cause Latin America to become a much larger importer of industrial and agricultural products. As a major producer and exporter of these products, the United States stands to benefit considerably from Latin American economic growth. We must, of course, also expect increasing efforts on the part of Latin Americans to sell their goods in our market. 

Most of the developing countries in Africa recognize that there are considerable benefits to be gained from increasing their mutual trade and promoting closer economic cooperation, especially between groups of neighboring countries. Increased trade and economic cooperation are not sought simply as goals in themselves but as means for promoting economic growth and development. There are in Africa a number of multinational organizations which were formed to accomplish these objectives. 

Some of these organizations envisage going beyond simple economic cooperation and eventually reaching one of the stages of economic integration, such as a free trade area, a customs union, or a common market. In some cases, significant progress has been made towards accomplishing this. 

Most of the African efforts directed towards regional economic cooperation and integration followed the creation of the newly-independent states in the past decade and thus are of relatively recent origin. Only future experience can show whether they will turn out to be effective instruments of economic integration and growth. Some institutions have made real progress. One African group, the East African Community (formerly the East African Common Services Organization), has been in existence for many years and has been quite effective in promoting economic cooperation and increasing trade between its members Kenya, Uganda, and Tanganyika (now Tanzania). 

This organization has faced difficult political and economic problems but, as a result of the signing of the Treaty for East African Cooperation last year, these countries are now in a position to attain further growth through strengthened economic cooperation. 

Historically, trade among African countries represents only a small fraction of their total international trade 27

(smaller than in other developing regions) and for this reason the basis for expansion of intra-African trade is limited. The trade of most of the African countries has for many years been oriented towards sending primary products to Europe. However, Africans see in economic cooperation and in the common market approach a means to increase trade by building industries and other parts of their economies to serve regional markets, thus taking advantage of economies of scale and overcoming the limitations of small national markets which now inhibit development. Plans for increasing intra-African trade within existing groups and between these groups continue to move ahead and will be an increasingly important factor in the future.

The Arab Common Market, which groups the United Arab Republic, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, and Syria, is too new to verify statistically whether it has increased its members' trade with each other. These countries have reduced or eliminated tariff charges on a large proportion of their mutual trade, but their restrictive systems are based much more on quotas and other barriers.

In Southeast Asia, a group of countries created in 1967 a new organization called the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) to increase their mutual cooperation for economic development and security. The founding members were the Philippines, Indonesia, Thailand, Malaysia, and Singapore. They have indicated their feeling that real economic integration is not a practical possibility at this time in view of the widely divergent languages, cultures, and governmental and economic systems in the area. The developing countries of East, South, and 

Southeast Asia have a greater share of their total trade with each other than is the case in either Africa or Latin America. 

However, this share decreased from about 28% in 1955 to about 22% in 1965. These countries' total exports to each other grew by only 14% during this period while their total exports were increasing by almost 36%. The member countries of ASEAN expect that, by working through this new organization, they can arrest this decline in their mutual trade and build new forms of economic cooperation on the basis of their existing mutual trade and the economic relations which accompany it. 

Such initiatives as the formation of the Asian Development Bank, to which the United States subscribed a large share of the total capital, show great promise for promoting closer cooperation for economic development among the free countries of Asia. B.

Border Tax Adjustments 

Border tax adjustments are made by governments in order to impose on imports the domestic indirect taxes applied to similar domestic products, and to relieve exports from the do- 28

mestic indirect taxes which would have been imposed on them had they been sold in the domestic market. During 1967 and extending into 1968 a number of countries have increased the rates and coverage of their border adjustments. These changes have stemmed from increases in domestic indirect taxation, from efforts by the members of the European Communities to harmonize their internal tax systems, and from attempts to equalize the incidence of domestic indirect taxes on domestic and imported products. During the course of the Kennedy Round negotiations, the United States noted that changes in border tax adjustments "may have trade effects that nullify or impair concessions or other benefits" received by the United States and reserved its rights under the GATT. 

At the request of the United States, the GATT CONTRACTING 

PARTIES agreed early in 1968 to set up a Working Party to examine the provisions of the GATT and the practices of contracting parties, in an effort to ascertain the effects of border tax adjustments on international trade. The Working Party began its examination early in 1968.

The United States had previously encouraged examination of border tax adjustments by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD). After extended discussions, a consultation procedure was established in February 1967 for the purpose of examining the trade effects of changes in border tax adjustments. 

It was agreed that countries would notify important changes in border tax adjustments to the OECD and that a country should have the right to request consultations when it considered that its trade interests were affected as a result of such a change or proposed change. This consultation procedure was invoked by the

C. Special Trade Problems of the Developing Countries 

Any consideration of the trade problems of the developing countries must take account of one central factor their heavy dependence on exports of primary commodities. About 85 percent of the export earnings of the developing countries as a whole are derived from exports of nonmanufactured primary agricultural commodities, crude minerals and metals, and petroleum. The dependence of many developing countries on exports of a single 

¹Throughout this report, the term CONTRACTING PARTIES (in cap ital letters) is used to refer to the signatories of the GATT acting jointly in accordance with the provisions of the Agreement. 29

product is even more striking. For example, coffee, cocoa, rubber, sugar, or cotton account for as much as 80 percent of the total export receipts of particular countries. With the exception of petroleum, the commodities which predominate among the export products of the developing countries are not dynamic and dependable sources of foreign exchange. They are generally characterized by a low elasticity of demand in developed-country markets; their prices fluctuate sharply because of cyclical variations in supply; several of them face growing competition from synthetic substitutes; and some are being produced in increasing quantities in the industrialized countries themselves. 

Partly as a consequence of this situation, the developing countries have been focusing their attention on increasing their exports of manufactures and semi-manufactures, including processed agricultural products. The growth of world trade in manufactures has consistently exceeded the growth of world trade generally. The developing countries want to reduce their dependence on a narrow range of products with an unpromising outlook. They hope to increase their foreign-exchange earnings rapidly in order to pay for their ever-increasing imports of capital equipment and other goods essential for development. 

Some of the developing countries have already achieved a measure of success in this regard. Between 1960 and 1966 exports of manufactures from the developing countries to the countries of the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) 

increased yearly by about 15 to 16 percent. This relatively favorable picture, however, must be interpreted with caution. 

First, exports of manufactures from developing countries still account for only 15 percent of their total export earnings; secondly, the commodity composition is fairly narrow and concentrated on certain products such as textiles where they cannot expect large increases (for example, trade in cotton textiles is rather rigidly regulated at the present time by international agreement); and, finally, only a small number of the more than a hundred developing countries are currently participating in the recent rapid increase in exports of manufactures and semi-manufactures. 

The United States in 1967 continued to work with other countries in an effort to help solve the special trade problems of the developing countries, and thereby to contribute to their economic development. In the Kennedy Round, the United 

States made tariff concessions covering over $900 million of U.S. imports from the developing countries in 1964. Included in this figure is $45 million of tropical products on which the duties were eliminated as of January 1, 1968 (see also Section II.B above). Aside from eliminating artificial barriers to the imports of most tropical products, the United States has cooperated with other governments in examining the problems of particular primary products and the practical possibilities for stabilizing and improving market conditions (see Section 30

VII for a detailed report on the international commodity activities of the year). Another approach to the solution of commodity problems is through the prompt provision of balance-of-payments assistance to developing countries experiencing severe short falls in their export earnings due to factors beyond their control. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) administers such a compensatory financing facility. There has been greatly increased use of this facility since its liberalization in 1966. Last year the United 

States, as a member of an international group established under the auspices of the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD), continued to study a scheme for supplementing the IMF facility by the provision of longer-term financing to avoid the disruption caused by unforeseen short falls in export earnings.

In recognition of the growing requirements of the developing countries for expanded earnings from the export of manufactures and semi-manufactures, the United States has given support to their efforts to expand markets for such products through regional eco nomic integration (see Section III.A). 

At the Summit Meeting of the Organization of American States in Punta del Este in April 1967, President Johnson declared the willingness of the United States to explore with other developed countries the possibility of reaching a consensus with them on a system of generalized tariff preferences for the developing countries. The President's statement reflected not only a desire to help the developing countries expand their earnings of badlyneeded foreign exchange through increased exports, but also U.S. concern over the trend toward the proliferation of special trading arrangements which discriminate among developing countries. example of such an arrangement is that between the European Communities (EC) and eighteen African countries, former colonies of one or another of the six Community countries. Other countries are seeking similar arrangements. The Latin-American nations, which have no preferences in any developed-country market, consider themselves particularly disadvantaged by these selective arrangements. An There is in fact a serious danger of continued erosion of the most-favored-nation principle and further fragmentation of the world into discriminatory trading blocs as a result of the proliferation of special preferential trading arrangements. The most feasible alternative to this prospect is some system of generalized preferences extended uniformly and without reciprocity by all developed countries to all less-developed countries. 

Another objectionable aspect of the existing preferential arrangements is the reciprocal preferential advantage given to the products of the developed countries in the markets of their developing-country partners. These "reverse" preferences are disadvantageous to the developing countries granting them because they deprive them of a source of revenue and tend to raise 31

their import costs, a trend which they can ill afford.

In early 1966 an OECD Special Group on Trade with the De veloping Countries (consisting of the United States, the United Kingdom, France and the Federal Republic of Germany) began to explore some of the issues involved in trade preferences. This work was given new impetus by the President's Punta del Este declaration on preferences. In the fall of 1967 the Special Group completed a report outlining the principles related to such key issues as the designation of beneficiary countries, product coverage, safeguards and exceptions, duration, depth of tariff cuts, existing preferential arrangements, and parallel action by the developing countries and the Communist countries. The members of the Special 

Group were able to arrive at a consensus on all the main issues except that of "reverse" preferences, the phasing out of which as rapidly as possible was viewed by the United States as a key element in any generalized preference system. The Group's report was reviewed in early December by the OECD Ministers, who agreed that the broad lines of its work should serve as a common basis for delegations of OECD member Governments at the Second 

UNCTAD Conference at New Delhi in 1968.

D. 

East-West Trade 

Although the trade of many other Western countries with the countries of Eastern Europe has grown substantially in recent years, American trade with them has not. In fact, U.S. exports to Eastern Europe, excluding Yugoslavia, decreased slightly from $198 million in 1966 to $195 million in 1967. Preliminary figures indicate that Western European exports to Eastern Europe increased by more than 10% in that period.

Among the reasons for the U.S. failure to share in the growth of the Eastern European markets are the U.S. laws and regulations restricting trade with this region. We still prohibit the export to the Communist countries of a somewhat wider range of materials and equipment than do other Western countries, limit credits to them more strictly, enforce certain shipping requirements in our trade with them, and embargo the import of certain furs from the Soviet Union. Also, we levy the high tariff rates of the 1930 

Tariff Act on the products of these countries, except Poland and 

Yugoslavia. 

The policy of denying strategic materials to the Communist countries cannot be dismantled under present circumstances. 

Nevertheless, in accordance with the basic U.S. policy of encouraging peaceful relations with the countries of Eastern Europe, the Administration has taken steps toward increasing our trading opportunities in nonstrategic goods. These have included the reduction of individual export-licensing requirements, the despatch of government-organized or industry-sponsored trade groups to Poland, Rumania, Bulgaria, and the USSR, and partici- 32

pation in Eastern European trade fairs.

In addition, passage of the proposed East-West Trade Relations Act would enable the United 

States to negotiate commercial agreements with the Eastern European countries when such agreements serve the interests of the United States and would thus enhance our opportunities to engage in the peaceful exchange of goods.

The proposed East-West trade legislation, submitted to the Congress by the President on May 11, 1966, was based on the recom mendations of the Special Committee on Trade Relations with Eastern 

European countries and the Soviet Union. The major substantive provision is authority to extend nondiscriminatory tariff treatment to individual European Communist countries to which it is presently denied, when such extension is determined to be in the national interest. Until such legislation is passed, the President has no authority to negotiate with any of these countries for the advantages that the United States might gain from offering them the lower tariff rates which have been negotiated under reciprocal trade agreements over the last 30 years and which now apply to imports from virtually all other nations with which we trade. The 

President's lack of authority to negotiate on this matter sharply restricts his ability to use the great economic power of U.S. trade as a bargaining instrument.

Authority to offer removal of tariff discrimination would allow the United States to bargain for any of several important U.S. objectives. In addition to providing for increased access to export markets in Eastern Europe, the commercial agreements could also establish the basis for the settlement of commercial disputes, the facilitation of travel by businessmen, the protection of industrial property rights, the settlement of financial claims, and other objectives. Indirectly, closer trade relations between the 

United States and Eastern Europe could favorably influence the economic reforms which have recently been initiated in Eastern Europe and encourage trends in the national economic policies of the Communist countries which are in accord with our own best interests as well as theirs.

GENERAL AGREEMENT ON TARIFFS AND TRADE (GATT) Introduction 

The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) is an international agreement aimed at expanding trade and contributing to economic growth through the reduction of barriers to the flow of world trade and the elimination of discriminatory treatment in international commerce. The agreement consists of (1) schedules of tariff concessions made by the contracting parties and (2) a code of agreed general principles and rules to safeguard concessions against nullification or impairment through other restrictive devices and to supplement the schedules by relaxing other barriers to trade.

Since 1948 GATT has provided the principal international framework for conducting trade negotiations. The Kennedy Round, discussed above, is the sixth general round of such negotiations. 

In addition, GATT provides a forum for the multilateral discussion of commercial policy matters, and the rules of the agreement make provision for consultation on trade problems and for negotiations when the rights or benefits of one or more parties are nullified or impaired by the action of another contracting party. 

Countries which apply the GATT account for over 80 percent of the world's commerce. On December 31, 1967, these included 35

75 contracting parties. In addition, Iceland signed the protocol of accession, subject to ratification, in October 1967. Twelve other countries apply the rules of the agreement and participate in GATT meetings under various arrangements. 

The problems discussed in this section were dealt with in the framework of the GATT. B.

Industrial Products.-A Committee on Trade in Industrial Pro ducts was established to explore the opportunities for further trade liberalization in this area. The Committee was charged particularly with making an objective analysis of the tariff situation as it will exist when all Kennedy Round concessions have been fully implemented and with drawing up an inventory of nontariff and para-tariff barriers affecting international trade.

It was also agreed that, should problems arise requiring immediate attention, GATT procedures, including those under Article XXII (consultation), would provide an appropriate context for discussions aimed at reaching mutually acceptable solutions.

Trade and Development. The Removal of Foreign Quantitative Restrictions Against U.S. 

Exports 

In 1967 the United States continued its efforts to gain improved access to foreign markets through the removal of quantitative import restrictions in other countries. During the year, nine countries -- Austria, Denmark, Finland, France, the Federal 

Republic of Germany, Iceland, Norway, South Africa, and Spain further liberalized their trade by removing quota restrictions on a number of products. Some of these countries also increased the size of quotas for other products still subject to import controls.

Yugoslavia, in preparation for accession to the GATT, instituted a trade-expansionary program which included removal of quotas on an important segment of its imports. The United States also had some success in obtaining removal of barriers other than quantitative restrictions.

Products on which quotas were eliminated by one or more of the above countries included: canned or otherwise préserved fruits, vegetables and juices, dried fruit, vegetable and animal oils, grains products, other food preparations, typewriters, cash registers, adding machines, motor vehicles, computers, plywood and 39

veneers, furniture, other wood products, some textiles, leather and footwear, paper and paper products, outboard motors, electric generators and motors, internal-combustion engines, water turbines, pumps, fans, air-conditioners and many other industrial items. 

The quantitative restrictions which the United States has been attacking were established by many countries following World War II in an effort to conserve scarce foreign exchange by limiting expenditures for nonessential goods and services. In the early post-war years these restrictions did not seriously affect the overall level of American exports. As the countries which employed these measures recovered from the war and acquired substantial holdings of dollars, their restrictions became significant in terms of trade and the United States Government began to press for their elimination.

As in earlier years, American efforts in 1967 to obtain removal of these trade restrictions were centered in the GATT. It prohibits quantitative restrictions except under certain circumstances, the most important of which is when a country is experiencing balance of payments difficulties. It also provides a framework for multilateral and bilateral consultations and negotiations on barriers to trade. 

The United States played a leading role in GATT multilateral consultations held in 1967 with ten countries which still maintain import restrictions on balance-of-payments grounds: Chile, Finland, India, Indonesia, New Zealand, Pakistan, South Africa,

Spain, Tunisia, and Turkey. The purpose of these consultations, in which countries are required to justify their restrictions, is to assure that quotas are removed as rapidly as external financial positions permit.

In another international forum, the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, the United States encouraged other countries to liberalize their trade.

Many of the actions of other countries to remove or modify their restrictions resulted from bilateral discussions with representatives of foreign governments in Washington and abroad. 

In part at least as a result of these continuing efforts by the United States since the late 1950's, most other developed countries have by now removed quantitative restrictions on almost all industrial items and on many agricultural products of interest to U.S. exporters. The industrialized countries of Western 

Europe, for example, have eliminated virtually all quotas on major non-agricultural imports of interest to the United States. 

In Canada, the only remaining quantitative restrictions of significance in the industrial sector are on used aircraft and used automobiles. Japan, however despite the considerable progress it has made in the liberalization of quotas 

still 40

maintains a significant number of them in force. 

Developing countries, most of which have balance-of-payments problems, generally maintain strict controls over imports to assure that their scarce foreign exchange reserves are used to promote economic development.

Generally the developing countries permit the importation of needed raw materials and capital equipment while limiting that of consumer goods and luxuries. 

Some success has also been achieved in the removal of trade barriers other than quantitative restrictions. 

For example, the European Communities was considering regulations on food preservatives that would have prohibited the use on imported citrus fruits of three preservatives widely used in the United States. The regulations threatened over $20 million of our exports. After extended discussions with the United States, the 

Communities approved a regulation permitting the use of these preservatives within tolerances acceptable to U.S. exporters. 

In Canada, the liquor monopoly boards of many of the provincial governments failed to list bourbon whiskey and other 

American alcoholic beverages. U.S. bourbon and other whiskies are now available in every province of Canada and progress has also been made on problems related to the price mark-up policies of the liquor monopoly boards. 

New German sanitary regulations for pork imports threatened to cut off multi-million dollar U.S. pork exports to that country. 

Through modification of the regulations by the German Government and a cooperative State-Federal program in the United States, the United States is now regaining this important market for its pork. 

Similar problems with U.S. poultry-meat exports to Japan, Italy, and the Netherlands and whole-poultry exports to Switzerland were resolved by demonstrating to these countries that the 

American inspection system would safeguard their consumers.

In such negotiations, the countries concerned are to endeavor (ordinarily through making new tariff reductions) to maintain a general level of reciprocal and mutually advantageous concessions not less favorable to trade than the level existing prior to the negotiations. Introduction DEVELOPMENTS IN COMMODITY TRADE 

In 1967 there was more than the usual amount of international consultation and action on the market situation of particular primary commodities. By coincidence, there were negotiations aimed at replacing or extending four of the five existing commodity agreements (wheat, coffee, sugar and olive oil) as well as the continuation of the effort to achieve an agreement on cocoa. There were also periodic meetings of the commodity councils responsible for administering the existing agreements and consultations in study groups or ad hoc meetings regarding the conditions of trade in numerous other commodities. The United States participated actively in most of these discussions, apart from the meetings on the Tin and Olive Oil Agreements, of which it is not a member. 

Much of this activity was concerned with the impact of commodity-trade difficulties on the foreign-exchange earnings of developing countries and possibilities of measures which might stabilize and increase these earnings. Prices were weak for many of the products exported by developing countries and there were also, in some cases, growing problems of maintaining markets against competition from synthetic substitutes of the products of developed countries.

The root of much of this trouble lies in the limited and unbalanced development of the economies of the developing countries individually and as a group -- their over-dependence on a few primary products with a limited growth potential in world markets, their slowness in shifting resources into more promising lines of production (including production of food for rising home needs), and their inability to finance the holding of surplus stocks or to match the production and marketing techniques of competitors in developed countries. Important elements in the total approach to the commodity trade problems of developing countries are, therefore, financial aid to development and diversification of their economies, with particular emphasis at this time on development of the agricultural sector, and technical assistance in production and in export promotion. The case-by-case examination of particular commodity situations also continues to play an essential role in identifying the nature of a commodity-market problem and its causes and in facilitating national and international action to alleviate it. 45

International Grains Arrangement. -On August 18, 1967, after more than four years of negotiations, representatives from 52 countries reached agreement on the International Grains Arrangement. 

The basic pricing and food-aid provisions of the new arrangement were negotiated in the Kennedy Round. Following the conclusion of the Geneva talks in June 1967, representatives of 52 countries and the European Communities met at the International Wheat Conference in Rome from July 12 to August 18, 1967. This conference built upon the results of the Kennedy Round grains negotiations and opened the arrangement to the widest possible participation. 

The principal new features of the International Grains Arrangement are a more precise definition of the price range, spelled out in terms of major wheats moving in world trade; more effective procedures to be followed when prices reach the extremes of the price range; and a 4.5 million metric-ton multilateral food-aid program. 

The arrangement carries over with some modifications the provisions of the International Wheat Agreement, which has been in effect for the past 18 years. It obligates each importing country to purchase a specified minimum percentage of its commercial import requirements from member countries, and the exporting member countries collectively to supply the commercial requirements of importing member countries at prices consistent with the price range. generally has the effect of continuing the provisions of the International Wheat Agreement when prices reach the maximum point in the price range. It preserves and strengthens the adminis- It trative and institutional structure of the International Wheat 

Agreement. 

The Arrangement comprises two parts, a Wheat Trade Convention and a Food Aid Convention, both of which will run for three years, beginning July 1, 1968.

The Wheat Trade Convention sets minimum and maximum prices for 

14 major wheats moving in world trade. Minimum prices for other wheats may be established as the need arises by the Prices Review Committee, a new body which has the responsibility of making sure that the objectives of the Convention relating to price are carried out. For U.S. wheats, the minimum prices are generally about 20 cents a bushel higher than in the old Agreement. Maximum prices are set 40 cents above the minimum for each wheat, providing a range in which prices may fluctuate in response to supply and demand. Procedures to be followed when prices reach minimum or maximum levels are also detailed in the Convention. It is recognized that an exporting country may offer its wheat at competitive prices even though such prices might be below the minimum levels specified in the Arrangement, if consultative procedures have failed to find a basis for prices at or above the established minimums. 46

A section on guidelines for concessional transactions included in the Wheat Trade Convention reaffirms and strengthens the previously recognized principle that grains transactions made on concessional terms should avoid harmful interference with normal patterns of production and commercial trade. 

The Food Aid Convention commits developed countries, importers and exporters alike, to a 4.5 million metric-ton annual program of food aid to developing countries. 4.2 million metric tons have been subscribed. 

The United States will supply 42 percent about 1.9 million metric tons; the European Communities, 23 percent about 1.0 million metric tons; Canada, 11 percent; and Australia, the U.K., and Japan, 5 percent each. Other countries will contribute smaller amounts. Contributions can be in the form of wheat, coarse grains suitable for human consumption, or their cash equivalent. Contributing countries may specify the recipients of their donations, or they may channel them through an international organization such as the World Food Program of the United Nations. 

A Food Aid Committee, consisting of representatives of the contributing countries, will be established. Its primary function will be to review the overall functioning of the Convention, and especially its effect on food production in the recipient countries. 

2. Coffee.-The International Coffee Agreement was negotiated at the United Nations in 1962. The Senate gave its advice and consent to the ratification of the Agreement in May 1963. The Agreement entered into force provisionally in the summer of 1963 and definitively in December 1963. The implementing legislation (the 

International Coffee Agreement Act of 1965) to enable the United

States to meet all its obligations under the Agreement came into effect in May 1965.

The major aims of the Agreement are (1) to smooth out price fluctuations, (2) to provide a steady and growing earnings base to the coffee-producing countries as world consumption rises, (3) to maintain reasonable prices for coffee consumers, and (4) to bring about a long-term equilibrium between supply and demand by encouraging the transfer of resources from the production of excess coffee to other crops for which there is an unfilled demand. 

The heart of the Coffee Agreement is the system of export quotas for producing countries. The Coffee Council, the chief organ of the Agreement, meets each year in August to estimate world demand for coffee in the following year. In the light of that estimate and an estimate of what non-member countries will export, the Council sets the total annual export quota. The total is divided among the member countries in proportion to their basic quotas negotiated in the original Agreement. 47

In Over the years, the Agreement has evolved in a creative fashion to deal with its various problems. During its early years, some small and medium-sized producing members did not always observe export quota limitations. In the past two years, quota-control measures have been strengthened in order to close the loopholes, particularly with the initiation of a stamp system which drastically reduces the possibility of overshipment of quotas. practice, it has also been found necessary to make arrangements for altering export quotas during the year and to adopt additional measures to adjust supplies to market demands. The present selective method for adjusting quotas in response to price changes of each of the four types of coffee is an important innovation making the Agreement more responsive to market demands and insuring consuming members adequate supplies of the various types of coffee.

The Coffee Agreement has achieved its major objectives. earnings for producers have stabilized at about $2.2 billion as compared with about $1.8 billion in the years immediately preceding the Agreement. While average U.S. import prices in 1967 were about 

10 percent higher than in the two years preceding the signing of the Agreement, they were almost 30 percent lower than during the 1950's. The stability provided by the Agreement has brought significant political and social benefits to the producing countries. 

It has also served the foreign policy interests of the United States. 

The Agreement expires September 30, 1968, as does the legislation implementing U.S. participation in it. In the hope of having a new and strengthened Agreement for the consideration of the Senate early in 1968, the United States encouraged an early start of the renegotiation process in 1967 and actively sought modifications in the Agreement in order to improve and strengthen its operation. 

Revised basic quotas were agreed on late in 1967. 4. Sugar.-The present International Sugar Agreement, which was concluded in 1953 and extended twice, currently provides only a forum for discussion and for the collection of statistics. An UNCTAD negotiating conference in late 1965 to revive the Agreement was unsuccessful.

Notwithstanding differences on major issues which continued to emerge during these discussions, a number of developing countries, faced with the prospect of continued low price levels in the world sugar market, pressed 49

2. Hard fibers.-Sisal, henequen and abaca are facing price difficulties and declining demand because of the competition of synthetic substitutes. Cotton Textiles

The Long Term Arrangement Regarding International Trade in 

Cotton Textiles (LTA) was negotiated in 1962 by the GATT Cotton Textiles Committee (CTC). The United States proposed this arrangement with a view to insuring a more orderly development of trade than had occurred in the 1950's, when the United States, as one of the few open markets, bore the brunt of the sharply-rising exports from new suppliers in the developing areas. The LTA provides for growing access to world markets for cotton-textile exports from developing countries, while providing for avoidance of disruption of established markets in importing countries. 

Specifically, it requires developed countries with import-quota restrictions inconsistent with GATT to reduce such restrictions progressively and authorizes importing countries to initiate restraint on disruptive imports of particular products from parti- 52

cular countries. Unilateral action to control imports may be taken under Article 3, which provides minimum restraint levels determined by a formula based on recent performance. These restraints must be increased by at least 5 percent if they are continued for more than a year. In special circumstances, however, growth may be less than 

5 percent for the first year a restraint is renewed. The LTA also provides for bilateral agreements between exporting and importing countries. This has become the principal means for regulating imports of cotton textiles by the United States, and 22 bilateral agreements are now in effect. 

The GATT Cotton Textiles Committee, which is responsible for overseeing the operation of the LTA, held two meetings in 1967. 

Both were primarily concerned with the consideration of a three year extension of the LTA through September 30, 1970. Discussions of the future of the LTA had begun both in the GATT and bilaterally in 1965 and continued in 1966. By the end of the latter year, the 

United States had entered bilateral discussions on this issue with most of its suppliers. In both the GATT and the bilateral discussions, the United States urged extension of the LTA in its original form as a basis on which world trade in cotton textiles could grow.

At the same time, the United States offered extensive liberalization of access to its markets.

At the CTC meeting in September 1966 it was ascertained that extensive bilateral consultations between exporting and importing countries remained to be carried out before a consensus on LTA extension was possible. This meeting was recessed until November 28, by which time it was hoped that the necessary consultations could be completed. However, considerable slippage occurred in bilateral consultation schedules, with the result that the CTC was still unable to reach any decision on extension at the November meeting and another meeting was scheduled for March 1967. At this meeting it became apparent that there was a clear sentiment for extension, but it also developed that a number of major exporting and importing countries still had not completed bilateral consultations on market access and were therefore not prepared to approve extension at that meeting. After the countries concerned were pressed to conclude the requisite consultations as quickly as possible, the March meeting also adjourned without action.

The CTC reconvened in April 1967 to consider a Draft Protocol extending the LTA in its original form for a period of three years beginning October 1, 1967. Subject to receipt of notifications from Sweden and the European Communities approving two minor modifications in the draft text, the CTC approved the Protocol. due course, all the LTA participant countries accepted it. In CTC participants, in view of the two meetings already held in 1967 and of the bilateral discussions still under way in September, decided to defer the normal annual review session until late 1968. 

It was considered that experience with the many newly-amended bi- 53

lateral agreements would, by that time, afford a better perspective regarding international trade in cotton textiles. C. 

Cases Under the Escape-Clause and Adjustment Assistance Provisions of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 

1. Tariff adjustment or adjustment assistance cases.-Under the 

Trade Expansion Act of 1962, the President is authorized to increase tariffs or provide adjustment assistance to an industry, firm, or group of workers after an investigation by the Tariff Commission and upon its report that, as a result in major part of concessions granted under trade agreements, an article is being imported into the United States in such increased quantities as to be the major factor in causing or threatening to cause serious injury to the industry, firm, or workers producing a like or directly competitive product.

During 1967 the Tariff Commission completed one investigation involving this provision. It resulted in a unanimous finding by the Commission that increased imports of eyeglass frames were not due, in major part, to trade agreement concessions. Consequently, the firms and workers in the domestic industry were found not to qualify for either higher tariffs or adjustment assistance under 

Section 301 (b) of the Trade Expansion Act. The investigation was instituted on April 12, 1967, at the request of a labor organization, which sought higher tariffs on imported eyeglass frames. The 

Commission's report was issued on October 6, 1967. 

At the end of the year, the Tariff Commission had in process five other investigations instituted during 1967, two under Section 301 (b) at the request of industries seeking increased tariffs on imports of barber chairs and broomcorn, and three under Section 301 (c) at the request of two individual firms in the barber-chair industry and of workers in a mosaic tile plant seeking determinations of eligibility to apply for adjustment assistance.

U.S.-Philippine Talks on Laurel-Langley Agreement 

During the September 1966 talks between President Johnson and President Marcos of the Philippine Republic it was agreed that preliminary discussions should be held the following year to consider a new basis for regulating U.S.-Philippine economic relations after the expiration of the Laurel-Langley Agreement in 1974. Each 

President designated a discussion team which together formed the Joint Preparatory Committee for Discussion of Concepts Underlying a New Instrument to Replace the Laurel-Langley Trade Agreement. 

After considerable preparation including public hearings held in 

Washington,. D. C., on October 2, 1967 the Joint Committee held its first meeting in the Philippines, November 20-30, 1967. 

The two teams discussed a wide range of problems in the trade and investment field. In general, the Filipinos expressed a strong desire to continue and enlarge their preferential tariff relationship with the U.S. after 1974, but on a nonreciprocal basis. The U.S. team noted that such preferential treatment would be difficult to justify to other friendly countries and would be contrary to U.S. efforts to seek a phasing-out of all existing discriminatory tariff preferences extended by other developed countries. The two teams discussed at some length problems arising from Philippine regulations controlling foreign investment and the discriminatory treatment of foreign investors. Also discussed were various proposals regarding trade in specific commodities such as sugar and coconut oil.

At the conclusion of the meeting, the Joint Committee declared itself to be a continuing consultative body to be reconvened at the earliest possible date, probably sometime in 1968, to consider and attempt to resolve outstanding substantive issues, thereby making possible the negotiation of a new instrument to replace the Laurel- 

Langley Agreement.

I 

I. INTRODUCTION 

The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) is the principal international trade agreement to which the United States and other major trading nations adhere. The GATT developed out of post-World War II proposals by the 

United States for a constructive, non-discriminatory solution to international trade problems. The number of countries which have become contracting parties to the resulting international trade agreement has grown from 23 to 75, and a number of other countries have acceded provisionally or maintain special relations with the GATT.

The GATT comprises four essential elements. First, a code of agreed rules and principles governing the import and export trade practices of the contracting parties. Second, the tariff schedules annexed to the General Agreement, which represent tariff concessions negotiated among participating countries and bound to the contracting parties under the provisions of the GATT. Third, periodic meetings of representatives of the CONTRACTING PARTIES, which provide a broad international forum for the discussion and settlement of problems arising in the conduct of international trade. Finally, the new part IV on Trade and Development, added to the text of the General Agreement in 1965, which makes special provisions for dealing with the trade problems of the less-developed countries.

Sessions of the CONTRACTING PARTIES have been held approximately every year since January 1, 1948, when the GATT came into effect. The Sessions serve the legal function of convening the contracting parties to undertake joint action as provided in the text of the General Agreement, and as such represent the highest legislative authority in the GATT. They have also come to be recognized as the principal forum for consideration and resolution of international trade problems. 

The United States has participated in every Session. 

Between Sessions, the work of the CONTRACTING PARTIES is carried on by the Council of Representatives of the Contracting Parties, committees, expert groups, and working parties. 

II. 

SUMMARY OF THE 24TH SESSION The CONTRACTING PARTIES to the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade 

(GATT) held their 24th General Session at Geneva, Switzerland, from November 9 through 24, 1967.

Throughout the Session, the attention of the CONTRACTING PARTIES was focused primarily on the development of the future program of work for the GATT. 

During the closing days of the Session, many of the contracting parties were represented by cabinet-level trade officials, to complete the negotiations and discussions on this important part of the Session's work. The GATT had passed a milestone in its program for the expansion of international trade with the successful conclusion of the Kennedy Round of tariff negotiations on June 30, 1967. In their closing communique, the CONTRACTING PARTIES agreed that the major problems remaining after the Kennedy Round which required attention were those barriers to trade in industrial products which would remain after the Kennedy Round concessions were implemented; the problems of trade in agricultural products; and the special trade problems of the developing countries. 

There were differences of opinion among the contracting parties as to what the role of the GATT should be in dealing with these problems in the immediate future, particularly in the agricultural field. A work program was adopted, however, which would permit meaningful study and discussion in all of these 71

important areas, and which would facilitate joint action on specific problems when such action seemed desirable. 

As is customary, the CONTRACTING PARTIES reviewed developments in the formation of those customs unions and free trade areas which include contracting parties in their membership. Particular attention was given to the agreements between the European Economic Community and its associated states, since there is still disagreement between those countries participating in these arrangements and many other contracting parties as to whether they are consistent with the provisions of the GATT. 

The CONTRACTING PARTIES also reviewed a number of reports submitted by certain contracting parties under the terms of waivers of the provisions of the GATT which had been granted to deal with particular trade problems. Included among them was the waiver granted to the United States authorizing import restrictions on agricultural products under the terms of Section 22 of the 

Agricultural Adjustment Act. 

Certain questions arising out of the Kennedy Round negotiations were considered, including a proposal for defining initial negotiating rights in Kennedy Round tariff concessions, which was adopted by the CONTRACTING PARTIES. 

It was agreed in principle to establish a Working Party to consider the question of subsidies and countervailing duties on goods imported at less than fair value, although agreement could not be reached on terms of reference for the group at the Session.

A number of other items related to tariff and non-tariff barriers to trade and export subsidies were also taken up by the CONTRACTING PARTIES. The Session again reviewed developments in commodity trade, with particular attention to the problems in cotton textile trade and disposals of commodity surpluses. A com- plaint by Malawi on the U.S. subsidy on tobacco exports was considered, although the report of the consultations was inconclusive as the effects of the subsidy could not be determined.

The work of the Committee on Trade and Development since the 23rd Session was reviewed by the CONTRACTING PARTIES. This Committee has the responsibility of implementing the provisions of Part IV of the General Agreement on Trade and 

Development. The Director General reported on his negotiations with the Secretary General of UNCTAD on the establishment of a joint International Trade 

Center, to be based on the existing GATT Trade Center. The CONTRACTING PARTIES 

approved this joint arrangement.

The 24th Session marked the 20th year of the existence of the General 

Agreement. Mr. Eric Wyndham White indicated his wish to retire from his post as 

Director General after having served in that capacity throughout the 20 years of the GATT's existence. The CONTRACTING PARTIES reluctantly accepted his resignation, and appointed Ambassador Olivier Long of Switzerland to succeed him.

In the first meeting of the Session, the Director General reported on the work of the GATT in the twenty years it had been in existence, including the recently concluded Kennedy Round of trade negotiations, and put forward some suggestions concerning the appropriate role and functions of the GATT in the future. In his opening remarks from the chair, Ambassador Lall of India, Chairman of the CONTRACTING PARTIES, had addressed himself to these same themes, stressing in particular the need to give urgent attention to solving the difficulties faced by the developing countries in the commercial field. 

Throughout the Session, the statements and discussions of the delegations related to the Programme for Expansion of International Trade reflected the various points of view of their governments on areas and problems which they considered appropriate for consideration by the GATT in its work program. A 

Drafting Group was established to coordinate and reconcile these views in a 'draft communique, with major areas of disagreement passed to the Ministeriallevel representatives for negotiation. 

The final text adopted by the CONTRACTING PARTIES reaffirmed their confidence in the General Agreement as the contractual basis for trading relationships and their determination to continue to cooperate in the trade field within the framework of the Agreement. It reviewed the achievements of the 

GATT in pursuit of the objectives of the Agreement over the past twenty years, and set out the future work program for the GATT. The main features of this program are: 

A. Industrial Products. 

A Committee on Trade in Industrial Products was established to explore the opportunities for further trade liberalization in this area. The Committee was charged particularly with making an objective analysis of the tariff situation as it will exist when all Kennedy Round concessions have been fully implemented and drawing up an inventory of nontariff and para-tariff barriers affecting international trade. B. 

Agriculture. An Agriculture Committee was established to examine the problems in the agricultural sector, to prepare the way for subsequent consideration of positive solutions which could be mutually accepted by all contracting parties concerned. It was also agreed that should problems arise requiring immediate attention, GATT procedures, including those under 

Article XXII (Consultation) would provide an appropriate context for discussions aimed at arriving at mutually acceptable solutions.

C. 

Trade of Developing Countries. It was agreed that a number of topics of special interest to the developing countries would be undertaken or continued by the Committee on Trade and Development. These include problems related to trade in tropical products, import restrictions on products of interest to the developing countries, and the expansion of trade among the developing countries. It was noted that a number of developed countries (including the United States) had agreed to implement Kennedy Round concessions on certain products of interest to the developing countries without staging, and the CONTRACTING PARTIES urged further action along these lines. 73

The International Trade Center (see Sec. V.b. below) was asked to assist the less-developed countries to take advantage of the new market opportunities opened up by Kennedy Round concessions. 

The full text of the future work program adopted by the CONTRACTING 

PARTIES is attached as Annex A. 

In formulating the work program, it was generally agreed that it would be necessary to evaluate the effects of the Kennedy Round tariff reductions more fully before new major initiatives in the trade field could be undertaken. 

The program was therefore designed to prepare the ground for possible negotiations in the future and as a means of dealing with the more immediate problems which may arise. Despite this basic agreement, there proved to be substantial differences of opinion on the specific role to be played by the GATT, particularly in the field of agriculture. Some countries--particularly New Zealand and Australia--sought a committee which would lead to the early solution of problems in agricultural trade, while others--especially the EEC, UK, and Japan--clearly would have preferred nothing at all. While the Agriculture 

Committee was given a mandate for a broad study only, the United States believes that this general study program, coupled with the procedure for consultations on products for which urgent problems exist, gives the GATT sufficient flexibility in the agricultural field for constructive work in the months ahead. Following the adoption of the work program, the Chairman noted that the discussions in the Session indicated that there seemed to be a particularly urgent problem with respect to dairy products, and it was agreed that the CONTRACTING PARTIES would have recourse to Article XXII to consult on dairy problems. The United States representative subsequently noted that there were problems related to other products which were of immediate concern to this country, and the United States Government would pursue these problems in accordance with GATT procedures. While the recommendations for the Industrial Committee were adopted without significant debate, some countries were disappointed that a proposal by the Director General for a sectoral approach to liberalization of industrial trade had not been adopted.

The recommendations with respect to the trade of less-developed countries were worked out primarily in the Committee on Trade and Development. 

It was generally felt that the work undertaken in the GATT should be directed toward significantly increasing the developing countries' participation in world trade, and that this work should complement that done by UNCTAD, rather than duplicate it. It is believed that the proposals adopted by the CONTRACTING PARTIES will permit the GATT to continue to play an important role in areas in which it has competence and experience.

The work undertaken by the CONTRACTING PARTIES will complement the trade policy study which the President directed Ambassador Roth to undertake within the United States Government. The GATT program will also facilitate coordination of the United States study with any similar efforts which may be undertaken by other countries.

TRADE OF LESS-DEVELOPED COUNTRIES A. Committee on Trade and Development 

The Committee on Trade and Development was primarily responsible for developing that part of the GATT work program related to its own activities. 

In a series of meetings preceding the Session, various proposals for action on behalf of the less-developed countries were considered, and a number of specific recommendations were included in the Committee's Report. These recommendations were taken into account by the Drafting Group which drew up the work program for the GATT, and are largely reflected in the final text of the communique (Annex A, Part C). 

1. Report of the Committee. The Report of the Committee reviewed its activities since the 23rd Session. It drew attention particularly to the eighth session of the Committee, held in Punta del Este, Uruguay, in January 1967, which concerned itself primarily with examining the progress achieved in implementing Part IV (Trade and Development) of the GATT and the Ministerial conclusions regarding the participation of the developing countries in the Kennedy Round. It made a number of suggestions and recommendations, designed to advance the interests of the developing countries in the trade negotiations. 

In July, following the completion of the negotiations, the Committee established an ad hoc group to assess the results of the Kennedy Round for the developing countries and reached preliminary agreement on other programs, which were approved by the CONTRACTING PARTIES at the 24th Session through the adoption of the communique.

In presenting the Committee's Report in the plenary, its interim chairman called the attention of the contracting parties to the conclusions of the Ad Hoc Group on the Assessment of Kennedy Round Results, which noted that some members of the Group believed that the most important problems of the developing countries taken up in the negotiations remained unsolved. Some members were also of the view that while the Kennedy Round had resulted in some tariff reductions on products of interest to the developing countries, these results had fallen far short of the objectives established for the negotiations. The Group identified specific problems which arose in the 

Kennedy Round, including: import duties and fiscal charges maintained by some countries on tropical products; protective considerations which had prevented 75

greater liberalization of both agricultural and industrial products; the desire of some developed countries to maintain preference margins granted to certain less-developed countries; the fact that trade in many products was restricted by non-tariff barriers rather than tariffs; the linking of action in some instances to a requirement that similar action be taken by other developed countries, which was not forthcoming; technical problems related to tariff nomenclature that does not differentiate between products produced in developing countries and those produced in industrial countries--in many cases an industrial country would have been the principal beneficiary if a tariff concession had been made on a product of interest to developing countries; the exclusion of some items from the negotiations because the principal supplier was not participating in the negotiations; and the exclusion of some industrial items considered "sensitive" by certain developed countries. The preoccupation of developed countries with major problems in the negotiations with other industrial countries was also cited as a factor preventing greater attention to the interests of the less-developed countries. The chairman of the Committee also drew attention to the recommendations in the Report for the reactivation of the Group on Tropical Products, the advance implementation of Kennedy Round concessions of special interest to the developing countries, the removal of import restrictions affecting exports of the developing countries, trade negotiations among the developing countries and the economic problems of the Chad. 

2. Economic Problems of the Chad. At the request of the representative of the Chad, a special sub-item was inscribed on the agenda to permit the 

CONTRACTING PARTIES to direct explicit attention to the economic problems of his country. Early in 1967, the Committee on Trade and Development had undertaken a review of the difficulties facing the Chad as a result of its unfavorable export position, particularly in light of a downward trend in the world price of cotton, its major export. The discussion enabled the Chad to draw the attention of the CONTRACTING PARTIES to the conclusions of the Working Party which noted, inter alia, that as a practical matter, the Chad must remain heavily dependent on cotton for its export earnings for some time. Since it is a marginal producer and cannot influence world prices, however, it can improve its export position only by improving its production techniques and by developing its extremely poor transportation connections with the sea. In the longer run, the Chad should try to diversify its exports. 

In view of the fact that the economy of the Chad is largely at the subsistence level, both financial and technical assistance from external sources would be needed to achieve these goals. A number of countries, including the United States, expressed their sympathy with the problems faced by the Chad. In conclusion, the Chairman of the CONTRACTING PARTIES expressed the hope that, in transmitting the GATT report on the Chad to governments and international organizations, specific action might be stimulated which would yield results beneficial to the Chad. International Trade Center 

The International Trade Center was established by the GATT in 1964 for the purpose of assisting the developing countries in the promotion of their exports. 

Its efforts have been enthusiastically received by both developed and developing countries, and the demand for its services has grown steadily in the few years of its operation. The CONTRACTING PARTIES reviewed specifically two questions related to the Trade Center; first, the report of the expert advisory group which is convened annually to provide technical guidance for the Center, and second, a proposal to merge the Trade Center with certain export promotion activities of UNCTAD and establish a joint GATT/UNCTAD International Trade Center. 76

1. Report of the Advisory Group. 

The expert advisory group, in which the United States actively participates, held its fourth meeting in June of 1967. The members of the group reviewed the functions performed by the Center and concluded that there should be increased emphasis on providing technical assistance to the developing countries in the establishment and operation of their export promotion services, and that additional training in this area would be particularly useful.

In discussing the report at the 24th Session, the Director General referred to the great interest of the CONTRACTING PARTIES in the Center to date, and said that whatever might happen to it in the future (as a result of the discussions with UNCTAD) he would not wish to see any reduction of this interest or any disruption in the momentum and efficiency of the Trade Center. 

Many representatives reaffirmed their governments' support of the Trade Center, and some of the developed countries pointed to contributions of training facilities, personnel, and other assistance that they had voluntarily provided to the Center as evidence of their support. 2. 

Negotiations for the Establishment of a Joint GATT/UNCTAD Center. 

With an agreement between the Secretary General of UNCTAD and the Executive Secretaries of the United Nations Regional Commissions to form a United Nations Export Promotion Programme, there was general concern among governments and in the Secretariats of GATT and UNCTAD that a duplication of effort in the trade promotion field might consequently occur. The two Secretariats were urged to seek ways of avoiding such duplication in their export promotion services. 

The Secretary General of UNCTAD suggested that this might best be done by joining together in the operation of the Trade Center. The Director General of the

CONTRACTING PARTIES. 

In introducing his proposals, the Director General noted that in his discussions with the Secretary General of UNCTAD there had been two principal objectives: first, that the two organizations should mobilize all resources available to them to meet the urgent trade and development needs of the developing countries, and second, that these resources should not be wasted by unnecessary duplication of work. While the joint supervision of the Center posed some problems, he was confident that it was possible to carry the proposals out. Many governments indicated their support of the Director General's efforts in this regard, and it was agreed that a Working Party would meet during the Session to consider the proposals in detail. UNCTAD International Trade Center as of January 1, 1968, was endorsed by the 

Working Party with a recommendation that working arrangements for 1968 be made between the Director General of GATT and Secretary General of UNCTAD. The CONTRACTING PARTIES should then review the operation of the joint Center either at the 25th Session or before the end of 1968, when definitive administrative and financial procedures would be adopted. The recommendations of the Working 

Party were approved by the CONTRACTING PARTIES and a number of representatives, including some of the Ministers who attended the Session, expressed their satisfaction with the cooperative arrangements agreed to by the Secretariats. 77

C. 

Fellowship Program and Technical Assistance 

The GATT's fellowship program, which is administered with financial assistance from the United Nations Development Programme, provides training in commercial policy problems for government trade officials from the developing countries. C. 

Application of Article XXXV to Japan 

Article XXXV permits the non-application of the General Agreement as between any contracting party and any other contracting party if they have not entered into tariff negotiations with each other and if either of the contracting parties, at the time either becomes a contracting party, does not consent to such application.

There are a number of countries which continue to invoke Article XXXV against Japan; almost all of them are developing countries. Since the last session three countries, Guyana, Barbados, and Trinidad and Tobago, disinvoked their reliance on the article. 

At the session Japan referred to the item as "a most disagreeable matter" and expressed appreciation for the steps of disinvocation taken by the abovementioned countries and by the United Kingdom on behalf of a number of its dependent territories. Japan appealed to the good sense of those countries which still do not apply the General Agreement in their relations with Japan to do so and thereby remove this impediment to a better trading relationship. Nigeria pointed out that it now applies the GATT fully to Japan on a de facto basis, and expressed the hope that its current efforts to take the legislative steps to disinvoke Article XXXV de jure would succeed before the next session. The United States expressed pleasure that a number of countries had disinvoked Article XXXV since the last session and urged countries which had not yet normalized their GATT relationships with Japan to do so as soon as possible. A number of other countries lent similar support to the Japanese position.

Review of the Work of the CONTRACTING PARTIES Through the Last Two Decades and Conclusions on Their Future Work Programme Adopted by the CONTRACTING PARTIES on 24 November 1967 Annex A 

During their twenty-fourth session, the CONTRACTING PARTIES to the 

General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade reviewed their work of the past twenty years and reached certain conclusions on their future work programme. Many contracting parties were represented by Ministers during the concluding days of the session. 1. 

The CONTRACTING PARTIES reaffirmed their confidence in the General Agree ment as the contractual basis for their trading relationships and their determination to continue to co-operate in the trade field within the framework of the Agreement. 2. 

3. The CONTRACTING PARTIES' discussion focused essentially on the GATT Programme for Expansion of International Trade. This Programme was inaugurated at a meeting of Ministers in 1958 and covered tariff reduction, trade in agricultural products and the trade problems of developing countries. It has provided, and continues to provide, the broad basis and impetus for the activi ties of the CONTRACTING PARTIES in these three fields. 

The CONTRACTING PARTIES put particular stress on the progress made over the past twenty years toward the attainment of the objectives of the GATT and the outstanding contribution that the General Agreement had made to the expansion of world trade and to international cooperation in the trade field generally. They welcomed in particular the substantial results of the Kennedy 

Round of trade negotiations which had been the most comprehensive and, in general, the most successful ever undertaken. 4. 5.

The CONTRACTING PARTIES noted that significant progress had been made in the field of agricultural trade. Nonetheless they recognized that problems remained and there were some sectors, of particular importance to contracting parties relying heavily on exports of agricultural products, in which progress achieved under the General Agreement had not matched that achieved in relation to industrial trade generally. The CONTRACTING PARTIES reaffirmed, in the context of the further pursuit of the objectives of the General Agreement, their determination to pursue solutions to the problems in the agricultural field. 6.

The CONTRACTING PARTIES recognized the pioneering work in the field of the trade problems of the developing countries that had been done by the 

CONTRACTING PARTIES and welcomed the progressively increasing attention being paid by them to these problems. They further took note of the adoption of 

Part IV of the Agreement. However, the results of the earlier efforts and the 

Kennedy Round had left many of the trade problems of most developing countries unresolved. They agreed that a maximum effort was needed in the GATT, and elsewhere, directed towards the expansion of the export earnings of the developing countries and thus towards the early resolution of their problems. 7. The CONTRACTING PARTIES agreed that an essential first task was to secure the full implementation of all the results agreed upon in the Kennedy Round. 

However, they considered it understandable that such a far-reaching reduction 92

of barriers as had been achieved in the Kennedy Round could create problems in some cases. In their view the appropriate way to deal with any such difficulties would be through internal measures of adjustment and not through the creation of new obstacles to international trade. Where problems arose, the established GATT consultation procedures were available to facilitate constructive cooperation between countries. It was recognized that any significant trade restrictive measures would be likely to spread, particularly if introduced by one or more major trading countries. This, in turn, would threaten to undo what had been achieved in the field of trade liberalization and jeopardize future progress. 

8. The CONTRACTING PARTIES took note of the interest in the work of the GATT of countries with centrally-planned economies and welcomed the accession of Poland to the General Agreement and the participation of observers from 

Bulgaria, Hungary and Rumania in the work of the CONTRACTING PARTIES. They agreed that the CONTRACTING PARTIES' approach to the question of trade relations with countries with centrally-planned economies should continue to be on a pragmatic, country-by-country basis. 9.

The CONTRACTING PARTIES reaffirmed that the liberalization of world trade by means of negotiations remains the primary objective of the General Agreement, but noted that, in the light of the recent conclusion of the Kennedy Round, no new major initiatives for a multilateral and comprehensive move forward could reasonably be expected in the near future. Whilst in the light of the foregoing, work undertaken at this time would be without commitment on the part of governments both as to its outcome and in particular as to any future negotiations, the CONTRACTING PARTIES recognized that it was important to proceed to prepare for further advances, with full regard to the objectives and provisions of the General Agreement, within the Programme for Expansion of 

International Trade. Further, the CONTRACTING PARTIES agreed that there was urgent need for additional positive measures for the expansion of trade and improvement of the trade position of developing countries.
