Southern and Ponderosa Pine by 

James M. Jarrett Economic Advisor to the Chairman Introduction 

Under section 722 of the Internal Revenue Code incorporated taxpayers may, under specified conditions, be entitled to relief from the excess profits tax which was in effect during World War II. Acting upon the provisions of this section, a considerable number of corporations engaged in the production of lumber have filed claims for relief. With few exceptions, these corporations contend that their earnings were depressed during the base period (the years 1936-1939) because, they allege, their industry was subject to a profits cycle differing, materially in length and amplitude from the general business cycle.1 This report is directed to an examination of the factors bearing upon the merits of their contention.

In appraising the merit of the lumber producers' contention it becomes necessary to determine whether the industry's base period profits were in fact depressed and, if so, whether the depression in profits was caused by cyclical rather than other, non-cyclical, factors. Low or depressed profits may, and frequently do, result from a variety of noncyclical causes that are relatively permanent in character. They may also occur, of course, during the trough of cyclical fluctuations.

The term "cycle," which looms so large in this consideration, connotes regularly recurring phenomena.1 As stated by one of the leading lumber producers, "the profit cycle is the result of that combination of factors which are characteristically repetitious in an industry." The problem is essentially that of distinguishing between cyclical or recurrent influences and non-cyclical or comparatively permanent changes. Its solution requires a close observation of the actual fluctuations of profits over a considerable period of time and, of no less importance, a reasonable explanation of the causes of these fluctuations. 

In undertaking to distinguish between cyclical and other factors affecting profits, this study reviews the development of the industry with attention focused on the principal économic forces acting upon the production and marketing of lumber. Also, careful examination is made of the actual behavior of profits during the 18 years 1922-1939, the maximum period which includes the base period and during which the industry was free from abnormal war influences. Inasmuch as most of the corporations involved were engaged in producing either southern pine lumber or ponderosa pine lumber, special consideration has been given to these divisions of the industry. From an examination of the industry as a whole, other divisions are found to be less directly affected by variant cycle influences, Under the law, interest in the development of the lumber industry' and the behavior of profits of the lumber producers ends with December 31, 1939. Accordingly, this report carries the analysis only through the base period and the outlook as of that time.

They supply more lumber than the nearest competitor, Douglas-fir, which, however, was approaching them in quantity up to the base period. 

The wood of the southern pines varies widely in quality, Typical longleaf and slash are heavy, hard, and resinous, while typical shortleaf is comparatively light in weight, soft, and has little resin. However, the wood of each species is subject to considerable variation. Loblolly may approach longleaf and slash in weight and hardness or may be comparatively light in weight, soft, and more like typical shortleaf. 

Southern pine has long been the world's leading source of naval stores. In more recent years it has become the leading United States source of pulpwood. With respect to lumber and sawmill products, shortleaf and loblolly pine lumber are used principally for building materials such as interior finish, ceiling, frames and sash, wainscoating, weather boarding, sheathing, subflooring and joists, and for boxes and crates, agricultural implements, motor vehicles, low-grade furniture, caskets and burial boxes, woodenware and novelties, and for slack cooperage. 

Those pines are used also for heavier construction purposes when treated with a proservative to increase their resistance to decay or insect attack. 

Longleaf and slash pine are used extensively in factory, warehouse, bridge, trestle, and dock construction in the form of stringers, beams, posts, joists, and piling. They are also widely used for flooring, and in lesser amounts for car construction and repair, and in ship and boat building Being well adapted to the many uses to which softwood lumber is put, southern pine is marketed in the Eastern, Central and Lake States and is exported to a number of foreign countries. In 1938, over 40 percent of the total was shipped outside the Southern States. (See Table 1.) 

Southern pine, therefore, is affected directly by the competitive conditions common to softwood lumber in both the United States and foreign markets. 

Ponderosa pine

Lumber produced from ponderosa pine consists of, yard, factory, and shop grades with a large proportion of box grades. None of this lumber consists of structural grades and sizes, which account for a substantial part of the output of Douglas fir and southern pine. Thicknesses of 6/4 inch and under predominate, but lumber up to 16/4 or over in thickness is produced for factory and shop use. Yard grades consist chiefly of finish and common boards, although some dimension is included. 

Ponderosa pine is used in factory and shop for sash, doors, frames, interior and exterior trim and general millwork; for cabinet work such as bookcases, shelving, and built-in installations; for paneling, lawn and garden furniture, trellis and fence, sheathing and roofing boards, industrial cuttings, farm use, boxes, crates and other containers. About 30 per cent is generally considered representative of the proportion of ponderosa pine that is box grade, although the proportion actually so graded varies materially among mills in different locations, In southern Oregon, for example, there is a large production of box lumber and shook for the California fruit and vegetable industry, some of the mills putting 30 to 40 percent of this output into shook. Similarly, the mills in central 

Washington and northern California produce large quantities of shook. 

Moreover, with changes in market demand, the mills vary the amount of lumber that goes into shook. As a rule, the demand for shook is comparatively stable and does not fluctuate with the general demand for lumber. 

In times of relatively great demand for common and shop grades of lumber, box grades may be sold for these purposes. Conversely, with a slack demand for lumber generally, shook mills may use other than box grades. 

About half of the total output of ponderosa pine is shipped to general markets in the Midwest and East. The remainder is either manufactured in the producing region and, in converted form, shipped to general markets, or is used for construction or other direct uses in the general markets of the Western States.1/ In 1938 local deliveries for the Western Pine 

Region as a whole, including intermill transfers, represented about 26 percent of the total shipments by producing mills. Shipments to destinations within the region, including the local deliveries, amounted to about 52 percent of the total. Shipments to destinations outside the Western Pine 

Region were made chiefly to Lake, Central, and Middle Atlantic States. 

Small quantities went to the New England and Southern States. (See Table 1.) 

Although ponderosa pine has certain uses for which it is particularly well adapted and others, notably structural use, for which it is not suited, it, or the products made from it, is competivie over a wide range of uses with other major softwoods. The most direct competition is in the field of common lumber for such uses as sheathing and roofing boards, fencing and similar work where price is the prime consideration. Moreover, about half of the output moves directly into the lumber markets outside the producing area. And a substantial part of the remainder, after further processing, is also sold in other areas. Consequently, the marketing of ponderosa pine, like the marketing of southern pine, is affected to a very substantial extent by factors common to the general market for softwood lumber. The competitive position of ponderosa pinc lumber in relation to other lumber in the Middle West and Eastern United States markets was described in considerable detail in a memorandum prepared in May, 1941, by Mr. P. J. McHugh, vice-president of the Edward Hines Lumber Company. This memorandum is given in full in Appendix A.

Principal producing areas 

Characteristically, the lumber industry has been a migrating one, exploiting first the most accessible forests and later moving on to newer areas where there was greater prospect of both speculating and operating profits.1/

For years, through 1869 in fact, the forests of the Northeastern States were the principal source of this country's lumber. Over the following two decades, the Lake States (Michigan, Minnesota, and Wisconsin) were the leading suppliers of lumber. By 1899, according to the Census reports, the Southern States2/ had forged into the lead as the principal source of native lumber. In the years preceding United States' entry into 

World War I, lumber produced in the Southern States approached, and in 1921 surpassed, 50 percent of the nation's total output. Thereafter the relative importance of this area declined and in 1929 lost its leading position to the Western States. The South continued to be a major competitor, however, and, by the base period, gave evidence of regaining its leading position at some future date. Within the Western States themselves, the production of ponderosa pine continued to increase, both absolutely and relatively, throughout the base period. In 1939 the Western States supplied 

48 percent of the total national production of lumber and ponderosa pine alone accounted for 13.5 percent. (See Table 3.)

Lumber was being produced in the Northwest during the last century and, with the opening of the Northern Pacific Railraod, sizable shipments were made to interior markets east of the Rockies. It was not until the 1920's, however, that the Pacific Coast States become a major source of supply for the large East Coast markets and a severe competitor of southern pine producers. Low cost water shipments, made possible by the opening of the 

Panama Canal, enabled lumber from the Pacific Coast States to capture much of the Eastern market. According to the study made by the National Recovery 

Administration, "although wages in the mills on the West Coast were two or more times higher than they were in the Southern mills, the efficiency of labor, the class of timber, and the facilities for manufacturing lumber on the West Coast were such that it could be shipped 6,000 miles by water and yet compete in price with lumber of other areas shipped by rail from 300 to 1,500 miles to the metropolitan centers. Further, it could be backhauled by rail 400 to 500 miles from the Atlantic Coast and still compete with southern pine shipped to such points as Youngstown and Cleveland, Chio."3

From 1919 to 1929 annual production of lumber on the West Coast increased over 5 billion board feet whereas, for the country as a whole, production increased only little over 2 billion board feet and production in the Southern States actually declined. In addition to enjoying favorable shipping costs after the Panama Canal was opened, other conditions were ripe for a substantial expansion of lumber production on the West 

Coast.

- Southern pine, The several species which comprise southern pine account for most of the softwood produced in the Southeastern States. Originally, the pine forests of the South Atlantic and Gulf States covered about 130 million acres and had a stand of timber close to 650 billion board feet. Of this amount, about two thirds consisted of longleaf and slash and the remainder of shortleaf and loblolly, By the base period, however, the stand had been reduced to approximately 197 billion board feet of which about 49 billion board feet were in the Middle South (Kentucky, North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, Virginia and West Virginia) and most of the remainder in the Deep 

South (Alabama, Arkansas, Georgia, Florida, Mississippi, Louisiana, Texas and

Not only was the base period stand of southern pine timber greatly reduced from that which prevailed originally, but also it consisted primarily of second growth. Probably not over 10 percent of the base period stand of southern pine timber, or in the order of 20 billion board feet, consisted of old growth.2/ The cutting of second-growth timber while the trees are comparatively small and the tapping of small trees for turpentine tend to limit the size and quality of second-growth material. Ponderosa pine. Ponderosa pine is the predominant species of softwood in the Western Pine Region of the United States. It represents more than 

40 percent of the total stand of timber in this region and accounts for about two thirds of the total regional output of lumber. Over extensive areas of this region ponderosa pine occurs in pure stands.4/ Total production, shipments, and mill stocks at the close of the year for western pine, together with the total production of ponderosa pine, are shown in Table 4. 

The Western Pine Region extends over the 11 Western States and western South Dakota, except that part of Washington and Oregon west of the Cascade Range, which comprises the Douglas fir or West Coast Region, and the north coastal area of California, known as the Redwood Region.2/ The bulk of the western pine lumber is produced in the area which extends eastward from the 

Cascades in Washington and Oregon, including northern Idaho and northwestern

Montana, and extending southward in a narrow belt along the Sierra Nevada Mountains in northeastern California to western Nevada. A secondary area occurs east and south of the principal region, the more important section of which centers in certain parts of Arizona and New Mexico with scattered stands and lumber operations in Colorado, Utah, Wyoming, and western South Dakota.

17Factors affecting production

As already indicated, major shifts were occurring in the total production of softwood lumber as well as in the production of lumber from the principal softwood species during the two decades ending with 1939. With the remaining stand of virgin timber in the Southern States virtually exhausted, the falling off of southern pine production pulled total softwood lumber cutput down to lower levels. The increasing production of pondero sa pine and Douglas fir was stimulated to a not inconsiderable extent by the need to liquidate timber holdings. Throughout much of the period readjustments were being made to meet basically changed conditions affecting the entire lumber industry.

Need for current return on investment. Investors had acquired large holdings of West Coast timber before the 1920's with the normal expectation, of course, of eventually cutting the timber and realizing on their investmont. In fact, the standing timber in this region had been the object cf speculative purchase since the period of land grants to railroads. The practice of investing in virgin timber in advance of its exploitation had, throughout much of the history of this country, handsomely rewarded the investors. The long-time trend of prices of timberlands, like those of farlands, had to this time been upward. Investors who were in a position to forego current income had, therefore, only to wait for the growing market for lumber to assure the success of their venture; The prospect of such gains appeared to be unusually favorable in the early part of this century when the receding area of easily accessible virgin forests became the subject of national concern. Many in the lumber industry became convinced that when the supply of virgin southern pine was exhausted there would be no further production in the South and the only available softwoods would be those of the West. Under these circumstances, speculators bid up the prices of western timberland to a point where large carrying costs--high interest rates and increasing taxes--forced the liquidation of part of these holdings. 

Excessive capacity. In discussing the character of its industry, one producer of ponderosa pine lumber listed a number of companies that were also producers of ponderosa, "This list," it stated, "does not include other sawmills which were constructed after 1925, since their output contributed to the temporarily depressed condition of the taxpayer and its industry." The corporation elaborates the point that the rapid development of the ponderosa pine industry in the 1920's led to depressed market prices. 

Its statement follows:

Under the economic pressure of high carrying charges, large areas of western timber were converted into sawmill products even though it was recognized that this center of production was handicapped by a location distant from the then existing centers of demand and that difficulty would be encountered in marketing such products in competition with tho se criginating in areas much more advantagec usly located. Many large and costly mills had been erected in the West, primarily in the coast region, before it was realized that the South would continue to be a major producer. Additional mills were built in order to liquidate the investment in timber. As a result there developed a substantial increase in capacity. Data compiled by N.R.A. indicate that rated mill capacity was equal to several times the annual effective demand for lumber.1/ In 1929, for example, rated capacity as compiled by N.R.A. was 2.1 times the quantity produced.

The capacity of sawmills to produce lumber is, at best, a relative matter. The data cn capacity compiled by the N.R.A. appear to be as good as any available and they were used as the basis of allocations made under the Code. In addition to the demand from the market for lumber, however, there are other reasons which prevent a given mill from operating at capacity and which give rise to wide variations in the operation of mills in different regions. The size of trees and compactness of timber stand as well as seasonal weather conditions and the terrain on which the timber stands affect operations. In the southern pine region the land is fairly level and the weather permits woods operation and consequently sawing practically throughcut the year. The West Ccast region also has generally favorable weather, but the terrain is more difficult. Much of the western pine region, on the cther hand, is located in mountain range country where sawmill operations are beset by severe seasonal disturbances and rugged terrain. Rated capacity of mills in this region, therefore, is very likely quite different from effective capacity to produce lumber.

After making full allowance for the fact that sawmill capacity does not represent a measure of ability to produce which is strictly uniform over the several producing regions, the conclusion remains that installed sawmill capacity greatly exceeded the demand for lumber throughout the years 1922-1939. Large inventories. Mills in the Western Pine Region, where ponderosa pine accounts for about two thirds of the total output, characteristically carry large stocks of lumber. The lumber is air or kiln dried and planed before being marketed. Operations are largely seasonal. According to a survey by the "estern Association, about 40 percent of the mills operate nine months or less during the year. Deliveries, however, are made throughout the year, being drawn from stocks during winter months, The over-extended capacity to produce lumber, moreover, tended to accentuate excessive inventories. To quote from the N.R.A. Report, *** in many instances the shadow of this unused capacity has forced other operating units into production schedules which they knew to be in excess of the effective demand for their product. 

With the unused production capacity in existence it has always been necessary for those sawmills producing lumber to avoid any semblance of under-production that would tend to a real or an imagined shortage of lumber. This was demonstrated about the time the Lumber Code was being discussed, and when it was expected that there would be a very considerable demand for lumber in the carrying out of the then proposed Government building program. It has been reported that many thousands of small mills in the Southern Pine Division were then encouraged to, and actually did, start operating under the reported prospective shortage of lumber as a result of that proposed building program. Not only did the small mills go into production but many of the large mills stepped up production, not only in the Southern Pine Division but in the West Coast Division and in the Western Pine Division; and this hopeful production was indulged in despite the fact that the industry then had on hand a heavy inventory of wholly or partially manufactured stock and the effective demand for the prior year had been at the low for more than fifty years."3 the Size of mills. Historically, as the lumber industry reached out into new territory and exploited the virgin stand of timber in that area conditions have usually favored the operation of large mills. Characteristically, also, there has remained in the older, largely cut over areas, a segment of the industry which consists primarily of small mills. These small mills have generally proved to be better adapted than large mills to the operations required in older areas. They are comparatively better able to expand or contract their operations as economic conditions warrant. While such segments of the lumber industry remain in each of the older areas, the one in the Southern States is much the most important. 

Large mills generally are expensive installations almost universally backed by extensive timber holdings which require large investment. Small mills, on the other hand, are almost universally without extensive timber backing and usually represent comparatively small investment in equipment. 

Consequently, the economic pressure of taxes and of interest tends to be less severe on the small mills areas than on those divisions in which the large mills predominate. Moreover, small mills operating sporadically secure their labor from people in the immediate neighborhood who necessarily provide themselves with other occupations when the mills are not operating. 

Frequently, sawmill wages are looked upon as merely supplementing other earnings. Such mills are able to suspend production at any time without seriously affecting a large number of workers and without jeopardizing the mills' supply of labor for future operations when conditions may be more favorable. Interruption of operations by the large mills has a much more serious effect on their labor supply. The larger mills were accustomed and expected to operate about 60 or more hours per week, It was necessary, therefore, for them to build a reservoir of labor of appropriate skills upon which they could depend for the sustained hours of operation. Consequently, labor migrated to the vicinity of the sawmills. Many towns and small cities were located or built up around sawmill activities. 

Thus it may be seen that the conditions under which they operate characteristically induce large mills to follow practices which are quite different from, or even the opposite of, those of small mills. In general these considerations have induced the large mills to continue operations in good times and bad, while the small mills have been under less compulsion to maintain continuous operations. This characteristic was somewhat accentuated by the operation of the N.R.A. Code. The Report states, "After the adoption of the Gode many hundreds of these small mills, faced with the necessity of paying a minimun wage and supplying information relative to their operations, did not again resume saving. The larger mills, however, were not able to dispose of this problem so easily, but were actually faced with the necessity of continuing their manufacturing operations. 

They paid the minimum wage specified in the Code; they operated the maximum number of hours permitted; they produced their lumber and put it on the market expecting to find it moving in the usual channels of trade at the minimum prices set by the industry Code Authorities, only to be faced with a volume of production from

other similarly located mills that, in the aggregate, was more than the market was demanding. These mills were virtually forced to continue production and their products had to be disposed of in some manner and at some price so that additional money could be secured for continuing operations: 

In the Southern States the period 1922-1939 was one in which the organization of lumber mills was undergoing change. Primarily, there was a shift from large units, originally established to exploit relatively large stands of virgin timber, to smaller units better adapted to working cut over areas and second growth timber. This shift was effected in various ways. Many of the units in existence at the beginning closed down during the period and passed out of production. A considerable number, including many incorporated ones, reorganized and set up a new unit with a smaller capital structure. Others, including many corporations, underwent no formal reorganization, but nevertheless greatly reduced their capitalization. At the same time this period witnessed the establishment of a large number of new, small units. 

Although these changes are generally known, official statistics reliably indicating them are not available. Substantial support for the belief that conditions in the South were as stated above is found in the reports of corporations filing tax returns. Of the returns filed in 1940 some 226 appear to represent corporations primarily engaged in producing pine lumber in the 10 Southern States. Two-thirds of these corporations, or 149, were incorporated during the years 1922-1939, and only one-third were in existence throughout the period. Moreover, although 29 of the 77 corporations which were in existence at the outset of the period were classed as large, only 6 of the 149 incorporated during the period were so classed. Probably each of these 6, furthermore, came into existence as a result of a reorganization of predecessor concerns. Comparison of the number of corporations engaged in southern pine production with the known total number of mills shows that the great majority of these mills is not incorporated.

Cost of production. As the lumber industry in the United States has been able, historically, to command higher prices for its products, it has incurred increased costs to supply them. The principal reasons for the historical increase in costs relate to the exhaustion of the more accessible stands of virgin timber and the necessity of turning to sources remote from the principal markets, to second growth, and to areas difficult to work. Over the years 

1922-1939 it is most likely, too, that the cost of producing ponderosa pine lumber moved upward. This would appear to be necessarily true in view of the facts that, during the base period, the volume of output was large, the prices received were about equal to the 1922-1939 average, and yet profits were low. 

It also appears to be borne out by the reported data given in the reports of the N.R.A. and the National War Labor Board. 

The lack of adequate cost information has been general throughout much of the lumber industry. "Here was an industry" states the N.R.A. Report, "that, most emphatically, was not cost conscious. * * * It had successively passed through many periods of famine and plenty, but up to, and even through, the Code period the great majority of the members of the industry had not been educated to the necessity of knowing their costs of production."2/ 

While some operators, particularly the larger ones, do have complete cost information, they represent only part of the total. Consequently, costs play an imperfect part in determining the total volume of output and many producers probably continue operations so long as their returns more than cover the operating, or out-of-pocket costs.

Despite the lack of adequate cost and mill realization data, there is considerable evidence that small mills operate at lower costs than large mills. This condition is indicated by our study of the southern pine lumber industry and the N.R.A. Report found it true for producers in the West Coast 

Division; corresponding data for the western pine producers were not available. "It will be noted," states the Report, "that as between the large mills and the small mills producing Douglas fir and hemlock, the costs of the small mills were approximately only two thirds of the costs of the large mills. It will also be noted that the reported realization of the small mills was $2,35 per thousand in excess of the cost but that the realization of the large mills was 60 cents per thousand less than the cost to produce."3/

Three major regional areas, the Southern Pine Region, the Western Pine Region, and the West Coast Region sought, over the two decades 19201939, to find their competitive place in the markets for softwood. The domestic market had to be aggressively defended against the competitive encroachment of other materials throughout the entire group of major uses to which lumber had formerly been put. Foreign markets, subject to trade barriers and other conditions largely peculiar to themselves, proved no more sure and stable than the markets at home. Moreover, throughout much of the two decades ending with 1939, the lumber industry displayed a tendency not unlike that frequently observed in agriculture of having production exceed the amount which can be sold at profitable prices.

It is not surprising, therefore, that the downward trend of total lumber consumption which began beforo World War I continued through the 1920's.

Lumber and wood products corporations 1 The Statistics of Income published annually by the Bureau of Internal Revenue show the profits of all income tax reporting corporations by broad industrial groups over the years 1922-1939. The profits given in this publication are takon from the tax returns as filed by taxpayers, before audit. Returns are grouped in industrial classifications on the basis of the predominant business of the taxpayer in a given year. All of the net income is tabulated in one industrial group even though the taxpayer may have derived its income from activities in several different categories, A taxpayer may be classed in different groups in successive years because of a shift in the source of its predominant business in those years, Profits themselves are "statutory profits," that is, they are determined on the basis of the income tax law in effect during the current year and change in succeeding years with changes in the law. The Revenue Act of 1934, for example, suspended the privilege of filing consolidated returns for affiliated corporations (except railroads). Application of the Standard 

Industrial Classification in 1938 resulted in some realignment of the corporations making up a given industrial group. For all of these reasons the several profits series which may be compiled from Statistics of Income are not strictly comparable throughout the 18-year period. Moreover, the lumber and wood products class, which includes lumber companies, also includes corporations manufacturing a considerable number of other products and it has not been possible to segregate the latter corporations.

Profits of 22 southern pino lumber producers A comparison of the compiled net profit or loss before Federal income taxes of the 22 southern pine lumber corporations with that of all corporations filing returns is shown in Chart 8. Examination of this chart reveals a marked similarity in the movements of the two curves from 1929 to the base period, or during both the impact of, and the emergence from, the great industrial depression. Up through 1929, however, the trend of profits of the lumber companies had been downward while that of all corporations was upward. And during the base period itself, although the direction of the movements of the two curves was similar, the amplitude of fluctuation of the curve representing the profits of the lumber companies was greater than that of all corporations. 

Although the curvo representing profits of the 22 southern pine lumber companies varies materially in some years from that of profits of all corporations, it does not follow that profits of the lumber companies were subject to a variant cycle. On the contrary, the curve of lumber company profits strongly suggests a pronounced secular (long-time, persistent) declino, particularly evident during the pre-depression years 1922-1929. allowance is made for such a secular trend, the curve of lumber company profits may be soon to move very closely with the cyclical swings of profits of all corporations. If The secular decline in the profits of southern pine lumber producers over the years 1922-1939 resulted from a combination of forces. As was brought out above, some of those forces (affecting production, primarily) were characteristic of the historical development of the lumber industry. 

Others (largely associated with the market) arose out of new, dynamically changing conditions. Both the production of southern pine and the markets for it were undergoing changes of an essentially basic and comparatively enduring nature. The southern pine industry, in common with industry generally, was subject to cyclical influences, but the evidence points very clearly to the conclusion that the cyclical behavior of this industry, as distinct from the secular trond, closely parallels that of industry generally. 

Most casily demonstrated, perhaps, and certainly one of the most conclusive among the evidences of a secular docline in the southern pinc lumber business is the persistent falling off of the capital employed by producers. 

Appendix Table X shows a remarkably steady decline in capital assots from 1922 to 1939. Boginning in the middle 1920's, net worth also moved steadily downward through the base period. Net sales and total compiled receipts were smaller during base period years than they had been in the 1920's, but nevertheless reflected a substantial recovery over their depression lows.

The return on invested capital during the base period compared favorably with that of earlier years which suggests, at least, that the southern pine industry was becoming adjustod to its changed economic situation. 

To be eligible for the relief under section 722 (b)(3)(A) a taxpayer must show that its industry is not only subject to a variant profits cyclc, but also that the taxpayer was depressed in the base period as a result of

the typical behavior of profits in its industry. In substance, therefore, the industry itself must have been depressed in the base period. Accordingly, attention is directed to an examination of the level of base period profits of the 22 southern pine lumber companies. 

Average base period profits of the 22 southern pine lumber companies were 93 percent of those over the entire 18-year period. This by itself might be taken as indicating that the lumber companies were moderately depressed in the base period. When, however, their experience is compared with that of all corporations filing returns it is found to be virtually identical with the standard which has been taken to be "normal." Average baso period profits of all corporations filing returns were also 93 percent of the 18-year average.

A second, and in some respects better, measure of the profits of the lumber companies may be had by comparing them with total compiled receipts and with cortain balanco shoot items. Ponderosa pine lumber producers are subject to the offects of factors common to the softwood lumber industry as well as those peculiar to their own operations. For the industry as a whole the two decades ending with 

1939 were characterized by roadjustments or attempted readjustments to permanently changed conditions. With the virtual deplotion of virgin timber in the South, that area lost its leading position as a source of lumber and the reduced output was supplied to an increasing extent by the comparatively low cost small mills. Increasing output of west coast lumber, particularly Douglas fir, contributed to the intense competition throughout the industry. This was also a period in which the markets for United 

States lumber were undergoing significant changes of a relatively perinanent character. Foreign merkets were reduced by increased competition from foreign producers and by increased trade barriers imposed on United States products. Domestic consumption of lumber declined in total amount as well as per capita from the high level attained before World War I. 

The comparatively high price of lumber oven after the break in 1920-1921; the changes which took place in industrial and mercantile practices; and the growing competition of now products combined to prevent the total domestic consumption of lumber from rogaining its former high level even during the building boom of the early and middle 1920's. Within the ponderosa pine lumber division of the industry, production during the years 1922-1939 was carried on at an accelerated rate despite the low returns. To some extent its accelerated rate of operations arcse out of the fact that it was in that phase of lumber operations where; the initial investment and development being made, it moves on to rapid exploitation of the virgin stand of timber. The pressure for liquidation has been found to be great. At the same time competition from other western lumber, particularly Douglas fir, was increasing. In his memorandum of May, 1941; P. J. Conclusion 

Profits of producers in the lumber industry as a whole as well as in those divisions with which we are primarily concerned declined over the two decades onding with 1939. Depletion of the moro accossible virgin stands of timber and their virtual exhaustion in the South brought about a widespread reorganization of lumber mills, particularly in the southern pine area in which small mills took over a large share of total lumber output. Competition from the small mills, which are usually comparatively

Loss of export markets, which affected directly the sale of southern pine lumber, increased the competition among all domestic producers in their scramble for domestic markets. These changes were basic and comparatively permanent rather than cyclical in character. 

Cyclical fluctuations in profits as distinct from the long time trend, closely parallel the cyclical movements of all corporation profits. The several classes of construction, including repairs, account for some three fourths of the total sales of softwood lumber, but these classes are themselves subject to different patterns of activity. The composite effect of their influence together with that of other factors bearing upon the industry resulted in no material variation in the cyclical behavior of lumber producers' profits.

Profits in the scuthern pine division of the lumber industry were not depressed during the base period. As shown by the experience of a sample of 22 corporations, which is believed to be representative of all southern pine lumber producers in business throughout the 18-year period, the relation of average annual base period profits to the 18-year average was substantially the same as that of all corporations. The average return on net worth was 9.39 percent in the base period compared with an average of 6.88 percent during 1922-1939. Moreover, our analysis of factors affecting the southern pine division supports the results obtained from the sample that base period profits were not depressed; in fact, many of the comparatively new, small producers probably had a more favorable profits experience than that of mills in existence throughout the entire 18 years. 

Not being depressed, the cyclical behavior of profits in the southern pine lumber industry is of little direct concern so far as the section 722(b)(3)(A) claims for relief are concerned. Actually, however, our analysis points clearly to the conclusion that profits of southern pine lumber producers followed the same cyclical pattern as that of all corporation profits.

Profits in the ponderosa pine division of the industry were probably low during the bese period in comparison with their long time average. 

Base period profits of the 16 corporations used as a sample were only 30 percent of the long time average and representod a substantially lower return on net worth. The experience of the sc corporations, however, cannot be taken to be entirely representative of the entire pronderosa pino division. Their experience was less favorable than that of ponderosa pino producers as a whole as shown by the failure of their production to keep pace with that of the entire division in the later 1930's. The conolusion that profits of the entire division were probably low is based on the facts that the sample companies account for one third of the total production, that 11 ponderosa pine lumber producers were confronted with intense compotition in the sale of their product, and that many of the large and middle size mills were under pressure to liquidate their timber holdings.

- With respect to the cyclical behavior of profits in the ponderosa pine lumber division, the evidence points to its being substantially similar to that of all corporation profits. If profits of the entire ponderosa pine lumber division were low during the base period, it was because of comparatively permanent changes in the industry rather than cyclical fluctuations. Possibly the building construction market accounts for a somewhat larger proportion of ponderosa pine lumber than southern pine lumber, although the former finds important outlets in the market for boxes, crating, and other products. In any event, profits of the lumber producers do not follow the fluctuations in residential construction activity, It might have been expected that the residential construction boom of 1925-1926 would have lent a special bouyancy to the profits of ponderosa pine lumber producers in those years, but actual experience shows that, even at this time, profits of these producers were so greatly affected by other factors that they continued their downward trend. 

In conclusion, the cyclical behavior of profits of the southern and ponderosa pine divisions, as well as the lumber industry as a whole, was not materially different from that of the profits of all corporations. 

As a practical matter the invested capital credit of most of the members of the industry, particularly the ponderosa pine lumber producers, far exceeds the constructive base period net income that might properly be allowed a taxpayer as the result of any variant cycle tendency. 

In a number of cases the invested capital credit amounts to as much as double the actual average base period earnings.

Profits of individual corporations vary widely among themselves and from the average for the industry. This fact, together with the great variations in conditions under which individual corporations operated, points to the necessity of careful examination of the record of any taxpayer seeking relief under section 722. It limits the application of generalizations concerning the industry to a particular taxpayer. Nothing, however, in the experience of the individual corporations which have been studied detracts from the conclusions stated above as applying to the lumber industry itself.
