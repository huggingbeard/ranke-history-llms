INTRODUCTION 

As President of the Cresson Rotary Club, early sponsors of the movement to honor and perpetuate the achievements of Rear Admiral Robert E. Peary, I gladly grasp this opportunity of expressing the interest, love, and admiration we should all possess for this internationally famous man, whose strength of body and heart urged him onward in the face of almost certain death, where cold, shifting ice, open leads, and hidden crevices awaited his destruction, just as it claimed many before him. Lame and maimed for life, he returned; his life shortened by hardships, but he carried the honor of being the discoverer of the North Pole, and brought glory to himself, to his country, and honor to Cresson, the town. of his birth.

The Author, Miss Mercedes Buck, has ably and interestingly handled this narrative, as she claims, "Peary in a Nut Shell." 

Every youth and adult should read this story with admiration, and find therein the extreme elements of courage and perseverance which burn in the heart of every true American. 

The Rotary Club express their appreciation to Miss Buck, to the Cambria County Historical Society, to Marie Peary Stafford, the Snowbaby, and all those who have aided in establishing the Admiral's true birthplace, and preserving the memory of Cambria 

County's International Hero. 

Tourist. Wesned

PREFACE 

The main purpose in writing this Memorial Book is to bring to its readers a life story of Robert Edwin Peary and a memorial story of his honors. Most of the books that have been written about the discovery of the North Pole, are large, technical volumes, which require days of intense reading. This little book is brief, authentic, and could be called "Peary in a Nut Shell."

The book has originated from a series of descriptive letters, clippings, and books which I have collected during the past nine years. As I am a native of Cresson I feel this community should be happy to extol the great Peary (Pearyaksoah). I have always admired his persistence and courage and I think he should be given the recognition he has earned as one of the greatest explorers recorded in history. I hope those who read this narrative, will enjoy it as much as I enjoyed the contacts I have made while writing it.

FOREWORD 

Before the Pilgrims landed at Plymouth Rock there had been many disastrous adventures in attempts to discover the North 

Pole. 

In 1845 Sir John Franklin took two ships of the British Royal Navy into the hazardous icy regions and never returned. Many years after the party's disappearance traces were found of the ship. 

Later natives told of seeing his men in the last stages of exhaustion and starvation. It was probably scurvy. 

Then Kane adventured from the American Navy. He discovered 

Kane Basin, but returned with a story of death and hardship. 

In 1871 Charles Francis Hall, an American, with Arctic experience, took his ship to a latitude of 82° 11' N. but died from overexertion on a sledge journey. 

DeLong of the American Navy used the Bering Sea route. He lost some of his men, his ship, and his own life. 

Greely sailed from the United States with a crew of twenty-five men and carried on extensive explorations of Ellesmere Land and the Greenland Coast. He reached a latitude of 83° 24' N. Because the relief ship failed to reach him, the winter of 1883-4 was passed in misery and horror. When help finally came just seven men, including Greely, were alive. 

Thus are mentioned some of the futile attempts to reach the 

North Pole.

When the baby, who was to be called ROBERT EDWIN PEARY, chose to be born on May 6, 1856, he selected a beautiful birthplace in Washington Township, now Cresson Township, Cambria County, Pennsylvania, near the summit of the Allegheny Mountains, which has an altitude of 2430 feet above sea level. The little settlement of Cresson Springs was near by. This is now the thriving town of 

Cresson. 

The modest two and a half room cottage in which his parents, Charles and Mary Wiley Peary lived, was enshrouded by Nature herself. On every side the trees of tender and sturdy growth struggled for supremacy. Creeping vines and climbing ones of many varieties were awakening again after a long winter's sleep. 

Although the pine was not so numerous, the beech, the hemlock, the oak, and wild crabapple abounded. 

A glance through the woodlands revealed many health giving properties. The pure mountain air fanned the water from the iron springs, which precipitated hydrated peroxide of iron. Sometimes this water was called the "Fountain of Youth." Then too, the alum and sulphur springs enhanced the complexion of the mountains over which their water spouted gloriously, showing hesitation whether to seek the blue Ohio or the winding Susquehanna.

Rhododendron filled vacant spaces where it could find a cool damp home.

Robins hopped from tree to tree and sang louder on this May day to announce the birth of a GREAT Baby.

Charles and Mary Peary were quite happy in their mountain home. He enjoyed the outdoor life in the woods and she was very proud of her home and was thrilled, as are all young mothers, with the antics and babyish prattle of her young blue eyed son. 

But their lives were suddenly changed. On New Year's Day 

1859 Charles Peary was stricken with double pneumonia and died about one month later. 

His young widow, scarcely thirty, took the body to Brown Hill 

Cemetery, South Portland, Maine, for burial.

She picked up the threads of her life in a small house in Cape Elizabeth, Maine, where she began to guide and teach young Robert.

She molded his character and inspired him to become a man worthy of his father and mother.

She told him that an ideal life is not complete with dreaming great things; a man must be a doer of them also to realize his ambitions.

CHAPTER II GREENLAND FASCINATES THE YOUNG ENGINEER 

Young Robert was devoted to his mother. Together they enjoyed the outdoor life of the Maine woods. When he hunted or fished, trophies and stories of his luck were always brought to her. 

He was a strong, hardy, lanky youth of normal boyish desires, but his outstanding trait was a longing to see the unknown and to do bigger things than play games with the other boys. 

He was graduated from Portland High School, second in scholastic standing. In the spring of 1873 he won a scholarship to 

Bowdoin College at Brunswick, Maine, and in the fall began the study of engineering.

Although only seventeen years old, Robert was six feet tall. 

He was erect and sinewy and fast losing his boyish appearance. 

When graduation day arrived in 1877, the marks of fifty-two members of the class showed that Robert Edwin Peary's ranking was second high.

After graduation the young engineer opened an office in Fryeburg, Maine, in the Saco River section. His work included rediscovering and restoring survey markers and establishing the true meridian of the town. Although he was busy every day and enjoyed this new work, it was not satisfying to him. He felt that his niche in life was something different. 

In 1879 Fate crossed his path in the form of an open position for a draftsman in the Coast and Geodetic Survey in Washington, D. C. He filed an application and submitted a specimen of his work. 

Within a few weeks he received the appointment at a salary of $10.00 per week.

Peary lived in Washington and worked as a draftsman for two years, but his mind still craved a change. Routine work bored him. 

He wanted to express himself, to use his initiative, to see the unknown.

Opportunity presented itself in 1881 in the form of examinations held for appointment to the Civil Engineering Corps of the United 

States Navy. Robert Edwin Peary, a young man of twenty-five, passed the examination successfully, and was one of four out of two hundred who received an appointment.

He was given a position in the Navy Yard and was made assistant to the well known engineer, Menocal. His first assignment was 

Inspector on construction of the naval pier at Key West. When --[ 6 ]—

the contractor failed, he was asked to finish the job and completed the pier at one-fifth the contract price. The pier still stands as a monument of excellent construction. 

In 1884 Menocal took him on an expedition to Nicaragua. 

There, as assistant engineer, making surveys for the Nicaragua Canal, he had his first exploration experience, which consisted of running lines through the tangled jungles and keeping the natives loyal and happy. These experiences helped him in his later life. with the Eskimos.

The poisonous insect life of the Nicaragua jungle was a subject on which he liked to talk when he grew older. Moreover, he enjoyed contrasting the sting of the Equatorial insects with the more. intense sting of the sub-zero Arctic climate. 

In 1885 an old musty book store in Washington made a change in Peary's future. After browsing around there, he found a fugitive. paper on the Inland Ice of Greenland. He read it and noted the experiences of Jensen and others. The old boyish desire to explore returned. He must see for himself the truth of this mysterious dark 

Interior. As yet, no thought was given to the discovery of the 

North Pole.

Peary collected charts, read books and encyclopedias. He learned that the interior of Greenland was an elevated unbroken plateau of snow. It was 1200 miles long and 500 miles wide. Moreover, it was lifted five thousand to eight thousand feet above sea level and although men knew little about it, it was called the 

Arctic Sahara. 

He pondered why every effort to reach the east coast of Greenland had failed. He finally decided there could be but three possible routes of crossing it. [7

CHAPTER III PEARY TASTES ADVENTURE 

In May, 1886, Peary, after repeated requests, obtained six months leave of absence from the Navy, borrowed $500.00 from his mother, and left Sydney, C. B., on the steam whaler "Eagle." The ship was bound for the whaling grounds. 

On June 6 he disembarked at Godhavn, a Danish station on Disco Island, and the ship sailed on to the north. 

He had no equipment, but after persistent efforts made a young Danish officer, Christian Maigaard, understand his desires. Through his influence, a boat, some sledges, and food were obtained. 

Although neither of them understood the other's language, they penetrated 100 miles over the great Glacial Ice Cap and attained an elevation of 7525 feet in a latitude of 70° N. The returning Eagle picked him up at Godhavn on September 6.

Unexpected difficulty was encountered in a roaring glacier stream and Peary almost lost his life. The ship sailed into St. 

John's harbor on November 17.

In Peary's heart, more than ever, was the desire to conquer the frozen North. He had attained a great height on the Inland 

Ice and penetrated farther than had any white man previously. -[8]-

CHAPTER IV A MARRIAGE IS PERFORMED IN WASHINGTON, D. C. 

On August 11, 1888, Robert Edwin Peary and Josephine 

Diebitsch, daughter of Professor H. H. Diebitsch, of the Smithsonian Institute of Washington, D. C., were married. It was an extremely hot day but the heat did not deter Mary Peary* from attending her son's wedding.

As Peary had already made a summer reconnaissance trip to Greenland, the young wife knew he planned to make Arctic exploration his life work. Of course, his decision to reach the North 

Pole came about gradually.

They went to live in a one-room apartment in New York. 

They were poor financially but rich in their love for each other and in the high hopes of the future. *Mary Peary died on November 4, 1900.

CHAPTER V A YOUNG COUPLE BRAVES THE FROZEN North 

On June 6, 1891, after obtaining eighteen months leave of absence from the Navy, Peary embarked from New York on the "Kite," a steam sealer. There were sixteen persons on board the

Kite. Seven of whom were members of Peary's party and nine. from the Academy of Natural Sciences, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. 

One member of the party was Mrs. Peary. She was interested in her husband's work and felt she was physically fit to withstand the conditions and environment of Greenland. Matthew Henson, a negro attendant, who had been with Peary in Nicaragua, again accompanied him. The destination was the frozen North.

A short stop was made at Godhavn and then the ship sailed to 

Cape York, Greenland. Ice was encountered in Melville Bay and the Kite was imprisoned in the packs. 

After three weeks the ice slackened and the ship plowed ahead. 

While she was ramming a passage through the ice, Peary's leg was broken by the ship's tiller. Fortunately, two doctors were on board to set the leg and with Mrs. Peary as nurse, Peary was made as comfortable as possible until the winter camp was reached. When the suggestion was made by a member of the party that "a man with a broken leg should be taken home," Peary quietly answered in the negative.

While the ship was forging ahead, the men from the deck shot several little auks and a polar bear, which weighed about six hundred pounds.

The Kite was run into an inlet known as McCormick Bay. 

This bay was about nine miles wide and fifteen miles long. The 

Pearys spent the first night in a little tent anchored to the rocks. 

The brave wife, guarded the entrance while her crippled husband tried to rest in his uncomfortable quarters.

The next day plans were made for a year's sojourn in this strange land. A two-room house was built. It was twenty-one feet long, twelve feet wide, and eight feet high from floor to ceiling. 

It was made of lumber which had been brought on the Kite. The outside was banked with snow. About four feet distant from the house, a protecting wall was built. The Pearys named this house "Red Cliff House," after the red cliffs behind it. 

On July 29 the Kite sailed to southern waters. - 10 -

Peary was now able to hop around with the aid of crutches. 

His position was 77° 40′ N. latitude, 70° 40′ W. longitude, and 740 geographical miles from the North Pole.

To vary the routine, it was proposed to celebrate anniversaries. 

The first of these gala occasions was August 8, which was Matt 

Henson's birthday. The next celebration occurred on August 11.

This day marked the third anniversary of the Peary's wedding. 

A special menu was prepared for these celebrations. 

The Eskimos, who were queer, dirty looking creatures, pitched their tupiks close to Red Cliff House. Peary, especially, noted their fur clad bodies. He realized that the aid of the Eskimos would be necessary if he were to survive in Greenland, and proceeded to treat them as friends and equals.

He learned their customs; copied their manner of dress, which was to wear light wind-proof furs instead of woolen garments, water tight boots, bird skin shirts with long sleeves, and short mittens. 

They laid in fresh meat during the hunting season, used tupiks (seal skin tents) for summer and igloos or snow houses for winter. 

An igloo for five men could be built in forty-five minutes, using a long knife and snow. The Eskimos taught Peary how to handle dog teams. He purchased dogs from them. Needles, knives, and sweets were the articles exchanged. After a few weeks, Peary trained the Eskimos to become his sledge drivers. 

On November 23 darkness set in and the sun did not return until about February 13.

Peary learned that during the one hundred ten days of summer the sun shines continuously for twenty-four hours; one hundred ten days in winter there is no ray of light on the frozen land; two intermediate periods of a little over two months in the spring and fall, there is day and night.

On April 18, 1892, he began the Ice Cap march. On July 4 he and his companion, Astrup, and eight sledge dogs reached a latitude of 81° 37′ 5″ N. in an altitude of 8000 feet above sea level and a temperature of 10° to 50° below zero. They had traveled. 

1200 miles by sledge across the Ice Cap. The insularity of Greenland, although not proven on this trip, seemed likely to Peary. 

Independence Bay, Academy Glacier, and Navy Cliff were christened by Peary. After traveling one hundred eight days by sledges, Peary and Astrup returned to the waiting Kite. -[ 11 ]—

Upon his return to New York, Peary was the man of the hour when Arctic exploration was mentioned. 

Mrs. Peary was the first woman to spend a winter north of the 

Danish settlements of Greenland. With her husband she made friends of the Eskimos and gave them trinkets when she left. The sixteen months in Greenland had made her strong and vigorous. 

She delighted in saying "the time which I spent there was a very enjoyable vacation."

One of Peary's party, John T. Verhoeff, lost his life in crossing a glacier and this loss caused much anguish and sorrow to Peary. "My Arctic Journal," which was written by Mrs. Peary, was published in 1893.

LECTURE TOUR 

With another Northern trip in mind, Peary again secured three years furlough from the Navy. But he was without funds. To secure money he began a lecture tour of the larger cities of the 

United States. To stimulate interest five Eskimos, sledges, furs, whips, snowshoes, pemmican (equal mass of lean beef and suet) tins, and stereopticon (slides) views of the North were used. Peary wore an Eskimo suit and delivered one hundred sixty-five strenuous lectures in one hundred three days. The team of full blooded 

Eskimo dogs in charge of Matt Henson took its share of the public admiration and delight. At the end of the tour the net sum of $13,000.00 was in the treasury. -[ 12 ]-

CHAPTER VI THE SNOWBABY IS BORN 

Seven weeks after completing his lecture tour, the Falcon was chartered for two voyages; to take Peary's party north and bring them back. Besides using his own money, the public, more enthusiastic than before, came forward with donations. 

On June 26, 1893, the ship set sail from Philadelphia carrying two women, who were Mrs. Peary and Mrs. Susan Cross, a Scotch nurse, (Mrs. Cross has been dead for many years) and eleven men, including Peary. 

Dogs and furs were purchased at Godhavn. Bowdoin Bay in

Greenland on the north side of Inglefield Gulf was reached on 

August 3.

A site for a one-story house was chosen. This house was made of wood, which had been brought on the Falcon. It was covered with tar paper on the outside and had a skylight the entire length of the roof to utilize the sun. As with Red Cliff House, this house was surrounded by a wall of food supply boxes. There were two rooms. Blankets covered the walls to keep out the cold. There was little furniture. In fact, Mrs. Peary's sewing machine was considered a luxury. Any necessary thing which had been overlooked or forgotten in preparation had to be forgone in this land where a store was unknown. The house was called "Anniversary 

Lodge," for if plans would materialize, possibly three wedding anniversaries (August 11) would be celebrated there.

As before, the Eskimos curiously watched their new neighbors. 

The Falcon sailed south on August 20. 

On September 12, 1893, a baby girl was born to "Mitty" Peary and "Pearyaksoah." These were the names given to Mr. and Mrs. 

Peary by the Eskimos. 

The baby was named "Marie Ahnighito" (pronounced Ah-neeghee-toe). This name, Ahnighito, was given to her because an 

Eskimo woman by that name sewed her first fur clothes to wear in the Arctic.

All of the Eskimos called to see the new baby. They brought her presents and touched her (much against Mrs. Peary's wishes) to see if she were made of snow, for her white skin was so unusual to them. Thus, she became known the world over as the "SNOW- 

BABY."

During the next months, hunting was engaged in and provisions were stored for the spring sledging.

A great blizzard, which added to the winter's darkness, distressed the men. One by one they became physically exhausted. 

On March 6, 1894, eight men, five Eskimos, and ninety-two dogs with loaded sledges, started across Greenland. The temperature was 

62° below zero. The intense cold not only froze the dogs but even the noses and fingers of the men. They soon gave up the march. 

Friends in the States, feared for the safety of the party and sent the Falcon one year earlier than planned. When she arrived all of the men, except Peary, Lee, and Henson were eager to get on board.

Mrs. Peary and her baby had survived the long winter, healthy but pale from lack of sunshine. After some persuasion, the young mother, fearing another year in the Arctic would endanger the health of her eleven months old baby, who needed fresh milk and vegetables, reluctantly sailed for the States on August 26, 1894. A twelve year old Eskimo girl, who was called Miss Bill or Billy-Bah, accompanied the party as a nurse for the baby. 

On April 1, 1895, Peary with Lee and Henson, six Eskimo drivers, six sledges, and forty-two dogs started toward the Inland

Ice. 

Their food supply was scanty. The men and dogs were saved from starvation by a chance musk oxen kill. 

However, with his two loyal companions, Peary completed a second 1200 mile sledge journey across the Ice Cap. From explorations on this trip he proved the insularity of Greenland.

After the Ice Cap expedition, Peary turned his attention to another objective, which was the "Iron Mountain," of Eskimo lore. 

He had won the confidence of the Eskimos and they led him across Melville Bay Ice to Meteorite Island where the "Iron Mountain" or "heaven sent" metal meteorites lay.

Days were spent trying to extricate these monsters. There were three of them. The "Woman," which weighed about 6000 pounds; the "Dog," about 1000 pounds, and the massive "Tent," about 

90 tons.

At last the Woman and the Dog were ready to load on the ship. 

But the Tent refused to budge. Peary had an inward feeling of success as he watched the ship start for home carrying the two -[ 14 ]-

captured meteorites. He realized, however, that the incompleted task was paramount now and he must loosen the giant Tent from its bed of rock and take it to the United States. 

Peary, fired with a great desire, again gained public assistance and left New York in July, 1896, in the Hope bound for Meteorite. 

Island and the Tent. He had taken what he thought was the necessary equipment for the great task ahead of him. 

The Eskimos helped him and marvelled at the huge hydraulic jacks and other tools, which were to them, just another superstitious means of taking away the "awe-inspiring" Tent, which had been their companion for centuries.

After days of hard work the Tent was extricated from its bed, but again Peary had to leave it, as his equipment proved too light to load it on the ship.

In August, 1897, Peary, with Mrs. Peary and Marie Ahnighito returned to the Arctic regions in the "Hope."

This time machinery proved capable of the work required and the Tent was finally loaded on the ship. After this task was done, the meteorite was christened "Ahnighito" by the little four-year old girl from the United States.

Friendships were renewed with the Eskimos. Miss Billy-Bah, who had returned to her native land in 1895, was very glad to see them. Anniversary Lodge was visited but to the Pearys' surprise it was a mass of black ruins, evidently having been burned by a careless Eskimo. 

The party returned home on October 2, 1897.

The three meteorites are now in the American Museum of 

Natural History in New York City. 

"Ahnighito" is the largest meteorite in the world. 

It was at this time Peary completed "Northward Over the 

Great Ice" for publication. - 15 -

CHAPTER VII NORTH POLE DISCOVERY ENTERS PEARY'S MIND 

The discovery of the North Pole was now uppermost in Peary's mind. He obtained five years leave of absence from the Navy and sailed northward on July 4, 1898, in the "Windward."

At Cape York, the Eskimos, who were his friends, met him and there ninety dogs and thirty-eight Eskimos joined the party. 

PEARY ARCTIC CLUB IS INCORPORATED 

While Peary was in the Arctic, the Peary Arctic Club was formed in the States. It was composed of men who were interested in Peary and in his work. Its chief object was to promote 

Polar exploration and to reach the farthest northern point. The 

Club was incorporated under the laws of the state of New York. 

Morris K. Jesup was the first President.

As radio was a thing unknown and there was no mail service to the Arctic regions, no word had been received from Peary since his departure. For this reason, the Club sent the "Diana," a sealer, to contact him. He was found at Etah on August 12, 1899. He was happy to see the ship's party. He accepted the provisions which they gave him but refused to return home because his furlough from naval duties had not expired. The Diana returned to New 

York and carried old scientific reports of other explorers, including the Greely records from Fort Conger, which Peary had unearthed. 

In January, 1899, a baby girl was born in Washington to Mrs. 

Peary. She was named Francine but the explorer father was destined never to see his child, as she died on August 8, 1899. Her burial was delayed until March 11, 1900, because of the possibility that he father might return to the United States during that autumn. 

On July 21, 1900, the Club again sent the Windward north.

Mrs. Peary and Marie Ahnighito were on board bound for Etah. 

After dangerous sailing through ice floes they arrived there and were told by the Eskimos that Peary had moved to Cape Sabine and had not been heard of for several months. Of course, Peary had not expected his wife and daughter on a ship, thinking the young baby at home would prevent such a voyage. 

At Etah the Eskimos boarded the ship and the trip further north across Smith Sound to Cape Sabine was begun. -[ 16 ]—

NORTH POLE DISCOVERY ENTERS PEARY'S MIND 

Mother and daughter had to control their feelings of joy for the expected family reunion a little longer.

Cape Sabine was the spot where Greely's men had starved to death.

Ice packs were encountered, but Captain Bartlett forced his ship through the bergs; eight days of anxiety and sleepless nights were spent by all on board and finally Payer Harbor at Cape 

Sabine was reached. 

The ship was anchored. Another disappointment!

Peary was not there. To make it worse for the party, the icebergs blocked a passage through the harbor, the water froze, and the ship was held firmly. 

Mrs. Peary, who by this time was accustomed to "Arctic troubles," made the best of their plight. She and Captain Bartlett rationed the food, so as to last until spring. Games were played and anniversaries were celebrated in the best way possible. She did not wish her child to realize the danger of their remote position or to have her childhood marred by fear. 

Thus the bleak, dark winter passed. 

On May 6, 1901, Peary's birthday, the absent one found them.

One can hardly imagine the supreme happiness of that little family, whose feelings were, without a moment's notice, transformed from uncertainty and sorrow to joy and gladness.

As Peary joyfully explained to his wife, some Eskimo hunters, with a smattering of English, had told him where an ice gripped ship was anchored and that a woman and a small child were on board.

When the Windward did not return, friends and members of the Club feared for her safety and sent the "Erik" to Etah. It was there that Peary and his family were found on the Windward. 

As before, Peary refused to return home. Mrs. Peary and her small daughter arrived in Sydney, C. B., on September 15, 1901. 

They brought a report of Peary's delineation in 1900 of the northern end of Greenland.

On the morning of March 24, 1902, Peary renewed his attempt to reach the Pole. With straight sledging he went northward over the Polar Sea. Open water, lack of food, panicky Eskimos, and tired dogs forced him to give up. [17 -

Since leaving home he had spent four years of agony; during that time he had frozen his toes and had to have all but the little ones amputated at the joints. During January and February, 1899, he had lain on his bunk suffering torturing pain from his frozen feet. 

This agony was mild, however, as compared to his anxiety as he anticipated the future and pictured himself, physically handicapped, but continuing his sledge parties. He had many other horrible memories which made him feel lucky to be alive. 

On April 21, 1902, Peary reached a latitude of 84° 17′ 27″ N. and was 343 sea miles from the North Pole.

When the Windward with Mrs. Peary and Mary Ahnighito on board, again, met him at Cape Sabine on August 5, 1902, he boarded the ship for the good old United States. -i 18 -

CHAPTER VIII THE ROOSEVELT IS LAUNCHED AND SAILS NORTH 

Robert Edwin Peary again resumed his duties in the United States Navy. In spite of defeats and discouragements, he was still determined to discover the North Pole.

New enthusiasm was aroused. Two members of the Arctic 

Club, Morris K. Jesup and Thomas Hubbard, each contributed $50,000.00. Another $12,000.00 contribution was received. once the next Polar expedition seemed to be shaping itself. 

The construction of a new ship was planned. Peary, after carefully studying other Arctic ships, designed it. The keel was laid on October 15, 1904, at Bucksport, Maine. She was launched and christened "Roosevelt" by Mrs. Peary on March 23, 1905.

President Theodore Roosevelt graciously allowed the ship to be named after him.

The Roosevelt was built strong enough to resist ice floes. She had powerful engines, and was capable of developing one thousand horse power. Her length over all was one hundred eighty-four feet, at water line only one hundred sixty-six feet. The beam was thirty-five feet and she had a registered tonnage of six hundred fourteen (tons). The sides of the ship were from twenty-four to thirty inches thick.

SEVENTH EXPEDITION BEGINS 

On July 16, 1905, Robert Edwin Peary, now 49 years old, sailed from New York in the Roosevelt for the Polar Sea.

He had effected a reconnaissance of Greenland Inland Ice; he had twice crossed the Ice Cap in a 1200 mile sledge journey and proved the insularity of Greenland; he had studied the customs of the Eskimos and copied them; he had become their friend and leader; he had succeeded in placing the three meteorites in their final resting places in the United States; he had written books and delivered lectures on Arctic travel; he had suffered the pangs of freezing (toes) and of hunger; he had been separated from his little family for years at a time; he had not received the ovation and encouragement due a man of such determination and iron will yet here he was, again, beginning a seventh trip to accomplish his Goal, which was the discovery of the North Pole. This was the prize for which men for four centuries had striven, and for which at least seven hundred men had heretofore lost their lives. Faithful Matt 

Henson, now 39 years old, again accompanied Peary. [ 19 ]

THE ROOSEVELT IS LAUNCHED AND SAILS NORTH 

On August 16 at Cape York forty Eskimos and two hundred dogs were taken on board to winter on the shores of the Polar Sea. 

Ice was encountered in Smith Sound but the Roosevelt stood up, much to the delight of all.

On September 5 the ship was anchored at Cape Union. Peary desired to reach the Pole from Cape Hecla, which is a point of land a little less than 500 miles from the Pole. 

Through the long winter, from November 1 to February 7, ice packs, extreme cold, and darkness were Peary's environment. 

Sledges were built and tents and fur clothing were made for the spring sledging. 

On February 22 Peary left Cape Hecla. Using the "Peary

System" of advance and supporting parties, best dogs and lightest sledges in front, the march to the Pole was on. This system enabled men and dogs to conserve strength.

The huge ice floes, piled against each other, were met; others forming great pressure ridges caused much delay. The rate of travel averaged two and one-half miles per hour. Peary suffered little from cold now. His native patterned clothing protected him. 

His thin face was always very observant of the drivers and dogs. 

The reduced rations, the tired looks of the men, and even the questioning Eskimos caused him much anxiety. 

He reached a latitude of 87° 6′ N. an unbeaten record, which was only 174 nautical miles from the North Pole.

Failing in this attempt, he boarded the Roosevelt, which sailed for the United States late in September. As the ship smashed through the ice, she lost her rudder and all but one blade on the propeller was severed. 

On Christmas eve, 1906, New York was reached.

Peary concluded from this trip, had there been a normal season in the Arctic, the Pole would have been reached. 

He realized now, beyond question of doubt, that his utilization of sledge parties, which were composed of men, sledges, and dogs, was the only way of conquering the frozen Sahara and reaching the 

North Pole. "Nearest the Pole" was now written by Robert Edwin Peary and it was published very soon thereafter. 

Peary lectured during 1907 and 1908 on his one great desire, which was to discover the North Pole and claim it for the United States. -[ 20 ]-

CHAPTER IX  

Morris K. Jesup, the President of the Peary Arctic Club, still had faith in Robert Edwin Peary. He discussed the former expeditions with him and promised to provide money for future exploration.

President Theodore Roosevelt was impressed by Peary's unflinching courage, iron will, and keen intelligence. He believed that the North Pole would be discovered by his fellow countryman,

Robert Edwin Peary. The public also had faith in Peary and sent generous contributions. Consequently, the discovery of the North 

Pole now was a nation-wide undertaking. It was to be an American adventure from beginning to end and the discovery of the Pole was to be an American victory. 

A sudden blow, however, in Peary's future trip was the unexpected death of Morris K. Jesup. Mrs. Jesup later forwarded the needed money and the Peary Arctic Club kept the "good work going." Additional gifts of money and equipment were received and within a short time a repaired Roosevelt was ready to penetrate the icy waters again. 

An extremely hot day, July 6, 1908, the Roosevelt left New York.

She had on board Robert E. Peary, Commander, Robert A. 

Bartlett, Master, George A. Wardwell, Chief Engineer, Dr. J. W. 

Goodsell, Surgeon, Ross G. Marvin, a Cornell graduate, who had been on the previous trip, Donald B. MacMillan, George Borup, Matt Henson, and fourteen sailors.

There were also about one hundred guests on board. General 

Hubbard, who was the new President of the Peary Arctic Club, Mrs. Peary and her two children, Marie Ahnighito and five-year-old 

Robert Edwin, Jr., were important persons included in the guest list.

On July 17 a stop was made at Sydney, C. B. There, the guests boarded a little chartered tug to return to New York. 

On that day, in their oft repeated farewell, the brave little wife, and the explorer husband, felt as they looked into each others' eyes, which were tearless, that this was the last trip northward. 

Somehow, this parting was less poignant than ever before. 

At Point Amour Light the last communications to home were sent. These were telegrams to the loved ones there. What news would the next messages contain and when would they be sent? -[ 21 ]

When the Arctic Circle was crossed on July 26, there was perpetual daylight. Cape York or Melville Bay is the dividing line between civilization and the Arctic. Peary realized that here his ties with his loved ones, his friends, and his home were severed; his Goal and dream of years was ahead of him.

The odds were with the elements and Nature. It was mere man versus the dangerous unexplored route to the Pole. 

At Cape York the Eskimos rowed out to meet the Roosevelt. 

They loved their Pearyaksoah (the great Peary) because of his past kindnesses and considerate treatment and they were again willing to follow him.

Peary understood the peculiar temperament of the Eskimos. 

He had taught them how to use civilized man's tools; had given them of his food and through these they learned to trust him and to obey his command.

When the Roosevelt left Cape York, besides her crew, she had on board two hundred forty-six dogs, forty-nine Eskimos of both sexes, seventy tons of whale meat and blubber and the meat of fifty walruses.

Peary, the man of adventure, mused as the crowded ship sailed from Etah on August 18, 1908: "Three thousand miles away at 

Sydney, C. B., I said farewell to all that is near and dear to me; now I have almost three hundred fifty miles of solid ice, all shapes and sizes, to penetrate before I reach Cape Sheridan, my future winter quarters."

The Roosevelt pushed through the lighter pieces of ice easily and was steered cleverly away from the larger ones. All of the 

Eskimos were kept busy. The men made sledges and dog harness and the women sewed fur garments. It was now constant daylight in the season of midnight sun. The white men spent their time telling stories and getting acquainted with the strange Eskimos. 

They tried to acquire a smattering of the Eskimo language, as Matt Henson interpreted what little he had learned. 

Day after day the Roosevelt met ice and fought it; she was kicked about as a football in an ice floe and once dynamite had to be used to sever a huge ice pack. 

On September 5 she arrived at Cape Sheridan, a latitude of 

82° 30' N. -[ 22 ]—

All on board breathed a sigh of relief. The scheduled time of arrival was almost exact. The Roosevelt had gained for herself the name of champion.

SECOND PART OF THE ADVENTURE BEGINS 

The first part of the adventure was safely over; the second part, which was sledging to the Pole, had to be attempted and the prize won.

Dogs, Eskimos, provisions, and equipment were landed on shore. 

The ship was safely anchored, her nose pointing north, in shallow water, to be away from possible iceberg barriers.

On the 8th of September the temperature was 12° above zero. 

A little village of packing boxes sprang up on the shore for use of the Eskimos. It was called "Hubbardville," having been named after the new President of the Peary Arctic Club. Immediately, the Eskimos began fishing and hunting. Three other houses were built for the white men. These were about fifteen feet long and thirty feet wide and were built of the larger packing boxes. 

During October supplies were moved on sledges 90 miles northward to Cape Columbia for spring sledging. From there it was possible to go northward over the Polar Sea. A polar bear and musk oxen were killed on these trips. On October 12 the sun made his last appearance for the year. The taking of photographs, which was always important on Peary's trips, had to be discontinued. 

By October 29 the temperature had dropped to 26° below zero. 

A long Arctic night had begun. There was starlight when the weather was clear.

The men were fairly well content to wait the four months of darkness, before they began the breathless drive to the North Pole.

This was a distance of 413 geographical miles over the Polar Sea. 

So far everything had been satisfactorily completed. During the moonlight period of eight to ten days of each month, hunting was attempted.

Although there were no strict rules for the party there was very little illness during the darkness. 

Some of the dogs died of piblockto or Greenland dog disease. 

This disease causes a dog to howl and snap and refuse food. Death generally occurred from convulsions. On November 25 only one hundred sixty dogs remained. Inasmuch as the whale meat seemed to lack the proper nourishment, rations of musk oxen were tried -[ 23 ]-

and seemed to make the dogs healthier. The dogs were important to sledging and without them Peary would have had to give up. 

December 22 was midnight of the "Great Night." From that day the sun started on the return journey North. All of the party were quite happy at the thought of the returning light. 

At ten o'clock on the morning of February 22, 1909, (Washington's Birthday and a patriotic day to undertake a patriotic discovery) Peary with two Eskimos, two sledges, and sixteen dogs left the Roosevelt for Cape Columbia. A light snow was falling and the temperature was 31° below zero. It was just light enough for safe traveling. His six advance sledge parties had started first.

He desired to be the last starting. There were seven white men, nineteen Eskimos, one hundred forty dogs, and twenty-eight sledges in the field. The march to the Pole was on. 

Peary had learned through his long experiences in this region, from 1886 to 1909, how to take care of every emergency that arose. 

Dogs died and had to be replaced; an Eskimo deserted, another took his place; advance parties gave out, supporting parties took their places; the Big Lead (open water) was encountered and Peary was forced to camp for six days in his igloo.

On March 11, 1909, seven Big Leads, varying from one-half to one mile in length, were crossed. These were covered with barely passable thin ice. All of the divisions of the party were now together. The 84th parallel was crossed. 

Broken sledges, freezing men, and tired dogs caused many delays. One by one the men turned back. Upended sheets of ice two hundred feet high, ridges of blocks of ice one hundred feet high, and areas of fragments of ice floating on the water discouraged the inexperienced white men. 

On March 26 at a latitude of 86° 34' N. Marvin, Bartlett,

Henson, and Peary were the only men on the march. Of course, there were Eskimo drivers.

Marvin, exulting that he had been enabled to travel to this latitude, with two Eskimos, turned south and toward his death. 

He never reached the Roosevelt. The Eskimos reported to Peary afterwards that he had been drowned in a Big Lead, when the thin ice broke.

Peary now had eight companions, sixty dogs, and seven loaded sledges. The temperature ranged from 30° to 40° below zero. 

---

This coldness was welcomed, however, for a higher temperature meant open water and delay.

At last the previous record made by Peary was broken when 87° 6' N. latitude was passed. He felt now, no matter what happened, another "record" had been made. He did not allow himself to hope for or build too much upon reaching the Pole, which was now 150 miles distant.

Peary replaced the poorer dogs from the other teams and reorganized his sledging parties. Intuitively he selected the four intelligent, ambitious Eskimos, who would go on without flinching to the Pole. 

At a parallel of 87° 46′ 49″ N. Bartlett with two Eskimos, turned back, having reached a northing higher than any other explorer, with the exception of Peary himself. 

There were now two divisions: Henson with two Eskimos and 

Peary with two Eskimos, five sledges, and forty of the best dogs which had left the Roosevelt. The distance was now 133 nautical miles to the Pole.

Peary's program was to make five day-long marches, totaling twenty-five miles, ending each march at noon, so that an observation could be made.

In spite of Peary's age, he was vigorous and in good condition. 

He had led a simple life and trained himself to be fit for this last attempt. His companion, Matt Henson,* was a good driver and with his years of experience, stood the test as well as Peary. As the days passed the sledge parties continued uninterrupted, until a latitude of 89° 25' N. was reached on April 5, only 35 miles from the North Pole. The temperature was 15° below zero. Men and dogs were beginning to tire. "One more day" and Peary felt that his Goal would be reached. 

The last march ended at ten o'clock on the morning of April 6, 1909, at a position of 89° 57′ N. latitude, or the North Pole Camp.

A "weary" Peary, from the constant strain of insufficient sleep and anxiety, was too exhausted to realize that his life's ambition was about to be attained. Igloos were built and he tried to rest and sleep, while Henson unloaded the sledges.

Peary also wrote a card to his beloved Josephine, and to their two children. This card could not be mailed bearing the post mark "North Pole," but it would be an everlasting memorial for them.

The Peary party spent thirty hours at the Pole taking soundings and observations. Pictures also were taken from different locations. 

A happy Peary turned his thoughts toward a homeward trip. 

When he reached there, he could tell the story of the discovery and rest with his Goal accomplished.

The return trip to Cape Columbia was without mishap. There, on June 12, 1909, Borup with Eskimo help, built a permanent Cairn to mark the beginning and ending of the party which discovered the North Pole. This monument consisted of a pile of stones heaped around the base of a guide post with arms pointing north, south, east, and west.

The Roosevelt was soon reached. Here, hot food, a cleansing bath, and pleasant thoughts made the men happy. 

The faithful Eskimos were richly rewarded. Presents which consisted of whale boats, tents, rifles, guns, shells, knives, and other treasures were given to them.

Soon a steaming Roosevelt was bound for the United States. 

She landed at Indian Harbor, Labrador, on September 5, 1909, from whence news of the North Pole discovery was flashed to the world.

The first message flashed was to Mrs. Peary.* It carried the glad tidings that her explorer husband had made good at last and had reached the Pole. He also said he was well and sent his love. 

This message was followed by other ones to the Peary Arctic Club and to members of the Associated Press.

CHAPTER X PERSEVERING MAN RECEIVES HIS HONORS 

Peary's journals, his records of observation, and his instruments were submitted to the Board of Managers of the National Geographic Society. On October 20, 1909, these men reported, that according to their findings, Robert Edwin Peary had reached the 

North Pole on April 6, 1909. 

On December 15, 1909, a Special Gold Medal, four inches in diameter, was awarded him by the Society, for the greatest geographical achievement.

From 1909 until his death more than a score of other medals, honorary degrees, and trophies were given to him, by both American and European societies and universities. 

Among these medals were the "Medal of Honor" of the Peary Arctic Club, a five-pointed star cut from a Cape York meteorite and awarded on April 6, 1912; the Great Gold Medal of the Explorers Club of New York City, which was awarded on the fifth anniversary of the discovery of the North Pole, April 6, 1914.

The public did not rejoice much. Men of the street are so absorbed in money making, prize fights, ball games, and sometimes love affairs, that exploration and the value of it do not challenge or thrill them.

Peary, however, felt happy. He was a success unto himself. 

The world would acclaim him later. 

His discovery marked the final chapter of the stone age explorations. Radio and aviation were achievements which followed immediately. 

On March 10, 1910, "The North Pole" was published by Robert

Edwin Peary. The introduction to this book was written by ex-President Theodore Roosevelt. 

On March 4, 1911, President Howard Taft signed the Act of

CHAPTER III *SIXTY FOOT MONUMENT AT CAPE YORK, GREENLAND IS BUILT 

"On a fiery hot day in June, 1932, the Peary Memorial Expedition left New York for Greenland. In the party were Captain Bob 

Bartlett, Marie Ahnighito Peary Stafford, her two sons, Edward 

Peary Stafford, aged thirteen and Peary Diebitsch Stafford, aged eleven.

The purpose of the trip was to erect an appropriate memorial to Peary in the country which had been the scene of his life's work. 

Cape York was selected because of its formation and location. 

It is a bold promontory fifteen hundred feet above the sea and a prominent land mark on the coast. It is known as "The Gateway to the North."

The form of the monument came from Peary himself. It was patterned after a rough stone Government monument erected on an island near Eagle Island (where Peary lived) to mark the ship's channel. He had requested that a replica of this monument be set upon his grave at Arlington, but because of a ruling governing the height of a monument in the National Cemetery, his wish could not be fulfilled.

The monument was designed as a three-sided shaft, sixty feet high with one angle pointing due north. It was topped by a nontarnishing metal cap, which would protect the monument at its weakest point, the peak, from damage by melting and freezing snow. It acts as a beacon to ships and aeroplanes by reflecting the sun's constant rays in summer, the only navigable season in those waters.

On each of the three sides are eight foot marble letters, the letter "P" standing for Peary and the Pole. The monument was built of native stone by Eskimo labor under the supervision of an engineer and by skilled stone masons.

Mrs. Peary paid all expenses in connection with the building of the monument. The rest of the expenses were shared by men interested in the Arctic and exploration and various Societies with which Peary had been affiliated.

CHAPTER IV FRYEBURG, MAINE, REMEMBERS PEARY, THE YOUNG ENGINEER 

On the northerly side of Main Street (Roosevelt Trail) in the village of Fryeburg, Maine, stands a rough granite monument. 

The inscription on the bronze tablet reads: 

FIFTY AND FOUR HUNDRED FEET NORTH LIE STONES ERECTED IN 1883* BY 

ADMIRAL 

ROBERT E. PEARY DISCOVERER OF THE NORTH POLE A FORMER FRYEBURG RESIDENT THESE TWO MERIDIAN STONES INDICATE THE TRUE NORTH AND THUS ENABLE SURVEYORS TO OBTAIN THE MAGNETIC VARIATION 

This monument was paid for and erected by Fryeburg Academy on August 17, 1930, at which time the name of Robert Edwin Peary was eulogized. Mrs. Robert E. Peary, Marie Ahnighito Peary 

Stafford and her two sons attended the ceremonies. 

From that time, August 17 of every year has been observed as Fryeburg Academy Day, and the custom of dedicating that day to some person or some event of importance has continued. 

In addition to this monument there are two small granite stones* in Fryeburg Village. One of them is located fifty feet from the monument and the other is four hundred feet away. 

These two stones are the north and the south meridian stones and were established by Robert Edwin Peary, when a young engineer. Each of the stones is one foot high and six feet square. 

Just outside of Fryeburg Village, Maine, stands a boulder or large rock. This rock is called "Jockey Cap," because of its resemblance to a jockey's cap. *This date (1883) is incorrect.
