ARMED SERVICES COMMITTEE DELEGATION TO VISIT THE PEOPLE'S REPUBLIC OF CHINA House Armed Services Committee Chairman Melvin Price (D-Ill.) announced today that a Committee Delegation will visit the People's Republic of China 

(PRC) during the forthcoming Congressional District Work Period, April 12–22, 1979. "With the normalization of relations between the United States and the People's Republic of China, the members of the committee felt very strongly that it was important that representatives of the committee have an early opportunity to meet with the principal leaders of the PRC to discuss matters within the committee's area of interest," Chairman Price said.

In addition to discussions with senior PRC officials, the delegation expects to visit military installations and activities and meet with appropriate representatives of the military establishment. 

Among the matters which the delegation hopes to discuss with leaders of the PRC are conditions on the Sino-Soviet border, technology transfer, the transfer of weapons by the Soviets and other nations to countries in Southeast Asia, and the security situation on the Korean Peninsula.

Chairman Price noted that the delegation's study would follow up the review of East and Northeast Asian security matters carried out by another committee delegation last fall.

Commenting on the significance of the committee's trip at this time, Chairman Price said, "We must recognize that the status of relations between the United States and the People's Republic of China on the one hand and between the PRC and the Soviet Union on the other, is of tremendous significance to our long-term national security interests. The normalization of relations and what appears to be an opening up of the PRC to mutual trade relations with major industrial countries are developments which impact on the work of many committees of Congress, as well as Armed Services. We hope the delegation's findings will be helpful to other committees as well as our own. 

The delegation plans to depart Washington, D.C. on Thursday, April 12, 1979, with an overnight stop in Tokyo and arrival in Beijing (Peking) on Saturday, April 14, 1979. The delegation will remain in the PRC until Saturday, April 21, with additional in-country stops at Hangzhou (Hangchow), Shanghai, and Guangzhou (Canton).

The delegation is scheduled to return to Washington, D. C. on Sunday, April 22, 1979.

The delegation will be headed by Chairman Price. Other members of the Committee on Armed Services include:

TRIP REPORT ON ARMED SERVICES COMMITTEE DELEGATION VISIT TO PEOPLE'S REPUBLIC OF CHINA, APRIL 12-22, 1979 PEKING (BEIJING) The Armed Services Committee delegation to the People's Republic of China began its visit with a one-stop 15-hour flight from Washington to Tokyo on April 12. After an initial layover, the 21-member delegation led by Chairman Melvin Price arrived in Peking early in the afternoon of April 14. The group was greeted at the airport by 

Deputy Secretary General Wang Zuoliang, the third ranking leader of the Chinese People's Institute of Foreign Affairs, a quasi-governmental organization which was hosting the committee's visit. 

Although the delegation did not have a prearranged detailed schedule for the entire visit it was apparent the Chinese had the details of the arrival well organized. The same organization was apparent throughout the visit.

Visit with Ambassador Hao Deqing 

Following the discussion of the itinerary with the Chinese liaison representatives, the delegation departed for the institute headquarters where its president, Ambassador Hao Deqing, met with the group for 1½ hours (see transcript at app. I). The discussion was wideranging, touching on several key aspects of Sino-United States relations and the most pressing international problems, particularly as they relate to military preparedness of the two countries. 

Following the meeting, the delegation attended a reception in honor of the delegation hosted by Ambassador Hao and the institute. 

Also present were members of the Chinese Foreign Ministry, Ambassador and Mrs. Leonard Woodcock and other Embassy officials. 

Easter Sunday in Peking

On the second day in Peking, Easter Sunday, the delegation visited the Great Wall and several members of the delegation took the opportunity to talk with local and foreign visitors. The delegation also visited the Forbidden City, and attended a briefing by Ambassador 

Woodcock at the U.S. Embassy. 

Some of the group visited the Ming Tombs, a historical site where Chinese archeologists have unearthed considerable relics of the 14th, 15th, and 16th centuries. However, most of the delegation joined the U.S. Embassy staff and dependents who had gathered to pass 

Easter Sunday morning.

Ambassador Woodcock, Deputy Chief Stapleton Roy, Economic and Political Counselors William Thomas and Charles Sylvester briefed the delegation on recent political and economic developments in the People's Republic of China. The group also discussed the 

Embassy's status in the Peking community, the future expansion of the Embassy and personnel and administrative matters. Assessments of the Chinese military action in Vietnam and the regime's prospects for modernization of national defense were key topics. (7)

Subsequently, the delegation visited the Forbidden City, the imperial residences which open onto revolutionary Tien An Men Square with its Martyr's Monument and the Mao Tse-tung Mausoleum. This area is now the site of the annual commemoration of Premier Chou En-lai's death, a commemoration that in 1976 had led to the purge of Vice Premier Deng.

The contrast between the orange-tiled roofs of the Forbidden City and the stark Socialist architecture of these monuments and the Great Hall of the People and the Historical Museum which flank them portrays the great contrast between the old and new China. Separating the two buildings, the Tien An Men Square and the broad Chang An Street gave testimony to the ever-moving surge of humanity in China. An endless stream of bicycles and buses flow through the square, which is typical of China's urban life. 

Visit with Vice Premier Deng Xiaoping 

On Monday morning, April 16, the delegation visited the Great

Hall of the People, the meeting place of China's National People's Congress. Here top Chinese leaders meet with state and foreign leaders. For approximately 2 hours the delegation had the opportunity to discuss China's political and economic development ambitions, the invasion of Vietnam and its results, and the Chinese view of Sino-United States relations with Vice Premier Deng Xiaoping, reportedly China's most powerful leader. (See transcript at Appendix II.)

The session with the Vice Premier was relaxed, affable, and presented an opportunity for a frank exchange of views. Apparently the penetrating questions set the stage for the Vice Premier's meeting with Senator Frank Church's group a few days later. At the latter time, Vice Premier Deng expressed his displeasure with the congressional handling of the legislation on Taiwan-United States relations, and enunicated China's position on the possible installation of monitoring devices to look into the Soviet Union. 

Visit with General Wu Xiuquan 

Following the meeting with Vice Premier Deng, the delegation had the opportunity to speak with one of China's leading officials, General Wu Xiuquan, Army Deputy Chief of General Staff. (See transcript at appendix III). Major topics included China's conflict with Vietnam, modernization of China's military forces and China's views of Soviet ambitions in the world. Speaking of the recent conflict with the Vietnamese, General Wu expressed confidence in the Chinese soldier, but admitted that the troops had difficulties, mainly with the terrain, during the early days of the engagement. 

That afternoon Representative Beverly B. Bryon had the opportunity to meet with Chinese leaders regarding physical fitness and 

Representative Jim Lloyd met with aviation industry officials, including Mr. Chen Guoxing of the China Aeronautical Technical

Import and Export Corp.

REPORT BY HON. BEVERLY B. BYRON ON PHYSICAL FITNESS AND SPORTS PROGRAMS IN THE PEOPLE'S REPUBLIC OF CHINA I. IS THERE A NATIONAL DIRECTOR OF PHYSICAL FITNESS AND SPORTS IN THE PEOPLE'S REPUBLIC OF CHINA (PRC)? 

There are two organizations responsible for all the athletic activities in China. The government arm is called the Commission for Culture and Sports and its director is Wang Meng. Under the Commission is the All China Sports Federation which is comprised of 34 regional commissions and its president is Shung Shitung. My conversations on physical fitness and sports were with Zhang Gian, head of the Inter national Department of the All China Sports Federation. (Note: I 

was unable to secure information on the other sports organization.

) 

II. WHAT IS THE STATUS OF PHYSICAL FITNESS AND SPORTS PROGRAMS IN THE PRC? ARE THE CHINESE INTERESTED IN FURTHER EXCHANGE PROGRAMS WITH THE UNITED STATES? 

Mr. Gian indicated that great emphasis has been placed on physical fitness in the last year for students as well as adults. Previously, stress was placed solely on studies and work habits, but now regular regimented exercise by the population is being given important attention. The Chinese feel that a healthy body and a healthy mind go hand in hand. This principle was indirectly confirmed during my early morning jogs through the cities. The Chinese do not generally excel in athletics but the PRC appears to have an overall well-disciplined, physically fit, trim, healthy and basically agile citizenry. The high proportion of individuals in seemingly top physical form may be due in part to the country's low level of technology and automation. 

Everybody, it appears, rides bicycles and this form of transportation is certainly a good body toner.

Mr. Gian said there is an intense desire to popularize sports in the 

PRC and thereby raise the athletic ability and standards of the people on an international level. He said his country would be interested in future sports exchanges in such areas as swimming, soccer and track and field. The Chinese gymnastics team will be coming to the 

United States for the world championships later this year. It should be noted though, that such exchanges may not occur overnight as the Chinese want to be sure that they are reasonably skilled for a good showing in competitive meets. In fact, this was the reason they gave me for turning down a U.S. invitation to participate in a Duke University track meet in May 1979.

Outside Nanking I spotted several well-equipped racing bicycles, as well as two 8-man sculls and six 2-man sculls, which would indicate a Chinese interest in these areas. Winter sports are also prevalent and 

Mr. Gian indicated to me that the Chinese are quite active in speed and figure skating and ice hockey. Basketball is also extremely popular and backboards and nets are quite conspicuous. Finally, running and swimming are being encouraged almost as a national duty.

I also brought up the possibility of future mountaineering and hiking exchanges and they seemed quite interested in this. 

In addition, I inquired about a marathon exchange. The Chinese admit they are poor runners and my own observations bear this out. 

Nevertheless, running is diligently encouraged among citizens as a good way of keeping healthy. I invited the Chinese to participate in future marathons in our country and I am hopeful the invitation will be favorably reviewed.

III. HOW ARE SPORTS AND PHYSICAL FITNESS PROGRAMS TREATED IN ELEMENTARY AND SECONDARY SCHOOLS AND COLLEGES? 

Mr. Gian reemphasized that in previous years scholastic achievement was basically all that was considered important for students. 

Today, physical development is also treated as a key ingredient in the educational process and there are standard levels of fitness all students must meet.

Intramural competition is fostered within schools, in addition to annual sports competitions among elementary and secondary schools and colleges on a nationwide basis. Even preschool children are encouraged to organize and play sports games. It is interesting to note that the middle school national competitions will be held next year for the first time since the cultural revolution of the midsixties. 

Athletes who excel at these competitions may eventually participate in the national games which occur every 4 years at Peking Stadium. 

There are 34 sports represented at these games which are preceded by eliminations contests on the province and regional levels. 

My overall impression is that physical fitness is ingrained in the individual at a very early age and then disciplined and reinforced through each successive year of schooling. The theory is that students will maintain their fitness level in the adult years as a natural extension of 15 prior years of rote training. In short, the goal is to have citizens think of their daily fitness regimens as another necessary living activity such as brushing your teeth or taking a shower.

IV. IS THERE AN EMPHASIS ON EXERCISE PROGRAMS FOR ADULTS? 

WHAT ABOUT SENIOR CITIZENS? 

The area of adult fitness proved to be the most fascinating portion of my interview with Mr. Gian. The majority of current adults, of course, have not undergone the rigorous discipline of physical training taught in today's schools. For this reason, adult fitness commissions have been formed at the local level to encourage daily fitness training for citizens of all ages.

Factories have a 10 a.m. physical fitness break in which all workers participate. In addition to general calisthenics which are done to music, there are special exercises tailored to the kind of job each factory worker does. Each industrial workplace also has sports teams such as volleyball, basketball, softball and ping-pong.

As I mentioned earlier, running is being encouraged by the government as a good form of keeping fit. Each spring, marathons are held throughout the country in many cities and citizens are encouraged to prepare for these races all year. This year, in Peking alone, over 

10,000 local residents participated in the run.

Swimming is another favorite form of adult fitness. Chairman 

Mao was very active in this area and Mr. Gian informed me that 

Chairman Mao wanted half the population to be adept in this sport. 

This is beginning to happen now that there is greater emphasis on staying in healthy shape. 

Finally, I was particularly impressed by the agility and vigor of the elderly. Each day I would see many senior citizens doing extensive early morning exercises in parks, on the street and on rooftops. Stretching exercises, shadow boxing and general calisthenics seemed to be the norm and the elderly often used trees and the walls of buildings as props in their morning routines. Peking impressions On the last evening in Peking, April 16, the delegation hosted a return reception for Ambassador Hao and other members of the 

Chinese People's Institute of Foreign Affairs. Ambassador Woodcock was also present.

Ambassador Hao urged the delegation to point out areas where the Chinese need improvement and where our two countries could cooperate. He also urged the delegation to again visit at the earliest opportunity.

Peking presented the delegation with a rather unusal initial impression of the country. As almost all things or efforts are to be functional in China, we were informed that the grass had been removed to prevent a breeding place for insects. Without grass and insects there were no birds, and within the city there did not appear to be any cats or dogs. In Peking, with virtually no exception, everyone wore the traditional Mao jackets and pants. 

Within the city, the main mode of transportation is the bicycle. 

Along the sides of most roads are asphalt bicycle paths and bicycle overpasses and bridges for the uninterrupted flow of bicycle traffic. 

There are relatively few cars for a city the size of Peking. Most automobiles appear to be styled on Russian models and the delegation was told that China has the capability to produce its own motor vehicles. Gasoline is obviously at a premium and a common practice is to turn off engines while waiting in traffic. 

NANKING (NANJING) 

On the morning of April 17, the delegation departed for a 2-hour flight to Nanking aboard a Chinese Aviation Administrative aircraft, a

British Trident. There, Kiangsu provincial officials escorted the delegation to the Nanking Guest House, a series of lodges in the eastern suburbs of the city which are set aside for official state guests. 

Visit to the 179th Army Division 

During the afternoon, the delegation visited the 179th Division, which is headquartered just outside the city. The group was offered an opportunity to view a series of weapons firing exercises and to examine some aspects of the daily life and training of the Chinese soldier. 

Accompanied by the head of the Foreign Affairs Section of Kiangsu 

Provincial Revolutionary Committee, Mr. Wang Liang, the delegation was escorted through an avenue lined with soldiers to a briefing room at the compound. Deputy Division Commander Wang and his staff, including a political commissar, a battalion leader, a regimental commander, a logistics commander and a nurse briefed the delegation.

Mr. Wang presented a brief history of the 179th Division, which is primarily an infantry division, numbering between 6,000 and 7,000 men. The division was established during the winter of 1937 from three guerrilla teams and since that time has participated in almost all of the important fighting taken place within China, involving over 600 battles and claiming over 80,000 enemy casualties. 

Commander Wang stated further that the division is not only a fighting unit but also a production team that is making every effort to strengthen its capabilities in developing production for the growth of the country. Troops are responsible for providing their own food. 

To provide better livelihood for the members of the division and their families, individual units are involved in the production of medicine and other commodities which are then sold on the local economy. 

Commander Wang indicated that although the unit itself had not been in battle for a number of years, 1,000 of its troops were sent to Vietnam where they were involved in the most recent conflict with that country.

Following the briefing, the committee viewed the Chinese soldiers' capabilities to repair weapons in the field. Individual soldiers were working on their weapons, repairing mortars, machine guns, rifles, and carbines. A small mobile machine shop was set up and truck repair techniques were also demonstrated. Despite the rather primitive quality of their tools, the repairs appeared to be carried out with precision.

The members visited a medicine bottling plant where wives of some of the soldiers were involved in the bottling process. The medicine was an all-purpose remedy used for all types of common ailments from headaches to tired blood.

The delegation visited a soybean factory on the base which also was operated by wives of the soldiers. Several different types of soybean products were on display and the committee sampled three of the varieties. Some of the products would be acceptable for use as combat rations while others were used in daily cooking and were sold locally in Nanking and to the soldiers themselves. 

The delegation also visited the barracks. The Chinese living quarters were stark and furnished spartanly. Plain wooden furniture set off the concrete walls and floors. Members also viewed some of the individual weapons stored in the barracks. AK-47's, SK rifles, and small hand-held B-40 rocket launchers were shown and demonstrated. 

Outside the barracks soldiers were engaged in bayonet practice, marching drills, and physical fitness exercises. 

The delegation was then invited to view a locally produced recreational performance by a company and a nearby production team. 

Next on the program was a shooting demonstration at the field range, where one company was lined up in preparation for a series of 20 or more specific firing exercises. The weapons included basic rifles, semiautomatic rifles, light and heavy machineguns, mortars, and 77-millimeter rifles. Ground assault tactics were also demonstrated.

First on the program was a rifle marksmanship demonstration against 14 steel targets set at a range of 100 meters. The next program demonstrated the setting off of mines, and semiautomatic rifle fire was the third item on the program with 10 targets set at 100 meters.

Another display was a series of four different moving airplane targets set on wires and strung along a course reflected against the hillside. 

The fifth target display was heavy machineguns fired against steel targets 200 meters from the firing line. The heavy machineguns were set on wheels and as each round was completed the weapons were dismantled and carried away by the soldiers. The next program called for light machineguns firing against steel targets at 100 meters. Semiautomatic rifle fire against steel targets at 100 meters by 10 separate riflemen firing at 20 targets was the seventh item on the program. 

Semiautomatic rifle firing at steel targets head size 100 meters away was the eighth competition. Then a series of targets were set up at various ranges, 100 meters, 150 meters, 200 meters and 250 meters. Light machineguns were used to fire at full-form targets, head targets, chest-sized targets, and machinegun implacement targets at those four ranges.

The Chinese demonstrated individual combat battle training. 

Soldiers crossed a small ravine by rope and then moved through areas of fire to destroy implacements, firing at objects as they arose from the ground or as the soldiers came upon them on the course. The soldiers move gradually from the lower firing area to the crest of the hill. 

Subsequently, two squads were lined up to demonstrate bayonet tactics using wooden rifles. The intensity of the individual combatants was noticeable.

Returning to the firing range, the next competition was against a series of targets at 100, 150, and 200 meters. Infantry squads competed against each other, firing first from a prone position at breast targets 200 meters away, from a standing position at breast targets 100 meters away and at breast targets 150 meters away from a kneeling position.

The next competition was with heavy machineguns firing against a series of targets, and mounted firing of a 75 millimeter recoilless rifle against blockhouse and tank targets at 350 meters.

Another display included light and heavy machinegun fire concentrated on targets at 200 and 300 meters. The last exercise was a fusillade of rifle and machinegun fire by a platoon. The target was fulllength portrait targets set at 400 meters.

The delegation was highly impressed by the performance of the Chinese troops. Their spirit, determination, and skill were of high quality.

After the display, members of the delegation talked briefly with some of the soldiers, congratulating them on their professional performance. 

Chinese theater

In the evening the delegation was taken to a traditional opera, "The Romance of the White Snake," in the Nanking music theater- The fact that the delegation was able to attend a traditional Chinese opera again indicated how much had changed since a committee delegation last visited China in April 1976. At that time, with Vice 

Premier Deng villified and the Cultural Revolution-era leaders in ascendancy, only Cultural Revolution period plays, operas and ballet were performed. Traditional Chinese operas were all discredited and criticized, and the "Romance of the White Snake" was purportedly personally banned by Chairman Mao.

Now that the old plays have returned and the change in the leadership apparently relaxed the degree of political tensions of the past, there was a noticeable change in the audience. Once again there was great attentiveness and an enthusiasm for the arts which was lacking before. Spontaneous applause for the performance and the dialog was marked.

Nanking and the Yangtze On April 18 the delegation visited one of Nanking and China's most important modern historical sites, the Sun Yat-sen Mausoleum. The mausoleum was built under the Nationalist leadership from 1926 to 1929 but remains important to the People's Republic of China since Dr. Sun is considered a patriotic leader to those who have led China. since 1949.

A large number of Chinese visitors were at the mausoleum and the delegation took the opportunity to mingle with them in an effort to discuss the area and learn something of the viewpoint of the average 

Chinese citizen.

The committee also visited a number of historical sites located nearby the Sun Mausoleum, including Ming dynasty temples and pavilions.

The itinerary included a visit to the world-famous bridge that crosses the Yangtze River. The Yangtze is China's largest river, traveling some 6,300 kilometers throughout China and emptying into the East China Sea. The Yangtze Basin is almost 2 million square kilometers in size and one of China's most fertile regions. The basin is occupied by one-third of China's 900 million people. The cultivated land is rich in rice, cotton, tobacco, and silk. Forty percent of the nation's grain comes from the Yangtze Basin area.

The delegation traveled up the river passing several large barges transporting freight to provinces deep in China's heartland. Along the riverbank were numerous factories and a shipbuilding yard. In earlier years, there were large ferries to transport the trains as well as ferries for passenger and vehicle traffic across the river. But now a new span of road and track in dual-level bridge construction reunites the north and south banks of the river. 

Subsequently, the delegation entered the base of the Yangtze River Bridge for a briefing on the bridge and its construction. Begun in 

1960 and completed 8 years later, the bridge comprises a four-lane highway and a double-track railway. The highway section runs some 

4,500 meters, while the rail section extends some 6,700 meters. The bridge is over 50 meters from the surface of the river and is supported by nine piers set into the river and the riverbed. Some 22 doublevaulted archways support the approach roads. The Chinese proudly claim that all of the construction was done with indigenous equipment and labor, and no foreign assistance or equipment was used. SHANGHAI On the afternoon of April 18, the delegation departed by train for the 5-hour run to Shanghai. The trip was particularly enlightening because the group was able to view China's countryside at close proximity. The fields were filled with growing paddy rice, millet, other grains, and vegetables. This area is one of China's major food baskets.

Industrial activity was also evident at what appeared to be small factory centers and at least one major petrochemical complex. However, the vast rural scene was characterized by the overwhelming absence of mechanized equipment. Occasionally animals were seen, but manpower apparently is largely responsible for getting the work done.

The commune 

The following morning the delegation had an interesting and informative visit to a commune named Feng Bang in Jiading country. 

It was described as a typical commune for the Shanghai area, apparently rich in agricultural produce, with an experimental fish station, pig and chicken raising facilities, as well as small-scale industries. 

These industries reportedly supply not only the local market in Shanghai and a larger regional market, but also produce items for export.

It was indicated that the commune has a population of some 23,000 people, and its main task is to produce grain, but is also involved in cultural, educational, and health activities for its inhabitants. 

The delegation visited a workshop where members of the commune were making what appeared to be quality clothing of typical standard pattern for sale in Shanghai stores.

Another shop was assembling crystal glass table lamps for export to Africa.

Moving back to more traditional rural activity, the committee visited an experimental fishpond where the commune was raising African carp, a species of fish which grows more rapidly than the local variety and which the commune hoped to develop into a cash crop. 

The delegation visited the chicken breeding section of the commune. 

The incubators were filled with hatching eggs, and the yards contained various types of chickens of local and foreign variety, including Rhode 

Island reds.

The pigsties again represented a breeding combination of the local Chinese stock with imported stock, some of which came from the 

United States.

The delegation examined the organization and structure of the commune and some aspects related to the commune's military defense posture. Asking how the leaders of the commune were chosen, the commune officials headed by Mr. Wang, chairman of the local Revolutionary Committee, stated that there are 23 members on the committee. 

Party membership in the commune represents only 3.5 percent of the commune's total membership of 23,000, or about 6 percent of the work force. Commune members numbering 900 are members of the party. Nevertheless, 22 of the 23 members of the Revolutionary 

Committee are party members.

Regarding the organization of the agricultural land, the delegation was informed that 93 percent of the farmland is collectively cultivated, and 7 percent is left for individual farmers' private plots. 

The delegation raised questions concerning the basic commune organization, from the production team to the production brigade, and it was explained that production brigades are largely independent, with coordination between brigades to accomplish communewide

tasks. It was also stated that there is competition between the brigades, with each attempting to achieve higher records of production and output. Of the 15 brigades at the commune, 9 were classified as model brigades. Asked about availability of a university education for the members of the commune, the officials stated that last year 10 members from the commune had been selected to attend the university. 

In addition, between 40 and 50 men annually leave to serve in the People's Liberation Army (PLA). As had been pointed out during the visit to the 179th Division, membership in the PLA is considered an honor and a step for advancement in the eyes of the people. Thus, there is reportedly strong competition for selection to join the People's Liberation Army, and the PLA has no lack of potential recruits to fill the 40 to 50 slots each year. Those who join serve between 3 to 5 years and return to the commune. There is no direct compensation by the PLA to the commune for the loss of these members from the commune work force. The commune, however, does give extra work points to the families whose members are selected and who have, therefore, lost productive capability.

Discussing the militia at the commune, it was stated that the commune has a regiment of militia and that companies and battalions are set up throughout the production brigades. Training is conducted during spare time, and approximately 6,000 men and women of the 23,000 people in the commune are members of the militia. Militia arms and weapons are kept in the armory. Drills and training are fixed with periods of regular drill instruction and some instruction with weapons. 

There is no specific police force, but two security men from Shanghai perform public security work for the commune. 

Visit to a school (comments of Hon. Anthony B. Won Pat) 

During my visit to the school, the level of which is comparable to our primary schools, I was favorably impressed by the high degree of discipline displayed by the students. I noticed a great deal of enthusiasm and motivation by the children to actively engage in class activities. I was also impressed by the program for English language instruction, even though the syllabus is necessarily rudimentary. 

It is clearly evident that education has again assumed a position of high priority for national development in the People's Republic of China.

CONCLUSION 

For all practical purposes, until most recently, China had been virtually a closed society for almost 30 years. With normalization between the United States and the People's Republic of China, we, as Americans, have slowly and gradually become exposed to the country as it appears from within. 

During the planning stages of the committee's visit to Mainland China, and more particularly upon arrival in-country, it became obvious that we would be allowed to see what the Chinese officials wanted us to see, albeit somewhat in the framework of the committee's basic request. As the itinerary reflects, when compared with

itineraries of others from the public as well as the private sector, the schedule was basically standard. That is, visits to Peking, Nanking, Shanghai, and Canton, with minor variances.

The delegation had little information as to an itinerary until arrival in Peking on April 14, 1979, although our first contacts with the State 

Department concerning the trip commenced in January 1979.

That is not criticism. It must be stated in all candor that itineraries are restricted, to a considerable extent, by the availability of accommodations, time, and transportation. Nonetheless, from the experience of the committee it can be fairly stated that there is little discretion as to in-country movements. That is all by way of saying that the committee had limited exposure to the People's Republic of China during the visit, and that fact necessitates a restraint on reporting. 

Nonetheless, the delegation was extremely pleased to have the opportunity to meet with Vice Premier Deng Xiaoping, Ambassador Hao

Deqing, President of the Chinese People's Institute of Foreign Affairs, and with General Wu Xiuquan, Army Deputy Chief of General Staff. 

The committee was able to construct verbatim transcripts of those meetings, which have been included in this report, in order that the details may be available to the public.

There are certain broad and obvious conclusions that may be stated with some degree of certainty:

The People's Republic has embarked on an ambitious program of modernization in four basic areas: agriculture, industry, science and technology, and national defense, apparently in that order of priorities.

The long years of being a closed society and the more recent cultural revolution, have taken a substantial toll in education, transportation, industralization, agrarian development, technological progress, public health, and social welfare, and, in all probability, will make the road to modernization a tortuous one. 

In many instances, the urban areas visited by the delegation were, to those who are able to recall, a reminder of the 1920's and early 1930's in this country when the automobile was in its relative infancy and consumer products were just making a modest entry into the market.

Current efforts to open the People's Republic of China to exchanges with the developed nations of the free world must be based upon a practical realization by all concerned that time cannot be collapsed, that is, progress can only be gradual in all areas of development. 

Intercourse with other societies cannot be accomplished in an economic, social, and political vaccum. With exposure, the 

Chinese population, particularly in the major urban areas, is curious about social welfare and human rights in the outside world. Recent events in the People's Republic of China have shown even greater reaction than our in-country observation of "curiousity."

P 3 །།་ In sum, the committee obviously had limited exposure to the leadership, the people, and the geography in the People's Republic of China. 

Those facts, taken with the short period that has elapsed since recognition and the relative lack of information concerning the People's Republic of China, are constraints on the delegation indulging in an admittedly tempting opportunity to list more detailed comments. 

Thus, at this point in time it would appear preferable to allow the available details concerning the visit and the transcripts of meetings. with senior Chinese officials to generally speak for themselves, particularly since detailed and lengthy findings at the hands of a congressional committee may suggest conclusions not now warranted.

CHINESE LEADER SAYS CUBANS IN CAMBODIA 

In the course of an extensive meeting in Peking with Vice Premier Deng Xiaoping of the People's Republic of China on April 16, members of the House Armed Services Committee were told that "there are signs of Cuban troops in Cambodia."

Deng continued, "According to what we have learned, the Soviets are the advisors and the Cubans are the soldiers." Deng made that statement while discussing the Vietnam invasion forces and Russian support of the Vietnamese incursion into Cambodia. 

The House Armed Services Committee delegation, led by Chairman 

Melvin Price (D-Ill.), visited the People's Republic of China during the period April 14-21, 1979, with the principal stop in Peking where conferences were held with Chinese Government leaders. 

The Vice Premier, in discussing the Vietnamese question, came down strongly on the Soviet cooperation with, and presence in, Vietnam. He alleged that the Russians established themselves in Vietnam "long ago in bases built by the United States, such as harbors and airports." He indicated that not only Cam Ranh Bay but also Da

Nang, were candidates for Soviet bases. "The Vice Premier and other Chinese leaders covered a wide range of subjects in our discussions," stated Chairman Price. "The common theme was a need for mutual cooperation and friendship between the United States and the People's Republic of China.' 

Mr. Price added: "The Chinese stated that they now know they need military modernization and the introduction of advanced equipment." 

General Wu Hsiu-chuan, Army Deputy Chief of General Staff, admitted that in the early days of the recent so-called border war with the Vietnamese they had trouble fighting on the Vietnamese terrain. Stated Wu, "One of the most important things we learned was to have good topographic information. During the first few days our forces were not well adjusted in fighting in that kind of terrain, but the commanders in the lower levels saw this weakness very soon and modified their tactics.”

The Chinese leaders brought up with the delegation the possibility of introducing U.S. advanced military technology into China so the 

Chinese could improve their military equipment. 

In discussing Taiwan, Vice Premier Deng, as well as Ambassador 

Hao Te-ch'ing, president of the Chinese People's Institute of Foreign 

Affairs, were adamant that Taiwan is China's problem but that as soon as Taiwan agreed to join the "motherland" the problem could be solved. "Subsequently," stated Deng, "they can keep their social system and people-to-people relations with the United States and others. 

The income of the people of Taiwan will not be lowered, their living (21)

standard and way of life will not change. We even say that they would be allowed to keep their own armed forces under the condition that they are a local government and a part of China." A similar description of that proposition was also put forward by Ambassador Hao.

In touching on a variety of other subjects of mutual interest to the United States and the People's Republic of China, the Chinese leaders agreed that they were sympathetic to President Sadat of Egypt and are trying to maintain good relations with all of the Arab countries. Also, the Chinese did not see a SALT II treaty as being "wrong" but were common in the belief that such an agreement would not restrain or bind the Soviets.

The Chinese see the Soviets viewing Vietnam as only another pawn on the Soviet chessboard. Russia's purpose, say the Chinese, is to control all of Indochina through a greater Indochina federation and then to move against the rest of Southeast Asia.

In Nanking the committee had a unique opportunity to visit the 179th Division of the People's Liberation Army (PLA) and was able to tour the base as well as observe an extended battlefield exercise with the use of live ammunition. That exercise clearly demonstrated the intensity of the training regimen. That visit was one of the highlights of the trip to China.

The delegation visited a Chinese commune of about 23,000 people, which consisted of a variety of activities, including manufacturing of clothes and electrical products, as well as animal husbandry and a fish hatchery. That stop was in Shanghai.

The final stop was at Canton, where at the request of the delegation there was an extended visit to the annual trade fair where the committee had an opportunity to examine the extent of the Chinese industrial base. "We intend to publish a report on the visit to the People's Republic of China at an early date, which will include our findings and conclusions, but I believe we can say at this time that we were pleased with our reception and found the attitude of the people we met to be generally cooperative and friendly," concluded Chairman Price.
