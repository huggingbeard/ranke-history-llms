BRITAIN'S COLONIAL POLICY AND RECORD 

The United Kingdom dependencies have a total area of just over two million square miles with 74 million inhabitants of many different races. They fall into 35 administrative units in six main regional groups-the Caribbean, West Africa, East and Central Africa, the Far East, the Mediterranean and the Pacific.

Most of the territories lie in the tropics, and their progress has been retarded by difficulties of environment. Without external aid there would be little prospect of these difficulties being overcome. 

In its present dependencies Britain follows principles which have long distinguished its policy in world affairs-promotion of the self-governing independence of peoples, the training of those not yet capable of it, and the maximum of self-government for units too small for sovereign statehood. 

The history of this policy goes back at least 130 years. 

Under British guidance the following countries have achieved sovereign self-governing independence : (1) Burma From dependence on Britain to independence, 1948 (Burma 

Independence Act, 1947; effective 4.1.48). (2) Ceylon From dependence on Britain to independence, 1948 (Ceylon 

Independence Act, 1947; effective 4.2.48).

British foreign policy, sometimes in part British arms, and British private individuals, have contributed to the attainment of sovereign self-governing independence by many other countries. 

In this paper, under broad heads-political and constitutional, economic and social—is briefly summarized Britain's record in respect of the countries and peoples at present dependent upon the United Kingdom. 2

Policy and Constitutional Advance 1. BRITAIN'S POLICY towards its dependencies is to help them to attain self-government within the British Commonwealth and to pursue their economic and social development so that it keeps pace with their political advance. 

When this development seems likely to be impeded by the smallness and poverty of individual territories, and geographical, political and economic conditions permit, the policy is to work for a federation of dependencies into a larger and more viable unit. 

The Federation of the Central African dependencies of Nyasaland and 

Northern Rhodesia with Southern Rhodesia in 1953 is the most recent example of this, and plans for a Caribbean Federation have been under discussion for some time with the Governments and peoples concerned. 

Some dependencies already have advanced constitutions placing responsibility for their own affairs largely in the hands of the local people, and in almost all territories there have been progressive revisions. 

2. PARLIAMENTARY GOVERNMENT. Nearly all dependencies have parliamentary legislatures. In 25, or about two-thirds of the total, the majority of members are local unofficial people. 

3. FRANCHISE. In 15 territories there is now universal adult suffrage, and limited adult suffrage exists in 12 others. In several, notably in Africa, systems of indirect election are bridging the gap between traditional forms of authority and modern democratic forms. 

4. Examples of RECENT CONSTITUTIONAL ADVANCES: 

(a) Malta has been responsible for her own government in internal affairs since 1947. (b) Gold Coast. A new constitution introduced in 1951, and based on a report by an all-African committee, provides for a majority of African Ministers, drawn from the largely elected Legislature, on the policymaking Executive Council. In 1952 the office of Prime Minister was formally instituted and an African, Dr. Kwame Nkrumah, was elected to the post. In 1953 the United Kingdom Government announced that it found broadly acceptable the Gold Coast Government's proposals for the creation of a cabinet of wholly African representative ministers drawn from a Legislature chosen by direct elections throughout the country. 3

(c) Nigeria. A federal type of constitution was introduced in 1951 after country-wide discussions. The Council of Ministers, the principal instrument of policy, has a majority of African Ministers. In the three 

Regions, Nigerians also have had a part in directing executive action. 

The Legislature is composed almost entirely of elected Africans. 

Representatives from all parts of the country met in London in 1953 to make proposals for a new type of constitution. (d) Sierra Leone. In 1953 the unofficial members¹ of the Executive Council were made Ministers and assumed portfolios. (e) Jamaica. The House of Representatives has, since 1944, consisted entirely of people elected by adult suffrage. In 1953, an elected majority was introduced into the Executive Council, the main policy instrument, eight Ministries were established, and the Ministers given executive functions. (f) Trinidad. Under a new constitution introduced in 1950 there is a large majority of members elected by universal suffrage in the Legislature. 

The policy-making Executive Council also has a majority of elected members and these constitute Ministers with departmental responsibilities. (g) British Honduras. A new constitution to come into operation in 1954 will introduce universal suffrage, a majority of elected members in the Legislature and a policy-making Executive Council with a majority of 

Legislative Council members. (h) Tanganyika. It has been agreed in principle that the unofficial¹ membership of the Legislative Council be divided equally between the three main racial groups-Africans, Asians and Europeans. (i) In Nyasaland non-Europeans have had seats on the Legislative Council since 1949. (j) In the Federation of Malaya the Federal constitution introduced in 1948 has stood up well to the strains and stresses imposed by the campaign against Communist terrorism. A quasi-ministerial system was introduced in 1951; in 1952 municipal elections were held as a step towards introducing elections at the State and finally at the Federal level; and in 1953 a Malayan Speaker was appointed to preside over the Legislature. 

5. THE CIVIL SERVICE. While political power is being increasingly transferred, steps are also being taken to ensure that the civil service in each territory is as far as possible locally manned. In the Colonial Service as a whole, officers born in the territories make up some 19 out of every 20 officers. 

To enable more locally born people to acquire the high qualifications necessary for the top administrative and technical posts, £1 million has been earmarked from United Kingdom Colonial Development and Welfare funds ¹Private individuals either elected or nominated by the Governor.

(see page 13, Finance). In addition the Governments of Nigeria and the Gold 

Coast have embarked upon a planned policy of Africanization. By 1951, there were over 450 Nigerians in the higher posts of the Service compared with 26 in 1938, and in the Gold Coast some 600 Africans by 1953 compared with 31 in 1938. In the West Indies more than 1,000 of 1,300 posts carrying a salary of £600 or over were held by West Indians by 1946. 

6. LOCAL GOVERNMENT. Side by side with developments in central government, democratic local government institutions are being built up in which people at town and village levels are learning to take responsibility for the management of their own affairs. (a) Municipal government in large towns is almost entirely in the hands of locally elected councillors. There are now 14 towns with city status. 

These include Nairobi which achieved it in 1950, Singapore in 1951, and Suva (Fiji) in 1953. (b) Rural local government ranges in form from village to county councils. 

For example: 

In Fiji a local government system having jurisdiction over all Fijians, dates back to 1876. In 1948 election of members by ballot was introduced to the Council of Chiefs and Provincial Councils. 

In the Federation of Malaya, in addition to the municipalities, legislation was passed in 1952 to enable village councils to be set up. 

In Sarawak, which became a British Colony in 1946, 17 local authorities equipped with treasuries and with jurisdiction over nearly half the total population had been set up by 1953. 

In Trinidad in 1952 executive powers and budgetary resources were conferred on the Colony's county councils.

II 

Economic and Social Progress 

1. Britain is pursuing the economic and social development of its dependencies to keep pace with political development. The full significance of what has been achieved can best be appreciated when it is remembered that most of these territories came under British administration little more than 50 years ago. 

2. To assist administration and promote development Britain gave or promised its dependencies a total of over £334 million during 1920-1953. 

3. Most territories have development plans. Those approved by mid-1953 provide for an estimated expenditure of £470 million, over £83 million of which is coming from Colonial Development and Welfare funds provided by Britain. 

4. To undertake or help in schemes of a commercial nature the Colonial 

Development Corporation was set up in 1948 with borrowing powers of up to £100 million. 

5. To help in financing economic and social research for the benefit of the dependencies over £12 million has been allotted under the Colonial Development and Welfare Acts since 1940. Some 33 per cent of this has been for agricultural research and over 14 per cent for medical research. 

6. Government major capital works in course of construction in 1951 had a total value of nearly £150 million. Of these about 24 per cent by value is for the provision of electricity and power, 23 per cent for railroad and telecommunications, 22 per cent for buildings, 15 per cent for waterworks, 8 per cent for ports and harbours, 4 per cent for drainage and irrigation and 

1 per cent for airfields. 

7. Capital formation, covering the creation of new assets by Government and other official bodies, commercial firms and private individuals, amounted in 1952 to some £400 million compared with £190 million in 1948. After allowing for price increases it is probable that the real value of capital formation was increasing by 5 to 10 per cent per annum over this period. (1) Communications 

ECONOMIC ADVANCES 

The first task in most territories was to open up communications, which had hitherto been primitive or non-existent. (a) Railways and roads have opened up the countries to development. 

For example, in the 10 British dependencies in Africa there were some

7,miles of railways in 1950 compared with 32 miles in 1900. By the same date 16,500 miles of trunk roads and 71,500 miles of secondary roads had been constructed where before there had, at the most, been only tracks. (b) Ports and Harbours have similarly been developed. 

Under the settled conditions brought by British control, the once unhealthy and sparsely populated island of Singapore has been transformed into one of the world's ten greatest ports. In 1952 it had a population of well over a million and its sea-borne import/export trade amounted to nearly £630 million. It is the chief centre of trade for South-East 

Asia, and a focal point for sea and air traffic between the Eastern and 

Western World. 

So, too, Hong Kong has developed from a barren island into one of the world's great ports.

Lagos Harbour, Nigeria, could not be used by ocean vessels until 1917-18 when improvements were effected. Now ships drawing up to 

25 feet can be accommodated and further improvements are under way to provide four extra berths for ocean vessels.

Takoradi Harbour, Gold Coast, was built between 1921 and 1928 at a cost of over £3,300,000 and is equipped to deal with the largest vessels operating on the West African coast. Extensions completed in 1953 at a cost of some £3 million have doubled accommodation alongside the main quay.

Work has begun on a new harbour at Tema in the Gold Coast which is estimated to cost £10,750,000. 

The Queen Elizabeth II Quay at Freetown, Sierra Leone, a 1,200-footlong deep-water quay, capable of accommodating three medium sized cargo vessels at the same time, is expected to be finished in 1953-54. 

At Dar es Salaam, Tanganyika, three new deep-water berths are being built, as is a new port at Mtwara. (c) Civil Aviation has been rapidly introduced and now plays an important part in internal communications and in linking the territories with the rest of the world.

In East Africa, the East African Airways Corporation set up in 1945 carried nearly 64,000 passengers in 1951, compared with some 9,000 in 1946.

In West Africa, the West African Airways Corporation formed in 1946 carried over 31,000 passengers in 1951, or more than double those carried the previous year, and its aircraft flew nearly two million miles. 

Nigeria has 30 airfields and at Kano airport over 20,000 aircraft movements, involving about 100,000 passengers, occur each year. 

At Livingstone, Northern Rhodesia, one of the largest airports in Africa was opened in 1950. The country now has 43 Government airfields.

Comet jet airliners of BOAC use the airport at Entebbe, capital of

Uganda, on the London-Johannesburg route, and for the purpose the airport was specially extended at a cost of £350,000. 

The British Caribbean territories, are now linked by air with Canada, the United States and Britain. In 1940 the first inter-island services were started with one plane; by 1950 there was a service operated by nine.

Nadi Airport, Fiji, built during the war, is an international airport used both for internal and external services. It links the Colony with other territories in the Pacific as well as with Australia, New Zealand and the United States. In 1952 over 780 planes called there. 

At Paya Lebar in Singapore a new £4 million airport is under construction. 

(2) Production and Trade 

The establishment of law and order and the building of communications, the first tasks of British administration, created conditions in which production and trade could be developed.

Primary Commodities 

(a) Production has been built up to a scale of world importance. For instance:

Rubber. Malaya grows about half of the world's supply. It was introduced there by the British in the 1870s from South America via the 

Royal Botanic Gardens at Kew, outside London.

Cocoa. Today, almost one half of the world's supply comes from British 

West Africa. The industry is entirely African, and provides the second most valuable export of the dependencies.

Oil-bearing Nuts and Seeds. The export of these has been greatly developed in West Africa. Nigeria exports some 170,000 tons of palm oil and over 400,000 tons of palm kernels annually-far more than any other country in the world.

Hard Hemp. Some 33 per cent of world production comes from the dependencies.

Sugar. About 7 per cent of world cane sugar production is grown in the dependencies.

Tin. Malaya and Nigeria produce about 40 per cent of total world production. 

Copper. Northern Rhodesia provides some 12 per cent of world production. 

Cobalt. Northern Rhodesia provides about 9 per cent of world supplies.

Manganese. The Gold Coast provides over 10 per cent of world supplies.

Bauxite. British Guiana is the second largest producer in the world. 

Exports have risen from some 447,000 tons in 1938 to over two million tons post-war.

Columbite. Nigeria is the world's chief supplier of this mineral which is in increasing demand as a constituent in alloys used for jet engine parts. (b) Other products of which there have been large increases include the 

Petroleum. Production in Brunei, the chief producer in the UK dependencies, has been increased from about 452,000 tons of crude oil in 1936 to some five million tons in 1952. 

Rice. British Guiana now grows some 76,000 tons compared with 

33,000 tons in 1924-29; Malaya which before the war produced about a third of its rice requirement now produces about a half-441,000 tons in 1952-53.

North Borneo could feed only 1 in 4 of its population from local production just after the war but in 1948 could supply 4 out of 5; production in West Africa has increased by some 30 per cent since before the war. Further increases can be expected from the schemes to be undertaken with £3 million specially allocated for the purpose by the UK in 1953.

Cotton. Production in the dependencies amounted to over 616,000 bales of 400 lb. in 1952 compared with an average of some 249,000 bales in 1929-32. Uganda, the chief producer, grew 380,000 bales, some 40,000 more than in 1950. (c) Many of these primary commodities, production of which has been built to world significance since 1900, are the result of peasant and not of plantation cultivation. For example, some 40 per cent of Malaya's rubber is produced from Asian smallholdings. Cocoa in West Africa is grown chiefly by African farmers. Almost the entire output of oilseeds in West Africa is from peasant farms. 

Industrial Development and Power 

(a) The development of secondary industries is going ahead wherever conditions are suitable. 

Examples of those recently established are: 

Singapore. Factories for aluminium ware, glassware, textiles, metal containers, dry batteries, soap, plywood, plastics and pottery. Factories registered in 1953 totalled over 1,500 compared with 450 pre-war. 

Federation of Malaya. Cement production began in 1953 at a factory planned to meet initially a quarter of the Federation's and Singapore's needs.

Jamaica. Factories for gypsum products, tool handles, glass, paper, cement and textiles. 

Gold Coast. Furniture workshops, sawmills, laundry, light engineering and food processing, brick, tile, pottery, charcoal and soap works.

Trinidad. Between June 1950 and July 1953, 38 new industries came into being. In operation or preparation are plants for glass-making, brewing, spinning, industrial rubber and plastic goods, electro-plating and other processes. Cement works are under construction. 

Northern Rhodesia. Cement works opened 1951. There are also woodworking, tobacco and textile factories, an iron foundry and other plants producing oxygen, soap, bricks and blankets. 

Kenya. By 1952 was producing some 80 factory products. In spite of the Emergency, factories which came into operation during the year included those for the manufacture of knitwear, steel drums and glass bottles. In 1953 negotiations were in hand for factories to make metalframe doors and windows, chemicals and textiles and to establish a second cement factory. (b) Several large-scale undertakings for the development of hydro-electric and thermal power are in hand or under consideration. These will remedy the lack of a source of cheap power which up to now has seriously limited the industrial potentialities of most territories. The principal schemes are:

Uganda. Owen Falls Nile Scheme for the construction of a hydroelectric station at Owen Falls near Jinja on the White Nile to provide a source of cheap electricity and add to the storage capacity of Lake Victoria. Work on the dam began in 1950. It will be put into operation by Her Majesty the Queen on 29th April, 1954. The power station will house ten generating units of 15,000 kW capacity each. The Uganda 

Government has undertaken to raise £13 million for the scheme, of which over £9 million has already been raised, mainly on the London market. Capital expenditure between 1948 and end of 1952 amounted to over £10 million.

Gold Coast. Volta River Scheme envisages the construction of a dam and hydro-electric power station on the Volta River, which would provide electricity for a large-scale aluminium industry using Gold Coast bauxite, and others. It would create the largest artificial lake in the world and also make water available for irrigation in the Accra plains. The estimated cost, including that of a new port and railways, is £150 million.

The Connaught Bridge Power Station in the Federation of Malaya will, when completed, have a total capacity of 80,000 kW, and afford an unrestricted power supply to industry in three areas. The first half, with a generating capacity of 40,000 kW, was opened in March 1953. 

The Kafue River Project, Northern Rhodesia, for which money is now being raised, will generate enough electric power to meet the minimum needs of the copper belt and supply 100 megawatts for industries in 

Southern Rhodesia.

The Colonial Development and Welfare Acts of 1945 and 1950 included in this list provide £140 million from the British Treasury for economic and social developments and research in the dependencies over the 10 years 1946-56. In 1953, the Secretary of State for the Colonies said that Parliament would be asked to vote further sums in 1954 for Colonial Development. He also announced the extra allocation of up to £3 million to promote rice production in the dependencies. 

The purposes to which the existing Colonial Development and Welfare funds are being devoted are, in order of the amounts set aside, social, medical and education services; economic development schemes; communications; research; physical planning and surveys; Colonial 

Service training schemes; administration. (b) The Colonial Development Corporation established in 1948 has borrowing powers up to £100 million from the British Treasury to further development projects in the dependencies. By the end of 1953 the 

Corporation had 60 projects in operation involving a total capital commitment of some £50 million. (c) Overseas Food Corporation. £6 million has been earmarked for the completion of a seven-year (1951-57) experiment by the Overseas Food Corporation to establish the economics of land clearance and mechanized agriculture in marginal land in East Africa. 

2. Loans raised on the London market are a further source of finance to the Governments of the dependencies. Money thus raised together with conversions, between 1948 and 1953, has totalled over £100 million. 

3. Total value of development plans in the dependencies (including money from United Kingdom funds, local resources and loans) now stands at over £470 million. 13

4. Private investment also plays its part. Barclays Bank (Dominion,

Colonial and Overseas), for instance, has a registered subsidiary, Barclays Overseas Development Corporation Ltd., with an authorized capital of £5 million. Its aim is to provide finance for development, and by March 1952 it was interested in 230 propositions and the total of loan and investment limits approved totalled over £5.5 million. Many of the loans and investments are devoted to industrial development but the Corporation is also taking an interest in agricultural, educational, co-operative and building schemes, and has approved applications from individuals, private and public companies and Colonial Governments. 

5. As a result of aid in development and increased production, total revenue of UK dependencies was £357 million in 1952, compared with £6 million in 1900.

Trade unions in the dependencies numbered 1,325 by September 1952, compared with some 300 in 1943. Membership rose from 680,000 in 1949 to 

865,000 in 1952.

There are Labour Departments in all but the very small territories. To help them experienced UK trade unionists are appointed. Training courses for labour officers are held in Britain as well as in the dependencies. (1) Health 

SOCIAL DEVELOPMENTS 

The establishment and expansion of medical services, research work, persistent health propaganda and increasing prosperity have already achieved notable results in overcoming the problems of tropical diseases aggravated by mass poverty and backwardness. (a) Financial contributions from the United Kingdom for such work have been devoted to many purposes. Between 1920 and 1950, for instance, the UK contributed over £38,000 to the Bureau of Hygiene and Tropical Diseases in London, which collects and distributes information on hygiene and tropical diseases. In the same period over £15,000 was given to organizations concerned with combating venereal diseases. 

Between 1946 and 1953 over £13 million was allocated from UK Colonial Development and Welfare funds for medical and health services. (b) The transformation in general health standards is illustrated by the following statistics: 

In 1952, the death rate of Singapore, near the Equator, was 11.20 per 

1,000 population, the lowest on record and as low as that of the United 14

Kingdom in the temperate zone. Its infant mortality rate in 1952 was 

70 per 1,000 compared with 130 in 1939.

By 1952 Cyprus had one of the lowest death rates in the world (7.65 per 1,000 population). In 1952 its infant mortality rate was 59 per 1,000 compared with 180 in 1942. By 1950, as the result of a mosquito extermination campaign launched in 1945, the danger of contracting malaria there no longer existed. In 1936 the Rockefeller Foundation had described it as one of the most malarious places in the world'. 

In Jamaica, Trinidad and British Guiana the average length of life has increased by 15 years since 1921. (c) The expansion of medical facilities is illustrated by the following 

Nigeria. In 1900 there were five hospitals and 89 beds. By 1952 there were 152 hospitals, 244 maternity homes and some 800 dispensaries, and over 9,400 beds.

In Kenya, hospital in-patients in 1952 numbered 174,000 compared with 49,000 in 1937, and out-patients numbered over one million compared with 460,000.

Sarawak. Districts once out of reach of the medical service are now served by a mobile dispensary service of 16 boats which treats up to 

160,000 cases a year.

Singapore. Annual out-patient attendance is now approaching 600,000 compared with 90,000 in 1938.

Further illustrations are provided by figures of government expenditure. 

For example, expenditure on the Medical Department in Nigeria rose from less than half a million pounds in 1928 to over £3.5 million in 1951, and in 

Northern Rhodesia from some £38,000 in 1926 to nearly £863,000 in 1952. (d) In the battle against disease notable successes have been won, and the fullest use is being made of modern scientific discoveries. 

Of the classical epidemic diseases, yellow fever is reduced to a few sporadic cases among non-inoculated Africans. 

Smallpox no longer reaches epidemic proportions.

Trypanosomiasis is kept under control by special measures which are constantly being strengthened by the results of research. In Nigeria, in 1952, only 7,000 cases were treated-the lowest number for 20 years. 

Malaria has been completely eradicated from Cyprus, where 18,000 cases were reported yearly before the war, and its transmission in Mauritius has been stopped. In British Guiana practically the entire population is protected from the disease by a DDT-spraying programme, and there, as in many other territories, the incidence is decreasing. 15

The treatment of leprosy with the newest drugs is proving successful and has brought hopes and means of being cured to many sufferers. 

The work of the Leprosy Research Unit in Nigeria on new drugs has attracted world-wide attention, and as the result of its efforts and those of a British firm a patient can now receive under expert supervision a treatment more efficient than ever before at a cost of some 15s. a year. 

Over 6,000 out of 30,000 patients in the Eastern Region of Nigeria were discharged as symptom-free in 1952. 

Tuberculosis is being tackled by many methods, both medical and social, including BCG vaccination campaigns. In Trinidad it is planned to vaccinate some 250,000 adults and children during a two-year campaign launched in 1952. Similar campaigns are in progress in 

Jamaica, the Far East territories and in Aden. Bermuda can now claim virtually total control over the disease. 

One of the chief drugs used in the treatment of yaws is penicillin, discovered by the British scientist, Sir Alexander Fleming.

Organizations for co-ordinating welfare of the blind now exist in 26 dependencies as the result of the United Kingdom Government and colonial Governments supporting the British Empire Society for the Blind set up in 1950 to deal with the problems of blindness in the dependencies. Already many teachers and organizers trained in the UK are at work in the dependencies; 12 new schools were established between 1950 and 1953 thus more than doubling school places for blind children, many vernacular languages have been adapted to Braille, and a three-year survey of the causes of blindness in West 

Africa began in October 1952. (2) Education 

(a) Primary and Secondary Education 

Some 50 years ago the number of children at school in the African dependencies was negligible. In 1952 there were well over two million. 

Nigeria, for instance, had some 4,000 children at school in 1900. In 

1952 there were over one million attending some 9,000 schools. Expenditure from Central Government funds rose from £300,000 in 1938-39 to £4,500,000 in 1951-52. In addition voluntary agencies and Native 

Administrations spent over £2 million. 

Kenya. Between 1945 and 1952 the number of aided primary schools nearly doubled and now totals nearly 1,300. 

Uganda. In 1910 there were 60,000 children at school. In 1952 there were nearly 289,000. Government expenditure on African education increased from £76,000 in 1935 to £1,082,190 in 1952. 16

Similar advances can be seen in other territories. For example: 

Jamaica. In 1952, 217,000 of the 300,000 children between the ages of 

7 and 15 were enrolled in Government and Government-aided primary schools compared with 95,000 in 1910. Government expenditure on all types of education was over £1.7 million in 1951 compared with £67,000 in 1910.

Malaya. 722,000 children were at school in 1952 compared with 

260,000 in 1945.

Hong Kong. The number of educational institutions increased from 

672 in 1947-48 to 1,039 in 1951-52. 

(b) Technical Education 

The great need for skilled technicians and craftsmen in the dependencies is being met by the expansion of facilities for technical education at all levels. Facilities at the higher level now include: 

Nigeria. College of Arts, Science and Technology with branches at

Ibadan, Zaria and Enugu, for which a UK Colonial Development and 

Welfare grant of £600,000 has been made, is just starting work at 

Ibadan and Zaria. 

Gold Coast. The College of Technology, Science and Arts at Kumasi, which started work in 1952 with 450 students, will have 700 students by January 1954. 

Hong Kong. Technical college founded in 1936, had 370 full-time students in 1951 and 2,600 attending evening classes in 1952. 

Trinidad. The Imperial College of Tropical Agriculture which serves all the dependencies, was founded in 1921, and in 1951-52 was training 79 students from many territories in agriculture, plant pathology, chemistry, sugar technology and so on, and carrying out research. 

(c) Higher and Adult Education 

There are now three universities (Malta, Hong Kong and Malaya) and four university colleges (in the West Indies, Gold Coast, Nigeria and East Africa) with a total enrolment in 1952-53 of 3,464. In July 

1953, 6,770 students from the dependencies were receiving higher education in the United Kingdom and Irish Republic, compared with 300 in 1939. Others are receiving higher education in other countries: for instance, there are some 1,300 students from the dependencies in the United States of America.

Broadcasting is being increasingly used as a medium of education. 

Kenya, in 1928, was the first dependency to set up a wireless broadcasting station; by 1953 there were 33 broadcasting and wired broadcasting services operating in the colonial territories. 17

One of the most important proofs of educational advance is to be found in the increasing number of locally born men and women who are rising to distinction in politics, the professions, business, and the civil service of their own countries. This trend is well illustrated by the composition of the committee appointed by the Governor of the Gold Coast in 1949 to recommend advances in the constitution: all its 39 members were Africans distinguished in some aspect of Gold Coast affairs, and its chairman, Mr. Justice H. Coussey, was an 

African Judge of the Supreme Court. 

(d) Financial Contributions from the United Kingdom 

Under the Colonial Development and Welfare Acts these totalled over £19 million by March 1953; of this sum £9.7 million was for primary and secondary education, £4.4 million for technical and vocational education, and £5.3 million for higher education. 18
