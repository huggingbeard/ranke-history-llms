POLITICS AND THE ELECTORAL COLLEGE By WILLIAM STARR MYERS Emeritus Professor of Political Science, Princeton University 

Research Director, Committee of Americans

THE ACTIVITIES of several recent Democratic State conventions in the South have brought to mind the understanding, or lack of it, by most of our people concerning the historical and political facts in connection with our Electoral College. The conventions in at least two of the 

States have passed resolutions that would prevent the electors of the Democratic party in their respective states from being bound to vote for the Presidential nominee of the National Democratic Convention unless certain demands of the South were met. As already stated, this raises questions of transcendent importance not only to the Democratic party as at present organized and led, but also to the whole nation. 581536 ys. [1]

HISTORICAL BACKGROUND 

T SHOULD BE RECALLED to mind that the makers I of the United States Constitution were not only really great men, but also experienced politicians. The Convention of 1787 was, in large part, composed of the same men who had led and won the Revolutionary War. While there were statesmen and theorists among them, in addition, most of them had held office in the Colonial or State, as well as National, Governments during the immediately preceding years. The success of the Constitution since that time is largely due to the fact that these men did not invent a new form of government as much as codify their experience as both British Colonial and American officials. 

One of their few inventions, and the one of which they were especially proud, was the method provided for the election of the President and Vice-President by means of "electors" in an Electoral College. It was noted most carefully when the Constitution was before the States for final adoption that this electoral method was one of the few things that aroused little or no controversy. It was not opposed at all, but, to a great extent, just because this was an invention, it was soon metaphorically cast into the waste basket on account of the practical necessities of party politics. The voters were supposed to choose men of ability [2]

and experience to serve as electors for the sole purpose of choosing a President and Vice-President. They were to meet in their respective states and each was to vote for two persons who in their judgment had the highest qualifications for the offices. The one having a majority of the electoral votes of all the States was to be President, and the one next in number, and with a majority, to be Vice- 

President. This was changed by the Twelfth Amendment to the Constitution in 1804 to be a designated vote for candidates for each of the offices. 

This beautiful and ideal plan was soon set aside by practical politics and the electors were supposed to be morally bound to vote for the nominees of their national party. These parties were organized within a few years after the National Government was established. This rule has been broken only once or twice. In the year 1820 James 

Monroe carried every State at the time of his re-election to the office of President. William Plumer, an elector in 

New Hampshire, cast his vote for John Quincy Adams.

Gossip says he did this in order that no person other than George Washington ever should have the honor of receiving all the electoral votes, but unfortunately this is probably untrue. It is more than likely that Plumer cast his vote in personal opposition to Monroe and also to advertise Adams as a candidate at the next election, in 1824. [3]

Horace Greeley, coalition candidate of the Democratic and Liberal Republican parties in 1872, died before the Electoral College met following the general election in November of that year. His electors scattered their votes among favorites but three of them, from the State of Georgia, actually voted for Horace Greeley, a dead man, because they were afraid to vote for any other. These votes were not counted by Congress when it declared Grant elected.

THE PRESIDENTIAL ELECTORS IN THE 1944 CAMPAIGN 

HE QUESTION of the legal responsibilities of the electors is not settled today, although the moral compulsion always has been stressed. It should be remembered [4]

that the national parties, the Democratic and the Republican, are, in a sense, legally established by the direct primary and other laws in a large number of States, but it is just here that the actions of Texas and Mississippi come into special importance. The regularly and legally organized Democratic Conventions of these two States instructed their delegates to the Democratic National Convention in Chicago to demand certain things, which it was well known the Convention would be unwilling to do. In that case it also was provided by the State Conventions that the nominees, by these same Conventions, of the State 

Democratic party for Presidential Electors, from Texas and Mississippi were not to be bound to vote for the nominees of the National Convention, if the voters at the polls elected these same electors. Now comes the question, will these electors follow the bidding of their State party conventions, and, if they do or do not, will their action be legal in either case?

A further problem, of more significance, would come to the fore in case their votes should be necessary for the actual election of a President or Vice-President by the Electoral College. It will be remembered that the Constitution further provides that such election must be by a majority of all the electoral votes cast, not by a plurality. 

In case such a majority is lacking, then the election is [5]

thrown into Congress. The Senators, voting as individuals, choose the Vice-President, and there is little doubt as to the result in the next Senate, since that body probably will continue overwhelmingly Democratic, and the party nominee would be chosen promptly and without difficulty. The choice for President, however, lies with the House of Representatives where the members vote as States, as the majority of the members of each State delegation may determine. Thus, the one member each from Delaware, or Nevada, or Wyoming would equal with his vote those of New York, or California, or Illinois, as the majority of the members from those respective States might decide. 

According to present laws, the Congress elected in 

November 1944 (the Seventy-ninth) will meet on January 3, 1945 and three days later, on the 6th of the month, open and count the returns from the States of the number of electoral votes cast during the preceding month of December for the various candidates. Now we will suppose that the electors in Texas, Mississippi or other States refused to vote for the party candidates and cast their votes for other individuals, thus preventing the election of the President or Vice-President by the necessary majority of votes. What will Congress do-especially the House of Representatives?

If the present status of political control remains in [6]

that body, it will be found that the delegations of 21 States are Democratic, and those of 22 States are Republican, but the delegations from Idaho, Washington and West Virginia are equally divided between the two parties. In the Wisconsin delegation the number of Republicans (5) is exactly equal to those of the 3 Democrats and 2 Progressivės. These Progressives thus would hold the balance of power for that State and could prevent the vote from being cast. This balance could have the same result for those of 

Idaho, Washington and West Virginia. Also, a dangerous power might lie in the hands of one man of definitely radical leaning, as far as political beliefs and activities are concerned. This man is the one American Labor party member from the 20th New York District, namely, Vito

Marcantonio. The delegation from his State in the present (Seventy-eighth) Congress stands at exactly 22 Republicans and 22 Democrats. What would Mr. Marcantonio do in case the situation continues after the November 1944 election? Naturally and quite honestly he could name his political terms, and what would he demand? Of course, the same holds true with regard to the 2 Progressives from Wisconsin.

One can easily see the possibilities of controversy and conflict, and the supreme importance to each and all parties of the political complexion of the membership of the new [7]

Congress, especially that of the House of Representatives. 

Also, there can easily be seen the possibilities of political maneuvering and bargaining, with the consequent effects upon the country. It is more necessary than ever that our intelligent citizens go to the polls in every election and cast their votes, not only for President and Vice-President but also for Congress, as the individual conscience and intelligence may dictate.
