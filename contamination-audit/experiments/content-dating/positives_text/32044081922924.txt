Not so long ago.. 

Molly Goldberg wore a path between CBS and NBC attempting to disprove the contention that "nobody would listen to a Jewish family's goings-on"...

That was the time when ...

Radio was a very young infant and the "biggies" of stage and cinema smiled patronizingly on it. Radio was forced to rely on a small group of amateurs for its first talent-the "amateurs" who today are its brightest luminaries.

Yes, the Radio industry owes a great debt to amateurs. 

And it must never forget that from the ranks of "amateurs' still come most of its top entertainers, newsmen, announcers, actors, and all the rest.

FOREWORD 

Radio is "here to stay." It is no longer possible to ignore, dispense with or neglect it. It is more than twenty years old and much of its infantile and adolescent "experimenting" is gone. It is approaching full maturity artistically, having long ago done so mechanically. It is as much a staple of the American environment, '43, as the electric refrigerator, the automobile and the plane-and equally as far-reaching in its effects as all three.

Maybe this brief "apologia" seems redundant to many of you. But we don't think it is for we've personally encountered a surprising degree of inertia and indifference among Catholic educators and lay leaders regarding the medium of radio and its use. Lately there have been signs of a belated awakening and we DO mean belated, considering the fact that for the past decade public school systems from coast to coast have been incorporating radio in their curricula, that many religious sects have taken to the air, and several universities own and operate their own stations.

The participation in and utilization of radio as another means of implementing the objectives of Catholic Action is not exactly a new idea, but it is certainly a neglected one! If we're now going to do something about it, there is one bona fide, proved way to approach the idea and make it dynamically functional. This approach is the Radio Workshop, the "lab" in which interested participants may learn the essentials of the medium the better to make it serve the ideals and principles of Catholic Action on the march. 

Radio is no "breeze." It has procedures, practices, standards, rules and taboos just as any other entertainment and communication medium has. These must be learned and learned well before radio can serve Christ's Church to its full potentiality. It is for this reason that this book was written. 

The author would like to emphasize that the book does not pretend to give the alpha and omega of radio. There is much that, of necessity, is not included, such as the commercial side of the industry and the discussion of frequency modulation 

-5-

and television. It is not intended as a text, but rather as a kind of Baedeker for the teacher and Sodality moderator on his first trip through the maze of sound effects, production problems, expository dramatization, music backgrounds and "mike" techniques. In short, it gives the recipe for a practical Radio Workshop. The directions, principles and guides given herein have been learned the "hard way," through six years of writing, production and program planning, together with much valuable experience in the workshop field itself. 

And this book might never have been written at all had it not been for the inspiring counsel of the Reverend Daniel A.

Lord, S. J., the excellent editing of the manuscript by the 

Reverend Fathers Leo P. Wobido, S. J., and Robert A. Johnston, S. J., the fine professional preparation of the materials for printing done so painstakingly by Miss Alice Clarke, the endless typing and re-typing of chapters by Miss Jessie Mary Kulage and Miss Jacqueline Emmons, plus the constant support and encouragement of Mr. Clyde W. So you see this is not essentially my book. It is a corporate production of many people who realized the need of such a book and who kept heckling me until there was no other alternative than to write it! We-all of us responsible for this publication-hope that it will be the “shot-inthe-arm" that will arouse Catholics to learn how to use radio in Christ's "interest, convenience and necessity."

INTRODUCTION 

For a long time nearly everybody has known that we have needed just such a radio manual as this-along with a lot of other things. 

The only question was: Who is going to stop talking about it and start writing it?

Catholics, like everyone else in America, are avid radio fans. Catholics, however, have had the abashed certainty that to the large chorus which is radio, they have lent but a minor and often piping voice. There have been great Catholics on the radio and great radio Catholics, but certainly not in proportion to our numbers and supposed influence. 

So everyone talked about the need for a Catholic radio manual and for more Catholic radio. Everybody felt that everybody else should be doing something. But who? And how?

This manual is a first answer to those questions. With considerable pride and no little satisfaction I take my post as toastmaster to introduce the two somebodies who have done something very definite about a manual and who are all set to do a great deal more for Catholic radio. "Ladies and gentlemen, I introduce to you the Reverend 

Leo Philip Wobido, S. J. and Mrs. Mary Agnes Tynan Schroeder." 

To drop the radio approach, let me say it is with real complacency that I introduce this first Catholic radio manual. 

The manual was developed with considerable dispatch. 

During the course of the Summer School of Catholic Action in 1943, Father Wobido gave several lectures on the subject of Catholic radio. These lectures had grown out of a very careful survey which he had made of religious radio. The interest which the lectures excited was proof positive that Catholics did want a place before the "mike," that they felt that there was need for radio education in our schools, that they were convinced that out of our numerous Catholic societies, notably the Sodality, there could and should develop radio writers, producers, and talent. -7-

But Father Wobido realized, as I think we all do, that amateur radio can be just about the most amateurish form of entertainment. The scripts are often dull and fumbling. The drama is conspicuously missing. The actors sound untrained. 

That suavity and speed, that mastery of the split second, that blending of music, speech and sound that makes for good professional radio, are almost entirely missing in the amateur performances.

Beyond this, it has been our experience that the best intentioned amateurs in the world soon drop by the radio wayside. We have watched them drop. The first program they present may be fairly good. In no time at all they are practically exhausted. Not knowing the medium through which they are working, they find script-writing a terrible bore, a weekly problem too difficult to sustain; and amateur actors and singers are notably temperamental. Even those amateur programs which start with a fanfare end up with scarcely more than a dying gasp.

So very wisely Father Wobido decided that what we needed was professional guidance. And at this point Mrs. 

Mary Agnes Tynan Schroeder entered our picture. I had known Mary Agnes from the days when she was a Junior at 

Saint Mary's High School in Chicago, a very dashing Junior whose temperament lived up to her red hair and who, at the Sodality Convention of 1930, made the best talk I have ever heard given by a high school student. In the interval she has become a trained public speaker, a college instructor in Speech and Drama and later, as an important member of the Radio Council of the Chicago Public Schools, she has climbed high in the radio world.

So when Father Wobido told me that Mary Agnes was to join us, I felt we were well on the way toward professional radio.

Before anything else, we all agreed, we needed a radio manual. There was, as far as we could discover, simply no textbook that had the Catholic viewpoint or that took into consideration those thousands of Catholics who ached for a hance to stand before the microphone and blend religion with entertainment, the great truths and ideals of the Catholic 

8-

Church with the technique of the newest and probably the most influential of modern media. 

Mrs. Schroeder arrived at our Sodality Central Office in 

St. Louis, was parked in her office, and instantly the chapters of the new book began to follow one another in rapid succession to my desk.

From the start, I knew the manual was exactly what we had wanted. Any amateur could read it and understand it. 

But any amateur who would read it and understand it would, by that very fact, have been given a professional polish. 

Mrs. Schroeder brought to the book a vast radio experience. She had written innumerable successful scripts and produced a wide variety of successful radio shows. The widely imitated radio broadcasting program of the Chicago public schools had owed much to her instinctive ability and to her trained and developed knowledge of radio in all its aspects. 

But even with the manual in hand, we were all convinced that this was just a beginning. While students of radio may use the textbook on their own and far from the Sodality Central Office, the manual would be more effective in the hands of a professional teacher. Hence the immediate need for a 

Catholic school of the radio. Hence the origin of the Queen's 

Workshop of the Air. In collaboration with St. Louis University and its radio station, WEW, Father Wobido and Mrs. 

Schroeder are presenting this new Catholic experiment. This 

Radio Workshop is supplementing the manual and setting about the training of Catholic radio artists and producers. 

As far as we know, nothing of this sort has yet been done. 

The response to the rumors about our radio activity were instant and enthusiastic. So we have high hopes for what will be done through the manual and the Workshop for Catholic radio. 

Father Wobido and Mrs. Schroeder will be delighted to enter into correspondence with you. The manual has been preceded and succeeded by a series of radio dramas which are now available for presentation over the air or for practice in the radio workshop of schools, parishes and clubs. This catalogue of radio dramas will grow. So, I confidently expect, -9-

will the whole new radio program. In addition, a monthly listing of the best network features for the coming month will be offered to teachers and workshop directors, together with a critical evaluation of these programs and discussion notes as a service to workshop groups.

It has often been said that if Saint Paul were alive today, he would be a journalist. Perhaps in this year of grace and science, it is more correct to say that if Saint Paul were alive today, he would be a broadcaster. There is no questioning 

Saint Paul's dramatic sense. When he strode in among the 

Athenians, he dramatized the altar of their unknown god to shock them out of their religious apathy. We may be sure he would not be content with mere sermons over the air. He would dramatize the tremendous truths of the Catholic Church in a compelling, emotional, convincing way that would gain for him millions of listeners and uncounted converts. 

So we present the new manual hopefully and confidently. 

We shall be deeply interested in what you think, and what will be your response to our entire new radio project. 

Quite sincerely we ask your prayers for its success. 

Feast of St. Francis Xavier, 1943.

DANIEL A. LORD, S. J.

CHAPTER I PRESENTING: THE FIFTH "R" 

Yes, there are five "R's."

And with exemplary dispatch a great majority of the citizenry can call off the first three of the so-called tools of all education: 

READIN' 'RITIN' 'RITHMETIC 

A smaller but nonetheless articulate segment can tell you what the fourth "R" is and what its importance is in the scheme of education. 

To Catholic teachers this fourth "R" is religion. To them religion is the nuclear center about which all education rotates. They believe, with G. K. Chesterton, that "the teaching of the simple formula that two-times-two is four can be made an apologetics lesson." 

But what of this fifth "R"? 

Now just what is it, please?

The fifth "R" is the result of the dreams and accomplishments of a cavalcade of scientists (among them Edison, Bell, Marconi and De Forrest); of their vision, failures and triumphs with astronomical amounts of tubes, wires, dials and that buoyant quantity called human faith. Each of these men in turn, plus uncounted thousands of anonymous helpers, aided in shaping this century's "magic key," which opened wide the doors of the world and brought us into instant contact with all parts of it. 

In short, these "dreamers" gave us radio.

RADIO AND EDUCATION 

Radio today is fast becoming education's fifth "R," a fact attested to by:

The score or more of educational broadcasting agencies. 

The special network departments of "Education" or "Public Service" (as some prefer to designate them). 

-11-

The university courses in the background and the practices of the radio industry. 

The trade schools which train radio actors, writers, technicians and producers.

The fact that several national conferences each year devote their entire agenda to the consideration of the problem of broadcasting for school use and, in turn, the use of broadcasts in the classroom.

The evaluation of educational radio programs is fast becoming an exact science. 

The proportion of radio time given to public service and educational broadcasting is hearteningly greater in each new survey.

The Columbia Broadcasting System has pioneered with a "Network-of-the-Americas' program for use in the schools of both North and Latin America.

CBS's great competitor, the National Broadcasting Company, has planned and is now broadcasting the "Inter-American University of the Air," attempting on an adult level what CBS has already done in the elementary and secondary school field.

Half a hundred teachers' colleges now offer to teachers courses in radio education. 

In brief, radio is here to stay as a basic aid to the learning process.

The broadcasters know it, for their attitude towards educational broadcasting has changed markedly in the past five years.

And there are thousands of public school classrooms the nation over which turn as easily to their weekly or daily program as they do to their wall maps, reference books and educational films. 66

This provision is the watch-dog of the American system of broadcasting. It assures the listening public at all times of the "right" of being heard. -12-

What is the force of this stipulation? Perhaps here a word of explanation is in place. 

There are approximately a thousand licensed broadcasting stations operating in the United States. Since these licensees are using part of the public domain-the "ether"no corporation may monopolize it. Hence the Federal Radio 

Act of 1927 regulates the use of the air-lanes for broadcasting purposes.

This law requires that broadcasters so operate their stations as to serve "the public interest, convenience and necessity" or else forfeit their franchises. These franchises are renewed every two years-only IF the Federal Communications Committee decides that the particular radio station has discharged its responsibilities to the public, performed its required functions adequately, and has generally proved worthy of its wave length. "Public Service" without religion is inadequate, hence religious programs have a definite place in any service which is designed to meet the needs and wishes of the public. 

The majority of radio stations have recognized their obligations to religion and have discharged them readily to the various religious groups requesting time on the air. 

Broadcasting is of tremendous aid to religion: 

Because the broadcasting of religious services, ideals and information gives simultaneous and extensive coverage. 

Because radio reaches in many cases isolated units of the population who because of geographic location cannot attend the church of their choice frequently. 

Because churches can, through the medium of radio, reach a greater audience at one time than by any other single means.

Because the very nature of radio communication makes it possible to approach a "congregation" informally, at any hour of the day or evening, within the home itself, which needs the aura and spirit of religion today as it never needed it before.

Yes, these points are on the credit side of the ledger as far as radio's service to religion is concerned. 

-13-

But on the other side, there are recognized drawbacks:

Because radio enters the homes of members of ALL religious denominations, the broadcaster feels that he must be very circumspect regarding the nature of the religious material presented.

For example, when some religious sect comes forth with a pronouncement that is sharply in conflict with the revealed truths of Christ as taught by the Catholic Church, it is indeed difficult and in many cases impossible for the Catholic apologist to take to the air and condemn this latest departure from the teachings of Christ. The broadcaster will tell him that the station dare not risk offending that part of its audience which is not Catholic. Again:

In many communities where the proportion of Catholics to the rest of the population is relatively small, the local station manager feels that he would be serving only a very minor segment of his "public" in giving time for a strictly Catholic broadcast, and would, in most cases, refuse to do so. 

Then there is the matter of "balancing" time on the air: 

Most small stations and all networks operate on the policy that in matters of controversy BOTH sides of the question must be given equal time on the air. 

In many small towns and cities there is often one active religious group and several lethargic groups of opposing denominations. When one group requests time, especially for a matter which will conceivably provoke a controversy, the local station will be loathe to give it knowing that it will be almost impossible to "adrenalinize" the opposition.

These two sides to the question of religious broadcasting must be understood and understood clearly before any sound program for making radio serve religion can be built. 

RADIO AND CATHOLIC ACTION 

What is the fifth "R" doing to implement the fourth "R" for Catholics?

Let's look more closely at the Catholic picture: -14-

There are upwards of 500 radio stations not carrying a regular Catholic broadcast, at least during a considerable part of the year. Of these stations without Catholic programs, so a survey shows, more than half acknowledged that they carried no Catholic program whatever. Around a hundred failed to respond to the request for data-indicating implicitly that they carried no Catholic programs. Over a hundred station managers, however, stated that they would like to carry a Catholic broadcast-if it were worthwhile. 

The same survey reveals that the combined radio activities of the various Protestant organizations and Jewish groups far outstrip those of the Catholics, the chief reason for this being their centralized bureaus which assemble materials to be broadcast and distribute the services to local units of the denominations. 

But what of the Catholics?

What of the Sodalities, the Catholic Action organizations, the societies and clubs, the discussion groups, evidence guilds and information forums? Should they not have a central agency devoted specifically to planning and broadcasting of programs tailor-made to their particular needs?

Surely if Catholic groups were prepared to offer the radio stations top-quality programs, the number of Catholic broadcasts could be more than doubled. 

This then presents the Catholic school and parish with at once a challenge and an opportunity. 

What can we do about it?

Plenty. First of all we can determine what makes a good religious broadcast.

WHAT MAKES A GOOD RELIGIOUS BROADCAST? 

In trying to find an answer to this question we are immediately confronted with a more basic problem. If we are going to determine what makes a "good" religious broadcast and what doesn't, we are also faced with the question of what makes good educational broadcasting since the primary objectives of both are identical: 

1) To inform. -15-

2) To create desirable attitudes. 

3) To motivate and activate. 

4) To inspire.

This opinion may put the writer out on a limb, but we believe that any broadcast which hopes to realize the above objectives must first ENTERTAIN.

There are as many different definitions of entertainment as there are forms of entertainment. We think that the simplest explanation of the term is "that which gives a pleasurable experience."

And in radio, of course, that would mean a pleasurable 

LISTENING experience. 

This does not mean that the pleasure derived is something vicious or trivial. It is above and beyond mere sense experience; the pleasure alluded to is a wholesome joy, an experience which consoles, encourages, uplifts and purifies. 

Surely the audience on the Mount found deep, singing happiness as each of the Beatitudes was enunciated. 

The fiery, arrogant Saul must have felt the exuberant glow of a joyful discovery once his long bitterness was done, a new road opened and the good fight begun. 

Certainly those who followed the fascinating Stranger from Galilee found an exhilarating pleasure while listening to those thrilling stories of hope and regeneration.

Plato knew all the devices for entertaining an audience in order to teach it. So did St. Francis and Shakespeare and 

Patrick Henry.

Behind every mass motivation there is always a strong appeal to the emotional nature in man-the presentation of the crux of the problem in terms of human experience. For that reason, we contend that an educational or religious broadcast should first seek to entertain its listeners in the best sense of the term and all other things will be added to it. 

An entertaining broadcast is an entree to practically all of radio's front doors! 

---

CAN CATHOLIC BROADCASTS BE ENTERTAINING? 

Why not?

The commercial side of the broadcasting industry maintains that if you have something worthwhile to sell-then the radio audience will listen to you and buy.

The most esoteric circle of established radio writers will tell you that if you have a GOOD STORY WELL TOLD, you'll never have to worry about an audience. (The "well told" reservation, however, implies the professional approach -the skilled writer and producer, the capable sound man and trained engineer.)

The most down-to-earth practitioners in the radio business, the men who have learned the "hard way" what audiences want and will listen to the program directors and station managers—all are unanimous in proclaiming that too few religious and educational broadcasters have ever studied the medium of radio with any degree of thoroughness. 

They'll frankly assure you that if you familiarize yourself with the requirements of the medium, abide by them, and then go ahead and broadcast your religious and educational programs in the nearest approximation you can make to such professional "sure-fire's" as CAVALCADE OF AMERICA,

LUX RADIO THEATRE, THE MAN BEHIND THE GUN and AMERICA'S TOWN MEETING, why . . . they'll tell you "You're in! You've made it!" 

And they'll ask you on the spot, "When may we schedule you?"

WHAT HAVE WE TO "SELL"? 

What have we as members of the Catholic Church, the Spouse of Christ, the legatee of all His riches, divinely constituted to guard and protect His truths, and commissioned by Him to teach all nations-what have we to "sell"?

We have the only divine, infallible body of religious truth that God ever imparted to man.

We have the only divine, infallible code of morals that 

God intended should guide mankind. 

-17-

We have the matchless Christian art and architecture, music, poetry and philosophy.

We have the only religion of love which can lift up and inspire a suffering and benighted world.

We can give something of the action-packed, deeply realistic drama of man's struggle against the evil that surrounds him and is within himself-a continuous drama with an ending to satisfy every hearer. 

We can plot the unending serial in which the beautiful Girl-Mother is the permanent protagonist with the scene of action running the gamut from heaven to hell.

We can make use of the most inherently dramatic story in the world—the story of the God-Man who died ignominiously on a cross, nailed there because He loved His brother and strove to do him good; the greatest failure of all times, from the human point of view, who out of the depths of His degradation and ignominy could raise His voice in tones of triumph: "Have confidence, I have overcome the world," the echo of which words reaches us today from the Solomons to Salerno-from underground chasms and miserable, horror-filled homes on the dark continents of Europe and Asia.

What a story to tell! But it will not tell itself; it is up to us Catholics to help give it to the world. 

CATHOLIC BROADCASTS AND SOCIAL PROBLEMS 

Catholicism is inexhaustible in its having in itself the means to meet every situation and every error. While Catholicism must always teach a complete way of life, nevertheless emphasis at different times varies according to the needs, and the needs of the present call for a special emphasis on the social aspect of Catholicism. Catholics and non-Catholics alike must be made aware of the very real social problems in the community, country and world at large. The Church is endeavoring at present to inculcate a deep sense of personal esponsibility which must be the foundation for any real and isting peace and social reconstruction. Hence social attitudes, ocial needs, social doctrine must be presented as an integral 

-18-

part of our faith, and not as something superimposed or casually added.

Today the attitudes of millions are being aroused and moulded almost entirely by de-Christianizing and frequently irreligious and immoral propagandists via the air waves.

Yet there are literally thousands around us who are not Catholics but whose ideas do not differ essentially from ours on social matters. When these people are effectively informed of the Catholic position, they are amazed to learn that the Catholic Church is not opposed to change, is not a dictator, is labor's greatest champion, is, in short, interested in social welfare of every kind, whereas they have always been led to believe the opposite. Ours is the task to correct such wrong impressions.

Catholic radio workshops have a unique opportunity of calling attention to the worthwhile achievements of individual Catholics and Catholic groups in the fields of Catholic social action. Catholic broadcasts can dramatize successfully those saints, canonized and canonizable, who were particularly social-minded; a Martin de Porres, a Mother Cabrini, a 

Damien and a Serra. Social doctrine from factual sources, e.g., our school co-op or parish credit union in action, the story of a local Catholic day nursery or welfare center, can likewise be "radioized" with marked success. 

Whenever feast days occur in the Church-Christmas,

Easter, etc.-even the commercial programs are saturated with their spirit. At such times stations are especially cooperative with religious groups.

Anniversaries and commemorative days such as our national holidays present unusual opportunities for Catholic broadcasts. Similarly anniversaries of Catholic historical personages like Columbus, the Carrolls, John Barry, etc., can supply the occasions for our asking for time on the air. 

Notable events like jubilees, centenary celebrations, Mission Sunday, World Sodality Day, etc., offer similar occasions for special broadcasts. 

What have we to sell? 

We have everything!

But will anybody listen to our broadcasts? 

This one's easy!

If the most devoted and fanatic audience in the world are the "soap opera" listeners (statistics upon request) and if this group listens to a day-by-day account of unending human frustration, misery, tragedy and unfulfilled ambitions (with only an occasional bit of yeast in the form of halfhearted humor and one happy-ending broadcast in a hundred to leaven the whole soggy, dolorous mass); if America's day time radio roughly from 9 A. M. to 5 P. M. is concerned with the wholly fictitious inadequacies of "Dr. Brent" and "Big 

Sister" and "Lonely Women," plus liberal amounts of "how to sparkle though fifty" and "keep your complexion looking like Gert Glamour's after a day over the washing machine or on the assembly line," and such programs still manage to hold and enlarge the biggest slice of listening in the business, then what we have to say as Catholics in the presentation of doctrine, manners and morals ought to present serious competition.

HOW DO WE BEGIN USING RADIO? 

By doing it! 

No flippancy intended, no sophomoric repartee meant.

The only way to use radio as a means of presenting religious and educational materials is to: 

LISTEN AND LEARN: 

Across the entire entrance foyer of one of the biggest radio advertising agencies there's a sign which reads: "You Have to Know 'Em to Sell 'Em."

With slight ramifications that injunction applies to us. If Catholic teachers and directors are determined to make use of the indisputable teaching advantages of radio, they first MUST know how it works: 

What is on the air. 

What the radio technique is. 

Who are the successful performers and why. 

What could be on the air. 

And why more Catholic material is not there. -20-

CHAPTER II THE RADIO WORKSHOP It's YOUR cue. 

Why mine?

Well, you've been convinced that radio can do an efficient informing, motivating, and inspiring job for you as a teacher in a Catholic high school or college, as the moderator or director of a parish or school Sodality, or as the leader of any other Catholic Action group. You've familiarized yourself with the scope and contents of what is being aired. 

You know the difference, for instance, between H. V.

Kaltenborn, Cecil B. De Mille and Andre Kostelanetz. 

By listening carefully and earnestly: you're now able to feel the essential difference between fifteen and thirty-minute dramatic shows; you're able to guess within fifteen seconds where the "middle commercial" will come on a commentator's broadcast; you're convinced that the NBC Symphony Orchestra must surely have the best engineer in the business, so perfect is the balance of the instruments; and you have a favorite radio show you wouldn't miss. In short, you've begun to grasp the whole picture of radio as it applies to the teaching process. 

Now what? 

It's your cue! 

To do what? 

To organize a radio workshop. 

And what is a radio workshop? Simply a laboratory in which the basics of radio-writing, acting, production, sound and music, etc. are taught to a group interested in learning them a group established under the auspices of some school or parish society and remotely patterned after the original CBS workshop in order to study and create types of religious programs that will fit the peculiar facilities of radio. 22-

HOW DO I START? 

One of the first steps to take in the formation of a radio workshop in a high school, college or Sodality group is to estimate your workers.

The very word radio connotes glamour, excitement and "big time." Having perhaps once or twice attended a broadcast at a local station and watched with inhaled suspense the mysterious, but apparently grimly important, gyrations of the producer and the "on-the-toes" reactions of cast, crew and musicians, most young people will respond with almost reflex rapidity to the suggestion that they form a radio workshop of their own-that they establish their own "clinic" in which the "mysteries" of radio script-writing, acting and production are uncovered and solved. 

That's all to the good. That's having about forty per cent of any job done for you-the demand already neatly packaged and waiting to be supplied.

But a common failing of youth is its sporadic approach. 

Inevitably it plunges into a new and inviting field with great and sometimes exhausting energy and enthusiasm. 

Then when the novelty of using terms like: "We were on the nose!" "Bring that music up in the B. G. of the sound,' etc., begins to wear off; when the microphone has become a commonplace, and the sound truck just a contrivance to be kept neat; then the curve of enthusiasm begins to descend on the other side of the graph and there is a falling off in attendance and a tardiness at rehearsals. 

So the first thing to consider in the formation of a group workshop is the character of the group as a whole, and the qualities of responsibility, endurance and cooperation of each member of that group. 

Once you've assured yourself of these qualifications 

THEN comes the business of organization.

BUT I'M NOT TRAINED IN RADIO! 

Maybe you're not and you're thinking that it's very unwise to undertake training others in the technique of a medium in which you've had no instruction yourself. 23-

That's a legitimate objection!

But you've got to remember that there's a task to be done, which is a pioneering task in many ways, and one that, as yet, cannot command a corps of skilled instructors at the drop of a "Won't-you-join-our-staff-and-take-over-our-radioactivities?"

Catholic educational institutions have not yet undertaken to train their ultimate graduates in the varied aspects of broadcasting. A far-too-small group of professionals who are employed in the industry and who, being Catholics themselves might be depended upon to provide the necessary instruction, are usually too busy meeting the demands of their jobs in the actual process of broadcasting to be able to give the benefit of their skills to new groups interested in learning them. 

Yet the task of forming and coaching a workshop must be done! The fifth "R" must be made to serve the fourth with its indubitable power and effectiveness. There's only one possible solution to the dilemma. 

You are it! You're the director-because : 

You know young people; 

You are that rare person-a good teacher, director or chairman; 

And you want to see radio serve Christ and His cause.

It is certainly safe to generalize that the person best fitted to organize and conduct a radio workshop is one who possesses a background in some other entertainment medium. 

In most cases this single qualification would nominate the drama, English or speech teacher in a school or the dramatics director in a Sodality parish group. 

If you are such a person, you are well acquainted with the elements of that nebulous, kinetic, demanding thing known as "showmanship." The drama is your well-known and well-loved ally. The discipline of public performance is no stranger to you; and the hard, hard work of training amateurs in that semblance of timing, tempo and "believableness,' ," known as "the professional touch," holds no terrors for you. You know the limitations and potentialities of those inder your direction. So you're nominated-unanimously! - 24-

ALL RIGHT, I ACCEPT, BUT WHAT NOW? 

First, consult your own calendar. What time have you that will best lend itself to a workshop period? "Well, let's see there's an activity period at 2:00 

P. M. each Friday" or "A third Tuesday evening of each month that's not down for any definite activity" or "I find that my voice and diction group really needs a new approach.'

Once the time has been settled upon-and to start, the workshop should meet twice a week-STICK TO YOUR SCHEDULE! DON'T MAKE IT ELASTIC! 

If the group is going to learn about radio, then impress upon them at the outset radio's great reverence for TIME and for the strict adherence to a schedule. 

WHAT ABOUT EQUIPMENT? 

Don't I need a lot of microphones and recording machines, studios and control panels and . . . and ... stuff?'' 

You do not! At least, not to start.

You're dealing with young people who, in the majority of cases, while they may be lacking in a lot of things like equipment and experience, make up for some of this lack in their great, unbounded, magnificent IMAGINATION.

A STUDIO? The classroom or club room or church basement or library will serve the purpose, especially when one corner is chalked off and labeled "Control Room" and the place for a future sound "truck" is circled off in another. 

A MICROPHONE? Here's one time that you have the perfect alibi-in case you need it? Radio equipment for the home front today is an unknown quantity. There is very little or nothing that you could buy right now even if you wanted to get equipment. So you improvise-as many of today's thriving workshops did when they began. A piece of cardboard cut to resemble the ordinary two-directional "mike" (there are pictures of these microphones in newspapers, magazines, pamphlets), an old broomstick from which the broom - 25-

has been removed, a wooden stand to hold it, and that's your "mike" for the moment.

BROADCASTING EQUIPMENT? You mean a control room, recording machine, volume indicator, and so forth? 

Forget them all! You're not nearly ready for them anyway.

When the time comes for these things you will find them available at your local station outlet. And here's a word to the carpers, who insist that it is impossible to teach "mike" technique without a "mike" and that improvised broadcasting is worse than no broadcasting at all:

The best workshop is the workshop that begins with the simulated broadcast. 

And here is a definition of a simulated broadcast: "A simulated broadcast is that type of activity in which a group prepares and presents a program that utilizes all the techniques of the radio medium but which is NOT broadcast." 

With this definition as a working guide for the first phase of your activity, the seemingly pressing question of what to do about proper equipment for broadcasting dissolves itself right in front of your eyes.

Simulate the mechanical materials. Improvise the radio environment. And get to work! 

Thus far: 

You've carefully chosen your members, you've rigidly scheduled your workshop periods, and you've learned how to improvise broadcasting equipment.

And here's a reminder to those fortunate few whose parish or school happens to possess a public address system. Your equipment problem is greatly simplified. You will find your

P. A. system wonderfully versatile in meeting the needs of your workshop, for it provides a very close approximation to a microphone and the atmosphere of broadcasting with no further props necessary to suggest it.

Read the booklet that usually accompanies such a set and familiarize yourself with the few simple rules for operating it; or, if possible, call your local distributor and have him give you a demonstration of the proper use of the instrument. 

Handle the set with care! Equipment is irreplaceable! 

But use it! – 26 —

THE AUDITION 

And what is an audition?

Why nothing more than the listening to a brief performance by a reader in order to determine his qualifications for a specific or general role.

It's the old theatre custom of the "try-out," streamlined to radio's needs and given a new name. Streamlined? Yes, because in an audition all you're listening for is the VOICE quality and capability of the reader.

The theatre director must take into consideration the size, appearance, carriage and general suitability of the persons he tries out for characters. The director of the radio workshop is completely free of these restrictions. 

There are millions of ardent radio fans who, when they were "privileged" to see the hero of their favorite program in person, have been abysmally disappointed to discover that the vibrant, romantic voice which has thrilled them for so many months belongs to a wizened little radio actor totally unlike their dream hero. 

An AUDITION is the first "clinical" procedure in a workshop and a highly important one! There are many ways to conduct an audition. Assuming that you're working with little or no equipment, suppose you try this method: 

Arrange the members of the group at one end of the. room. Number them from one to whatever number is necessary. Then have them fill in and put their numbers on the audition cards (a model for these follows) and collect the cards.

Rig up an old scrim curtain or sheet or left-over stage "drop" (but a THIN one, please!), and place yourself on one side of this curtain. (This procedure is to be used by those groups who do not happen to have a P. A. system.)

Next, instruct the group to come up one by one, in numerical order, to the other side of the curtain and to read the material each has chosen. As each member finishes, call the next number. - 31- 

---

This procedure will help you to concentrate solely on the voice-quality of the reader without undue pressure being brought by his infectious smile, her soft brown eyes, or the fact that he or she is your favorite history student! It likewise helps to make the audition run along at a good clip, especially when you have a large number to hear. 

THE PUBLIC-ADDRESS SYSTEM AUDITION 

If you're lucky enough to own a P. A. "mike," preface the audition with a few remarks on the correct position for the speaker, which usually is eighteen inches from the microphone. 

Caution against the use of too much vocal volume, shouting or "strangling," a common failing of those who have never been near a "mike" before. (Pictures of the young 

Bing Crosby affectionately clutching a "mike" to his manly chest and the bad example of large numbers of stage and night-club performers who handle the P. A. "mike" as if it were a baton have not helped to give the layman the proper respect he should have for this sensitive equipment.) 

IMPRESS UPON THE GROUP: 

Never handle studio microphones or equipment unless expressly asked to do so by station or studio authorities.

JUDGING READING ABILITY 

Have you been wondering about that one term on the audition card-"COLD" READING? A confusing term, you say? Not at all! 

- 33 -

And that brings us to the subdivisions of an audition: 

1) PREPARED MATERIAL 

2) "COLD" READING 

Now is it beginning to clear up? "Cold" reading is nothing more nor less than having the person who is auditioning read for you material he has not had a chance to read over beforehand.

When an audition is announced it is usually good practice to instruct the performers to bring their own reading material. This procedure gives them a chance to choose the things they THINK they do best; and, also, it gives them an opportunity to perfect a few brief excerpts from a selection and work them over until they achieve some small skill in delivering them.

Prepared reading, however, gives you only a small index of the reader's capabilities.

The very manner in which programs are broadcast in American radio emphasizes "time is of the essence." With the usual large network outlet broadcasting some 25,000 programs a year, and with the amount of trained, popular actors and producers for that station being well under one hundred, it is fairly obvious that there can be little of the three or four months of rehearsals which are stock routine in the presentation of a good legitimate play.

In radio there just isn't time for the protracted discussion, infinite rehearsal of "business," and prolonged study for interpretation that one comes to expect of a Guthrie McClintock or a Brock Pemberton production.

No, radio acting requires what the theatre calls a "QUICK STUDY," but it has a different definition for it.

Radio presumes that upon being presented with a script and assigned as a certain character, the competent radio actor can read his part (even for first reading) with skill, smoothness and a minimum of "fluffs." 

Take a good long look at that last word. You're going to be hearing it in your nightmares from here on in if you've ecided to train an amateur radio workshop! 34- 

---

A "fluff" is the somewhat inadequate term given to mispronunciations, stutters, or reversals of word order. And radio's full of 'em!

There's the immortal one, for example, committed by an announcer whose name had long been synonymous with smooth performance:

Over a coast-to-coast hook-up one fine Sunday afternoon, he urged his listeners in one of the most delightful voices extant to buy a carton of Coca-Cola for "The Cause that Depresses!" (He was seen studying a handbook of welding the next day.)

Then there's the classic blunder of the announcer on one of the country's top dramatic shows. At the close of this particular broadcast he intoned solemnly: "This program has CAME to you from Hollywood. This is the NASH... (GULP!) . . . er . . . I . .. I mean THE COLUMBIA BROADCASTING SYSTEM!” 

... 

So those are fluffs. And oh! how radio tries to avoid them! A rare few are to be expected even among "top" performers, but a would-be radio initiate who makes a career of them does not belong in your workshop. 

But to get back to the problem of "COLD" READING:

It is wise to provide yourself with some mimeographed copies of a number of selections which run the gamut, so to speak, as far as radio acting is concerned. 

You can cull these from anywhere. They need NOT be pieces taken from radio scripts, but they SHOULD be selections in which ONE and not more than TWO characters speak-in short, preferably a selection from a MONOLOGUE 

or SOLILOQUY. 

Select several different kinds of material: comedy, tragedy, farce, pathos, straight exposition, that which is subtle and that which is done with a sledgehammer. Then perhaps you will also want two brief scenes, highly different in content and using DIALOGUE, so that towards the close of your audition you may try out some of the candidates to see how they fulfill the demands of the dramatic scene.

Auditions should be SAMPLES of ability. 

Caution your group to keep their materials BRIEF. 

FIVE minutes for each is usually enough. 

For "cold" reading, select BRIEF selections.

RADIO HAS MANY SIDES 

Impress upon your group, from the beginning, that there are far more phases to radio than the acting. 

Radio may be said to be divided into the work of: 

1) THE WRITER 2) THE ACTOR AND PRODUCER 3) THE ENGINEER 

There are, of course, many other possible ways of division -the commercial, the public service, etc. And there are scores f sub-heads-administration, technical, agency, etc. 

-36-

But the three big categories listed above are, for all practical purposes, the three great radio fields; and from a workshop point of view, the three most important. The most beautifully written script in the world is of no value if it is ruined by stupid production or faulty acting. 

The finest script, perfectly interpreted and produced, doesn't mean much if it comes into your loudspeaker in fits and starts; if one moment the actors seem to be whispering and the next, the music thunders through your home-in short, if the engineer doesn't know his business.

All three divisions are completely and mutually interdependent, and there is no better time than the first workshop meeting to impress this fact upon your group.

This inter-dependence necessitates TEAM WORK. 

It means that ALL members are going to take turns:

Acting Producing 

Doing sound Writing Engineering

Planning programs 

Finding appropriate music

A radio workshop is not, primarily, a school for acting. 

It is not a haven for prima donnas. It is just what its name implies: Not a "SHIRK" shop but a WORKSHOP.

And tell your group early where the accent is in workshop!

THE EVOLUTION OF THE SCRIPT 

In the "prehistoric" days of the medium what was said on the air was usually unplanned or ill-prepared. Since radio, in its beginnings, was the property of a small band of engineers, inventors, and "crackpots," it chiefly relied upon these intiated few for the preparation of its continuity. "Hey, Joe! We're putting the President of the local 

Chamber of Commerce on the air at 1:30 P. M. today. Dash off an introduction and a close, will ya?" 

And Joe would, in all probability, take a few minutes out from dials and plugs and lines to "hunt and punch out" three or four minutes of "copy" on some battered portable.

But as the infant industry began to grow, as more and more stations were licensed and an ever-increasing number of receiving sets purchased, it became obvious that somebody was going to have to spend all his time preparing announcements and talks and later-plays. 

Give this evolution a "fanfare"... for that's how "the script" was born! -45-

In search of a merchandising angle that would lure the thus far totally unimpressed advertiser into the radio fold, some smart fellow must have been leafing through a magazine one day only to discover what had been right in front of his eyes all the time!

Why did so many magazines include the "continued in our next issue" tag on the major part of their fiction section! 

Why? Because it made people look forward to the next issue of the magazine.

Now it suddenly occurred to our smart fellow that maybe, just maybe, the same thing could be done on the air-a kind of continued-story broadcast once a week with an installment just one step this side of a climax. 

Oh, yes! It could be done, and was. And when many listeners flooded the stations with calls to have their favorite feature presented more frequently than once a week, the "fivea-week" or "strip-across-the-board" serial evolved. 

We still have this type of radio serial with us today. 

From nine A. M. to five P. M. it vibrates across hundreds of different wave-lengths and into millions of homes. 

The "soap-operas" or "washboard weepers" (as the industry itself calls these "five-a-week" serials, because so many of them are sponsored by soap companies) have a motto, which some acidulous individual hung on them and which their writers have gleefully adopted as a kind of creative credo: "Nothing succeeds like excess." And in this instance, they're not exaggerating!

As more and more writers were being absorbed by the fast-growing business of broadcasting, a few decided that writing for radio was distinctly and essentially different from every other kind of creative writing. 

The drama script and "movie" scenario could rely upon both visual and auditory images to tell its story. But radio is only heard.

Whatever comes over the air must inform or activate or inspire exclusively through the auditory sense. 

No magnificently colored set can suggest the atmosphere of the scene nor can interpretative illustration depict the 

-46-

author's meaning for the reader. No fast-moving, versatile camera can encompass each character's change of mood or reaction to a situation.

No, radio has only the aural approach in which story content, mood, suspense, climax and characterization must come through sound alone. 

A real problem? You're right!

But the radio writers with vision, who "heard" in their minds a new dramatic form long before the air lanes did, sat down and went to work. They wrote, and discarded, and wrote again. At first, they made some rather laughable experiments.

They had a bitter time trying to interest the proper authorities.

But finally they did it-men like Arch Oboler and Orson 

Welles, writers of the calibre of Sandra Michael and Norman 

Corwin. Programs such as "Plays for Americans" (Oboler), "Thirteen by Corwin," "The Mercury Theatre" (Orson 

Welles, producer, writer, actor and general "genius"), “The 

Columbia Workshop" and, more lately, "The Man Behind the 

Gun" (Ranald MacDougall) all attest to the present status of radio writing as a forte for artists.

The better part of twenty years was consumed in this evolution of good radio writing technique; and we'd like to reiterate that technique of this stature cannot be mastered over-night.

Legends accumulate in radio. The story is told of the famous and well-known director of Broadway hits, who was invited to appear on a network broadcast and discourse upon the subject of "The American Theatre...1942.”

Everything was in readiness when he appeared at the studio. As the actual time of broadcast arrived, however, he was startled upon looking in the control room window to see the gentleman in charge of the show throwing a fist at him with the index finger pointed menacingly at his face!

Just as he was about to recover from this initial shock, he saw the program charge d'affaires fling his arms out and begin to make gestures frantically-gestures that looked like nothing so much in the world as pulling homemade taffy! 

A few more minutes passed and the now-perspiring "authority" once again turned his eyes in the direction of that glass booth of bedlam. This time, the fellow had his right hand doubled up with only the index finger pointing upward and he was waving it around in a mysterious circle. Convinced once and for all that he was in the power of a madman, the speaker finished the last few minutes of his copy and then turned belligerently towards the source of all his discomfort. 

This time the gesticulator was making a most intimidating motion. He was whisking his full hand back and forth across his throat in an unmistakable sign of threat. Without waiting for the call letters that would conclude his broadcast, the distinguished theatrical director ran mumbling from the studio muttering under his breath, "Crazy! Absolutely and positively crazy! The whole pack of them!" 

Yes, Mr. Theatre Tycoon, although they say that you don't have to be crazy to be in radio, it helps! Especially in the production business, for this is the branch that requires talent... skill . . . understanding sympathy. . . imagination... artistry . . . infinite patience. . . technical knowledge musical background . . . respect for the human equation ... -54-

in brief, a combination of the talents of George Abbot, John Golden, Simon Legree, George Gershwin, and any good psychiatrist you can find.

WHAT IS RADIO PRODUCTION? 

Radio production as it is practiced looks like insanity or genius, depending on the point of view. But production is nothing more nor less than the readying of the show for airing PLUS the actual direction of the show during the broadcast. 

If similes will make it any easier-the producer in radio is the equivalent of the director in the theatre. Both have the major responsibility for the preparation, rehearsal and presentation of the show.

The producer's duties, upon enumeration, don't look too imposing. He must: 

1) Read the script 

2) Determine its objectives 

3) Cast it 

4) Rehearse the actors 

5) Rehearse the sound 

6) Rehearse the musical portions 

7) Blend cast, sound and music 

8) Time the show 

9) "Dress" it 

10) Get it on and off. 

Now that line-up doesn't look like any brief for genius, does it? But it is a good example of over-simplification. Each of the outlined duties of the producer requires a back-log of experience and skill that cannot be achieved in a few weeks or even a few months.

It seems wise, at this point, to consider some general production basics, and to try to answer that old, old question: "Just what is GOOD production?" We offer this definition:

Good production is that which translates the objectives and flavor of a script to the listener.

And the more we think of it the better we like that word "translates," for the analogy between production and translation could be carried to great length. Both are primarily -55-

dependent upon the written words of someone else. Both must draw mightily upon the imagination and resourcefulness of the one handling them. And the radio producer must go even farther, however; he must have a sound, balanced knowledge of the possibilities and limitations of his medium.

Then I certainly don't need all those qualities you've been enumerating.

Well, perhaps... but where am I going to learn how to produce a radio program? 

Right in your own workshop!

You see, that's one of the great advantages of a workshop. The members of the workshop group are not the only ones who can learn! There is no statute that says a workshop director cannot learn something along with the group. 

Minerva is about the only character on record who sprang into full being. The Wright Brothers started at Kittyhawk in a contraption that Sid Luft wouldn't go near today. The early De Mille "epics" are rather crude "tries" compared to some of the present crop of motion-picture masterpieces. "Great oaks from little acorns" applies to the workshop director and embryo radio producer. What if the first two or the first ten scripts you work on with the group are sad failures? If you realize that they are-that's something!

In fact, that's quite a good deal. It will mean you're learning to discriminate and to listen to what is on the air with newly acute ears and a sharpened imagination. As you develop this acuteness, you will be astonished to hear the -56-

amounts of needless "dead air, "the sloppy cues and the bad musical bridges on the radio today. And you'll begin to think that maybe there's a chance for you in this business after all! 

And there is! 

Right in your own workshop-right there with the young people who are your special charge.

THE STEPS IN PRODUCTION 

Now we're ready to start, so let's get ourselves a copy of the first script you're going to attempt to produce and take a good over-all look at it.

The first thing we'll notice is that SOUND and MUSIC cues are indicated by capital letters. That device makes it possible to glance through the script and to see just how much of these two highly important elements there is. 

Now we're ready to take the previously outlined duties of a producer, one by one, to see what each entails. 

READING THE SCRIPT 

That's not a very "tough" assignment. The usual fifteenminute show takes up from ten to eleven and a half doublespaced, typewritten pages, 812 by 11 inches. Carefully analysing the script, searching for its mood and tempo, and determining its purpose or objective is not too arduous a task. 

The one you have in front of you this minute may be a simple script on the life of Father Damien, the hero of Molokai and a great example to all men of true unselfishness and devotion to humanity. Or it might be a biographical drama on the life of Mother Cabrini, whose courage, resourcefulness, faith and heart are a deathless contribution to America's heritage. 

DETERMINING THE OBJECTIVES 

Upon finishing the script, you, as the producer, must answer these questions for yourself. 

1) What is the writer's main purpose in giving this particular segment of the Damien or Cabrini biography? 

2) Is the script intended to be an heroic tribute to the central character? Is it an attempt to explain the reasons for Damien's going to Molokai or Cabrini's coming to the -57-

New World? Or is it a plea for greater aid to the suffering and underprivileged, using Damien or Mother Cabrini as an example? 

3) Is the general mood of the script one of cosmic grandeur, heroic fortitude, childlike faith, joyful sacrifice, or what? 

4) Is the show conceived to give the whole story or does it purposely highlight a small chapter to prove a point? 

Once you are able to answer questions of this kind, you advance automatically to the third of the producer's duties. 

CASTING THE SHOW 

This task certainly need be no bugaboo. Most workshop directors and producers find that they share one great casting restriction in common: The lack of mature male voices. 

Knowing of this lack, they must realize from the very beginning of all workshop activity that casting a show will necessitate compromise. If you haven't a Hugh Studebaker or a 

Carlton Beck, you'll have to do with what you have.

One cardinal point to remember in choosing the various members of the group for their parts in a particular script, is to try always to achieve what is called VOCAL BALANCE. 

For example: 

If you have two characters playing a two or threeminute scene together, try to get two voices which complement one another. DON'T cast two actors whose voice quality, timbre and resonance are similar! Otherwise, the audience will not be able to distinguish between the characters. And a confused audience quickly becomes a bored audience. 

Never forget that radio is an aural medium!

It's also well to recall that radio long ago abandoned its notion that everyone who is on the air must sound like Elizabeth Hart or Nelson Case. As the medium grew up it learned that frequently a too-perfect voice could destroy the very illusion of reality it was striving to create. If a voice is distinct, has personality, and is believable, it is radio material. 

Beware of chronically hoarse voices, extremely nasal voices, voices with too obvious a sectional dialect. - 58

(The chapter on Radio Acting and Speech will further discuss this matter of casting.)

Suit the needs of the role. This is just another way of saying: Don't cast little Emmy Lou, who is sixteen and sounds for all the world like a "hep" canary, in the role of Elizabeth of England. No, not even if Emmy Lou has always had a "hankerin'" to try the role or is naturally an excellent "cold" reader.

As the radio script developed, it adopted an old Greek device to help it in its problem of telling a story: THE NAR- 

RATOR. You'll be seeing that word a great deal on the lefthand margin of script pages.

A narrator is one of radio's favorite "characters-of-allwork." He gives the writer a handy way to condense exposition, to jump time sequence, to philosophize, etc. 

The narrator carries a big burden. Since the narrator is the one who ties the continuity together, sets scenes, heightens suspense, and amplifies climaxes, choose him with care. Make sure he is someone who reads with fine communication, tonal coloring, and better-than-average diction. 

And it need not always be a "he"! Many gratifying experiments have been made in the use of a female voice for narration. If you decide to try a female narrator, remember that women's voices generally do not "mike" as well as men's. 

Choose one with a mezzo or contralto quality. The "mike" is always kinder to low frequencies than to high ones. 

In the last analysis, the casting process is nothing more than fitting the requirements of the script to the talent (or lack of it) on hand.

REHEARSING THE ACTORS 

Here's a proved, workable recipe for your rehearsal :

After assigning parts, arrange for the "first reading." 

Have the dramatic cast (not the entire cast, which would include the sound technicians, engineer, music, etc.) sit down with the producer and read through the script without sound or music.

Instruct each member of the cast to indicate with pencil each speech of his part. For example: -59-

If Jane Brown is assigned to the part of Mary Stuart in the following script, her script should look like this:

MARY -I have seen but a poor likeness... but I believe this is Elizabeth. 

ELIZABETH—I am Elizabeth... may we be alone together? 

MARY -I had hoped you could come. wrote you were not sure... 

When last you 

ELIZABETH—If I come so slow and tardi-grade, my dear... 

It's not for lack of thinking of you... Rather because I've thought too long. . . and carefully... 

MARY -Have I been so much a problem?

Each line that "Mary" has in the entire script is thus indicated.

If there are crowd noises with several different voices indicated, each shouting a different speech, assign each complete speech to a separate actor. 

For example: Suppose the sound cue reads: 

SOUND-THE CROWD IS ANGRY HERE ... MANY MUTTERINGS OF "HE CAN'T GET AWAY 

WITH THIS!"' “WE'LL SHOW HIM!”

WE'VE BEEN QUIET LONG ENOUGH!" "COME ON, LET'S STORM THE GATES!" 

It's good procedure to say: "Now, John, you take that first speech-the one that says, 'He can't get away with this!' 

Bob, the next one. Mary Louise, next; and Jerry, the last one. 

Now I want all of you to repeat the last speech. That'll give it more punch. Each of you circle your own crowd speech.' 

Assigned in this way, a good crowd noise should emerge. 

Right here it might be well to mention the difference between a SOUND and a BUSINESS cue. There are many writers who use both in the creation of a script. There is a minor controversy over the exact distinction between the two, but this differentiation will serve all practical purposes. - 60 - 

---

A SOUND CUE usually refers to the domain of the sound man. For instance: the pounding of horses' hooves, the roar of a storm, the ticking of a clock, etc. 

A BUSINESS CUE usually refers to some kind of activ ity on the part of the cast. For instance: "MUTTERING OF THE MOB GETS CLOSER," "THE CHANTING OF THE MOURNERS FADES ON," "THE COMPANY OF TIT- TERING GIRLS IS SUDDENLY SILENCED," etc. 

This differentiation will not always hold true; but in the cases where both SOUND and BUSINESS cues are included in a single script, the above definitions will indicate the essential difference.

After the "first reading" is finished, the cast has a pretty fair idea of the contents, purpose and requirements of the script, difficult passages of dialogue have been found and simplified and bad word order has been changed by the producer. 

Never forget that what looks good on paper does not necessarily sound good on the radio. It may be necessary for you, as the producer, to cut sentences with excessive sibilancy or recast phrases that are sure to cause fluffs. 

The "first reading" does NOT require a microphone. 

Once the "first reading" has been finished, however, and the necessary explanations, revisions and indications have been given, put your group on the "mike," even if you are merely improvising equipment. 

If you have a P. A. system, now is the time to use it. 

Run through the script again, this time stopping the cast when it is not getting the mood or "feel" of the scene. 

Explain where they are missing the mark, telling them precisely what it is you want. 

Try NEVER to read a character's lines for an actor.

If you do, you'll soon have a group of parrots, all cheerfully reading the lines just as if they were the producer himself-a bad case of multiple personality. Suggest, explain, analyze, and give them time. They'll get it sooner than you think. - 61-

Only in extreme cases, when the actor or group has failed even to approximate the script's requirements, should you step in and interpret for them. But, only in extreme cases! 

Now that you've finished the second reading, the one on the "mike," take the script and polish it up, scene by scene.

There is always one scene that is worse than the others. Take time to rehearse it adequately (the other members of the cast observing a courteous silence and attention). 

Then, put all the singly-rehearsed scenes together for the THIRD general rehearsal. When this is finished, tell your cast to "take fifteen," that is, to take a fifteen-minute rest while you proceed to:

REHEARSING THE SOUND 

There are two kinds of sound: "LIVE" SOUND: that which is made by the sound man himself. "CANNED" SOUND: recorded sound-sound effects which have been recorded on wax discs, or "platters." 

Technisonic laboratories specialize in sound-effect records, and they can give you anything from a needle dropping into a haystack to the sound of the U. S. S. Bunker Hill being christened and sliding down the hatchways. These firms will gladly send you their catalogues upon request.

The workshop that is just beginning, that's operating on a minimum of equipment and personnel, will in all probability be mainly concerned with "live" sound. 

You, the producer, and the sound technician work as a team. When you first assigned parts in the script you also assigned the sound man. After the third reading, your sound man should have a good idea of what the script requires and should have brought all necessary materials for making these sounds to the workshop meeting. Listen to the sound effects worked out by the sound man. If possible, listen to them across a "mike." Then cue the sound for the sound man.

This means nothing more than reading the two or three speeches immediately preceding the sound cue. Do this for each sound cue. - 62 -

Then, once you are satisfied with the nature and quality of each effect, run through all sound cues in sequence, reading speech cues for each sound cue. Do this until the sound man is able to reproduce his effects quickly and accurately and at the right time. 

Now you're ready for:

REHEARSING THE MUSIC 

If you've ever heard a radio show (that is, a dramatic show) in which there was no music, or one in which the music was poorly done, you will know the inestimable importance of good music properly utilized.

There is no better place than a workshop to demonstrate the beneficial effects of music on a dramatic show. In a workshop, in all probability, you'll be working with recorded music exclusively. 

Radio music is either: "LIVE MUSIC": just what its name impliesmusic which is produced by live musicians in the studio. "CANNED" MUSIC: recorded music.

For workshop purposes the use of recorded music presupposes the possession of a victrola, "play back,” or record player. No problem here! Many of the Tommy Dorsey, Harry 

James and Frank Sinatra fans count a record machine as their most cherished possession. And nearly every parish hall or school has an old victrola. So make some kind of record player standard equipment in your workshop. 

Borrow your recordings! Scout among your members and find out what records they have on hand. Most of them will be glad to lend some of the better ones to the workshop. 

Appoint a "Record Librarian," whose duties will be to keep the records filed, clean and in good condition. 

So now your music problem is settled. 

I beg your pardon? Oh you have a marvelous choral group in the parish? And a fine string quartet at the high school? Well, that's all to the good, but you won't be needing them for a long time. There's no need to bring a large group 

- 63 -

of musicians to your rehearsal periods while you're still in the experimental stage. 

Use only recorded music for the time being.

Later on when you've arranged to give a performance for the assembly or the Sodality Union you might try using one of your musical groups. (IMPORTANT: Familiarize yourself with the laws of 

The American Federation of Musicians regarding the use of recorded or "live" music on the air.)

Appoint someone to handle the music problem just as you do to handle the sound. Rehearse each music cue in the same manner as you do each sound cue. From suitable recordings choose the particular spot on each record that best suits the mood and requirements of the scene in which it's used. 

Caution: Don't "bridge" two highly dramatic scenes with a light, frothy selection. Don't end a comedy scene with the last eight bars of Beethoven's "Eroica." DO choose music that helps the telling of the story and fits the mood, character and pace of the script.

BLENDING CAST, SOUND AND MUSIC 

This is the "sew-up" procedure. This is the time when the well-rehearsed cast, the sound man and musical director all put their energies and skills together. And it should add up to a show!

Yet, even at this stage, you may find it necessary to change a sound or music cue because you realize they are unsuitable when you hear the entire scene. Or you may haveto add a line or so to give meaning to the excellent sound which your technician has worked out. It is in such cases that you will have to rely on your own taste, sensitivity and skill. 

Once cast, sound and music have been blended without any hitches or any "dead air"—those awkward pauses which are the producer's nightmare-we're ready for Step Number Nine:

TIMING THE SHOW 

Here's one place where there's no room for "rugged individualism"!

Even in a workshop production which is not going to be "aired," even for an assembly program or a performance 

- 64-

before the Altar and Rosary Society, you're using the radio medium. So play ball! 

A 15-minute show should time to 14 minutes 30 seconds. 

A 30-minute show should time to 29 minutes 30 seconds. 

An hour show should time to 59 minutes 30 seconds.

That is radio's inexorable law. 

If a program is being broadcast over a local station or network, the remaining 30 seconds of each of the time-brackets listed above are needed for switching purposes or local announcements. And, since you hope to invade the actual field of broadcasting one day, learn its code today. 

And insist upon your workshop learning the code, too. 

If the process of timing your show demonstrates to you that you're a minute and a half "long" (that's in excess of the 

14-30 or 29-30 limit), sit yourself down with cast and crew and make cuts. 

If, on the other hand, you find that you're forty seconds "short" (that's less than the 14-30 or 29-30 limit), then point out to the cast certain sections of the script which can be played more slowly, add a few more bars of music in your "bridges," or re-insert a section of one of the cuts. 

As you progress in the production field you'll be able to approximate the length of a show merely by looking at the script and seeing the amount of speech, sound and music it includes. Even before your "first reading," you will have made what are known as tentative cuts. But remember this "feel" for approximation in timing comes gradually. 

A few "do's" to remember in cutting: 

1) Cut WHOLE speeches wherever possible; a lineby-line cutting is slow and not very effective. 

2) Have the entire cast-actors, sound man and music director-make the cuts in their scripts as you indicate them. 

3) Be sure to explain all cuts and see that they are 

CLEAR to everyone. 

"DRESSING" THE SHOW 

This term indicates the equivalent of the dress rehearsal in the theatre. Now we have the final polishing, the last - 65 -

checking. The rules that bind the theatre "dress" should hold here make no further cuts after the "dress." 

Give the cast at least five minutes for a "breather' before air time.

GETTING IT ON AND GETTING IT OFF 

You're on the air!

At least, you're pretending that you are. And now perhaps comes the most exacting part of all the demanding jobs of production. For you'll find that what seemed to be a perfect 14-30 show in rehearsal is bogging down. At the 3-minute mark on your script you may find on consulting your stopwatch that it's actually 3-50!

Or maybe your cast has decided to indulge in an all-out marathon. At the 5-minute mark on your copy you discover that they're a page and a half beyond it! 

You certainly can't run in and tell them to slow it down or hurry it up. Nor can you instruct Helen Jones to come closer to the "mike" nor tell Johnnie Carter over at the sound table that you wanted only a brisk nor'wester in that New 

England scene-not a typhoon ! 

Or can you? 

Yes, you can!

And that brings us back to the flustered speaker of the first page of this chapter and the so-called madman, who gesticulated at him from the control room.

CHAPTER VI 

"'MIKES' ARE HARD MASTERS" 

Radio acting looks easy.

MRS. SMITH (UPON VISITING BROADCASTING STUDIO FOR FIRST 

TIME) "I'll never understand why they pay that man seventy-five dollars for just fifteen minutes work! All he does is stand in front of the microphone and read what somebody else has written and here he makes more in a day than my John does in a couple of weeks! Hmph!" 

Now it's true that the above "quotation" may be a bit exaggerated; but, in general, Mrs. Smith's opinion is shared by a large segment of the American listening public. 

Apparently, all that the "top flight" performers have to do to get themselves into Morgenthau's clutches is to have a good voice, a little-better-than-ordinary reading ability, and some dark and devious "pull" with the "right" producers. 

Nothing is further from the truth.

Radio acting is one of the most exacting of the creative arts. Radio acting is a skill which can be mastered only after long practice, continuous rehearsal and a good endowment of strictly "radio" talent from the start.

If you doubt that this statement is true, consider the sizable number of stellar theatrical personalities who have refused every siren call of radio because their first "mike" appearances were to use "Variety's" patois - "strictly turkey!" 

Katharine Cornell, Maurice Evans, John Gielgud, the 

Lunts, Katharine Hepburn, Judith Anderson,-all have at one time or another made radio appearances. Many of them have had tempting lucrative contracts offered them because of their reputations. Except for an isolated appearance here and there, however, they have all refused such contracts because, they were good enough troopers to realize that, although they had perfected themselves in one medium, they could not adapt hemselves to the basic differences of another. 

-70-

Yet, on the other hand, of course, we know of the radio success of such performers as Helen Hayes, John and Lionel 

Barrymore, Victor Moore and Basil Rathbone. But these make up a rather small minority alongside the actual number of stage luminaries.

And that brings us precisely to the fundamental point to be considered in acting for radio. 

RADIO VS. THE THEATRE 

Radio acting is basically different from acting for the theatre.

A lot of workshop experience proves that the majority of the group which is attracted to the idea of a radio workshop will in the first place be made up of people who have had some kind of theatre training.

This training may be elocution, Little Theatre work, or a course in Creative Dramatics. Those who have had previous training will come determined to be heard whether it be on the platform, behind the proscenium or over the air. This fact is both your greatest blessing and misfortune! 

Previous theatre training is a "blessing" because: 

1). It gives the aspiring radio performers the basics of all public performance : a) Poise b) Interpretative skill 

2) It instructs them in the essence of drama: 

a) Characterization b) Building of suspense c) Achieving a climax d) Thinking in terms of the essential "conflict" 

3) It usually demands: a) Good diction b) Accurate pronunciation c) Flexible enunication 

Previous theatraining is a drawback because:

It is employed in the projection of character and plot. Radio conversely is concerned with the injection of character and plot. 

- 71-

The theatre works on the "fourth-wall-removed" theory, attempting to give a portrayal of a situation with the audience acting the part of a silent interloper. 

Much of playwriting technique is based on well-documented principles of motivation and psychology as they apply to a group of from one to five hundred people massed together under one roof to witness a specific offering. With a few possible exceptions this audience has chosen to come and has paid an admission fee, because of a favorite star or playwright or because, more rarely, they like the writer of the musical score. 

In the theatre, the demands upon the actor are varied and heavy. He must be heard by the last row of patrons in the house. His costuming and make-up must serve to project the physical appearance of the character he is playing. He must exaggerate, to a certain extent, the voices, gestures, movements-in short, the entire concept of his interpretation -to assure its being received by all members of his audience. 

He must at all times be en rapport with his audience. He must learn to adapt his timing, pace and characterization to meet the varying moods of different audiences. 

Radio needs none of these techniques!

Unlike the theatre with its limited audience of paying patrons-radio plays to millions in homes, offices, institutions, automobiles, in all the varied places where you'll find a receiving set in America today. You'll find them in duplex apartments and tenements, in financial establishments and foxholes, in living rooms, rumpus rooms, kitchens, jaloppies, submarines and B-24's. 

Quite an audience, you say? 

You bet it is! 

And it doesn't cost a cent or take a minute's time to turn on the switch, twist the dial and tune YOU in. But never forget-it takes even less time and effort to tune you out! 

It's obvious, therefore, that two such different media will require two essentially different communicative techniques. 

Whereas the theatre actor is always subconsciously concerned with the problem of projecting voice and characterization, the radio actor is constantly aware-or should be of the necessity for injecting himself into his listener's range. 

-72-

The microphone is the ear of the listener.

There is no proscenium-only thousands of different homes, recreation centers, cars, etc. 

The radio program with its actors, producers, "crew,' and engineers, is a guest in whatever listening center admits it. 

And a GUEST does not harangue his host in ringing, stentorian tones; does not present matter which is offensive or lacking in good taste; and does not bore his host to death with bombastic phrases, ersatz sincerity or deafening presentations of his own or others' experiences.

Instead: 

He converses. 

And there's the touchstone!

The old chestnut about " 'one man's meat being another man's poison" iş applicable to the Theatre vs. Radio consideration. "One medium's technique is another's obituary."

It cannot, therefore, be reiterated too often that theatre experience does not necssarily qualify an actor for radio. 

We now come to the second reason why previous theatre training is a drawback:

Too many Little Theatre and theatre-school groups put a premium upon what has been called "standard American diction," a radio menace.

You've no doubt heard the story about the lad from the Bronx, who having determined to go the way of all klieg lights, found himself in a prominent school of the drama in the Middle West. He applied himself to his stage-diction lessons with a vengeance, only to be bewildered completely by the hysterical laughter of his classmates and instructor the day he decided to discourse upon the subject of “lahffinggaaaaaas. 

In a country, which has been sub-titled "The Melting Pot" and "Immigrant Paradise" and whose language is subject to horrific mutations in certain sections, it is obvious that the problem of dialect is a knotty one and that "standard 

American speech" is something of a Shangri-La.

Who is to say which region's diction shall become standard? And where is there much to be gained from erasing the -73-

more delightful sectional characteristics of American speech such as the Virginian's almost non-existent "r's" or the 

Marylander's precise, lovely vowels? 

It's true that some denizens of "Dem Wunnerful

Bums' "' native heath have practiced a little mayhem on the tongue of Milton and Newman; but did you ever hear a Chicagoan pronounce "opportunity"? or a Texan say "Arkansas" or a Georgian call: "How are you?"

It would be easier to find a Republican sponsoring a low tariff bill than to suggest to any of these regions that their speech be basically altered or modified. 

The theatre's emphasis upon rippling middle "r's,' broad "a's," bristling "t's" and "d's," ringing "ing's"; upon such pronunciation phenomena as "hawt dagg, "cahffee," "nawt everything you see, dear Brewtus, is in the ahffing," etc., is most assuredly not for radio.

Such diction concepts may serve a small group of theatrical producers and stars; but as far as radio is concerned, "It would never make it in Muncie."

And, whereas Cornell and Company, the Lunts, and the whole Theatre Guild for that matter, would as soon consider Muncie for a "long run" as they would Port Moresby; radio has some of its most ardent devotees, its most faithful fans and fanatics, in what Broadway likes to refer to as "The Provinces.' 

And Americans are a wonderfully "touchy" people.

They'll take graft in high places and taxes and wars and depressions; but just try making them feel inferior some time! Try speaking to them in a language which, though related to their own, seems only distantly so. Try presenting something to them, cloaked though it be as entertainment, couched in a speech-pattern they have come to associate with "the clahsses," those who "toil not, neither do they spin." 

And then run-don't walk-to the nearest shelter! No insurance company would consider you much of a risk if you persisted in your attempts to "standardize" America's speech habits, or to communicate with it in what is essentially a false manner. -74-

Therefore: 

1) Radio speech should be acceptable to all regional groups if the broadcast is to be aired over a network. 

This accomplishment is not impossible. Bob Hope has as many listeners, proportionately, in Natchez as he has in Montpelier, Ripon or San Diego. "Report to the Nation," which frequently uses dramatic portions to clarify and fortify its factual reportage, hits the perimeter points of the country. 

In network broadcasting it's always wise to remember if you're personifying a Southerner, that there's nothing the 

South hates worse than a fake Southern accent oozing with "you-all's" and "honey-chile's." 

2) Radio speech should personify the best characteristics of a specific region if it is to be aired locally. 

There are always some cacophonous sectional characteristics which can be modified or erased without a whole speech re-education course. 

In local broadcasting always keep in mind that you are entering the homes of your friends, acquaintances, neighbors and fellow-citizens-of-the-community. You want them to be entertained by what you have to say. You want them to enjoy your visit. Speak to them with that purpose in mind.

TRAINING FOR RADIO ACTING 

Now your workshop group is ready to learn some "mike" technique. 

First: Impress upon them the delicacy of and the impossibility of replacing at this time the instrument with which they're working. 

Second: Instruct them in the high fidelity of most "mikes" today. Tell them "mikes" magnify slight speech faults, increase "breathiness," and mirror uncertainty and fright. 

Third: Let them hear themselves speaking. You can accomplish this suggestion ideally if you own a recording machine and some cheap glass-base discs. Make a "cutting" (recording) of each member's voice. Many schools of speech and some radio workshops follow this practice and find it a 

-75-

useful way to check on the progress of the group in detecting specific speech faults and providing the perfect way for each member of the group to hear himself.

But I'm operating on a shoe-string! I can't possibly afford a recording machine. 

All right.

All of your group still have a pair of hands apiece, I haven't they?

Well, one of the oldest tricks in the bag for speech re-education is the "acoustic mirror." It can work for you as efficiently as it has for many a teacher of speech. 

What in the world is an "acoustic mirror"?

It's the simple device of having a person clasp or cup his hands around his ears and listen to himself speak. This method, it is true, won't give you the same kind of reproduction you'll get from a "mike" or a recording; but it will serve to give each member at least some idea of how he sounds to others.

Radio acting demands that those who want to learn it must have some idea of the limitations and good points of their own vocal mechanism. So, we repeat: Let them hear themselves, in whatever manner you can improvise. 

Next, give them enough microphone practice.

We're ready to admit that it is difficult to do so if you're working with simulated equipment. But the injunction stands, nevertheless. If you have only a broom-stick "mike,” use it every time you conduct a rehearsal or a lesson (except for "first reading," of course). Make the group conscious, from the very first, that the "mike" is the focal point of their activities. 

Don't tolerate a laughing, "what-won't-he-think-ofnext?" attitude toward your mock utensils. 

Until the Fathers' Club or Alumni or some local philanthropist comes through with something better, that broomstick with its cardboard cylinder is your microphone. Never let your group forget it!

Microphone practice always involves some simple techniques:

THE FADE: Which is the technique of walking to or away from the "mike," according to indications in the script.

For example: 

JOHN (FADING OFF SLOWLY)—I don't know whether it will work, Bob... I really don't...

This type of action would ordinarily mean that John would start walking backwards, away from the "mike," along with the words "it will work.'

Or:

Mary would stand about four or five feet from the "mike" and "call" the first four words. Then she'd walk towards the "mike" while reciting the other lines so that she'd be in proper position-the usual eighteen inches from the "mike"-by the time she was reading "I'd hear today."

THE "OFF MIKE" LINE: This means what it says. 

The script indicates that a certain line is to be read "off mike."

That means that the actor reading that line is not directly on the "mike" beam, i. e., not in position for a direct "mike" pick-up. This device serves to give a three-dimensional touch to broadcasting. It is achieved by placing the actor away from the "mike" in a spot from which he sounds as if he were apart from the other characters at that moment.

THE "MIKE" WHISPER: This might be subtitled: 

"How to paralyze engineers and blow the station off the air!"

Here again we come up against the essential difference between stage and radio.

The stage whisper, which tramples on reality, has fallen into disrepute since the days of "East Lynn." If three hundred persons in a theatre can hear you "whispering" about your conspiratorial plans to blow up the gas works, reason says it's a little too much to believe that the sheriff standing next to you won't have some inkling of your plans!

Oh, it's dramatic license, we know, that permits the stage whisper and it can be highly effective when handled by such skilled artists as Lunt and Fontanne in the "Taming of the

Shrew." But when the whisper is used in radio, we want to insist that it's done with far less volume and emphasis than on the stage.

Whenever a script requires that a speech or scene be whispered, remember that it means WHISPERED. Amateurs have the disturbing habit of putting their faces a hair's breadth from the "mike" and then belaboring it suddenly with enough vocal energy to overload the transmitter. 

A good "mike" whisper can very seldom be heard by other members of the cast standing in the far corners of the studio. Since the "mike" is the listener's ear, the "mike" whisper must be done with delicacy.

THE SHOUT OR SCREAM: Another "nightmare for future reference!" Newcomers in radio, in the heat of interpretation and the tension bred by any "mike" performance, usually forget on their SHOUT or SCREAM cue to step far back from the "mike," before they let go. This failing has served to prejudice a number of professional radio-station men against the appearance of amateur groups.

THE CROWD NOISE: The same rules for keeping all excesses in volume and pitch away from a "speaking" position at the "mike" holds for the "crowd noise." 

In the above example it is obvious that we wish to create the illusion of Bolivar speaking to a large number of his countrymen. The listener must feel the quickening of the pulses, the acceleration of the heart beats among men who begin to feel a renewed hope. 

Now that doesn't mean that you must have seventy-five to a hundred people to reproduce a crowd. Ten or twelve good male and female voices, mixed preferably, will do the trick. Be sure to place them in a group, a goodly distance from the "mike," and have them exert themselves! 

You, of course, like the good producer that you are, will have assigned each of the separate speeches in a crowd to a specific actor; and then you will add a great shout on the part of all to "cinch" the effect. As a result, you will have a crowd noise that is believable and contributes at the same time to the entire dramatic effect. 

To sum up then :

Radio acting is just what its name implies. It is the art of acting applied to the unique and particular demands of this newest communication mechanism-radio. 

In it, there is no substitute for experience. - 79 -

There are very few of radio's biggest "names," who will not honestly admit to "mike fright" every time they are confronted with a microphone. 

Radio acting is a demanding, exacting, hard game. But when its performers can regularly command a nation-wide audience of twenty-five to thirty millions each week, when it results in programs like "The Columbia Workshop," "Author's Playhouse," "The March of Time," and "The

Man Behind the Gun," IT'S WORTH THE EFFORT! 

Radio acting is a field in itself. It must satisfy the demands of a tremendously varied audience including the man in the street and the woman behind the ironing board. 

Radio acting is succeeding in doing that. 

Help your group to learn to do it, too, by serving a sound, profitable apprenticeship in radio acting. -80-

CHAPTER VII CREATIVE CREDO-WRITING THE SCRIPT 

The workshop is beginning to work! 

The first days of trial and error are gone. You're starting to get the "feel" of the medium. And your group is gradually assuming the character of a smoothly functioning unit. 

You've presented four or five programs in the classroom or during assembly, in the parish hall or for the Mother's 

Club. Everybody thought them novel, compact, entertaining!

And now your schedule is beginning to include more and more "bookings." A new group is eagerly awaiting the time when you'll be able to start training them. 

Then suddenly-the apple-cart gets a severe jolt!

Lila Belle Jones-or it might just as easily be Bob Smith or Mr. Brown, who is a perfectly serious business man by day-comes in to you with a copy of "a little something I just sorta whipped together." 

Yes, you guessed it! It's a SCRIPT! That fast-catching cacoethes scribendi, dormant up till now, has finally begun its invasion from within. This creative germ has become catching and, before you know it, it will be infecting the whole membership! 

At one time or another they'll all want to WRITE. "Everybody thinks he has at least one good script in him" is the dour prophecy of those "hacks" who earn their living at the precarious business of radio writing. 

It is therefore wise to warn the director that once his workshop group has mastered the fundamentals of broadcasting, they'll want to start "creating" on their own. If you've been working with scripts borrowed from recognized broadcasting agencies, in all probability you've had suitable, wellwritten material. 

And when you read scripts, writing them looks so easy! 

Says the would-be writer: - 81-

"Hmmmm... ten or eleven pages of copy . a sound effect here...a music cue there... a theme... an opening and a closing announcement. Why, I could bat one out after dinner! And there's that story that I've always wanted to write... been thinking about it ever since I was a sprout of twelve or so... think I'll give it a try... and... maybe...' (Here's the danger, dear director!) 

"Maybe our workshop will give it its premiere performance! Where's that typewriter? Get me some carbon and bond! No! I don't need any erasers!... now... let's see …….I'll have the first scene take place at a desert outpost of the Foreign Legion... or, perhaps, a beautifully appointed drawing room on the Champs Elysees... hmmm." 1 And so, a few hours or days or even weeks later this mighty effort will be handed to you and there will be that child-like search for approval--that exaggerated casualness. "It's not much, but... well, I thought you'd like to see it!" 

And that look! 

You see with sudden clarity that this is ONLY THE 

BEGINNING! You sigh heavily and realize that there's only one thing to do. 

And you're right! Show them the right way!

BASIC FACTS FOR RADIO WRITING 

In order to keep you from the time-consuming occupation of digesting several books on radio writing or badgering the local Continuity Editor for "tips," here are a few basics of radio writing, which ought to prove helpful in meeting the inevitable workshop situation.

Radio writing is written to be spoken. It is designed to be heard. 

If it's possible for you to impress these points upon your would-be Corwins from the first, the greater burden of your gargantuan task has been lifted. 

But playwriting also is written to be spoken, isn't it?

Yes, but the playwright also has a scenic design, costumes, make-up and lighting, as well as the skill of interpretative 

82-

artists who know the mechanics of audience reactions to bolster the writer's words.

Radio, on the other hand, can rely only on sound and music.

And it is in the handling of these two supports that the radio writer must be expert, as opposed to the playwright, who has only to indicate the general nature of his set and the approximate time of his action, relying for the details to be filled in by a Robert Edmond Jones, an Adrian and an Elizabeth Arden!

The radio writer, in contrast to the playwright, must conceive every word, every bit of sound, every musical mood, and blend them together into a satisfying whole. 

So suppose we take a long look at all sides of this writing for radio to see just what makes it "tick" and "click.” 

Radio continuity consists of: 

1) The Unit Drama (Example: "The Man Behind the Gun") 

2) The Serial 

(Example: "I Love a Mystery') 3) The Episodic Serial (Example: "Vic and Sade") 

4) The Adaptation 

(Example: Philip Morris Playhouse) 5) Straight Narration (Example: Nelson Olmsted's "Great Stories") 6) Dramatized Narration (Example: John Nesbitt, "Stories") 7) The Talk (Example: The Catholic Hour) 

8) The Commercial (Pick your own!)

Those are the big divisions. It's possible to think of twice as many in order to cover all the continuity demands of the medium, but those listed will serve admirably as the main types. 

You will note that the "unit drama," like Abou Ben

reason. As long as human nature remains what it is, as long as the people are interested in the telling of a story, there will be an audience for the skilled raconteur. 

The unit drama tells a story, a dramatic story, in the most successful way yet found-by using the speech of the characters themselves to tell the story. (Call the unit drama a play if you like. The name isn't important; but the vehicle itself is.)

A unit drama is a complete story of fifteen, thirty or sixty minutes' duration. (The shorter time brackets, such as the five and ten minute spots, do not lend themselves very readily to the creation of a true unit drama.) 

Examples: 

"The Cavalcade of America" (30 minutes) "That Men May Live" (15 minutes) (60 minutes) "Lux Radio Theatre" "So Proudly We Hail" section of the "Prudential Hour," whose tributes to outstanding musicians, Americans, and "Fighters for 

Freedom" are complete unit dramas and usually run fifteen minutes.

There are noticeably few fifteen-minute unit dramas on the air today, except for the programs written especially for educational broadcasting agencies, such as The Radio Council of the Chicago Public Schools, WLS-School Time, WHA- Wisconsin School of the Air, WBOE-Voice of Cleveland Pub lic Schools, and others of a similar nature. 

There are practically no full-hour shows, except those which commemorate a special occasion. (For example, the program commemorating the signing of the Bill of Rights, written and directed by Norman Corwin in 1942 and carried by all networks.)

The half-hour, therefore, seems to be the most popular time bracket for unit dramas, as proved by the perennial popularity of such programs as: "That Brewster Boy," "The

BREAKING IN FOR RADIO WRITING 

Although the half-hour unit drama is the most popular, the best advice that we can offer new radio writers is: "Begin with the fifteen-minute show."

It is much easier, for one thing, to write a good fifteenmintue "unit drama" than a thirty-minute one. The mastery of the fifteen-minute show, furthermore, is an excellent beginning for the eventual conquest of the thirty-minute-unit writing problem.

Point Number One: 

To start, concentrate on the mechanics of the fifteenminute drama. 

Let's see how it's done. 

Suppose you have a plot, at least you think you have, and it goes something like this. We'll say you've always been interested in the life of George Washington and that you've believed that the earlier sections of his biography are always the most sketchy; and let's suppose that you have the added incentive of a special George Washington Day celebration in the offing. Well, whatever the reasons may be, you have a plot. And that brings us to a second point that's a help to new or amateur writers.

Point Number Two: 

Let your first "try" be a biographical drama.

There are a good many writers who might differ violently with this direction. But this book is no place for a controversy among "scriptors." We are merely offering the suggestion to you from the backlog of our experience in workshop activities. We've found that far better results are obtained when workshop writers are assigned a biographical dramatic script than when they are left to their own devices in the matter of plot. -85-

We'll grant that some biographies do not lend themselves to radio dramatization on a number of counts. They may lack objective data to verify obscure portions of the subject's life; or there may be a questionable quality in some of his beliefs or actions; or his life may be generally unimportant in relation to the verities of the elemental broad human emotions, such as fear, hate, love, etc. But there is a maharajah's wealth to draw from in biography, nonetheless; and it is always good pedagogy to proceed from the known to the unknown.

Hence we adhere to our original thesis: the new writer should start with a biographical drama, and preferably one of a very well-known personage. 

In so doing a great deal of research is eliminated. (Proper use of research data is a skill that comes only from long practice.) When the writer of your group chooses biography, the rest of the group can concentrate on technique, that is, on the way the biographical facts are made into a radio drama.

(A) You will note that the directions for the first speech call for "coming on cold." This means that the character speaks without an introduction of any kind from the station announcer. (B) The directions (ELEVEN YEARS OF AGE... ROUGH AND 

TENSE HERE) help the producer as well as the actor to approximate the characterization quickly. Also, the very first word used by the character serves to identify the person he is addressing-a good point to remember! Get your identification of characters in as soon as possible, at least by the names they will be known by for the greater part of the script. (C) "Lawrence" identifies the person addressing him and not fifty words have yet gone by. After only three lines of dialogue the audience is made aware that something is bothering a young boy and that he wants to talk over the matter with an older man in whom he has confidence. Naturally, the audience wants to know just what it is that's bothering the boy, so you've got an "interest catcher" in the first thirty seconds—a trick worth mastering-catching your audience's interest as quickly as possible. (D) Lawrence's wanting to know what's bothering a "lad of eleven years" directly informs the audience of the boy's age, whereas they are able to judge the older man's years by his voice and manner of speaking. (E) George says that what he has to tell his elder isn't easy to express. This admission strengthens or builds up the "problem" stated in the boy's second speech-and it's all accomplished within the first broadcast-minute. (F) This question gives the locale of the action. giving it than having the announcer say in the opening: "Our scene takes place in the family home of America's first president back in the year 1743." The latter method is greatly overworked. Lack of time, in this instance, prevents any further development. (I) An attempt to put a kind of "tag" or "punch" line at the end of a dramatic scene. At this point the audience realizes that it's hearing about the future first president. 

Hence there's apparently a hint of prophecy in Lawrence's words "some day you will be proud that you are a Washington of Virginia."

Remember, the end of a dramatic scene always requires a "punch line." Thus, when writing a scene, conceive a mental picture of how that scene would look if it were being staged; then mentally ring down the curtain on your character's last line. Invariably this is highly effective. A "curtain" line must always be a strong one since you've got to keep whatever interest and suspense you've managed to accumulate past the music "bridge," just as in the theatre you must sustain interest throughout the intermissions that separate the play.

Also in section (J) you will notice that Sarah bridges the time for us, too. She says she has been worried ever since "George left on Lord Fairfax's surveying party." Any audience knows that as a youth Washington was a surveyor, but not as a boy of eleven. Almost reflexively the characters themselves make the necessary time-jump.

For such transitions radio is a wonderfully supple medium. (K) Sarah hopes that nothing has happened to George. At the same time she holds a letter from him in her hands. 

True, this letter business is a time-worn way of keeping the audience interested, but is there any formula older than "boy-meets-girl"? (L) Lawrence refers to the girl as Sarah, and so we have her identified in the second speech of this particular scene.

-- content to stay at Mount Vernon... Aha! reference to the original conflict-the boy who didn't want the appurtenances of a gentleman-planter but who had to accept them... and conflict is the essence of all drama. 

Check any tentative plot you devise for one or more conflicts of: 

Man against man 

Man and his environment 

Man and nature 

Man with himself

You can take practically any plot all the way from "The 

Trojan Women" to "Tomorrow the World" and it will center about one of the above conflicting situations. For example: "Romeo and Juliet"-man against man. "The Rise of Silas Lapham"-man and his environment. "Typhoon"-man and nature. "The Song of Bernadette"-man and his environment. "Shadow and Substance"-man with himself.

Personally, we are convinced that there is no such thing as radio drama without conflict; whether it be the hilarious conflict of Henry Aldrich's adolescence with his family, his school or his community; the perennial conflict of the soap opera which manages to include every variety of dramatic conflict; or the majestic conflict of America's Navy men against the forces of nature, the Axis and the environment of battle, as portrayed in "The First Line." You can do vignettes or episodes "little cross-sections" of life with no conflict from here to television and it's our private guess that you won't sell much soap or good will or Catholicism.

Be sure, therefore, your plot of its very nature has conflict in it. Then it's worth writing. HOW TO GET TIME ON THE AIR 

You're still a bit afraid of professional broadcasting and broadcasters, and you haven't the faintest notion of how to go about securing time. 

Here is a good way to proceed:

Contact the program director of your local station. 

He is the executive with whom you must deal in the matter of securing time. It's easy to find out who he is. If you don't know his name, simply call the station and ask for it. 

Then either call him by 'phone or write asking him for an appointment at his convenience.

When asking for time, it's usually more profitable to see the program director personally and to tell him something of your workshop's history and accomplishments than merely to request time of the station in a letter.

Assure the program director that your group has spent many months learning the fundamentals of radio; and that it is no longer, in the strictest sense of the word, amateur. Show 110

him a copy of the script you are eager to air, but be sure that this script has some reason for existence.

The script should be timely. Perhaps it is a dramatization of the life of one of your parishioners who recently was announced "missing in action," or it may be a good account of a graduate of your school who is among the first "paratroop" nurses.

Or the script may be seasonal. Don't bring the program director a great story of the Revolutionary War around Thanksgiving time, unless there is a specific tie-up between the two such as there would be in telling the story of the first 

Thanksgiving after the declaration of war by the colonies. 

And don't bring in a Christmas script on December 20.

RADIO TIME IS LIMITED 

You must remember that the ordinary program "log" or schedule of a station is set up weeks in advance. There are regular series of programs such as news broadcasts, danceband pick-ups and market quotations which are standard features. And if the local station has network affiliations, a large portion of its time is given to airing network features. 

The amount of time, therefore, that the station has left for broadcasts originating in its own studios is limited. A station cannot give away most of its time. Just as any other business, it must operate on the credit side of the ledger. 

While there are some local stations which are maintained on a non-profit basis, their number is dwindling all the time. 

The station must pay its personnel, rent and general operating costs. The chief means of doing so is by selling time. TIME is radio's main commodity. When you ask a program director for time, you are asking for it gratis. It is no small gift. 

Check the rate-cards of any of the local stations in your community to find out what the charge is for fifteen minutes of day or evening time. If the station is only a small 1000-watt outfit, the charges won't be high. But see what a network outlet in your locality costs. 

By Federal Communications ruling, however, a station must sustain a certain amount of its air-time; it cannot sell 

- 111-

all of its time for commercial broadcasts. But it is a mistake to assume that, since a station has to sustain a portion of its air-time, you have an inalienable right to this time and can have it whenever you desire it and for whatever purpose you choose. Always keep in mind that you are just one of the many groups who feel that they have something worth being heard on the air.

Other organizations may not be as inclusive as yours. 

Their standard of performance may be much lower. They may have an outmoded, time-worn message or one contradictory to your own to bring to the radio audience. But remember! They are part of the community also, and the station must serve the whole community. 

As a result, the program director of a local station may, in a single day, be bombarded by calls from as many as twenty-five different organizations, all of which believe that they should be heard. Most radio station executives are scrupulously careful in trying to give time to all groups that seem to have a legitimate reason for requesting it. 

If, because of heavy commitments on the part of the station, your first request for time is refused, don't be angry or disillusioned or vindictive or heartbroken. The station will try to assign time to you when it can, provided: 

You have an entertaining, well-produced show. 

You have approached the proper authorities with a sound knowledge of their operating problems and a courteous attention to the recognized procedures for securing time. 

If you live in a large city:

Don't decide to make your air-debut on the fiftythousand watt network outlet!

Go to a small station with only one or five thousand watts and request time there. These smaller outlets have more time to give you.

The small stations rarely carry network features. And their authorities are much easier to contact and interview personally. They can assign you more rehearsal time. Also, there is a much better chance for you to get on the air over the smaller stations because they are more attuned to community needs. -112-

Before the war the local stations were very generous in assigning time to community groups. It saved them a great deal of planning and writing their own programs, and it helped them immeasurably in the building up of good willa priceless station possession. 

You've probably heard some of your community groups on the air, St. Chrysostom's Evidence Guild, the B'nai Brith, the P. T. A., the C. Y. O., etc. Yes, they've been numerous and varied.

With the entry of the United States into the war, however, the local stations were asked to carry an increasingly heavy burden of war messages for half a hundred different government agencies. "The Treasury wants war bonds plugged!" "OPA requests that you announce local ceiling prices!" "OWI wants you to allocate some United Nations' 'blurbs'!" "WMC is heavy for United States Employment Office talks!" "Red Cross needs Nurses' Aides and Blood Bank spots!" "Selective Service needs time for the clarification of the new Senate bill affecting drafting of fathers!" "The Junior Land Army must have thousands of recruits from your section!" 

And on and on and on...

In a nation at war extremely heavy burdens are naturally placed on its greatest medium for mass communications; and the local stations have done a magnificent job in answering all the demands made upon them. Consequently stations do not have time in the quantities they once had, that is, time for sustaining local features. So keep this fact in mind when you make your request for time and also when you plan your program.

One-minute spot. Page 30. The sixty seconds used by many commercial sponsors, also in wide use today by government agencies. Grabbing the "mike" stand and speaking into the "mike" with mouth an inch or two from it. A most annoying habit. 

P. A. system. Page 32. Public Address system, used by groups of all kinds in hundreds of different audience situations. 

Consists of a portable microphone attached to a portable "control panel." Can be plugged into an electrical walloutlet. A very versatile instrument. "Strip-across-the-board." Page 46. A five-a-week program, that is, a program heard at the same hour Monday through 

Friday. Usually used in connection with the "soap opera. 

Low frequencies. Page 59. A term from physics, referring to sound waves which vibrate a certain number of times per second. Those which vibrate under two hundred times per second are called low frequencies. The number of times a sound wave vibrates per second is its frequency. "Signature." Page 66. The musical selection usually associated with and identifying some specific program, such as "Thanks for the Memory" for the Bob Hope show, "When the Blue of the Night" for Bing Crosby, etc. 

Theme. Page 69. Same as the signature.

Call Letters. Page 66. The letters which identify a station. "This is WMAQ, Chicago." "WEW, the Voice of the 

Apostleship of Prayer." "KMOX, the Voice of St. Louis." 120-

Program credits. Page 66. The identifications at the close of a program, in which writer (only recently has radio decided that the author ought to get a by-line!), producer, musical director, etc., are named. If the program is produced in cooperation with some organization, that organization is named here. These credits should be accurate and brief. "Variety." Page 70. The daily "trade" paper of the entertainment world.

Standard American Diction. Page 73. The term applied to a non-existing form of speech. Actually, it is an attempt to standardize the wide variety of dialects throughout the nation, eliminating the Eastern "er" for "oi," the Middle-West's omnipresent "r's," the South's elongated vowels, etc. Mainly used by theatrical performers. 

High-fidelity. Page 75. A term used by engineers to evaluate the quality of the sound being transmitted. Also used as an adjective for microphones, indicating their ability to transmit the exact quality and proportions of the sound waves which strike them. High-fidelity transmission has been an engineering problem for twenty years and American physicists and engineers have given us some of the best.

Glass-base discs. Page 75. The usual record "blank" used by recording studios and schools of speech. Not as satisfactory as aluminum-base "blanks," but aluminum's going into P-38's these days. "Up Fast." Page 87. A term usually applied in a music or sound cue to show that the desired effect should be brought on "mike" without any pause whatsoever. "Sneak in." Page 94. A sound or music cue. Means that the sound or music effect is to be brought in as unobtrusively as possible, while the actor goes right on with his lines, paying no attention to the sound effect. 

Expository device. Page 96. The script writer's method of giving the audience the necessary data for the understanding of the plot, situation, etc. For example: 121

-- "I didn't know twenty years ago when I married him that this man would one day be a hero...ours was just like any other courtship... though I think he was a little shyer than most... and more tender... now he has blown up an enemy cruiser... and I still do not feel that he is any more ruthless or callous to suffering...” Now the audience knows that the woman speaking has been married twenty years, that her husband is a hero, that he is a quiet, kind, gentle man, etc. It's a much better way than having the announcer say: “John and his wife have been married twenty years. John is in the Navy now.

He is a pilot. He sank a Jap cruiser off Bougainville. 

John is a gentle fellow. You would never think he could deal out death..." etc. See?

Crosley. Page 109. The listening survey, which for many years was radio's Blue Book. It refers to the survey conducted by the Crosley Corporation to determine the number of people listening to a specific program. Works with scientific accuracy and is the bugaboo of writers and actors for it indexes their popularity.

AFRA. Page 110. The American Federation of Radio Artists, the union which admits actors and producers into its ranks and which determines wage scales, working hours and conditions, etc. "Blasting." Page 110. A sudden, excessive "peak” in volume. 

Usually refers to an actor's overloading of the "mike.”

Log. Page 111. The program schedule or schedules of operations of a broadcasting agency. A complete listing of their time on the air, plus names of producers and engineers assigned to specific programs, etc. "Pick-ups." Page 111. Usually synonymous with "remotes": (remote control locations). Refers to the broadcasting of a program outside the studios of the station or network proper, such as the pre-war "Man on the Street" broadcasts, broadcasts from ball parks, football stadia, night clubs, etc.

Sustaining programs. Page 111. Those paid for by the station or network. The time is donated by the outlet over which they are broadcast and, in many cases, musicians, producers, writers and actors are contributed, too. 

Commercial program. Page 112. "Sponsored" shows. Those for which an advertiser purchases the time, pays the entire talent bill, and from which he gains the profits. 

Rate Card. Page 111. A station's list of the charges it makes for various kinds of time. 

Network Outlet. Page 112. Refers to the station in a community which is owned and operated by a specific network, affiliated with a network or "chain," one which reflects the policies, broadcasts the programs, and promotes the good will of a network.

ADDITIONAL EXPRESSIONS COMMONLY USED IN RADIO 

Monitoring. The operation by which the engineer adjusts the volume of voice, sound, and music coming over the microphone. "Riding the gain." Much of the same as monitoring, the careful adjusting of "peaks and valleys" in the mike output. "A cutting at 78." A record which has been "cut" or recorded at 78 revolutions per minute. The disc which you buy at the music store, whether it be a Harry James or a Horowitz, whether it sells for thirty-five cents or a dollar, is cut at 78 r. p. m. A "78" can be played on any victrola, combination radio-phonograph, etc. "A cutting at 33 and 1/3." A record which has been recorded at 33 and 1/3 revolutions per minute, and usually referred to as "an electrical transcription." This kind of disc cannot be played on the ordinary phonograph but requires a special "play back" machine, which is especially geared to play "33 1/3" records. 

123

"On the beam." A term borrowed from aviation. Literally it means to be flying in the right direction, to be on course. 

In "mike" technique, it usually means to be in proper "mike" position so that the "beam" or "live" area of the "mike" is picking up the speech, sound or music and sending out the sound waves. "Broadcasting." Another "trade" organ, a magazine published weekly. "Radio Daily." Another "trade" newspaper. This one is specifically devoted to all aspects of radio. 124
