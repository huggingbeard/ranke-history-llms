My Christmas pamphlet this year is about Lady 

Sarah Lennox, 1745-1826. I am one of the many whom she has fascinated, and I hope that my presentation of some episodes in her career may give others the same pleasurable experience.

I hope to have the good fortune to write a full biography of her. In the alternative, she may provide the material for the opening chapter in my never-forth coming book "Seven Bad Women Who Have Made 

Good." 

Who should be nominated as the other six? My selection is Rahab, Mary Magdalen, Cleopatra, Theodora, Rachel, and Sarah Bernhardt. 

Yours with seasonable good wishes,

LADY SARAH LENNOX 

ADY SARAH LENNOX was born in the purple, daughter to the second Duke of Richmond, greatgrand-daughter of Charles the Second and of 

Louise, Duchess of Portsmouth in England and 

Aubigny in France. 

Louis the Fourteenth of France selected Louise

Queroualle as the most expert light woman at his court and sent her over to be mistress of Charles the Second, that she might hold him to the French interest. She did that well. And as late as 1887 their descendant, the Duke of Richmond in England and Aubigny in France, was drawing an annuity of £19,000 a year from the British treasury for no other reason. He then commuted it into government bonds. From Charles the Second and Louise are descended the great ducal family of the Richmonds, now combined with the Gordons, always important in the British aristocracy, and often in British history.

Lady Sarah was born of the marriage of the second Duke to the Hon'ble Sara Cadogan. That marriage somewhat influenced her career, and was itself a romance.

The first Duke, the royal bastard, "had the easy pleasant manners of his father," Charles the Second. Research provides that quotation from the "Complete Peerage." He was a "shallow coxcomb." There I am quoting Dean Swift. He was "an unprincipled adventurer through life, and was in his old age addicted to drunkenness and other vices." (See the "Dictionary of National Biography.")

Being such as this, he lost £5000 at cards to General Lord Cadogan, and thereupon sold his son in marriage to pay the debt, so says a memoir by Lady Sarah's son; and later I confirmed it from another source. Cadogan, the baron and general, appears in "Henry Esmond," wherein Thackeray vents real hatred upon him. Through him our heroine has an ancestor a regicide, Sir Hardress Waller, who helped cut off the head of her other ancestor, Charles the First. The fortune of the bride, the Hon'ble Sara Cadogan, was £20,000. Maggs, the bookseller, in 1930 offered the original contract of marriage settlement and let me have a photostat of it. This marriage resulted in per-

fect love and perfect marital happiness. So says the family tradition reported by Lady Sarah's son Henry in his memoir of her early life. 

From this marriage Lady Sarah was born, February 14, 1745. 

Her father died in 1750, and her mother in 1751. There are three reasons assigned for the death of the Duchess. Horace Walpole says it was because she had been with child twenty-seven times. Lady 

Sarah's son, in the memoir, says they "lived so happily that she died of a broken heart within the year of his decease." And the third and most authentic account introduces the child Lady Sarah to you, as we would wish to begin her tale. 

The final illness of the Duchess was described and accounted for in a letter written by Lady C. Digby to Lord Ilchester. "It was thought (she wrote) to be greatly occasioned by her fright for Lady Sarah, who had cut an artery and would certainly have bled to death had not the Duchess very luckily remembered a story in the newspaper by which she found a very effectual way of stopping the great effusion of blood, and in that way saving life." 

Our wilful heroine had thrust her hand right through a glass bird-cage. It is her presentation to you. 

By November, 1759, at the age of fourteen and a half, she was considered ripe to be presented at Court, and came from Ireland to live with her much older sister, Lady Henry Fox, at Holland House. 

George the Second took quite noticeable notice of her, and she was presented at his Court. George the Second died, and his young grandson succeeded, October 25, 1760, as George the Third.

The first episode opens early in 1761. It begins on February 19th, when the King is already in love with Lady Sarah, and its end is in sight on July 8th, when the King in Council declares for his marriage with a German princess.

When it begins, Lady Sarah is under the care of her sister, Lady 

Georgianna Carolina Fox. This sister eloped with Henry Fox a year before our heroine was born. Her first child is as old as Lady Sarah. 

By this time her son Charles James Fox is a bright, popular fag at 

Eton. About Henry Fox himself, a cabinet minister, later first Lord

Holland, I refer you to Horace Walpole and to a passage in the poet Gray which is a masterpiece of deserved vituperation. Henry Fox was a politician so adroit that the aristocrats, who hated him, had to use him. You are to think of him now as Lady Sarah's adroit campaign manager.

George the Third held Drawing-rooms at St. James Palace every 

Thursday and Sunday.

"On Thursday, February 19th, at a Drawing-room in St. James 

Palace, the youthful monarch talked to Lady Susan Fox Strangways: 

The K.: Would you not like to see a Coronation? Won't it be a much finer sight when there is a Queen? 

She: To be sure, Sir.

The K.: I have had a great many applications from abroad but 

I don't like them. I have had none at home. I should like that better.

She: (was frightened-blushed-thought he might be leading up to a declaration of interest in her-wisely said nothing). 

The K.: What do you think of your friend? You know who I mean; don't you think her fittest? 

She: Think, Sir? 

The K.: I think none so fit.

And with that he went across the room to Lady Sarah and bid her ask her friend what he had been saying, and make her tell her, and tell her all." 

You will wonder at the directness with which I report this episode.

But Kings live in public and are of intense interest to their aristocracies. To the Fox-Lennox connection this was something every bit of which was to be recorded—and they did it with photographic fidelity. That line having died out, its great possession, Holland 

House, and its manuscripts, history, tradition, and treasures, are held and cherished by the collateral line, who hold the Earldom of Ilchester, and are descended from the brother of Henry Fox. Most of my quotations are from a "Life and Letters" of Lady Sarah by a dowager countess of Ilchester. Moreover, all the aristocratic world thought Lady Sarah was a comet, to be recorded. So a great deal comes from other good sources.

Henry Fox wrote a sort of aide memoire when this was all over, and at this point he goes on to tell us that "next Sunday or Sunday se'en night" the King expected to continue the affair. It was February 

22nd. Lady Sarah was a few days past her sixteenth birthday, but she was already a lively baggage. She had had no warning to expect these royal approaches, and had been and was much engaged in another direction. First, she had flirted and campaigned to get a lover away from Lady Caroline Russell of the ducal house of Bedford. Then she was so much pleased with her success that her vanity became en- 

Igaged, she, mistakenly, thinking it was her affections.

Her judgment was immature and bad. The young man, according

to Horace Walpole, was "a vain, insignificant puppy." He was a courtesy Lord; an eldest son in line for a Marquisate. Now Lord

Newbattle, he later became Marquis of Lothian. His sister was married to Lady Sarah's brother, Lord George Lennox. 

On Saturday, February 21st, Lord George and his wife went to the home of Henry Fox. Behind the back of Lady Caroline Fox, they got Lady Sarah and took her out to the Park to meet Lord Newbattle. 

It was settled that parental consent should be asked. The young man's father, then Earl of Ancram, was applied to. Things moved so swiftly that by Sunday the Earl and his Countess had dictated, and the young man had sent, a letter resigning all claim, and Lady Sarah had been crying all Sunday morning. "The moment the King saw her, he go's to her. 

The K.: Have you seen your friend lately? 

She: Yes. 

The K.: Has she told you what I said to her? 

She: Yes. 

The K.: Do you approve?" 

And thereupon the lady "made no answer, but looked as cross as she could. "H. M. (the king), affronted, left her, seemed confused, and left the Drawing-room."

To get her out of the way of Lord Newbattle, she was packed off on Monday to Goodwood in Sussex, the seat of her brother, the Duke of Richmond. But Lord Newbattle "overtook her on the road, unsaid all he had wrote," and at Goodwood, with the treacherous assistance of his sister, Lady George, continued the affair. 

Lady Sarah already had a beloved girl confidante-Henry Fox's niece in the Ilchester line, Lady Susan. All through life they were steadfast and invaluable correspondents. The letters begin on Wednesday, when Lady Sarah writes from "My dear Susan,- "Goodwood, Feb. 25, 1761 "I have but just time to tell you that my sister Caroline will tell you, if you ask her, what has passed between Lord Newbattle and I upon the road. 

Adieu, yrs. 

S. Lennox. "P. S. My brother begs you'll go to Court, and let me know what 

HE says to you."

From Goodwood Lady Sarah was sent on to the Ilchester countyseat, Redlynch, near Bruton, in Somerset.

Some time in March she had an accident and broke her leg, and by breaking her leg she lost the opportunity to be Queen of England. 

She was returning to Bruton in Somerset from Longleat in Wilts. 

Longleat was the seat of Thomas Thynne, Viscount Weymouth, later Marquess of Bath. She "had the misfortune to fall from her horse in the town of Maiden Bradley and break her leg." The horse put his foot upon a stone which broke, and it was impossible he should not fall. The pain was terrible, from the motion of the coach, until she got to Stourton, the Hoare country house near by. 

I had a happy digression from the royal courtship to trace her journey. The English place names and surnames fascinate always. 

To see where everybody lived, and who each character was, I took county maps of Wilts and Somerset in my "British Atlas" of 1810. 

A vast green spot upon the maps then and now denotes the great park at Longleat, and the seat of the Thynnes, who then had the Viscounty of Weymouth. They hold their title and town and country house today. In London, when you see a sign "Bath House" on the door of an imposing establishment, do not expect Turkish or Russian baths, or a swimming-pool, but ring the bell and call on the Marquess. 

In Wilts you will find his seat and park so large that it lies in three hundreds-Heytesbury and Warminster in that county, extending into the hundred of Frome in Somerset. It lies just south of the cross on the map which marks Frome Woodlands Church. Below it is a crossroads where you will find the chaste village of Maiden Bradley, and below that, again, a cross for the church at Stourton, where even today the Hoares have their country estate. Sir Robert Hoare advertised for a valet-butler in Field for April, 1932, from his seat, Stourhead. Westward lies Bruton, in the hundred of Bruton, and in the south part of that hundred, near Shepton Montague, is the cross for Redlynch. Frome is the market town, and Frome cheese fair is on the last Wednesday in September. Frome is a name for cottage cheese, or one kind of it.

Lady Sarah's broken leg was set and "very well cured" by the local surgeon, Mr. Clark of Bruton, after which "she was brought the next day upon men's shoulders, in a very pretty bed made for the purpose, to Redlynch, singing a great part of the way." This account comes from the Redlynch house book. The entry is dated March 31st, and is in the handwriting of Stephen Fox, Lord Ilchester. 

But there she was! The flirtation with Lord Newbattle had sent

her to Goodwood. The pursuit by the swain, with the assistance of 

Lady Sarah's brother George, had required her to be sent on to Somerset. Then the broken leg had stretched out the absence, so that it was going to be six weeks before the King's interest would have another chance to grow, and his passion another chance to struggle with statecraft.

Henry Fox was holding the fort at St. James until Lady Sarah should return. He was also priming Lady Caroline Fox with every bit of detail which could spur and excite, and could help her in preparing Lady Sarah to return to the attack. So he wrote from London to his wife at Redlynch: "Tuesday morning, April 7, 1761 "The King asked Connolly yesterday a hundred questions about Lady Sarah, wonder'd and was concerned she should be left to the care of a country surgeon. Connolly told him Hawkins (a fashionable 

London surgeon) had been sent to, and declar'd there could be no use in his going; that she was very well, very cheerful, etc. H(is) 

M(ajesty), I find enquired very tenderly." 

On Monday, April 13th, Fox went to Court "determined... that

HE should speak to me if I could bring it about." After a loose question or two the King gave him an opening to mention Lady Sarah. "Now I have you" is what Fox thought that second; at least, he wrote so the next day. "At that name his voice and countenance, gentle and gracious already, softened, and he coloured a little. 'I am very glad to hear she is so well.'" Fox made the King feel the pain of the break and the terrible pain from the motion of the coach. When the King "made the countenance of one feeling pain himself," Fox says he thought to himself, "you shall hear of that again."

Fox went home and wrote it all out and sent it down to Redlynch for his wife to use in moulding Lady Sarah to their ambitions: "Don't tell Lady Sarah that I am sure he intends to marry her, for I am not sure of it. Whether Lady Sarah shall be told what I am sure of, I leave to the reader's judgement. I am sure that he loves her better than Ld. N(ewbattle) do's. "I have shorten'd, not exaggerated a word, in this account.' 

On April 19th, 1761, Henry Fox to his wife: "If Lady Sarah don't be quiet, it will be the longer before she can dance and show her pretty self to advantage." 10

"In a short time"-I am returning to the note by Stephen Fox,

Earl of Ilchester, in the house book-"she was visited from London by Mr. Conolly and Lady Louisa Conolly, Lord and Lady Kildare, Mr. Fox and Lady Caroline, and the Duke and Duchess of Richmond."

The story is continued in the memorandum which Henry Fox wrote after the event: "On May the 22nd, Lady Sarah returns; on the 29th, goes to the

Play." The King went too, and "it is certain he shew'd great pleasure on seeing her. The Sunday after, as soon as his eyes found her in the Drawing-room, which he did not expect, he colour'd, & came up to her eager & in haste & talk'd much and graciously. But on Thursday (that was June 4th), at the Birthday ball, he had no eyes but for her, & hardly talk'd to any body else. He brought her (by leaning forward & stooping often) to come forward & stand by the side of his fauteul; all eyes were fix'd on them, & the next morning all tongues observing on the particularity of his behaviour, if it can be thought particular that a young King should not be able to avoid shewing the strongest symptoms of love & of desire for the prettyest creature in the world; for, if possible, she look'd prettyer that night than ever. Her Ladyship, with modesty very natural to her, and yet with looks as unaffected, returned the fondness of his eyes & gallantry of his discourse as much as ever he could wish. He is in love with her, & it is no less certain she loves him; and if she now ever thinks of Newbattle it is to vex & hate herself for the foolish transaction I before related. It were impossible to write down so much discourse as the King held with her, nor was that so remarkable as the language des yeux. In this pretty way did these two lovers entertain one another & the eyes of the whole ball room for an hour. 

He stopp'd very remarkably as he was going, and turn'd & spoke again & again, as if he could not force himself from her." 

As to the birthday ball, Horace Walpole reported to the Countess of Ailsbury, writing: "Strawberry Hill, June 13, 1761 "... Do you remember one of those stories, where a prince has eight statues of diamonds, which he overlooks, because he fancies he wants a ninth; and to his great surprise the ninth proves to be pure flesh and blood which he never thought of? Somehow or other Lady Sarah is the ninth statue; and you will allow has better white and red than if she was made of pearls and rubies." 11

Another account comes from an eye witness, Lady Macartney: "At the Court Ball on His Majesty's first birthday, June 4th, 1761, Lady Sarah (Lennox, the only unmarried daughter of the Duke of Richmond) had her place of course at the head of the dancer's bench nearest His seat. Lo! The royal chair, heavy as it was, moved by degrees more and more to the left and He who sat thereon edged and edged farther the same way. And the conversation went on till all dancing was over, and everybody sat in suspense, and it approached one in the morning ere he recollected himself and rose to dismiss the assembly." 

Henry Fox goes on in his diary or account sent to Redlynch: "On Sunday, June the 7th, he look'd out of sorts & melancholy, said nothing to her but what he might have said to any other young lady, look'd languishing, & as if he lov'd her, talk'd a great while to her, & with great favour & civility to Lady Caroline & Lady Susan, but there were not those ardent looks of fondness, nor any particular expressions to Lady Sarah. The ladys say he had been tutor❜d." 

From Redlynch, on Saturday, June 20th (misdating her letter the 19th), Sarah wrote to Susan: "After many pros and cons it is determined I go to-morrow, and that I must pluck up my spirits, and if I am asked if I have thought of... or approve,-To look... in the face, and with an earnest but good-humoured countenance say that, 'I don't know what I ought to think.' If the meaning is explained, I must say, 'that I can hardly believe it,' and so forth; if instead of that, you should be named, I shall say that you were so much confounded and astonished that I believe you did not understand the meaning; if the answer is- 'I hope you do understand,' I shall say, 'that the more I think of it, the less I understand it,' (I hope that won't be too forward). In short, I must show I wish it to be explained, without seeming to suspect any other meaning; what a task it is! God send that I may be enabled to go thro' with it. "I am allowed to mutter a little, provided the words astonished, surprised, understand, and meaning are heard. "I am working myself up to consider what depends upon it, that I may me fortifier against it comes-the very thought of it makes me sick in my stomach already. I shall be as proud as the devil, but no matter... 12

And on Sunday she went on in the same letter from London: "Well to-day is come to nothing, for we were so near your namesake and her mistress (Ly Susan Stuart & Princess Augusta) that nothing could be said, and they wacht us as a cat does a mouse, but looks & smiles were very gracious; however I go with the Duchess 

Thursday, I'll put a postcript in this of it. I beg you won't shew this to anybody, so pray burn it, for I can tell you things that I can't other people you know. Adieu, Dear Suke."

Fox tells us that in consequence she went to "the next and the next Drawing-room," say Sunday 21st, Thursday 25th. Fox says: "He spoke & look'd with great fondness but said nothing particular."

Having held over her letter, Sarah reports of the Sunday at Court (that is, the 21st), "Well to-day is come to nothing" and continues by P. S. and P. S. No. 2. "P. S.-My love (if I may say so) to Ld and Ly Ilchester, and compliments to the rest. Pray desire Lord Ilchester to send my mare immediately, if he don't want it, for I must ride once at least immediately in Richmond Park. Much depends on it. "P. S.-I went Thursday but nothing was said; I won't go jiggitting for ever if I hear nothing I can tell him.” 

Henry Fox records that: "On Sunday, June the 28th, he fix'd his eyes & hardly took them off from her all church time. She did not that day go to the Drawing-room; Thursday, July 2nd, Lady Caroline went, & Lady Sarah with her. Lady Augusta, & her Lady of Bedchamber Lady Susan Stewart, & Lady Bute watch'd the King impertinently & indecently, who spoke to Lady Sarah but said nothing particular." 

But the day before that, on Wednesday, July 1st, a Council had been summoned to meet Wednesday, July 8th. "On Saturday," July 

4th, “how were we surprised to know," says Fox, "that the Council was to hear H. M. declare his intended marriage with Miss

Charlotte of Mecklenburgh! This must have been settled with his consent long before Thursday, June 18th."

Yes. It was. Before that date, and before the Council, but I should surmise after the warm passages of June 4th, George the 

Third wrote Lord Bute "a very curious holograph letter" in which the monarch, after consenting to give up, on the minister's advice, 13

his intentions of "proposing" to Lady Sarah Lennox, desires the Earl forthwith to procure him a consort of royal birth. 

My authority is an anonymous correspondent of Notes & Queries in 1908 who had seen "a copy of undisputable authenticity." From another independent source I infer that the King's daughter, Princess Charlotte, many years after spoke to Lady Susan O'Brien of having read this letter.

For the present we must reconstruct it by conjecture. If we consider what a human King in love would have said as he ran away, I think we should expect to find the echo of the court ladies who were fighting against having a subject promoted to be Queen. If we consider what would be in a State paper, I think it must have said that no King could stand right with his nation if he were supposed to have been seduced by a woman thus pushed forward by that old gold-digger, Henry Fox.

On July 1st the whole membership of the Privy Council was summoned to meet at St. James on July 8th. Now the King alone is a quorum when the Great Council meets. If he be not there, three are enough. Ordinarily the summonses run to a selected few concerned with the business to be done. Henry Fox was a Privy Councillor; he was at Holland House, and presumably he got his summons that day or the next. By July 4th he had heard from the Duke of Bedford, from 

Woburn, what the Council was for. Its very solemnity must have told him, for he knew that the summons "has been sent all over 

England... making Privy Counsellors come 200 miles... too many to be consulted, that is certain." The ambitious Fox had too much to hope for, and he hoped against hope until the very announcement. 

From him "no secret was ever kept like it. I saw Ld. Bute and Lord 

President (Earl Granville) yesterday (July 6th); they would not give me a hint." And his note to brother Stephen the day after the Council is for him of unexampled shortness. 

The official announcement says: "All the members of the privy-council were fummoned to meet at St. James's on the 8th, when the king was pleafed to make a moft gracious declaration of his having chofen for his confort the Princefs Charlotte of Mecklenburg-Strelitz, and likewife to appoint Tuefday the 22d day of September next, for folemnizing his coronation; and to order, That a proclamation fhould be iffued for notifying the fame." 14

And this is the Proclamation itself: "At the Court of St. James's the 8th day of July, 1761 "His Majefty being this day prefent in council, was pleafed to make the following declarations, viz. "C 'Having nothing fo much at heart as to procure the welfare and happinefs of my people, and to render the fame ftable and permanent to pofterity, I have, ever fince my acceffion to the throne, turned my thoughts towards the choice of a princefs for my confort; and I now, with great fatisfaction, acquaint you, that, after the fulleft information, and mature deliberation, I am come to a refolution to demand in marriage the princefs Charlotte of Mecklenburg Strelitz; a princefs diftinguifhed by every eminent virtue, and amiable endowment, whofe illuftrious line has conftantly fhewn the firmeft zeal for the proteftant religion, and a particular attachment to my family. I have judged proper to communicate to you thefe my intentions, in order that you may be fully apprifed of a matter fo highly important to me, and to my kingdoms, and which, I perfuade myfelf, will be moft acceptable to all my loving fubjects.' "Whereupon all the privy council prefent made it their requeft to his majefty that this his majefty's most gracious declaration to them might be made public; which his majefty was pleafed to order accordingly." 

Where Henry Fox still hoped against hope even on the 8th, Lady

Sarah was informed, convinced, and garrulous to Lady Susan on the 

7th. And this is what she wrote: 

"Holland House, July 7, 1761 "My dearest Susan,—I return you Tony Martin, in perfect health,

I hope. I take the opportunity of writing by him, as I think this should not be trusted to the post. To begin to astonish you as much as I was, I must tell you that the is going to be married to a Princess of Mecklenburg, & that I am sure of it. There is a Council to-morrow on purpose, the orders for it are urgent & important business; does not your chollar rise at hearing this; but you think I daresay that I have been doing some terrible thing to deserve it, for you won't be easily brought to change so totaly your opinion of any person; but I assure you I have not. I have been very often since I wrote last, but tho' nothing was said, he always took pains to shew me some prefference by talking twice, and mighty kind speeches and looks; even last 

Thursday, the day after the orders were come out, the hipocrite had 15

the face to come up & speak to me with all the good humour in the world, & seemed to want to speak to me but was afraid. There is something so astonishing in this that I can hardly believe, but yet Mr. Fox knows it to be true; I cannot help wishing to-morrow over, tho' I can expect nothing from it. He must have sent to this woman before you went out of town; then what business had he to begin again? In short, his behavior is that of a man who has neither sense, good nature, nor honesty. I shall go Thursday sennight; I shall take care to shew that I am not mortified to anybody, but if it is true that one can vex anybody with a reserved, cold manner, he shall have it 

I promise him. "Now as to what I think about it as to myself, excepting this little revenge, I have almost forgiven him; luckily for me I did not love him, & only liked him, nor did the title weigh anything with me; so little at least, that my disappointment did not affect my spirits above one hour or two I believe. "I did not cry I assure you, which I believe you will, as I know you were more set upon it than I was. The thing I am most angry at, is looking so like a fool, as I shall for having gone so often for nothing, but I don't much care; if he was to change his mind again (which can't be tho'), & not give me a very good reason for his conduct, I would not have him, for if he is so weak as to be govern'd by everybody I shall have but a bad time of it. Now I charge you, dear Lady 

Sue, not to mention this to anybody but Ld and Ly Ilchester, & desire them not to speak of it to any mortal, for it will be said we invent storries, & he will hate us all anyway, for one generally hates people that one is in the wrong with and that knows one has acted wrong, particularly if they speak of it, and it might do a great deal of harm to all the rest of the family, & do me no good. So pray remember this, for a secret among many people is very bad and I must tell it some.'

That distinguished historian Sir George Otto Trevelyan, in a serious history book, writes in praise of this letter. He calls it: "A letter the spelling and punctuation of which prove that the writer was worthy to occupy the throne of Anne and Mary of Orange." 

And he goes on to say: "The letter in which Lady Sarah announces to her friend Lady Susan that her hopes were at an end is the most charming of all the documents which bear upon English history. The most delightful passage, if it were possible to make a choice, is that in which Lady Sarah carefully defines the extent and nature of her 16

disappointment where she says, '... the thing I am most angry at is looking so like a fool, as I shall for having gone so often for nothing.' 

Princess Charlotte did not stop in Mecklenburg to mourn her mother, who literally died for joy at the news, but headed for England, and the marriage date was tentatively fixed.

Lady Sarah went to Court July 16th. "The prudent will have it so❞ is the restrained comment of Henry Fox to brother Stephen.

Again her own account to Lady Susan is quite differently garrulous. "July 16, 1761 "I went this morning for the first time. He looked fright'ned when he saw me, but notwithstanding came up, with what countenance I don't know, for I was not so grascious as even to look at him: when he spoke, our conversation was short, here it is; 'I see riding is begun again, it's glorious weather for it now.' Answer,-'Yes it is very fine,' add to that a very cross & angry look on my side & his turning away immediately, & you know the whole." Remember that we are writing and thinking about a child just turned sixteen. You hear the child in her. She talked, before the 

Duke of Devonshire, of liking to be bridesmaid. He then considerately wrote her a letter formally enquiring whether she wished to be commanded to be a bridesmaid by himself as Lord Chamberlain. "She answered with reserve and pride enough (making her compliments to Ld Chamberlain) that if asked she would attend.

Then "the sickness of her squirrel immediately took up all her attention," and when it dy'd grief "soon gave way to the care of a little hedge-hog that she saved from destruction in the field." "She bought it yesterday" (I surmise from the haymaker), July 29th. It breakfasted with the Holland House family July 30th and she kissed it very much, while mourning not only the squirrel but also her pretty horse Beau. And that day her sister, Lady Kildare, was brought to bed of Lord Henry Fitzgerald, ancestor of the present 

Baron de Ros, brother of Lord Edward Fitzgerald, the Irish hero.

And the day after she took up the tale again to Lady Susan: "August 1, 1761 "Dear Pussy,-I have only time to tell you that I have been asked to be brides-maid, & I have accepted of it. I am sorry to say it's against my sister Caroline's opinion a little; I beg you will tell me what your opinion is. And fifty-seven years afterwards, on April 25, 1818, Lady Susan listened to Lady Sarah and recorded in her journal: "She talked enthusiastically of the King's looks and manner at his Coronation which she saw in the Abbey. So fine a figure in the robes he wore; of the chair he sat in; of his grave and solemn manner, and great attention to the great charge he was undertaking—this contrasted with his youthful countenance, his beautiful teeth. The

And here, on September 22, 1761, the day of the Coronation, we may end the episode of the royal courtship.

Only nine months later Lady Sarah was married to Thomas Charles Bunbury, Esq. Next came her profligate married life and her passionate elopement.

From 1761 to late in 1766 was a quieter period. We may almost believe her assertion in those days that "I own I am never quite happy when I am without" Sir Charles. Then she went to Paris. A very remarkable and little-known autobiography by the Duc de Lauzun leaves no detail of her fall to the imagination of his readers. 

He quotes her as saying to him within a few days of their first meeting: "I love you, Monsieur de Lauzun, and seeing how unhappy and how sensitive you were I have been persuaded that your love existed and then I could not resist the pleasure of relieving you by the avowal of mine. You must remember that a lover is ordinarily a mere incident in the life of a French woman but it is a great event in life for one of us English. From that moment everything is changed and she ends by losing all her comfort and perhaps her existence for a sentiment which in France has only one or two pleasant and incidental results without danger. But the certainty of losing everything does not stop every Englishwoman. It is true we choose our husbands and therefore we have less reason for not loving them. At any rate the crime of deceiving them is never pardoned in England. 

Judge for yourself if a woman in my position should have a lover or can have one.' "To this I replied: 'I prefer your happiness, but no power on earth can help me from adoring you whatever the consequences.' "Then we promised that we would never slip from the greatest circumspection and the greatest and most strict prudence, and proceeded to violate those oaths as soon as possible. Lady Sarah loved 20

me dearly but she granted me no favors. Our good faith and our gayety gave the public an interest and on this occasion they were quite indulgent. Lord Carlisle kept silence for he hoped that Lady 

Sarah would forget me when she left France. I continued to be on the best of terms with Sir Charles Bunbury."

I wish there were space and time in which to go on even to the scene in a roadside inn on the return journey from Paris. After that he followed her to England and was her husband's guest. He boasts about what he did in the absence of his host, giving an account which Thomas DeQuincey wanted to disbelieve but couldn't.

Then she offered to give up everything and elope to the West 

Indies. And then she dismissed him and cut the affair off short “because," she said, "you did not have confidence enough either in your constancy or mine" when put to such a test.

We shall never know how many affairs she had between 1766 and 1769. The Town and Country Magazine, reporting her elopement, says that if the injured husband went alphabetically to work challenging every man who had wronged him he would be ten years before he reached the letter G. Here we must leave them, for lack of time and space. The episode ended because Lord William Gordon was just no good at all. Her brother the Duke of Richmond reclaimed her from him, and then she lived quietly at Goodwood, the ducal estate in Sussex, until, in 1781, she became the wife of Colonel the Honorable George Napier. 

We have seen her as the fair maiden loved by the King and by everybody else. We have seen her as the young married woman of easy virtue. There is a fine story to tell of the noble and faithful wife. 

Then another of an able and intriguing widow, and the pension she got out of her old flame the King. And finally, in the full flower of life, we see her as the mother of the Gracchi. Of her seven children, four sons were distinguished, some of them famous. You see them with Wellington in the Peninsular campaign as young and chivalrous officers. You see them in maturity making history and also writing it. And you will always see that it was because of her influence and her training that they had the ability and character to make the name of Napier famous.

She survived until 1826 and witnessed a large part of their glory. 

We must acknowledge that in 1761 she was unsuited to be Queen of England. When George the Third jilted her, he showed sound political sagacity-perhaps the only evidence that he was fit to be a King.

But when we see what she came to be, we do regret that she was never Queen Sarah the First of England, Scotland, France, and Ireland; that she never had the chance to exercise her character and influence as the Queen Consort of the man who did everything wrong, and to whose wrong head we owe our independence.
