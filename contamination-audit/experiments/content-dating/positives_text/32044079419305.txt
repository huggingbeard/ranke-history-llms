In presenting this book on Loudoun County, a brief glimpse of its interesting history, sketching its progress from the days of King Carter to the present time, the publishers dare to express the hope that it may be deemed worthy of a permanent place in the archives of the county and in the homes of its citizens. Few counties in the United States, if any, can lay claim to as many beautiful, historic homes as one finds in Loudoun. ROKEBY 

Rokeby, near Leesburg, has a distinction. which belongs to no other private residence in the United States—that of having been the capital of the nation. When the British entered 

Washington during the War of 1812 and President Madison and his cabinet were forced to flee, the State papers were carried to Leesburg and were kept at an unoccupied building for one night. The following morning they were taken to Rokeby where they were secreted for two weeks. The house is very old, but a modern front and porch have been added in recent years. Mr. B. Franklin Nalle is the present owner.

RIVING THROUGH the pleasantly rolling and prosperous farmlands of Loudoun

County, it is difficult to realize that this pastoral country was an impenetrable and somewhat mysterious jungle many years after settlements farther south had got well under way and were reaching a highly developed civilization.

The primeval condition of the country, the small number of settlers, the absence of roads and the presence of hostile Indians l conduced to keep the early colonists in the less foridable section of Tidewater Virginia. Men and women ere had already built their houses and towns. They were ising families, making laws, paying taxes, dining and ancing and hearing stories of the dread Iroquois who mauded on all, white and redskin alike, who came into their rritory in the Northwest. Then the Piedmont country was art of that vast, reputedly macabre wilderness stretching to le blue Pacific.

But as soon as a firm foothold was established in Virginia d the permanency of the colony assured, the urge to expand to wider and perhaps more desirable territory brought terprising pioneers into the Northern Neck. In 1722 Govnor Spotswood took a determined stand against the Indian zards and succeeded in negotiating with the Iroquois in pact known as the Treaty of Albany. In this the Indians edged themselves not to cross the Potomac east of the ue Ridge, or the Ridge itself, without a pass from the overnor of New York. The treaty, of course, greatly reeved the tension throughout Virginia and contributed eatly to the influx of immigrants into the fertile country at is now Loudoun County.

Formerly a part of Fairfax and situated in the extreme orthern part of the State, Loudoun County presented its st settlers with the characteristic topography of the Piedont section, a plain with rolling hills and gentle slopes. › pioneers came from three directions and represented as any different peoples. The English Cavaliers, their fears suaged by Governor Spotswood's treaty, came up from the rlier settlements in eastern Virginia and located in what now the eastern part of Loudoun. Germans, exiled from e Palatinate and invited by Queen Anne to settle in her lonies, came by way of Pennsylvania and Maryland and ok possession of the northwestern section. Quakers, who so came by way of Pennsylvania, and a liberal sprinkling Scotch, Scotch-Irish and Dutch, found a location for emselves in the central section of the country. 

Though representing differing nationalities and ideologies, e fathers of Loudoun County were mostly of the practical oneering class. They did not come to the new territory ith retinues of servants and workmen, but they came to do their own work. And, the records show, they knew pretty well how to take care of themselves. That may perhaps account for the fact that seventy per cent of the farms here today are owned by the people who work them. 

Soon they began organization. In 1742 the vast Prince 

William County was divided and Fairfax created, named for Lord Fairfax, the proprietor of the Northern Neck, which embraced all of northern Virginia. Fifteen years later, in 1757, when the French and Indian War was at its peak, Fairfax was divided by the creation of Loudoun County, which followed Difficult Run from the point where it joins the Potomac to its source and thence to the corner of Prince 

William County.

The new county was named for the Earl of Loudoun, who was Commander-in-Chief of the British forces during the French and Indian War and titular Governor of Virginia. 

A Scotchman, he was born John Campbell in 1705 and succeeded his father to the title in 1731. In 1756 he came to 

America to command the army, but returned to Great Britain the following year. The history books have little to say about the Earl of Loudoun except that circumstances conspired in having one of Virginia's most charming counties named after him.

Homely characteristics marked the early Loudoun settlers. 

Although there were a few large houses with plantations worked by slaves, the majority of the inhabitants, hardworking farmers, cultivated their own land. Houses were small and rudely built, and corn was ground out in a hand mill. Perched on three-legged stools, many of the people ate from wooden platters with the assistance of hunting knives. Wooden bowls and gourds, with a few pewter spoons and dishes, comprised the household furnishings, while beds consisted of a bear rug or a few blankets laid on the floor. 

For many years there were no courts in this frontier community, but the people, essentially honest and industrious, managed to govern themselves without difficulty. 

Of course there was some semblance of social life among the eastern Virginia planters, attracted to Loudoun by its rich soil, but even this was simple. Here the chief mode of social intercourse was effected in extended visits, where the great houses, filled with young people, echoed for weeks with song, dance and rollicking fun.

The small farmers also had their amusements, the main one being "shucking bees", which combined business and pleasure, another evidence of the practical nature of the people. Stacks of corn in the husk were divided into equal piles, and at a given signal parties of merry-making huskers started in on the piles with a prize awaiting the first finished. 

The function wound up with a dance, usually jigs and Virginia reels danced to the music of a fiddle. of Loudoun was long a frontier community. Farming was, course, the main business of the people, and the accent of - 11 3-

their labors was placed on tilling the soil and reaping its crops. Personal comfort was at first unimportant. There was no glass in the windows of their log cabins and no locks on the doors. The settlers were building up a flourishing farming country-working in clothing of deer skins and moccasins made from buffalo and other hides. Religious needs were attended to in prayer meetings, held from cabin to cabin. 

But these early settlements in the wilds were followed after a time by the establishment of villages, to which merchants, lawyers and physicians came—educated men who had seen life in more metropolitan centers-bringing the customs. of civilized society into the frontier regions. With the newcomers from the east also came the European fashions, which attracted and evidently distracted the women. "How could the ladies dance anything but the stately minuet", asks at county historian, "when their heads were veritable pyramids of pasted hair surmounted by turbans, when their jeweled stomachers and tight-laced stays held their bodies as tightly as would a vise, when their high heeled shoes were as unyielding as if made of wood, and their trains of taffeta often as much as fifteen yards long, and great feathered head dresses compelled them to turn around as slowly as strutting peacocks?"

The men of fashion of the period dressed for festive occasions in gay silk and satin coats and wore large wigs, an essential item, of course, in the costume of any gentleman. 

These were the first participants in a social life which has become nationally famous. Gay and sparkling, the house party was a perennial affair. Kith and kin came to spend a week, a month, and tarried indefinitely. They dined, danced, rode in the tournament, raced their fine horses and followed Reynard across the "rolling hills and gentle slopes".

Then, as now, the Virginian loved a good time. 

But there is considerably more to the history of Loudoun County than farming and social festivities. The very position of the county brought it prominently into the events of the nation's early wars. Leesburg, the county seat, was originally a stockaded town, thrown up in the troubled days of the French and Indian War. The first Court House was built in 

1761, and the village was surrounded by a stockade to provide a place of refuge for the farming people in time of Indian attacks and also to serve as a stronghold for the Colonial troops. Later it was named for Francis Lightfoot 

Lee, the first "County Lieutenant", who took up residence on the land owned by his father in the county and supervised the construction of the Court House on the land of Nicholas Minor.

The people of Loudoun were as much interested as any of their countrymen in the events antedating the Revolution. 

A meeting in Leesburg on June 14, 1774, considered the best method of preserving the rights and liberties of the colonists and of relieving the people of Boston. Then one of the most densely populated counties in Virginia, Loudoun in 1780 boasted a militia of 1746 men, which seems to be a larger number than that of any other county. A few of 

Loudoun's sons probably served with Morgan's riflemen v went to Quebec in Arnold's expedition.

OTHER OF STATES and of statesmen, Vir ginia looks proudly over her picturesque countryside and stops with more than a fond eye on the verdant area of some five hundred and nineteen square miles in the northern neck of the State called Loudoun

County. Here, where sturdy pioneers once cut their way through mountains and the heavy growth of wilderness country to drive out hostile Indians and build up their communities, one finds the essence of Viria's charm and stature. Here old ideas of living and old als of life have met the new trappings of modernity and ctical science and fused into a paragon design for livingentirely pleasant milieu-productive, reliable and ever asant and gracious.

Loudoun is by no means the largest county in Virginia the most industrious. Few giant stacks send volleys of oke rolling over her quaint little towns and wide fields 

1 it is only rarely that the shriek of a steam whistle interts the rhythm of her essentially agricultural enterprise. there is sowing and reaping and threshing in the great ds that roll green and brown beneath the Blue Ridge. And re are fat Guernseys and Jerseys and Percherons and thorghbred sheep in the pastures—and full pails of high grade k in the dairies. And, perhaps more important, there are endly and courteous smiles in the narrow streets that able through the villages, and the felicity that comes when n and women work industriously and live simply together. 

Staccato tempos and the noise and brash of more metroitan centers are off key in Loudoun. Like their forefathers, o came in from in the North, South and West, they are of land, and, whether mastering a great estate or tilling a all farm, their main concern in life is in the good earth and work. By far the large majority of the population is arian in the specific sense of the word, and sixty-three per t of the residents of Loudoun own their own farms, some hem as large as two hundred and fifty acres. Their busis is their crops and their livestock and their dairying, but y come into the little towns to pay their taxes, buy ceries, get the mail and exchange "hellos" with neighbors. 

Cut out of a rather formidable wilderness because of its rability as farming land, Loudoun is highly favored in ing a variety of soils, the overlapping of which makes sible a diversified agriculture. An always colorful plain rolling hills and wooded slopes, the chief soils are clays loams, largely what is known as the Cecil Clay types. è heavy clays are strong wheat, grass and alfalfa soils, ducive to livestock breeding, while the loams are better ed for corn. The sandstone types of soil of the hills are to advantage in the production of fruits and timber. A d drainage system prevents excessive erosion except on the steep hillsides. The elevation ranges from four hundred to six hundred feet in the plain region to one thousand six hundred feet in the more mountainous sections. 

Dairying, of course, is one of the main businesses of the county, and in the past two decades a thriving dairy industry has been built up. Loudoun cows are among the best in the 

East, the Guernsey breed predominating, and this county was the second center in the United States to adopt Cow Testing Association work. Approximately $1,500,000 is realized here from the dairy business every year. 

Approximately $6,500,000 is realized every year from the enterprise of Loudoun County farmers. Their swine and sheep and poultry, butter, eggs, corn and milk go out by truck and train to the markets and kitchens of Baltimore and Washington and some even to Philadelphia and New York. 

Wheat goes south to the mills in Alabama and Georgia, but most of Loudoun's produce heads north.

Although at the time of the last census, there were only ninety-eight persons engaged in the industrial life of Loudoun, one does find some manufactures here. Milling receives considerable attention, but comparatively little of the county's wheat and only part of its corn is milled at home. 

The lime kilns of Loudoun produce a fine grade of lime, which is being used to some extent, and experts say that the marble in the Goose Creek Marble Works is of superior grade, but little has been done with it. 

Other manufacturing industries in the county number one power distributing plant, a creamery, an ice cream factory, two ice plants, saw mills and three printing establishments, which, of course, print local newspapers. But the bulk of Loudoun's industry has been, is and probably will continue to be agricultural. Its principal branches are stock raising, poultry and egg production, dairying, horticulture and general farming. The principal crops raised are corn, wheat, hay, apples, peaches and vegetables.

While saliently an agricultural community, there is more to life in Loudoun than the simplicity of working the land. 

Forty-nine school buildings rise up from the county's rolling plain, and nine religious faiths have houses of worship within its limits. The county seat, Leesburg, a town of some commercial import, is modernly appointed and governed, with model library and postoffice buildings as well as two banks and a sprinkling of industry. Three state highways, several bus lines and a branch of the Washington and Old Dominion Railway bring business and visitors in and out of the county.

One of the most striking things about the section is its unity of interst and enterprise. Most of the building, for instance, from the architect's blueprints to the locksmith and painter and paperhanger, has been and is done by residents of the county.

Looking down on this flourishing farmland are the great 8 19 3.

houses, around which considerable of the national history has revolved and which still draw the curious and the interested from all sections of the country. Especially in the 

Middleburg district, wealthy Northerners have recently settled, buying up the large estates and entering readily into the horse breeding, racing and hunting atmosphere that have made this section one of the most famed in the land. The 

Middleburg development has made Loudoun one of the wealthiest counties in America in personal property, and it is said that more millionaires receive their mail here than at any other postoffice in the country.

CHURCHES 

Nine denominations are represented in the religious life of Loudoun County. Those having the largest membership are the Methodist, the Baptist and the Episcopal. 

The first church of the county was formed as a rural chapel in 1730 and known as the Chapel Above Goose Creek. It was also known as the Chapel of Ease, because its existence made church going easier for the early settlers of Loudoun. Augustine Washington, father of the nation's first President, was instrumental in founding the county's first parish, Truro, and, though later subdivided, its church still goes by the name of St. James and its denomination is, of course, Episcopal. 

SCHOOLS 

Since the War Between the States, when education in Virginia was at a low ebb, Loudoun County has gradually built up an excellent school system. There are now forty-four public schools in the county, twenty-four white and twenty colored. The largest high school in the county is the one at 

Lincoln, but Loudoun's finest school is the one at Leesburg, which combines both high school and elementary grades. 

The county's school enrollment is over four thousand. 

In addition, Loudoun boasts several nationally known private schools. Prominent among these are the Foxcroft School, of which Miss Charlotte Noland is Head Mistress, and Llan gollen, Mrs. Francis Patton, Head Mistress. A new school for boys is the Farmhill School, of which Mr. Edward C.

Willcox is Head Master. 

Mr. Oscar L. Emerick is Loudoun Division Superintendent of Schools, while his Board of Education is comprised of the Messrs. E. Neville Bradfield, August Zerega, Charles J. 

Ford, Jack Potterfield, M. Bernard Costello, William T

Smith, and P. J. Coleman. Mr. Charles S. Monroe is principal of the Leesburg School. 

LOUDOUN COUNTY HOSPITAL 

It is largely due to Mr. P. Howell Lightfoot's efforts and untiring interest that the Loudoun County Hospital became a fact in 1912. It was founded then as the Leesburg Hospital in the big brick house on Market Street, the former Garrett residence.

But soon the need for expansion became urgent, and the men and women of the county pooled their energies and resources in raising money for a new hospital, which in 1930 became known as the Loudoun County Hospital, Incorporated. Up to date, it is now a twenty-five bed hospital, comprised of four wards and six private rooms, a nursery of eight bassinets, five children's beds, a delivery room, a modernly equipped operating room, a complete X-ray room and machine, and a Nurses' Home.

THE THOMAS BALCH LIBRARY 

In the purity of its Early Republic or Jeffersonian style of architecture, the Thomas Balch Library, in Leesburg, is surpassed by no other building in Loudoun County. A filial memorial to Thomas Balch, one time resident of Leesburg, the library was incorporated December 11, 1918, and now, unusually rich in biographies and reference works, boasts some ten thousand volumes. The building was donated by the sons of its namesake, Edwin Swift Balch and Thomas 

Willing Balch, of Philadelphia.

Ten years before the donation, however, Mrs. William C.

The Chronicle is a sporting chronicle of hunting, horse show, hunt racing and breeding interests, covering not only Virginia, but a much wider field. It also carries news concerning the social activities of the hunt set in Middleburg and surrounding communities. 

The paper is published in a quaint little building known as the Bishop's House, which has an interesting history. 

Improvements have been made to the one hundred and fifty year old structure; however, the quaint ante-bellum appearance and atmosphere have been preserved. The white paneled fence about the front dooryard has a hitching ring attached to each post, an ancient brass knocker is used by callers, and the old oak floor boards have been left intact. 

It is said that long before John Brown's raid on Harper's Ferry in October, 1859, a man by the name of Bishop was living in this house, finishing guns that were manufactured at Harper's Ferry. Many of these Bishop guns, it is said, were found later on Southern battlefields.

INNS 

"West's Ordinary", near Aldie, dates back to 1740 when William West established his residence at the junction of the Colchester and Carolina roads. Forerunner of the taverns, inns and hotels, the ordinary was the goal of the weary traveller in Colonial days. In 1748, George Washington mentioned "West's" in his diary and, in 1757, Lord Fairfax was a guest. It is now known as "Lacey's Place". 

Several inns in Loudoun County provide comfort and hospitality for travellers today. Among these are the Leesburg 

Inn on King Street, adjoining the Court House grounds, and the Colonial Inn in Middleburg. 

HOMES 

Other nearby counties may claim fame in thriving industries, busy fraternal organizations or stately public buildings, but there are few purely agricultural counties that can equal the elegance and magnificence of the homes of Loudoun. 

Oak Hill, Rokeby, Belmont, Oatlands, Selma and Raspberry

Plain, and the home of Charles Fenton Mercer, near Aldie, described on other pages, are of historic importance. 

The Wirt House, where William Wirt, born near Bladensburg in 1772, studied law in the office of Thomas Swann, is one of the points of interest in Leesburg. Here Wirt, who later became Attorney General of the United States and is remembered as the prosecutor of Aaron Burr and as the leader of the Anti-Masonic party in National politics, was recommended by the Loudoun County Court as qualified for admission to the bar. Also in Leesburg is the home of Colonel 

Thomas Mott who made a brilliant record in the World War, serving on the staff of General Pershing and, later, on Marshall Foch's staff.

On the pages that follow are pictured a few of Loudoun's beautiful homes-homes of the Old Dominion, known the world around as the abode of hospitality and gracious living.

IRGINIA'S homes and gardens are noted not only for their beauty and charm, but for their historical associations as well. Every 

Springtime visitors come from all over America to wander through the lovely gardens and stately rooms where, in the long ago, lived men and women who played important roles in Colonial days and in the early years of the Republic.

Such is Oak Hill, in Loudoun County, once the home of President Monroe, now the home of Mr. Frank C. Littleton. This beautiful place takes front rank among America's historic homes, for not only was it the Summer White House during the Monroe regime, but it was here, according to tradition, that President Monroe wrote the message to Congress embodying the immortal Doctrine which bears his name.

Oak Hill is situated in a beautiful, rolling country between Leesburg and Aldie, about thirty-two miles from Washington. Nearby is Goose Creek which once bore the name Gohongrestan (the River of Swans). According to tradition, the Indian tribes came here from near and far to hunt the wild fowl whose feathers were of great value in their ceremonial life.

Carolina Road, which passes Oak Hill from North to South, was one of the great Iroquois trails over which the tribes of the five nations passed from their Summer to their 

Winter headquarters.

The house has a perfect setting, surrounded by a grove of magnificent oaks, with the Blue Ridge Mountains not far distant. It is an imposing structure built of brick which were all burned on the place, while all the inside woodwork was cut out by hand. Massive Doric columns, thirty feet high, impart an imposing appearance to the front of the mansion. Thomas Jefferson designed the house which was built by Captain James Hoban, who worked on the reconstruction of the White House and the Capitol after the War of 1812, and the five large columns on the south side are typically Jeffersonian. So far as is known, "Oak Hill" is the only private house outside of Washington which is the result of Mr. Hoban's work.

The many spacious, attractive rooms are a veritable museum of choice pieces of furniture which belonged to the 

Monroes, Washington, Jefferson, John Adams, Madison, George Mason, John Marshall, Daniel Webster, Nelly Custis and other famous Americans of that early period. The collection includes porcelains made for Louis Phillipe. In the dining room and library are pieces designed by Stanford White. Most important of all are the exquisite marble mantels in the East and West Drawing Rooms, ordered for President Monroe by Lafayette and sent over from France. 

The estate of 1800 acres was originally a part of that vast area known as the Northern Neck, inherited by Lord 

Thomas Fairfax from his mother, the daughter of Lord

Culpepper. It next passed to Robert (King) Carter, and from his descendants to Judge Joseph Jones. Judge Jones bequeathed it to his nephew, James Monroe who, after he became President of the United States, built the house, the foundations being laid in 1817 and the work completed three years later. In 1850 Colonel John W. Fairfax purchased the estate from Samuel L. Gouverneur, President Monroe's sonin-law. In 1870 Colonel Fairfax sold it to Dr. George Quinby of New York, from whom it was purchased in 1885 by 

Henry Fairfax.

Colonel Fairfax inherited a large fortune from his father and prior to the War between the States, lived the life of a country gentleman, entertaining in grand style. He served in the Confederate Army and was thrice promoted for gallantry in action. Henry Fairfax was a member of the Virginia Senate.

It is interesting to note that President Monroe, builder of the mansion and its first occupant, was a member of the first two Virginia State Constitutional Conventions, in 1788 and 1829, while Henry Fairfax, owner of the property from 1885 until his death in 1916, served as a member of the last Constitutional Convention.

Mr. Fairfax was a popular and prominent Virginian. He was a progressive farmer and America's foremost breeder of 

Hackney driving horses. He imported Matchless of Lornesborough from England and the home of that celebrated sire became internationally known. Mr. Fairfax, president of the

Hackney Society of America, had on his farm at one time as many as one hundred and sixty horses in training, broodmares and foals. 

Frank C. Littleton, a Virginian and the present owner of 

Oak Hill, purchased the estate in 1920. He has beautified the place and keeps it in perfect condition. He occupies the mansion and entertains in a most delightful way. Mr. Littleton very courteously opens Oak Hill to the public during Garden Week in Virginia, and hundreds of people from all over the United States visit it each year. 

Mr. Littleton is greatly interested in farming and has been successful in raising beef cattle for market. He also has more than a thousand head of sheep roaming the beautiful, rolling fields.

More than a century has passed since Monroe bade farewell to Oak Hill. But today, as then, an atmosphere of tranquillity envelops the place. Strolling through the spacious grounds where the mighty oaks cast a welcome shade on a Summer's day, one feels it was eminently fitting that he should spend the last years of his life in this peaceful spot, in the shadow of the Blue Ridge. Few Americans have left a mark as indelibly upon the pages of history as did Monroe. He served his country and his State in many capacities and, said his good friend, James Madison, "Few men have made more of what may be called sacrifices in the service of the public." Soldier, legislator, ambassador, cabinet minister and president, his one great idea, from the beginning to the end of his career, was that 

America was for Americans.

James Monroe was born in Westmoreland County, Virginia, on April 28, 1758, the son of Colonel Spence Monroe. 

In 1774 he entered William and Mary College and one year later, at the age of seventeen, joined the Revolutionary forces. 

At the close of the war, he had attained the rank of Lieutenant-Colonel. He was severely wounded at the Battle of 

Trenton and ever after carried the ball in his body. 

After the war he was elected a member of the Virginia Assembly and was a delegate to the Virginia Convention to ratify the Constitution of the United States. He was a member of the Continental Congress and a member of the Committee "which chose the site on the Maryland side of the Potomac for the residence of Congress." He served a term as United States Senator and twice was elected Governor of Virginia. He was appointed Ambassador to France during 

Jefferson's Administration and on May 2, 1803, as representative of the United States, he signed the treaty by which Napoleon, in the name of France, sold to America the whole of the Mississippi Valley for fifteen million dollars. Later, he was appointed Ambassador to England and to Spain. 

Josiah Quincy spoke of Madison and Monroe as "James the First and the Second." They were warm friends throughout many years. When Madison became President, he offered 

Monroe the portfolio of Secretary of State and it was accepted. Later, during the War of 1812, Monroe was appointed Secretary of War.

James Monroe became fifth President of the United States upon his inauguration on March 4, 1817. He was re-elected four years later, and on December 2, 1823, his Message to

Congress contained a declaration based upon this nation's right of self-protection which has been immortalized as "The 

Monroe Doctrine". Dr. Lyon Gardiner Tyler, the historian, appraised it thusly: "The most living State paper outside the 

Constitution is the message of James Monroe announcing the Monroe Doctrine." It would seem that the only State paper of any great importance during his time with which Monroe had no connection was the Declaration of Independence. 

John Quincy Adams declared that "Had Monroe been ten years older in 1776, he would have gone down to posterity as a signer of that immortal document". 

Oak Hill was completed in 1820 and it is safe to assume that it was the Summer White House for the next five years. 

If the walls of the old house could speak, they would tell how history was made there in the early days of the Republic, of the master of those broad acres, and its gracious mistress. 

Mrs. Monroe was Elizabeth Kortwright, the daughter of 

Laurence Kortwright, a member of one of the Knickerbocker families of New York. They had two daughters, Eliza Kortwright Monroe married Judge George Hay, of Richmond.

In his "Life of James Monroe", George Morgan speaks of him as "a stately Virginian, polished in manner, who was always dressed in a dark blue coat, buff vest, small clothes and top boots. He wore a cocked hat of the Revolutionary style and has been called 'the last of the cocked hats', as he was the last of the Presidents to adhere to the fashions of the past century".

Mrs. Samuel Harrison Smith in letters to her family, the 

Bayards, described Mrs. Monroe as "an accomplished woman, with a beautiful face-a tall, graceful person, with an elegant manner". The Monroes were familiar with fashionable life abroad and they introduced in the White House many English forms of its etiquette.

OXCROFT SCHOOL, founded in 1914 by 

Miss Charlotte Haxall Noland, near Middleburg, Loundoun County, Virginia, ranks as the foremost school for young ladies in America. Only about twenty-five girls were enrolled that first year, but the School gained steadily in popularity and soon there was a long waiting list. For years Miss Noland endeavored to limit the enrollment to seventy-five, but the number of applicants became so large that last October the twenty-fourth academic year opened with the names of nearly one hundred on its roster. "Miss Charlotte", as she is affectionately called by the girls, through her charming personality, unflagging energy and sincere interest in young people, has placed her school in the top rank of similar institutions of learning in the country. What she has accomplished makes Virginians, especially those from Loudoun 

County, proud of her. 

Two years before Foxcroft was founded, Miss Noland opened a camp at "Burrland", her mother's home near Middleburg, taking the children of her friends to the country for several weeks during the Summer months. "Burrland" is a large house, but so was the Noland family when the members all gathered there for the Summer. In order to make room for the children of the Camp, sleeping quarters were improvised on the roof of the long, spacious porch which was encircled by a railing. An awning provided shade and shelter from summer rains. The girls kept their clothes and other belongings in closets and wardrobes in the rooms lead ing to the roof, and at night stepped through the windows to sleep on their cots. The boys of the Camp had tents in the yard.

Such good times as these little city children had, for Miss Noland always was thinking up something more and more interesting for them to do. There were horseback riding, driving in buggies and carts around the coutryside and into Middleburg for the mail, damming Little River for a swimming hole, riding on hay wagons, sliding down hay stacks, picnics, marshmallow roasts, hay rides and any number of other fascinating things to do. 

Wallis Warfield, of Baltimore, now the Duchess of Windsor, was one of the first year girls at the Burrland Camp, and the first time I saw her she was helping dam Little River for that swimming hole, working hard-throwing in two rocks to one of each of the other little girls. When it was finished, the children had a marvelous time swimming there—no matter if the bottom was muddy and sometimes leaches stuck to them! It was all a part of the fun. and presented it to the Alumnae, ownership to take effect at her death.

The academic standard is high. In addition to the School's examinations, a girl must pass two College Boards in order to qualify for a diploma.

Foxcroft is a country school, situated in a neighborhood particularly suited to a healthy, out-of-doors life. The beautiful Piedmont Valley in Northern Virginia, about thirty miles long and twelve miles wide, is bounded on the north by the Potomac River and on the east by the historic Bull Run Mountains. On the west the Blue Ridge Mountains run south until they converge with a group known locally as the "Cobblers". Foxcroft School is located near the middle of this valley, on an estate of six hundred acres, from which country produce and supplies are obtained. Forty-six miles from Washington and fourteen miles from the nearest railway station at The Plains, the school's closest contact with town life is the historic and picturesque village of Middleburg, four miles away.

As agriculture and stock grazing are the main industries of the Piedmont region, the country has preserved a rural aspect. 

There are large estates, an abundance of streams, great green fields of blue grass and large, splendid woods. The chief diversions of the inhabitants are fox-hunting, shooting, fishing and riding. The region, conceded to be the best foxhunting country in America, has five packs of hounds and one pack of beagle hounds.

As you drive through the entrance gates and on up the driveway to the door of Foxcroft, an old brick house, you have the feeling that you are visiting a lovely home. It is the natural home life the girls have there that makes them love the school and brings them back year after year to see the place, and to visit their beloved "Miss Charlotte". The place also possesses the charm of historic interest, for, according to tradition, here the father and mother of George Washington spent many happy days. It played its part, too, during the War between the States for Mosby, the great Confederate raider, made it his headquarters, and around it many skirmishes took place. 

The Brick House, the main building at Foxcroft, was the first brick mansion erected in Loudoun County. The exterior has been carefully preserved, while the interior has been thoroughly remodeled and attractively furnished with Colonial furniture. It contains the reception rooms, dining rooms, teachers' sitting rooms and offices. 

The Porch and Wing House, close to The Brick House, is where the first year girls live. This is a long, low building in the form of a half-square and looks out over gardens and tennis courts, with a view of the mountains in the distance.

OUDOUN COUNTY always has been one of the greatest horse centers in the United States. Foxhunting, horse shows, race-meetings, polo games, horse sales, horse breeding farms—everything pertaining to the horse is of vital interest to the people that live in this beautiful section of the State. 

In the past twenty years a large number of persons interested in these sports have come from other parts of the country to make their homes and to enjoy the life. 

Fox hunting has been an important part of life in Virginia, and especially Loudoun County, since George Washington as a young man hunted with Lord Fairfax of "Greenway

Court". It is said that Lord Fairfax, a true sportsman and keen follower to hounds, came from England to live in Virginia about 1736, to enjoy a quiet life and foxhunting. 

When George Washington was twenty years old, an engineer surveying the present road that runs from Winchester to Alexandria, he spent much time with his friend, Lord Fairfax. Among their many mutual interests was their love of horses, hounds and hunting. Forty years later, George

Washington was still enjoying foxhunting at Mount Vernon, where he kept the best hounds for that sport. He would take his hounds far afield when after the fox, and did much hunting in Loudoun County. 

This part of the country in latter years has been called "The Leicestershire of America". In Loudoun County are two packs of organized hounds and a number of private hounds are kept. The Middleburg Hunt, going out in season three times a week, turns out more riders, perhaps, than any other hunt in America. Mr. Daniel C. Sands, for years

M.F.H. of Middleburg Hunt, now has a joint-master, Miss 

Charlotte H. Noland, and at the opening meet, November first, when hounds meet at Benton, the home of Mr. and Mrs. 

Sands, and at the Thanksgiving meet, always held at Foxcroft School, there are sometimes several hundred riders to start off on a morning's hunt. And a beautiful sight it is!

Mounted on thoroughbred horses and wearing the smartest riding togs, they follow the joint-masters down the driveway to jump into a field, and are then away for a run. 

Middleburg Hunt is the largest and most important in Loudoun County. It was organized in 1906 and Mr. John Townsend was the first master. The next year Mr. Percy Evans was elected M.F.H. Mr. Samuel Fred of Middleburg was the third master.

Mr. Sands, the present master, has held that office for over twenty-five years. He has raised a pack of hounds that take first prizes all over America; and he has done more for foxhunting than any other man in Virginia. The past few years, Miss Noland has been joint-master with Mr. Sands.

Mondays, Thursdays and Saturdays. The "Fields" are large, the sport of the best, and Middleburg Hunt is one of the most popular in the State of Virginia.

Leesburg, the county-seat of Loudoun County, is so placed by the conditions of Nature that it is one of the greatest fox hunting centers of America. Situated in the heart of a beautiful hunting country, easy to reach from Washington, from which it lies about thirty-five miles northwest, on the banks of the Potomac River, it offers many attractions to the hunting man. 

Always a country of famous riders, horsemen and fox hunters, in 1894 a number of the residents organized the Loudoun County Hunt Club. Among them were Mr. Arthur 

Mason Chichester, Jr., the Club's first President; Mr. William 

A. Metzger, the first Secretary; Mr. E. B. White, Mr. Henry 

Fairfax, Mr. William C. Eustis, Mr. George Carter, Mr. David B. Tennant, Mr. William Heflin, Mr. Henry Harrison and a number of others. They had great sport, which was enjoyed thoroughly by the members. In 1905 Mr. Tennant resigned the mastership, greatly to the regret of everyone, and Mr. David B. Stevenson was elected in his place. The new master's term of office was very short, as he found himself forced to move to the North for business reasons. During his mastership a drag pack was also maintained and used up to November first, before the crops were harvested.

During the Summer of 1906 Mr. William Eustis, who was then Acting M. F. H., bought the pack of Piedmont 

Foxhounds from Mr. Richard Dulany and presented them to the Club; and a few months later, Mr. Westmoreland 

Davis, who had recently purchased the large and beautiful estate, Morven Park, near Leesburg, was elected M. F. H. 

Mr. Davis set to work to make Leesburg the great hunting center which he had always hoped it would be. 

Mr. Davis bought hounds from Mr. Bywaters of Culpepper, Virginia, and Mr. Heflin, a devotee of the sport, from 

Loudoun County, who kept a private pack, donated five of six couple to the Club. Hounds went out two days a week during the season of 1906 and were hunted by Robert Dodd, with George Glascock whipping in to him.

RACE MEETINGS 

Horse races have been held in Leesburg for many years. 

Back in the days of the horse and buggy there would be coaching parties that would come for many miles and stay for days to take in the events. Vehicles of all kinds and people on horseback would line the roads-everyone would get there, just as they do today with the automobile. 

Race Meetings have been held at Middleburg for the past nineteen years; and now, twice a year, about the middle of 

April and again the middle of November, The Middleburg Hunt Racing Association, Inc. runs six races each day over the beautiful Glenwood Course on the estate of Mr. Daniel C. 

Sands. The steeplechases run there over the timber and brush courses draw the best jumpers in the country, and the hardest riding amateur jockeys; and among the thousands of spectators one sees the smartest looking and most representative tors one sees the smartest looking and most representative members of the sporting world.

Both the spring meeting and the fall meeting are very gala occasions, drawing sportsmen from all parts of the United 

States. No lovelier spot could be found than the Glenwood

Course, a natural course of rolling green grass fields. The 

Course was laid out some years ago by Mr. Louie Leith, of 

Middleburg, a horseman and sportsman who has no peer in Loudoun County. He was assisted in laying off the course by Mr. William Hulbert. 

The last meeting was held on Saturday, April 13th, when six good races were run. The Twentieth Annual running of the Middleburg Cup Steeplechase, about four miles across country over a flagged course, was the feature event of the day. It was won by Black Sweep, a horse hunted, schooled and trained in Loudoun County by Turner Wiltshire, of Middleburg. Black Sweep won the same race last spring, when he was owned by Mr. Wiltshire. He sold the horse to Mrs. Frank M. Gould, of New York, and this year 

Black Sweep won the race for her.

HORSE SHOWS 

The late Colonel Richard Dulany, of Welbourne in Loudoun County, started the first horse show in America—the 

Upperville Colt and Horse Show-in 1853. Colonel Dulany, considered the best judge of horses back in those days, was called upon one day by two local horse breeders to decide an argument between them as to which owned the better of two yearlings. He selected a spot in a shady grove of oak trees on his place to decide the question, and one June morning the two colts were brought there for the matter to be decided. About a hundred persons who had heard the colts were to be judged, arrived and so much interest was shown in the decision the Colonel was to make that after it was over he told them that the following June he would give prizes for the best colts they would bring to the same spot. 

In that way he stimulated their interest in good breeding of horses. Every year since, except for the four years of the and Horse Show has been held the second week in June in

War Between the States-1861 to 1865-the Upperville Colt and Horse Show has been held the second week in June in the same beautiful grove of large oak trees where the two yearlings were brought that June day so long ago. 

Always the most interesting classes to real horsemen at this horse show are the classes for thoroughbred colts and fillies, the largest purse and best prizes are given in these classes. Here, the public sees for the first time yearlings that are taken to Saratoga in August for the sales and from there go out into the world to become some of the best race horses in the United States. The men of this section always have been interested in well bred horses, and to their sons (and daughters, too,) the knowledge they have acquired is handed down from generation to generation. In Loudoun, where there are many horse breeding establishments, you can find the very best horse flesh in America.

FAUQUIER-LOUDOUN POLO CLUB 

The Fauquier-Loudoun Polo Club was organized in 1920 by Mr. William P. Hulbert, who came to Middleburg and bought a home here before the World War. He organized the Club and was elected president and manager (offices he has held ever since) and coached the players, making many excellent players. To Mr. Hulbert should be given the praise of the very good polo here down the years, games that have been of great interest and which has been throughly enjoyed by everyone in the surrounding country. 

The first meeting was held at "Belvoir", the home of the late Mr. Fairfax Harrison, near The Plains. Mr. Harrison was chairman of the meeting. Mr. Raymond Belmont, at that time an International player, was a prime mover in starting the club. He played on the first team they had and for years was one of the best players here. Mr. William Skinker was another greatly interested in polo and was one of the top players on the first team. Mr. Hulbert built a field on his farm, where he coached the players and where they had many practice games. Later, that first season they played games on the field that had been built by the late Mr. Jack 

Swan, some years before when he had coached the local men and had quite a good team.

The Fauquier-Loudoun Club then built a field on Goose Creek, in Loudoun County, on the Welbourne estate, owned by Mrs. Fanny Dulany Lemmon, who gave the club the privilege of playing there. Among those first players besides 

Hulbert, Belmont and Skinker, were the Messrs. John Walker, William Slaughter, Rodney Woodward and Freddy Bryan. Everyone took an interest in watching the games and soon every horseman in the country wanted to learn to play. 

Mr. Hulbert taught them with great patience. As the members increased those seen playing were the Messrs. Daniel 

C. Sands, Turner Wiltshire, Richard Kirkpatrick, Baldwin D.

LOUDOUN GOLF AND COUNTRY CLUB 

The Loudoun Golf and Country Club was organized in 1928. A golf course was laid out that year, and since then tennis courts and a swimming pool have been added which make the Club one of the most attractive spots and one of the most popular in the neighborhood. 

The golf course lies directly west of and adjoining Purcellville. This being a natural grass country, the fairways are excellent and the unsurpassed greens of Washington Bent receive praise without stint. The many attractions, together with the large groves of natural oak trees which surround the Club House, make the Loudoun Golf and Country Club property a favorite place for picnics and entertainments. All the fairways here have water hazards and each hole has two tees, so the yardage is different, which makes for greater variety and adds to the number of ways one can drive his ball into the creeks.
