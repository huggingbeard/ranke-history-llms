PREFACE 

"Understanding and solving problems are considered the chief goals of mathematical study." 

This significant statement is found in the new handbook for mathematics issued by the New York State Department of Education. It is typical of the renewed emphasis being placed upon this phase of the work by schools all over the nation. Practically all new courses of study make an effort to stress the importance of problem solving that is accompanied by genuine understanding. There is unanimity of opinion that this is the most difficult goal that confronts junior high school mathematics teachers. 

The great weakness which a large proportion of our pupils show in problem solving may be attributed to the following 

a) Lack of sufficient, varied practice b) No effective system of attack c) Mechanical application of routine methods d) Problems unrelated to pupils' experiences In the past too much time and attention have been devoted to the working of abstract examples. Steele reported in the 

Twenty-ninth Yearbook of the National Society for the Study of 

Education that teachers were spending only fourteen per cent of their mathematics time on concrete problems. Later investigations have shown that from eighty to ninety per cent of the problems found in textbooks are drill material, leaving only from ten to twenty per cent of problems dealing with concrete situations. 3

PREFACE 

This book has been prepared to help remedy the situation. 

The authors believe that if teachers would reverse the ratio of time now spent on drills and concrete problem solving the results would be both gratifying and beneficial. Over two thousand problems suitable for junior high school classes have been included in this volume in order to make it possible for mathematics teachers to offer pupils greater opportunities for additional and more varied experiences in problem analysis and solution.

Unless the pupil has some standardized method of approach to the analysis of problems the results will not be satisfactory. 

This may vary as to the exact system employed by each pupil, but it is the teacher's task to see to it that every member of the class achieves this. The principal import of a sound system is that it will cause the pupil to analyze the problem before rushing into useless computation. Some teachers find it helpful to have pupils write out their analysis of problemsuntil they can demonstrate that this is unnecessary in their situation. The Handbook of the New York State Department of Education offers the following patterns of thinking as aids in analysis and understanding: a) What is asked for in the problem? b) What is given in the problem? c) How shall the facts of the problem be used? d) What is the answer? e) Show by checking that the answer is correct.

Since pupils vary greatly in the nature and extent of their experiences, a gradual approach is very desirable to all new mathematical principles and processes. The problems in this book are arranged in order of difficulty, but the teacher may often find it advisable to begin with problems which are still more simple, and which deal directly with some recent happenings in the child's own living. Tests will be found at certain intervals throughout the book, covering a wide range of subject matter. The median scores are stated at the end of each test and also the number of failures on each problem, prorated for a class of forty pupils.

TO THE TEACHER 

The following method of written analysis has been used successfully in teaching problem solving. The teacher may require this or some similar method. A plan for the problem to be solved should be written out by each pupil until it is evident that a definite and logical analysis is being made. 

The pupil should read the problem carefully before beginning his analysis; then note: (1) what is asked for; (2) what facts are given; (3) how to use the facts in solving the problem correctly.

ARITHMETIC  

It should be understood that you find only gross profit by taking the difference between original cost and selling price. 

To find net profit you must take account of other costs also. 

In the case of a merchant, these include rent, wages, fuel and light, and many other items. What is left out of the selling price, after the original cost of the goods and these other operating costs (often called overhead) have been deducted, is net profit. Gain, as used here means gross profit only. 

In ordinary business, profit is usually reckoned on the selling price; that is, a certain percentage of the receipts is profit. In selling something outside the usual course of trade, or in real-estate deals, profit may be reckoned on the cost price. You should be careful to note which way of reckoning each problem requires.

STOCKS AND BONDS 

Stocks and bonds have two values par or face value and market value. The par value is that which is printed on the face of the stock certificate or bond. The market value is the quotation on an exchange. When the market value is below par the stock or bond is selling at a discount. When the market is above par the stock or bond is selling at a premium. 

Stocks may have any par value, but often $100 is par. Bonds usually have a par value of $1,000 but they may have less and are then called "baby" bonds. If they have more they are called "jumbo" bonds. This book will consider $1,000 par for bonds unless otherwise stated. 

It should be kept in mind that stock quotations are given in dollars and fractions of a dollar while bond quotations are percentages of par value. For example, a $1,000 bond quoted at 96 would cost $960. 

The return or yield, as it is commonly called, on an investment in bonds may be determined in two ways. The better and more accurate way is called "yield to maturity." This means reckoning not only what the holder will receive a year on his investment but also what he will receive when the bond matures or falls due. If he buys in 1938 for $960 a 

4% bond due in 1948, he will receive not only his interest of $40 each year but also, if he keeps the bond until 1948, $1,000 at that time, or $40 more than he paid for it. So really his total yield will be more than the $40 a year interest. 

Calculation of “yield to maturity" involves a complicated mathematical process and bond tables are used to find it, while " current yield," which is merely yield per year, may be found by dividing the annual interest by the cost. 

In these problems brokerage fees for bond transactions need not be considered unless mentioned. Transfer taxes are not taken into account. In stock transactions the table of fees (top of page 84) for brokerage will be used.
