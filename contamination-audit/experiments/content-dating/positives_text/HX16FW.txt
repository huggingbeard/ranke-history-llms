FOREWORD HE FOLLOWING BRIEF COMMENTS upon the vital issues of our times and other matters of equal interest and importance are a reprint of articles contributed to a Montrose (Colorado) daily newspaper during recent years under the general heading of "OBSERVATIONS." These comments are selected from a file of considerably larger volume and are reprinted with only a few slight alterations. 

In some of them there may occur reference to others not included here. Some of them concern merely local matters; others deal with speeches, articles, or events of a few years back. They do not, therefore, make up into an ideal presentation of principles or argument in logical sequence. But all deal with vital issues which command the attention of all thinking people in our distraught world today.

Truth in a new setting is always welcome, and I trust that that which follows will touch a responsive chord in the hearts of some lovers of truth and lend inspiration and hope amid the general atmosphere of gloom and despair, for, while the present is dark with evil forebodings, a morning of light and truth, of blessing and joy, is to follow this night of trouble and darkness.

"THINKING IN THE CLOUDS" SHORT TIME ago an esteemed townswoman stopped me on the street and said, "Mr. Hartman, I read what you write for the paper and enjoy it very much, but it is all up in the clouds, nothing down on the ground." I take it that she meant by this that the things I write are on too high an ethical plane to appeal to the ordinary mind; they are too spiritual to be practical in our everyday affairs. 

Perhaps there are others who think similarly, and I want to take this occasion to say a few things under this head. I have no apology to offer for writing things of a spiritual nature. 

I believe there are quite a number of people who appreciate a column of this kind in the paper. The response that has come in expressions of appreciation has convinced me of this and encouraged me to continue. 

We live in a very troubled world, a very confused world. And the fundamental reason for this trouble and confusion centers in morals. The greatest problem perplexing the world is that of the more equitable distribution of the good things of life. It is evident that we do not lack for physical equipment or mental capacity to solve this problem easily and effectually. The only element lacking is love. Human nature is too selfish and grasping ever to be able to distribute things equitably. The strong and keen-witted dispossess the weak and more simple-minded. All

In human history attests this fact. spite of all that science and research have done for us we are no better than our fathers. Our present civilization is in a tailspin and threatened with destruction.

If things are ever to be different, if there is a scintilla of reality in the things for which men have lived and hoped, sacrificed, suffered, and died, if human beings are ever to dwell together in love and harmony, if gladness and joy are ever to supplant fear and despair, the source of such realization must be from above. 

It must be altogether divine. It must be because a God of infinite mercy and love begets within the human breast a new life and spirit. 

Therefore, in a time like the present, when the foundations under our feet are crumbling and fear takes hold upon the hearts of men, it is essential that we look up to the only source of hope and salvation. It is vitally important that our hearts be stayed by an assurance that abides when all visible comforts forsake us. 

Such an assurance is possible; it is for all those who forsake the vanities and follies of things visible and transient for the enduring things of spirit. 

It is my business to point men upward and away from the turmoil and strife about us to that great source of life and truth. It must be my very life and meat to witness concerning the surety of a "world to come" "wherein dwelleth righteousness” and

to appeal with all fervency and persistence to all men everywhere to stop in their mad rush for money an1 pleasure and give to these matters the consideration due them. 

If someone says to me, you write of things up above us, of things up in the clouds, I must confess that I thank him for the compliment, for it is on a plane much above the world that I myself want to think and live, and from what little experience I have had at it I find the atmosphere so invigorating and the outlook so altogether grand and glorious that 1 want everybody to come on up and share it all with me.

Man is a thinking animal, not only a reasoning and thinking animal, but one possessed of a moral sense; he discerns between right and wrong. He is so much superior to the brute creation by these characteristics that his plane of living should be much higher. But the animal instincts have thus far predominated in prompting man's course, his chief occupation being the seeking of those things which minister to his physical needs and pleasures. 

Because of this dual nature of man there has been an irrepressible conflict, within the breast of every son of Adam, between the fleshly instincts and the spiritual promptings of conscience. To live primarily for present sense pleasure tends to degrade the mind and deface the image of God in man, while to restrain sense desires and cultivate the higher instincts and qualities tends to enrich and ennoble the life and bring the true and lasting joy which alone can satisfy the heart's longings. It is the attitude that the individual takes toward the temptations and besetments of the flesh which determines whether the course of his life shall be downward toward degradation and enslavement to fleshly desires, or upward toward God and the glorious liberty of his children. The greater the freedom of rein he grants to lust, the more rapidly will he be carried downward to the depths of degradation and despair, and the more assiduously he gives himself to the cultivation of the moral qualities of his nature the more rapid will be his progress upward toward the true heaven of the overcomer. 

Few people have an adequate conception of the capacity of the spiritual sense for training and cultivation. We all have read how the blind cultivate the sense of touch so that they are able to read by passing the fingers over slightly raised points. 

The ear likewise and the eye can be trained to discern nicety of distinctions. But the spiritual sense has a greater capacity for training and development than any of the physical. 

As one cherishes the right and heeds the "still small voice" of conscience his heart is made capable of keener discernment of right. He ascends continually to a higher plane of moral attainment. Passing on from the subjugation of the grosser forms of lustful indulgence, he finds a host of more spiritual enemies seeking to hinder his progress. There is fear, worry, pride, and a lot of other outcroppings of the natural man which are to be subjected to the will of love. 

There is a scripture which says. "They that wait upon the Lord shall

It is the resistance of the air on the wings of the eagle which enables him to rise from the cliff and ascend into the clouds. Likewise it is the resist- ance afforded by apparent enemies and those circumstances in life which seem to be against us by which we rise to the higher realms of spiritual attainment. It is a very high realm of glory and virtue to which God has called his earthly children. Let us not hesitate, then, to use our wings and ascend into the clouds.

Best Governed Whom Least Governed? 

T HAS BEEN said that those peo- IT ple are best governed who are least governed. Whether this is true or not depends upon the character of the people themselves. If they are a people of high moral character, they are best governed when least governed, but if they are criminally inclined, they are best governed when most governed.

If we can picture in our imagination a commonwealth of unselfish people, those who live the Golden 

Rule, we shall see a nation without courts of law and jails and sheriffs and soldiers and battleships, a very considerable portion of what we call government. On the other hand, if we can picture a nation where almost every individual is out for what he can get for himself irrespective of the means used or of the rights and interests of others, we must include as a necessary part of this picture a very powerful centralized government with every means available in the way of policemen, jails, and officers of law and force whereby to restrain the evil deeds of men. Otherwise such a people would soon destroy itself from the earth.

All this is so self-evident that it needs only to be stated to be instantly acknowledged by all as being the truth. Civil government has been instituted primarily for the protection of human society from those of its members who seek their own inter-

ests and pleasures irrespective of the rights of others. Therefore the higher the moral standard of a people the less and simpler is the task of civil government, while the lower the moral standard the greater the requirement for law and courts and all manner of means of restraint to protect society. 

I say, "means of restraint," for it is not possible for civil government to use any other than force as a means to accomplish her ends.

All laws are made effective by providing some manner of punishment for disobedience. It is the fear of punishment which restrains the individual from the overt acts of wrong. 

Civil government, not being able to use any other means than force, can never reform the individual or elevate the moral state of society. This is because no one is better in the true sense, that of moral worth, for being restrained from doing what is in his heart to do. What is in his heart to do is that which measures what a man is, in the scale of moral values. 

What he is forced to do by law and punishment, however necessary and worth while for the welfare of society, does not make him a better man. 

Only by a power above and beyond that of man himself, which captivates his heart and mind, which wins his glad and willing obedience, and by its love transforms his inner nature, can the individual, and finally human society, be reformed and perfected and the world be made the desirable dwelling place for which men in all ages have hoped.

Prohibition 

IN THINKING thru the problem of prohibition it is well to have distinctly in mind the boundary line between the realm of civil government and that of religion. Civil government has jurisdiction over our outward acts. Its business is to see that we do not openly violate our neighbor's right of life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. Religion has jurisdiction over secret thought and motive and seeks to purify these of all selfishness and guile. harbor ill-will against his neighbor or desire revenge against an enemy, but so long as he does not permit these thoughts to bear fruit in action the state cannot touch him. But religion enters here with its conviction of right and does not allow peace to abide in that life until surrender to right is made.

Theoretically, therefore, the state should have nothing to say about what we eat or drink, as these are very private matters about which we may do as we choose. But practically it has been found that when a man imbibes too freely of alcoholic liquor he becomes a menace to society. He is quite likely to go home and beat up his wife; he may pick up a gun and do devilish work with it that he would not think of doing when he is sober; he may menace the lives and property of others in many ways. He put the lives of those who were with him and of those in every car he passed that afternoon in jeopardy.

Exit Prohibition 

T LAST the day so much desired by many and so greatly dreaded by others has arrived; it is here, the day of beer. And has there been enthusiasm? Some drove down to Read and others to Grand Junction in order to have it as soon as it was available. People have acted like a lot of children on circus day. I am surprised at the enthusiasm over beer's return. This enthusiasm was largely due, no doubt, to a sudden sense of freedom after having been bound about by legal restrictions for so long, and it will quite naturally abate as we become accustomed to beer on the daily program. 

The hilarity and zeal over beer prove what a failure it must ever be to seek to regulate men's lives by hard and fast rule, to try to make them be good by law. It is just human nature to want to do the thing the rule says you should not do, and especially is this true when the rule relates to some indulgence which is not considered very much of an infraction of the moral law. There are many people who see no more harm in taking a glass of beer than in drinking a glass of water, and they resent the effort to keep them from this pleasure as a piece of damnable officiousness. Therefore they have broken away from restraint and are in for a good time.

Material Possessions vs. Character 

F ALL the lessons which the depression has served to point cut there is none more worth while considering than that of the vanity, the comparative worthlessness, of all material possessions. Only just yesterday, it seems, so many of us were well secured in the comforts and many of the luxuries of life, and possessions sufficient to make the continued enjoyment of them reasonably certain for the remainder of the natural span of life. And Mr. Hoover and others were leading us to believe that we were nearing the time when poverty would be abolished. Everyone would have abundance of the good things of life, and the world would move on in unbroken prosperity and progress thruout the ages. How pleasing it all was to our natural desires. Like the Romans we were saying, "Give us bread and games forever." How we do love prosperity and ease.

But now our cherished program has been completely set aside by a wise and loving Providence. Many who were so well secured in material possessions have actually become de. pendent upon charity. Poverty and distress have multiplied. Everywhere the heavy hand of adversity is felt. 

A real test of the stability of our institutions and of individual character is here. And in this test the only man who is not worried or anxious or perturbed is the man who has been living for character. 

Character is the one thing of real worth. It is the only object worthy the supreme effort of all the powers of your mind, body, and spirit, of your undying enthusiasm. Character will endure; no power of men or devils can take it from you. It is your title to eternal possessions.

But there is no such thing as character without test; there is no such thing as character without suffering. 

The man who lives for character knows in advance that it is by means of trials and suffering that he must climb the ladder to perfection of spiritual attainment. By such means his affections are transferred from the material to the spiritual realities. By such means things spiritual become the more real, the more desirable, and things material, the less so. 

Nothing can be against character or the man who lives for character, neither depressions nor bank holidays nor foreclosures nor bankruptcies nor poor houses. Nay, in all these things he is more than conqueror thru Him

Gambling FEW WEEKS ago I had occasion to go into a drug store in Grand

Junction to have a prescription filled, and while waiting I became intereste 1 in watching a boy, probably about 15 years of age, playing one of those gambling devices where you pull a spring and it shoots a little ball which finds its way around thru various and devious ways into one or another pocket. The lad was on tip toe with interest and anticipation, and there were two or three other boys and a man looking on. He told them about the success he had had playing another machine in some other part of town, and stepped about with an air of expectancy similar to that of the miner who claimed he was within a hundred feet of a.million dollars.

As I stood there watching the proceedings a feeling of sorrow for the lad came over me as I thought of how easily he was being duped by the idea of getting something for nothing, and how fascinating and destructive of character and usefulness the gambling habit becomes. 

But this boy playing this chance game in hopes of winning big returns is a true picture of the way many of us play the game of life. The re sponse on the part of the public to anything with the element of gamble in it is evidence sufficient of how completely men are dominated with the thought of getting something for nothing. We think there is such a thing as being clever enough to get big return from little output. 

But, really, the trick cannot be turned. The cards are all stacked against us. There is no such thing as our getting something for nothing. 

God's great law of compensation has taken care of that. If you expect to get a great deal out of life, you will have to put a great deal into it. You will have to put into it a lot of thinking and sacrifice and faithful, earnest endeavor. You will have to put into it a high and noble purpose and a measure of humility that is willing to be corrected and to learn. There are a lot of things you will have to put into your living if you ever are to get very much out of it. 

Let no one think that he can live easily and carelessly and thoughtlessly, and then expect that life is going to yield him any great measure of real lasting joy and satisfaction.

Robert G. Ingersoll to

FEW DAYS AGO I read an arti cle in a religious journal which spoke very derogatory of Robert G. 

Ingersoll, the outstanding infidel writer and orator of the last century. Among other things this writer said that Ingersoll was "bold, vicious and vulgar."

This may have been more true of him than I am inclined to believe. He certainly was bold in his attempts show inconsistencies in the scriptures, but my impression is that his attacks upon the Christian religion were inspired by his hatred of hyprocrisy and unfair dealing rather than by deliberate malice. He saw so much done and taught in the name of religion which outraged his sense of right and justice that he could but speak out in condemnation of it.

One of the teachings of the church which was particularly repulsive to him was that of an ever burning hell as a reward for the unsaved; and indeed it does require great acrobatic gyrations of the reasoning faculties to reconcile such an idea with that of a 

God of love. Mr. Ingersoll's mind could not accomplish this feat and for this he can scarcely be condemned. 

It is true that he saw the Bible and 

Christianity from only one viewpoint.

He read the scriptures to find the "contradictions" and "inconsistencies" and found plenty of them. He could see only the "Mistakes of Moses," the impurity of David, and the like things all the way thru. The great characters of the Bible appeared to be no different from himself. If he could have been changed into another man and have read the same scriptures with a renewed and spiritualized vision he would have been amazed at their beauty and harmony and captivated by their message. But his mind was blinded to those things probably not altogether thru fault of his own, but partly from a heritage of false teaching and an environment of inconsistent living.

The people of Russia today cannot be condemned for their revolt against religion because for centuries religion with them has been identified with tyranny and oppression. It has meant the enthronement of privilege and the oppression of the poor. They have not had any pure teaching or example worthy the name of religion. Their minds are blinded to the truth and you cannot condemn a blind man for getting muddled in his directions. Can we believe these people completely lost at death to the love and mercy of God?

One of the greatest of Bible characters persecuted the church of Christ and wasted it, and later, after he had seen the light, he said that he obtained mercy because he did it ignorantly in unbelief. Are we sure that Ingersoll did not carry on his campaign against the Bible and religion largely in ignorance and unbelief, and that he will not yet obtain mercy of the Lord?

This is an age of darkness, of ignorance and of misapprehension of God.

DITORIALS like that one from

Not all are blind, not all are corrupt. In every age there have been voices raised against the prevailing iniquity and warnings of the consequences to follow upon the continuance in a wrong course. But what are a few voices against the clamor of the multitude? What can a few individuals do when the very spirit of the age is against them? 

Mr. Hamlin seems to think that the corruption begins at the top and spreads downward, that the source of our trouble is with party leaders and with government. He says, "American public opinion is essentially sound," and he sees a hope, although he admits "it is no large hope," that "the Republican party may find itself" and that "Democratic leaders may be able to wrest their party out of the hands of the political selfseekers." Then might these "old agencies" "be restored as active instrumentalities of representative government." 

That is to say that the American people are sound but they have been betrayed by politicians and parties, and that government has ceased to be representative. If we could wrest the power from those who hold it, the "political self-seekers, and restore representative government, that would be our salvation." 

But would it? Is it not true, rather, that the American people themselves are corrupt, that there is no soundness in our social structure either at the top or bottom, and that a corrupt government and corrupt parties and politicians are truly representative?

Everybody knows that corruption prevails everywhere. It manifests itself in petty political affairs of every community, in over-reaching in business, in wire-pulling for positions and jobs, in petty thievery and misrepresentation, in immorality and crime. 

The truth is that we have corrupt politicians and government because we are a corrupt people. The individual American has corrupted himself and the infection has spread to the home, from thence to the community, and finally to the whole nation. 

The only deliverance and salvation must likewise begin with the individual. Let every man, instead of enlarging upon the corruption of the world about him, turn his magnifying glass within and endeavor to discover the impurity within his own breast.

In this way only is there hope for the nation or the world. All life begins from the center and proceeds outwardly. Our chief responsibility to our community and to our nation is to seek to square our own individual lives with moral principle.

National Used Car Week 

AST week was National Used Car week, so designated by the Asso ciation of Automobile Manufacturers. 

Retail dealers thruout the country had become so overstocked with used cars, very many of them repossessions, that it became necessary to move some of them. Therefore an intensive drive was put on to sell used cars. No doubt this condition has been brought about by overselling of new cars.

Modern business has developed what is considered one of its most important elements, the science an 1 technique of selling. Every manufacturer maintains a corps of experts in the art of persuasion who travel about the country holding sales meetings, training and instructing salesmen. And the work of these experts is augmented by the profligate use of printer's ink and paper with which a constant stream of sales literature is kept flowing out to these salesmen, teaching them and beseeching them to sell! sell! sell!

As a consequence of high-pressure salesmanship very many people have been "oversold." There is a verv considerable number of people who need to be encouraged to save rather than importuned to buy. They are already too much inclined to spend their money before they get it, and for fleeting pleasures rather than for substantial good. Such are not a match for the engaging salesman who comes along with such a fine car or some equally desirable piece of home equipment, who asks only a small sum to bind the sale and the easiest of "easy" payments, whose one interest and thought is to sell, and who is, in all probability, working for some substantial prize to the man who makes the largest volume of sales in his district. 

Yes, I say, many people have been importuned to buy when they should have been encouraged to save. In the long run the interests of the salesman and of the purchaser are identical, and when the salesman importunes the prospective customer to buy, when he should encourage him to save, he is helping forward that process which culminates in disaster both to himself and to his customer.

To the customer there comes discouragement and bitter disillusionment; to the salesman, loss of employment. 

You just cannot continue forever overselling people. The time arrives eventually when all the prospective customers have become disillusioned purchasers and the dealer is overstocked with repossessions. And this brings business to an impasse. 

Your neighbor's interests are your interests. If you are not considering his interests when you are selling

Sunday Movies 

INDLY allow me thru the columns of The Press publicly to congratulate Mr. Frantz on the restoration to him by the city council of the privilege of exercising the right to conduct his moving picture business on Sunday. I say "privilege of exercising the right" because the right has been his all along. It is one of those "inalienable rights" which "all men" receive from the Creator and which the founders of our nation sought to secure to every citizen of the republic.

But Mr. Frantz has been denied the exercise of this right all these years because a few well-meaning but misguided religious people, thinking that the ends of religion can be furthered by law, persuaded the city council to outlaw this source of pleasure on Sunday that people might be turned from the picture show toward the church. 

Insofar as civil government is concerned, that which is right on one day of the week is right on every day, and that which is wrong on one day is always wrong. If the movie picture is sufficiently within the realm of respectability to be shown at all under the law, it is equally so on every day of the week.

It is the religious nature of Sunday that makes it of interest to religious people that those who are not religious shall do differently on that day.

But under the constitution of the United States, and even under God, a man has the right to be religious or not, as he pleases.

The first amendment to the constitution says that congress shall make no law respecting the establishment of religion or prohibiting the free exercise thereof. This is simply an acknowledgment by the founders of our nation of the evident truth that any service rendered to God which is not from free choice is valueless. They had clear enough vision to discera the distinction between the realm of religion and that of civil government, and the baneful consequences which must follow when one encroaches upon the other.

There was a time when the church embraced all human activity. Her will was law in the state, in the church, and in the home. But that was the darkest time of the earth's history. Do we want to turn our steps backward toward those dark days?

Tho true religion be the great need of the hour, and tho the most of our moving pictures are demoralizing in their influence, yet no good can come from a religion that seeks to pass laws to keep people away from worldly amusements. If the things of God and religion are not transcendently the best and most glorious, the loveliest and most to be desired in the world; and if their power of attraction is not sufficient to captivate the heart and mind and to draw men away from the fleeting vanities, the unsatisfying pleasures of the world, then they are nothing at all, and all the laws we may pass to take the place of that power can do no more than make hypocrites of us.

It is difficult to convince the ordinary mind that it is wrong to conduct a movie in a theatre on Sunday and right to conduct one in a church, with no other grounds for distinction than that the one is free and the other is not. Manifestly the council has done the wise and consistent thing by extending the privilege to all alike, and for that is to be commended.

Mother's Day

ERHAPS it is not too late to jot down a few observations regarding Mother's Day recently passed. 

This special day of remembering our mothers seems to have become quite a permanent institution. All our churches have special Mother's Day services, and most people seem to think that they have not done quite right by Mother unless they buy her flowers or a present or send her a telegram or a greeting card.

Now, all of this must have grown out of an inward sense of remissness of duty toward our mothers. What life and love had failed to do day by day, we have proposed to do in a great show at a set time. This might have been very fine as a spontaneous outburst of enthusiasm for Mother a time or two, but when it fastens itself upon us as a custom, a constantly recurring observance, it loses whatever vital significance it ever had.

The fact of our making so much of Mother's Day is not an evidence of growing love and regard for Mother, but quite the contrary. It has been written down in prophecy that people in the last times would be "without natural affection." How often do we hear it remarked how light is the tie which binds families together now-adays; also how motherhood is rather shunned, many women preferring a life of ease and pleasure rather than to assume the responsibilities of a family.

When one considers the actual condi ions in modern society he is led to think these days of special devotion and honor to be largely savored with hypocrisy. He is reminded of what Isaiah wrote: "Bring no more vain oblations; incense is an abomination unto me; the new moons and sabbaths, the calling of assemblies, I cannot away with; it is iniquity even the solemn meeting. Your new moons and your appointed feas.s my soul hateth; they are a trouble unto me; I am weary to bear them." 

Let no one think that he is duty bound to do anything especially diferent for his mother on Mother's Day than upon any other day. We should assert our liberty and not allow ourselves to be brought into bondage to these various customs which are only matters of tradition, highly commercialized, entirely lacking of any vital significance. 

And let not any mother think that she has been forgotten or neglected because she has not been the recipient of special favors upon Mother's Day.
