CHAPTER I The Establishment of The College 

Limestone College is the oldest institution for higher education of women in the state of South Carolina and with the exception of Wesleyan College at Macon, Georgia, and Judson College in Alabama, it claims to be the oldest one in the entire South. It is located one and one-fourth miles from the depot in Gaffney, South 

Carolina, in a lovely and romantic spot.

The rare beauty, the splendid climate and the medicinal qualities of the Limestone Springs attracted wide attention in the early history of our country. It was noted throughout the South and more especially in South Carolina, long before the railroads, the telegraph, the automobile and the flying machine had brought the people of our country and the nations of the world so close together.

I quote from the Calciid, the Limestone College Annual of 1920. "Indian Legends still cling around the loveliest spot in Cherokee-the site of the beautiful Dogwood Springs of Revolutionary days, the Limestone 

Springs of Confederate history. Interesting legend, thrilling folklore and inspiring fact have woven a delightful romance about the historical Limestone Springs. 

As delightful as these are to the Limestone girl of today, they were not the chief attractions to the people of the state in 1835. The beautiful site, which has an elevation of 1,000 feet above sea level, and is situated in the Piedmont Belt amid the rolling lands that extend southward from the Blue Ridge, dividing the waters of the Pacolet and the Broad Rivers, and has as fine a climate all the year round as the continent affords-this

was the cause of the erection of a summer hotel (now 

Cooper Dormitory) by a joint stock company in 1835."2

This building cost the company between sixty and seventy-five thousand dollars. They borrowed $10,000 from the South Carolina State Bank. And for several years the beauty and the culture of our grand old state strolled down the stately avenue (now Curtis Avenue) of gigantic water-oaks which led to a grove of romantic sylvan beauty (our recreation grounds and athletic fields), or sat upon the beautiful green terraces and chatted beneath the stately elms and water-oaks of the hotel place (our College Campus), and looked out from their quiet seclusion upon bold and magnificent surroundings."3

The hotel building was two hundred seventy-four feet long, forty feet wide, having a large dining hall in one end and corresponding drawing rooms in the opposite wing, with small parlors for families. It contained over one hundred rooms and "every office necessary to a large establishment."4 Besides the principal building there were two frame houses, a story and a half high, each containing a parlor, a drawing room and six chambers. There were "nine double cabins, two stories high, containing eighteen chambers."5 The buildings were of the best material. The walls of the main brick building were twenty-seven inches thick and although it has weathered the seasons of almost a century the brick are practically in a perfect state of preservation.

The spacious grounds were laid out in circling walks and broad driveways. Money had been spent lavishly and artistic skill had been employed in making it a place of striking beauty.

In 1844, Mr. Benjamin Wofford of Spartanburg County agreed to give the South Carolina Bank $10,000 for the Limestone Springs property. It was his purpose to establish a college for boys under the direction of the Methodist Church. When the details were being worked out the bank wanted Mr. Wofford to pay the accrued interest on the money loaned to the hotel company. Mr. Wofford refused to pay it and the trade was called off. So near did Wofford College come to being located at Gaffney rather than at Spartanburg.

During this time the town or village of Gaffney did not exist. It grew up after the War Between the 

States. 

In the early part of the century leading up to 1845 there was a felt need for the education of girls in South Carolina. On account of the geographical conditions, there were two sections-the up-country and the lowcountry which produced almost two distinct civilizations. There were men of wealth and influence living along the coast who realized that their standing was due in a large measure to education and culture. Their sons were often sent across the ocean to England, France or Germany to receive the finishing touches to their education. But how about their daughters? Upon them devolved the duties and responsibilities which make and adorn the home.

As a consequence of this feeling a number of "finishing schools" for young ladies were founded in Charleston. To these schools came the girls along the seaboard and from far inland to receive the finishing touches so coveted by men and women of every age. But the

demand grew greater and the circle had to be widened. 

The line of demarcation between the two sections of the state was never more closely drawn than in the period of Nullification, and the state was suffering as a consequence. What better plan to heal the growing breach could be offered than to operate a school in the Piedmont section in which the girls of both sections would receive their education? No better place could be offered for a school of this kind than at Limestone Springs. The wonderful hotel at this place had been a failure and the plant was idle. It was at this time, 1845, that Thomas Curtis, LL. D., who lived at Charleston, and his son, William Curtis, who lived at Columbia, came to Limestone Springs, purchased the property and received titles to the buildings and 300 acres of land by paying $10,000 due the State Bank. F. H. Elmore deeded the interests of the hotel company to the two 

Curtises, father and son.8 

Thomas Curtis and his son William Curtis, distinguished Baptist clergymen, had been born and educated in England. They had come to America about ten years previous to this time. These gentlemen seemed to have possessed considerable means. They made changes that were necessary in the interior of the building and established a school for young women under the name of Limestone Springs Female High School.

The one in mid-summer was the big event of the year and usually took from two to three days. The second term began immediately afterwards, so there was no time lost. Vacations were given in the winter because it would save fuel, and the climate in summer did not become too hot to study with comfort. The planters in the low-country appreciated the opportunity of having their daughters in the delightful climate at Limestone Springs during the hot summer months. 

Then, too, the studies of the young ladies were not interrupted by the Christmas holidays. Vegetables were raised to supply the tables during the summer which was another factor that enabled them to run the school at such a low cost. "The Curtises had a oneacre farm brought up to the highest degree of fertility, upon which they raised immense quantities of fresh vegetables for their tables which could not have been utilized for a winter session."5

Water from the great Limestone Spring was used for most purposes but they obtained freestone water from a spring a quarter of a mile away. This water was brought across the ravine into the kitchen by means of a line of pine logs which had been bored lengthwise and closely fitted together. Captain Griffith said in 1921, "It has not been many years since some of these logs were dug up and found to be in a perfect state of preservation." Iron pipes were either unknown or too costly to be used in the Ante-bellum days.

The faculty was composed largely of Northern teachers while others came from Europe. In equipment the school far surpassed the average schools of this period.

The tone of education was set high and especial care was taken to "cultivate the mind to points of refinement and elegance. CHAPTER III 

Troublous Times, 1859-1873 After his father's death, Dr. William Curtis carried on the work of the institution. For a few years the school was successful, but there were troublous times ahead. A comparison of the course of studies as found in the Curtis period with that found in the Cooper-Limestone Institute is of interest. There does not seem to be much difference as to difficulty. Theology was taught by the Curtises but not in the early period of Cooper- Limestone. Mathematics probably was carried further in the later period.

Music was given special emphasis in both periods. 

However, the earlier period offered the harp which was not taught in the later period. The making of wax fruit and flowers was offered at Cooper-Limestone but not by the Curtises. More of the foreign languages were given in the earlier period and ornamental needlework was stressed also by the Curtises.

The institution was controlled by this Board, which elected the president of the school for a term of years, and upon him devolved the duty of selecting the professors, teachers and other officers without even the approval of the trustees.17

The outstanding personality of the Cooper-Limestone period was Captain Harrison Patillo Griffith. It was he along with Professor R. O. Sams who resuscitated the school in the early eighties. Later the entire burden fell upon his shoulders and he "carried on" in a wonderful manner. It was through his indomitable energy and courage that the school ran on in the face of almost insurmountable difficulties. The building needed repairs and was dying of timber rot. Captain Griffith said, "You may say that Limestone is immortal. If anything could kill it, it would have been dead long ago."18 

Captain Griffith upheld the noble and high traditions set by the Curtises. His pupils loved him almost to adoration. His influence is still a power of good in the lives of those with whom he came in contact.

CHAPTER VIII The Presidents and Other Outstanding Personalities 

Dr. Thomas Curtis was born May 10, 1787, at Wisebeach, England, of sturdy and highly respectable parents. In early life he was eager for knowledge and was soon known for his scholarly attainments. He was mainly self-instructed, but acquired a good knowledge of Latin, Greek and Hebrew. He joined the church in his early youth and became a preacher of the gospel, and later an eminent theologian. He wrote a number of poems of real merit. He was pastor of a Baptist 

Church in London for about twenty-two years. He was, for years, editor of the Encyclopedia Metropolitana and later of the London Encyclopedia, being an associate in this relation to Thomas Coleridge. He was, for a time, employed to revise the King James Version of the Bible and discovered no fewer than ten thousand errors. He gave up this work because he was afraid that it might be injurious to Christianity. After the death of his wife (nee Miss Reynoldson) he moved to the United States, in 1833, with his four children. He landed at Newfoundland barely escaping with his family from the sinking ship. They were forced to swim ashore.

He settled in Maine and was pastor of the Baptist Churches in Augusta and Bangor for about six years. 

Finding the climate too rigorous he moved to Macon, Georgia, in 1839, and became pastor of the First Baptist Church. He afterwards was induced to accept the pastorate at Penfield, Georgia, which was then the seat of Mercer University. In 1841, Dr. Curtis moved to 

Charleston, South Carolina, as pastor of the Wentworth Baptist Church. He served it with marked ability for four years.

In 1845, he, with his son, Dr. William Curtis, came to

Limestone Springs where they established a school for girls. It was here that he did his greatest work. His influence was felt far and near. He was president of the South Carolina Teachers' Association in 1850. 

On January 29, 1859, while returning from a trip to 

Brooklyn, New York, and Baltimore, Maryland, the ship "North Carolina on which he was a passenger burned in the Chesapeake Bay. All passengers escaped except Dr. 

Curtis. His body was never recovered."

He was ordained a Baptist minister at Columbia, South Carolina. The honorary degree, LL. D., was conferred on him by South Carolina College. After the war Captain Petty farmed at Wilkinsville until he became head of the Limestone Springs Female High School in 1874, and held this position until the latter part of 1878. The next year he became editor of the Carolina Spartan and continued in that position until the Spartan was merged with the Spartanburg Journal. He wrote editorials for the papers until about two months before his death, February 23, 1915.

CHAPTER IX Marked Development, 1935-1937 During the years 1935-37, the college made considerable advance in material equipment and development, as well as in educational outlook and arrangement. The stability and educational integrity of the college was being recognized increasingly year by year throughout an ever widening area.

During the summer of 1935 Eunice Ford Hall, a three-story dormitory, was constructed. This hall was arranged in suites, and accommodates fifty students. 

Mr. T. B. Stackhouse and Mr. J. N. Lipscomb were generous contributors to this development. Several of the other citizens of Gaffney also made contributions, among them being Mr. R. E. LeMaster, who, during the years, had a marked interest in the college. Several members of the alumnae association assisted in this much needed addition to the college plant. 

At the time Eunice Ford Hall was built, arrangement was made for a large addition to the rear of Hamrick Hall to take care of the library of the college. The library had been constantly expanding during the years between 1925-35, and adequate accommodation was very much needed. One of the most attractive places on the campus is the library. It has about it a certain air which invites the presence of students and faculty members.

The campus underwent a decided improvement from 1925 to 1937. Thousands of shrubs, trees, and plants were artistically arranged. One of the particular charms of the college has always been the naturalness of the setting. Both the front and back campuses were vastly improved during the period mentioned above. In the summer of 1937 plans were perfected for the widening of the campus, relocating the roads to the right and

left as one approaches the college. This plan brought into the campus of the college between four and five additional acres of land.

A general repair and remodeling program was begun as soon as college closed in the summer of 1936, and continued during that summer. Adequate baths and hall flooring, with new lighting, was installed in East Cooper and West Cooper dormitories. The expenditure involved in this work in 1936 was $20,000.00. This remodeling and improvement program was completed in the summer of 1937 with an expenditure of between $8,000.00 and $10,000.00. In 1937 new flooring was laid in Cooper dormitories, new light fixtures installed, the woodwork painted and the walls papered. Dixie Lodge dormitory was entirely "done over." A beautiful floor was laid in the dining hall of the college, and many improvements in housing accommodations were completed. During the summer of 1937 the entire college plant was put in excellent condition. The college now has adequate first class accommodations for 280 boarding students and for 20 teachers. CHAPTER IX Alumnae Association, College Publications, College Activities 

Limestone Alumnae Association was organized in the spring of 1898 with Miss Mary McKie, a teacher at the time, as the first president. The next year Mrs. 

Clarke Waring, an alumna of the Curtis days, a woman of great charm and a writer of considerable ability, succeeded Miss McKie. The third president was Miss 

S. Margaret Wertz, at that time principal of the 

Seminary Department. 

The Alumnae Association began its activities with many such small favors as fitting up and furnishing an "Alumnae Room" in the college.

After a number of years the Association heeded the pleadings of Miss Wertz to endeavor to raise funds for a pipe-organ. The Association accomplished its first big undertaking by installing the organ costing $3,500.00.¹ 

The Alumnae raised money to endow the Lee Davis 

Lodge Chair of History and the Griffith Chair of Eng lish. In May 1920 they pledged a $15,000 foundation for the maintenance of a trained nurse in the college.2 

The greatest piece of work done by the Alumnae was in 1926 when the Association undertook to raise $100,000 during the Endowment Campaign. They went "over the top" in their assignment and it was their enthusiasm and assistance throughout the State that largely accounted for the success of the whole campaign.

The Limestone Alumnae Association is an organization distinguished for its loyalty to Alma Mater and its unbounded enthusiasm for a greater Limestone.

It contained an interesting short history of Limestone and also the histories of the two literary societies. The book is well written and is a credit as a beginner for Limestone.

Other numbers were issued at intervals, but they have come out each year now for more than a decade. 

They are the ever-changing picture of college life from the students' point of view. 

The Limestone Star began in October 1899. In the first number a great deal is said about the Winnie Davis School of History. This magazine was published regularly by the students until 1929, when the name was changed to The Lantern which is now the monthly publication of the college. 

The Limestone College Studies began about 1900, and numbers came out somewhat irregularly for ten or fifteen years. The publication carried lectures delivered by some member of the faculty or by some distinguished speaker at Limestone. Most of the articles were by 

Dr. Lee Davis Lodge and are masterpieces of literature.
