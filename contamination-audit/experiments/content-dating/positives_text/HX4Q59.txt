FRAFRSEL CARL SCHURZ 

1829 - 1906 "I love my Fatherland; but that does not lessen my love for America."

CAR 

ARL SCHURZ, probably the most emithe m nent American citizen of German parentage, has now been dead over a quarter of a century. His words and deeds have become history, and, with the evidence before us, we can weigh and judge them. In this process, he loses nothing, for his reputation has gained an added luster with the passage of time. His virtues were not ephemeral. Posterity has placed him permanently among the great.

Schurz's contribution to American life had many phases. With amazing versatility, he was soldier, diplomat, orator, lecturer, newspaper editor, senator, cabinet officer, biographer, and business man. Always and everywhere, however, he labored for good government and the improvement of humanity. Public service was the recurring motif throughout his life. He was not perfect. His logic was sometimes faulty, and [3]

he occasionally lost his temper. But no man in the nineteenth century, either in the land of his birth or the country of his adoption, stood more courageously for what he believed to be right, and few displayed more sanity, intelligence, and unselfishness. Schurz was the ideal statesman, blending an enlightened liberalism with a patriotic devotion to the cherished traditions of our republic. Professor Monroe Smith, in 1899, said of him accurately: "He has written and spoken and fought, in the old world and the new, for the great causes of our century, for national unity and individual freedom; and what his youth longed for, his age has seen fulfilled." 

Carl Schurz was born March 2, 1829, in the little village of Liblar, just south of Cologne, in the Rhine Valley. His parents were good but inconspicuous people, who made sacrifices in order to send him to the University of Bonn.

He withdrew, however, in order to take part in the Revolution of 1848,-an outbreak of democratic feeling directed against the autocracy of the Prussian king, Frederick William IV, and aimed at the establishment of German unity [4]

under parliamentary rule. Two romantic incidents of this period brought the youthful Schurz an almost European fame. The first was his dramatic escape through a sewer from the fortress of Rastatt, after that citadel had been captured and his execution seemed inevitable. 

The second was his sensational rescue, in November, 1850, of the patriot-poet, Gottfried

Kinkel, from the penitentiary at Spandau, outside Berlin. The revolution itself was futile, but these two exploits made the exiled Schurz a hero. 

Unable to return to Germany, Schurz, like others of his countrymen, turned to the United States, which seemed to him, as he said, “a nation where everybody could do what he thought best, and where nobody need be poor, because everybody was free." On September 17, 1852, with his bride, Margarethe Meyer, he sailed up New York harbor to the land of which he was to become a foremost citizen. He knew only a few English words, and, in pessimistic moods, the future lay before him "like a mysterious fog bank." But he was so alert and ambitious that he could not long remain obscure. [5]

He deliberately devoted two years to study and observation. Then, equipped in language and knowledge, he settled in Watertown, Wisconsin, where he joined the young Republican Party, slowly entered politics, and developed through experience into a persuasive platform lecturer. Considering his handicaps, his rise was extraordinary. Less than seven years after he landed at Battery Wharf, the German immigrant spoke in historic Faneuil Hall, in Boston, on the subject, "True Americanism." His accent was rapidly disappearing, and he conversed with equal facility in German and in English.

During the presidential campaign of 1860, Carl Schurz took a conspicuous part, not only because of his "terrible sincerity" as an orator but also because of his position of power among German-Americans, whose votes the Republicans needed badly. He was Chairman of the 

Wisconsin delegation at the Chicago Convention, where he supported Seward but approved of Lincoln, whom he already knew and admired. He was a member of the committee named to notify the candidate of his nomination, [6]

n and he later carried on a strenuous speaking schedule throughout the East in Lincoln's behalf. When Lincoln was inaugurated, Schurz was appointed Minister to Spain. Thus the ed once proscribed rebel was transformed into an mambassador to a monarchical court. Schurz's adventure in diplomacy was, however, merely an interlude of calm before the storm of civil war.

Isolated in Madrid while many of his friends were enlisting in the Northern army, Schurz was restless and finally, in 1862, returned home and induced the President to commission him as a Brigadier General. He fought gallantly at 

Second Bull Run (August, 1862), Chancellors- = ville (May, 1863), and Gettysburg (July, 1863); but in each engagement he and his command suffered defeat, although through sheer bad luck rather than through any incompetence or weakness of his. Finally, in the autumn of 

1863, he was shifted to the Army of the Cumberland, where he chafed under inaction. As a soldier, General Schurz did not get along well with other officers. He was a rigid disciplinarian and an inspiring leader under fire; but he [7]

was critical of his superiors, even of President 

Lincoln, and his frank condemnation of policie to which he was opposed did not increase hi popularity with those under whom he was serv ing. He was given leave of absence in 1864 in order to do campaign work for the Republican 

Party. Discharged in the spring of 1865, he re sumed civilian life "with a profound abhorrenc of war as such." Although he was proud of his record, he never capitalized it. He himself seldom used his title of General, and he did not appeal to the soldier vote. He had had enough of war.

Schurz occasionally assailed Lincoln for inaction, but he regarded him as one of the noblest of statesmen and later wrote a brief biography of him which has become a classic. 

During the Reconstruction Period, Schurz was in sympathy with Thaddeus Stevens and Charles 

Sumner rather than with President Johnson and, after a tour of the South as the latter's personal emissary, angered him by making a report hos. tile to his conciliatory policy. In 1867, Schurz moved to St. Louis to edit the Westliche Post and became a citizen of Missouri, from which [8]

en state he was elected United States Senator in ie 1869. His one term in the Senate has been dehi scribed as "the brightest and most useful part of his very useful and interesting life." Unable to endure patiently the ineptitudes and scandals a of the Grant regime, he denounced them scathingly and brought upon himself the wrath of the orthodox Republicans. He was chiefly respon- 

Osible for organizing the somewhat quixotic Liberal Republican movement of 1872, and was : painfully disillusioned when its convention nominated Horace Greeley. As a consequence of his heresy, he lost his seat in the Senate in 1875. His influence was of some significance in the presidential campaign of 1876; and, when

Hayes was officially declared elected, Schurz became his Secretary of the Interior. Thus 

Carl Schurz had attained to the two highest positions open in this country to citizens of foreign birth,-United States Senator and member of the President's cabinet. Carl Schurz had now revealed himself as a reformer, characterized by independence, fearlessness, and honesty. As Secretary of the Interior, he put into operation a merit system for [9]

the civil service, thus defying some of the less idealistic Republican office-holders. He fought tenaciously for justice to the Indian and the Negro, and was successful in abolishing a corrupt "Indian Ring." He incurred the enmity of "grafters" by trying to conserve our natural resources. No Secretary of the Interior ever accomplished more good during his period of service.

Up to this time, Schurz, although occasionally in revolt, had been identified with the Republican Party. In 1884, however, he refused to be cajoled or coerced into supporting James G. Blaine. He had a large share in starting the "Mugwump" movement and promptly declared himself for Grover Cleveland. It was, indeed, largely through Schurz's tireless efforts that 

Cleveland was victorious.

Schurz never again held public office. For a brief period he was editor of the New York 

Evening Post, with Godkin as his associate. He attempted, with only partial success, to enter business as American manager of the Hamburg- American Packet Company. He wrote for the 

American Statesmen Series a biography of 

[10]

Henry Clay, one of the best books of its type to appear in the United States. Through his contributions to magazines and his addresses at popular gatherings, he expressed himself on current questions; and his influence was increased while he was editorial writer for Harper's Weekly, from 1892 to 1898. He opposed free silver, the war with Spain, the acquisition and retention of the Philippines, the protective tariff, and the Boer War; and he advocated without cessation the extension of civil service reform. In 1888 and 1892, he was for 

Cleveland; in 1896, he was for McKinley; in 

1900, he was reluctantly for Bryan; and in 

1904 he was for Parker, or, rather, against Roosevelt. In each campaign he had very positive reasons for his choice. 

Carl Schurz received honorary degrees from

Harvard, Columbia, and Wisconsin. In his later years, he became a municipal or national institution, whose photograph often adorned the newspapers and whose views were awaited with interest. In his old age, he was tall and slender, with an erect bearing acquired in the army. He retained his flowing beard, which lent him a [11]

foreign look, and his noble head was crowned with a mass of grizzled hair. His eyes sparkled whimsically, often satirically, behind steelrimmed spectacles. He was proud, as a man had a right to be who had talked with Prince Bismarck as one statesman to another. Yet he was also very genial and full of fun. In moments of relaxation, he sat down at the piano to play his favorites from Bach or Beethoven, or strolled along the woodland paths of his estate on the shores of Lake George, or indulged in reminiscent chat on the veranda with his intimate friend, Dr. Jacoby. 

Schurz's wife died in 1876, leaving him with four children. His two sons died relatively young, Herbert in 1900 and Carl Lincoln in 

1924. The two daughters, Agathe and Marianne, lived with him, watching him carefully and guarding his health. On May 14, 1906, after a pleasant winter in the South, he contracted pneumonia and died in New York City, his last words being, "Es ist so einfach zu sterben." A great memorial meeting held in

Carnegie Hall later in the year was attended by 

Grover Cleveland, Charles W. Eliot, and many [12]

others. Since then, several monuments have been erected to him, including a bronze statue on Morningside Heights in New York City, dedicated in 1913. 

What were Carl Schurz's political ideals? In the first place, he is one of the finest examples in our history of complete, untrammeled independence of party. "I have never been able," he wrote in 1870, "to look up to a party as a deity that has supernatural claims upon my veneration;" and he declared some years later that "the overthrow of party despotism is the condition precedent of all reform." He loved his country, but not blindly, and he was not sparing of caustic phrases when her policies seemed to him ill-considered or wrong. In the second place, he was a genuine reformer, unabashed and incorruptible. He could not understand how any honest man could defend the "spoils system" in government administration or why any incompetent person should receive an office merely as political strategy. By his open criticism, not to say denunciation, of existing practices, Schurz made enemies, who searched his record for flaws. But he had lived a spotless life [13]

and was as little vulnerable as any statesman of his time. "In his company," asserted Moorfield 

Storey, "one felt that he was breathing a purer air."

Carl Schurz was both scholar and man of action, both thinker and doer,—a rare combination. He could have turned in either direction with entire consistency and ultimate happiness.

That he became a statesman instead of a philosopher was largely accidental. Under other conditions, he might have been a professor at Bonn or Columbia.

No foreigner has ever more thoroughly adapted himself to American methods or become more completely American in spirit. He learned our idiom so that he spoke it better than most natives; he saturated himself in our history and literature; he was concerned with our affairs. 

He loved his Fatherland and used its tongue in the intimacy of his family circle. The way to his heart was often through the Muttersprache. He was not ashamed to be called a "Dutchman,' but he deeply resented being called an alien. In every essential respect, Carl Schurz, the immigrant of 1852, was as thoroughly American as 

[ 14 ]

any member of the Society of Mayflower Descendants. He upheld in theory and in practice all the ideals which we like to think of as American, enlightened democracy, equal opportunity to all, and justice to everybody, regardless of race, creed, color, or financial standing. His contribution to American life can be likened to that of Daniel Webster or Grover Cleveland or

Theodore Roosevelt. 

At the close of his eloquent remarks in dedicating the Schurz Memorial on Morningside

Heights, Mr. George McAneny, his close friend and admirer, said, in words which may well round out his brief sketch of Schurz's career: "A little way up this river, near Sleepy Hollow, there is a knoll from which every prospect is restful and fair; and from which you can watch the water coming down from the hills that stand about his beloved lake. There, so near us, Carl Schurz lies. Like the river, the pure stream of his life has reached the eternal sea. But, like the river, it will still flow on, green-banked in the memory of the people and still enriching all the land." [15]
