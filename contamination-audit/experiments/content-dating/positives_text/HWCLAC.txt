CHAPTER I. 

THE LAND OF THE SOUTHERN SLAVS 

The map of Europe has undergone many changes since the war of 1914-1918. Several countries have had tɔ be renamed, and others have had to be readjusted to altered conditions. Some countries have enlarged their borders, while others have been obliged to reduce the size of their territory in order to fit into this strange jig-saw puzzle. 

Yugoslavia, the Land of the Southern Slavs (which is the literal translation), is one of those countries which has come into its own since the war. Whereas formerly it was divided up and ruled over by foreign 

Powers, in recent years the Slav dominions have been united into one kingdom.

But Yugoslavia is not a new country; it is an old country with a new name. This part of the world has always been inhabited by Slavs, as you will realise later on, when understanding something of its history. 

For many centuries Austro-Hungary dominated Croatia, Slovenia, Dalmatia and other provinces, while Turkey occupied Serbia for five hundred years. Notwithstanding this fact, the Slavs never lost the sense of their nationality. Although they had to submit to being called Austrian, Hungarian and Turkish subjects, it was only an outward form, and in their hearts they remained true to their patriotic ancestors. 

They clung to their own language, in spite of being obliged to speak a foreign tongue, and they never relinquished the customs of their forefathers or their traditions. In the schools, which the Slavs were forced to attend under Austro-Hungarian rule, all the teaching was in German or Hungarian. The Turks, however, provided no means of education for the Serbs, which was even worse. It is only since the middle of the nineteenth century, since Serbia regained her freedom and semi-independence, that schools have been established in this country. 

All the Slavs in Yugoslavia speak a common language that is to say, a language which comes from the same root. Of course each part of the country has its own dialect, but the people can always understand each other. The speach of the Slovenians is the only one that has very little resemblance to that of the Croatians or the Serbians, and yet it is purely Slav in origin.

There are many different provinces in Yugoslavia. As well as

Serbia, Croatia and Slovenia, there are Bosnia and Herzegovina, the 

Voyvodina, Montenegro and Dalmatia. It something like a huge family of brothers and sisters, uncles and aunts, cousins and distant relations, all living together on one estate. Of course there are differences of character, of religion, of opinion, of taste and so on, and they do not always agree on all points. But the common tie of love for their country, and being of the same blood, keeps them united. 

Serbia was a very small country before the war, with only 3,000, 000 inhabitants, but since the Southern Slavs have joined forces the new kingdom has a population of close to 14,000.000 people. 

CHAPTER II. 

A SUMMARY OF SERBIAN HISTORY 

Serbia suffered long and valiantly in the struggle to gain her freedom. She was dominated by the Turks for five centuries, and it was not until the year 1867 that the enemy was driven out of the country and she became an independent principality. During all the long, weary years of submission to a foreign Power, the Serbs never gave up hope, or lost faith that they would eventually win back their beloved country and regain their freedom. 

Can you imagine what it must have been like to live in those times? 

The Serbs were little better than slaves, although living in their own country, and the Turks took away all they could from inhabitants and installed themselves as masters. You cannot wonder that this tyrany roused rebellion. 

On several occasions, in spite of great difficulties and the vigilance of the enemy, the Serbs rose in insurrection; but they failed time after time, owing to the overwhelming numbers fighting against them. But when defeated they picked up courage again, remembering the glorious deeds of their ancestors lɔng ago. The epic poems and ballads which tell of the valour and bravery of heroes in past history are very dear to the heart of the Serb. Through these poems they would often be fired with fresh determination to recover freedom. But the struggle went on from generation to generation, and sometimes their faith must have burned very low.

During the twelfth, thirteenth and fourteenth centuries Serbia was a rich and powerful empire. The lives of some of the great men of that period will stand for ever in the history of this people. Tsar Lazar is perhaps the best beloved of all the national heroes. He was the last of the Serbian emperors and did much good in his prosperous reign. 2

At the Battle of Kosovo, in 1389, he was betrayed by one of his followers and beheaded by the enemy. The Serbs, having lost their leader, were defeated, and the country fell into the hands of the Turks. This tragic event ended the most glorious chapter in Serbia's history, and the anniversary of the great battle-Vidov Dan-is still commemorated throughout all the land.

The memory of Kosovo, and the overthrow of the Serbian Empire, was like a burning fire which smouldered for centuries in the breast of the entire nation, stirring them to revenge. That day was long in coming, and it was not until the year 1912, in the Balkan wars, that Kosovo was recaptured from the Turks. 

In the beginning of the nineteenth century the darkness which had seemed interminable, at last ended, and the dawn broke for Serbia. 

During the year 1804 Kara-George, great-grandfather of the present King of Yugoslavia, inspired his followers to rise and overthrow the Turks. The Serbs managed to smuggle arms and ammunition into their ranks, and a revolution broke out all over the country. After prolonged fighting the Serbs were victorious and the foundation of their independence was laid. However, the struggle was not over yet. 

Some ten years later, in 1813, the Turks made a fierce attack and hurled all their forces against the Serbs and vanquished them. 

Kara-George fled from the country, which was thrown back into slavery and despair.

A couple of years later a new insurrection, known as the Insurrection of Tatovo, was planned under the leadership of Milosh Obrenovitch. 

It ended in a decisive victory for the Serbs, and thereafter the road to freedom lay clearly before them. Serbia now became a vassal state to Turkey, and under the rule of Prince Milosh a new era began. He was succeeded by his son Michael, whose reign was an epoch-making one. 

The Turks, who had held the fortress of Belgrade up till 1867, left the country once and for all on this memorable date. 

Think what it must have meant to the Serbs to get their country back again after all these centuries. But they had to make an entirely fresh start. Imagine a country with no railways, no roads, no canals, no gas or electricity-nothing beyond the bare necessities of life. 

Fortunately the Serbs who had emigrated to Austria and been educated there now hastened to assist the new state in her political organisation and developement. There was everything to be dɔne, and few men who were capable of undertaking the giant tasks which lay before them. 

A great work was carried through during this century by two men, 3

Dossitie Obradovich and Vuk Karadjitch, whose lives were devoted to the education of the masses. Vuk Karadjitch is known as the father of Serbian literature. He preserved the Serbian language by writing a grammar, reforming the spelling, composing a dictionary and publishing many books. For, under the Turks, education had been entirely neglected, and even the Serbian language had become corrupted. Dossitie Obradovitch was a writer and teacher. He travelled in many distant lands, and even spent some time in England. Owing to his efforts a degree of culture and learning was brought to Serbia and the foundation-stone of education was laid. 

The history of Serbia is full of stirring events, and many a chapter bears record of turbulent times. In 1882, under Milan Obrenovitch, the country was proclaimed a kingdom. When his only son Alexander became of age and chose to marry beneath him, King Milan was exiled, and died, an unhappy monarch, in Vienna. King Alexander and his wife were murdered in 1903 by the people who rose in revolt against his misrule. After the tragedy Peter Kara-George was elected successor to the throne. Serbia prospered under his rule, and in the Balkan wars of 1912-1913 defeated the Turks, who had combined with the Bulgars and Greeks.

During the European War in 1914-18 Serbia fought on the side of the Allies, and King Peter took an active part in the campaign. When the Serbian army had to retreat over the Albanian Alps, in the middle of winter, the aged King accompanied his people on their tragic flight. 

But he lived long enough to see Serbia conquer her enemies and the Slavs united into one kingdom. His son Alexander now reigns in his stead, not as King of Serbia, but as King of Yugoslavia. 

CHAPTER III. 

BELGRADE 

Belgrade, the capital of Serbia, has been completely rebuilt within the last eight or nine years. Strangers who knew the place long ago cannot recognise it to-day.

The city was heavily bombarded and set on fire during the European war, so that when the inhabitants eventually returned to their homes they found nothing but a heap of ruins. Some of the houses which had escaped, as though by a miracle, from being shattered to pieces, had been emptied of all their contents. Furniture, linen, pots and pans, everything of any use, had been carried off by the enemy. 

The shops had not a thing left in them, and consequently there was

nothing to be had for love or money. In those days you were lucky if you could buy sewing-cotton by the yard! People went about in strange garments made out of blankets or old curtains. Belgrade was like a dead city. Everyone seemed dazed an unequal to grappling with the situation.

However, the need to make the place habitable was so great that building quickly became the city's chief concern. It was a regular epidemic. Houses shot up on every hand with the most amazing rapidity. There never was such activity as at that time. The whole of Belgade was seething with work-people. Cart-loads and cart-loads of bricks and mortar filled the streets, and the dust raised was quite indescribable.

Not satisfied with housees of one or two storeys, the inhabitants of new Belgrade now erected huge tenements, five or six floors high. 

The changes were radical, but necessary to cope with altered circumstances. Belgrade has gained importance which it dild not have before, and the inhabitants, instead of numbering merely 80,000, have increased to over 200,000. Building is still going on apace, but you can easily understand that as the population has become more than double, the demand for habitations is great. More and more houses are constructed every year, and improvements are visible on all sides. 

I have heard Belgrade called "The Concrete City," which is rather a descriptive name for it. Practically all the buildings are made of concrete. Whether owing to the speed of construction, or whether from lack of architectural sense of beauty, the city unfortunately has few buildings which really please the eye. They are mostly heavy in appearance, devoid of style, and there is no originality. Nevertheless the Serbs may feel proud of what has been done in such a comparatively short time, and some day Belgrade will be a very fine city. 

Belgrade actually means White Fortress. In ancient times it was nothing more nor less than a fortress, and the town gradually spread beyond the ramparts. Until the middle of the last century there was a wall surrounding the town, which could only be entered through guarded gates. While the Turks occupied Belgrade their sentinels were very particular as to whom they let pass. At night the gates were closed, and no one was permitted to go in or out. 

The ruins of the fortress, which saw much fighting and bloodshed, still exist. They overlook two rivers, the Danube and the Save, and you get a splendid view from there. On a fine evening, if you go for a stroll in the Kalemegdan—the gardens adjoining the fortress-

you will always find a number of people gazing across the River Save to the wide plains beyond. They are mostly men, old and young, who love to spend half an hour in the twilight, dreaming of the past or the future. Until 1918 the plains belonged to Austria, and the Serbs were consequently hemmed in and cruelly hampered. But now they look across to where their brothers dwell on the other side of the river, and you can imagine how glad they must be to feel that there is no frontier to divide them.

The Serbs do not go for long walks in the country as one may do in England. They go out for a little stroll in the evening, when the corso, or principal street, is thronged with an animated crown all bent on taking some exercise and meeting friends. Everyone is dressed in the latest fashion, and ladies use quite as much, if not more, powder and paint than in Paris. The feminine mind is taken up with the question of clothes to about the same extent as the masculine mind is absorbed by politics. Women spend a great deal of time and money on dressmakers, and in the buying of beutiful materials, silk stockings, dainty shoes and such like. They would rather go without other things than be behind the fashion. From this you must not think that they are empty-headed. Far from it. At the Belgrade University there are nearly as many girls as young men studying for various careers. 

In warm weather all the cafes and restaurants put rows of tables and chairs out on to the street, where there is more air, and customers can see all that is going on while they sit over a glass of beer or sip a cup of black coffee. It is the recognised thing, in the morning after office hours and in the evening before supper, for men and women to have some refreshment. They are hungry after working all the morning on a light breakfast, and a sandwich, with something to drink, does not seem to spoil their appetite for dinner. The same thing applies to the evening. The Serbs take no tea in the afternoon, and so they feel the need of something later on. 

The little tables, with their white cloths, are very inviting in hot weather. During the summer you will see people sitting in these openair cafes till long into the night. It is a little cooler in the streets after dark than in the house, where the sun has perhaps been beating all day. 

The heat is very fierce in summer, and the cold is equally intense in winter. Usually there are heavy falls of snow, and if it lies long enough carts and carriages are put on runners, and the tinkling of the sleigh bell is a welcome change from the sound of the motor horn. 

Children get out their toboggans and fly down steep side-streets at

breakneck speed. Others go out across the hill to Toptshider, to get some ski-ing. Toptshider is a fine old part, a tram drive from the town. 

Long ago Prince Milosh, the first ruler of Serbia when she became a semi-independent state, had his summer residence here. The house, built in the Turkish style, is picturesque old place and is still in existence. 

It is not surprising that the Serbs have not been able to give much to organising museums and other public institutions. Ever since regaining their freedom they have still had to fight for their existence, and often had to neglect everything else and take to arms. However, in spite of turbulent times and the bombardment of Belgrade, the greater part of a valuable collection of historic treasures has been preserved. 

The national Museum and the Ethnographical Museum have many interesting things in them. The latter has a fine exhibition of costumes from different parts of the country. 

Extremes and contrasts are the most striking feature of Belgrade. 

You see opposing forces everywhere: in the streets, in the houses, in the lives of the people even. Side by side with the peasant in homespun clothes and sandalled feet, walk the smartly dressed people of the wealthier classes. The creaking ox-cart has the right of way alongside the luxurious limousine car, and tall modern structures tower above dilapidated little houses in the strangest fashion. Thus East meets West in a curious jumble, and in wiev of such extremes and contrasts you cannot but feel that there is a gap somewhere. The connecting link between the one and the other is missing, and so you constantly find that you suddenly drop into the gap.

CHAPTER IV. 

CHILDHOOD DAYS IN SERBIA 

The average Serbian child has rather a poor time of it until able to go to school. Of course, when I speak of the children of this country, I mean those who live in the provinces. In Belgrade there are many advantages, both for children and grown-ups, which do not exist in other parts of Serbia.

For instance, such a thing as a nursery is unheard of anywhere except in Belgrade. As a rule, tiny tots are looked after by an elder sister or a grandmother, and there is no proper provision made for children. They have no nursery rhymes, nor picture books, seldom even real toys to play with, and none of the jolly games of children as in England. But conditions in the homes of Serbia are totally different

to those in the west of Europe. Life is a pretty hard struggle for most people in this country. Everybody is busy with work of some kind, and there is little time over to devote to the children. The peasant woman works quite as hard as a man, sometimes harder. 

Suppose a mother of a family has no one with whom to leave her children (I speak of the peasants), she must take them along with her to the fields. During the time she is away hoeing or weeding, the little ones are left in charge of each other, under the shade of a neighbouring tree. You frequently see a peasant woman going off to work with a baby in a kind of sling, carried on her back. While she is busy in the fields she leaves the babe in its sling, tied to the boughs of a tree. Gently rocked by the breeze, it sleeps peacefully in this improvised cradle. 

As soon as a child outgrows babyhood it is very much left to its own devices, and does not get much training from either of its parents. 

A certain amount of obedience is insisted upon, but it is not taught anything much before going to school. Education and training are mostly left to the school teacher. Children sometimes sit for hours in the company of grown-up people, who pay no heed to the fact that the little ones are taking in all that goes on. What they learn in this way is not as a rule desirable.

You may find it difficult to believe that, except in Belgrade, there are no real toy-shops in Serbia. You can buy simple toys at a stationer's shop, perhaps, but the supply is sure to be very limited. Should a little girl get a present of a doll or a stuffed animal, she would hardly ever think of playing with the toy, but would put it in the window for passers-by to admire. It is a strange idea, but eventually, after they have stood long enough on show, the child has a good time with the presents. The joy of a little boy's heart is a walking-stick, "just like

Daddy's," or a satchel for carrying books to school. The peasants make very little distinction between clothes for children and for grownup people. Tiny tots are dressed like miniature men and women and look very quaint sometimes.

At the age of six or seven children begin to go to school, and they love their lessons. This must seem very strange to most people, I expect. But then you must remember what a dull time most of these youngsters have at home, and consequently they find school life full of interest and enjoy studying.

CHAPTER V. 

IN A SERBIAN HOME 

If you have to come and spend a few days in a Serbian home, you would soon realise how different life is here to what it is in Great Britain.

Almost everybody in Serbia possesses a house of his own. It may be quite a tiny place, just a cottage, but even the peasants all own a bit of land on which they can build. Therefore, in the provinces, there are very few people who require to rent houses. Of course in Belgrade it is different. Conditions are not at all the same as down country, and there is a great demand for houses to let. 

In Serbia there are neither very rich nor very poor people, and class distinction does not exist as in other countries. This is an agricultural land, and the educated peasant can rise to practically any position. I once knew a man whose parents were humble peasants. 

He was very clever and studied abroad. In due course he became a diplomat and earned the respect and admiration of his colleagues. One of his brothers, a peasant, worked on the farm and used to bring us cheese from time to time. The fact that one brother was a farmer and the other a diplomat made no difference, and when the latter came home he was just one of the family. 

Owing to frequent wars and hardships in their past history, the Serbs have learned to be very frugal. They prefer to put by some money, rather than spend it all. One of their proverbs runs, "Keep white coins for black days." Even those who are well off and could afford luxuries live very simply, more from force of habit than anything else.

The construction of Serbian houses is quite different from those in the western part of the world. As land is not expensive in the provinces, people do not build houses of several storeys high, but prefer to spread out and have everything on the ground floor. The rooms all lead one out of another, so that if you leave the doors open you can see right through the house from end to end. These communicating rooms are not very practical, however. How would you like to be obliged to go through the dining-room in order to get to your bedroom? 

Or would you prefer people to go through your bedroom in order to reach the dining-room? True, bedrooms in Serbia are not like ordinary bedrooms. Of course there are beds to sleep in, but by day they are arranged in such a manner as to hide this fact as much as possible. 

There is little else to accomodate the sleeper, except

the beds. You naturally wonder, where do the inmates of the house wash; where do they keep their clothes; where do they do their hair and so forth? Usually there is a place set apart for the daily ablutions, and here also you may find cupboard and a looking glass, which suffices for the whole family.

In the country a bathroom in the house is very unusual, and till quite recently such a thing was absolutely unknown. You must understand the grave difficulties in the way. With no canalisation, there is no proper water-supply and no provision for waste pipes. None of the houses have water laid on, so that every drop has to be fetched from the pump outside. Think what that means. How would you ever get a whole bath full of water?

You will never find people still asleep at half-past seven or eight o'clock in Serbia! Everyone is astir by five or six in the morning, and the best of the day's work is done before ten or eleven o'clock. Meals take place at very irregular times. In the early morning, before breakfast, it is customary for everyone to help themselves to a spoonful of slatko-preserved fruit-and a glass of cold water. This is followed by a tiny cup of black coffee, and many a Serb goes off to his business without touching enything else. But he would not miss his cup of black coffee for the world!

Naturally those who have been working hard all the morning since early dawn get hungry long before midday. Therefore, whenever the breadwinners come home they sit down to table, whether the other members of the family are there or not. Thus very often one of the essential elements of home life is left out, and it is not until suppertime that the family can gather round the familiar board. 

The Serbian wife and mother has no easy task waiting on her family. She does not sit down to a meal before all the food has been brought to table. Everything is served more or less at the same time, otherwise the unfortunate mother would not be able to have her dinner until the meal was practically over. 

While you are taking your soup the meat and vegetables also appear, and unless you eat quickly it all gets lead cold before you are ready to begin on the next course. 

Fortunately nobody seems to mind this.

Peasants working in the fields all day will content themselves with bread and cheese, or bread and onions and some fruit. In spite of such frugal meals they are a strong race, with great physical and moral endurance.

There is never any afternoon tea in a Serbian house. Slatko and 10

black coffee are served, as in the morning, and always offered to visitors. 

A Serbian home lacks any real comfort. You will never find a cosy sitting-room or a place to lounge in. The room set apart for receiving visitors is always austere and depressing in appearance. No one ever goes into this "state" room, except on high days and holidays, and then you sit on the edge of your chair and feel very uncomfortable.

In some parts of the country it is still the custom for families and their descendants to live in a community under the same roof. These joint households are called Zadrugas, and the domain is handled down from father to son. This way of living is a relic of old patriarchal imes, and often as many as fifteen or twenty members of a family are united in one group.

When the sons marry, they bring their wives to the father's house, and the daughters leave their parents' roof and join the Zadruga of their husbands. The wife's place in the house is according to age. Most is expected of the youngest members, but all take their share of cooking, washing, cleaning, or working in the fields. All the inmates of a Zadruga look up to the head of the house, the father or the eldest son, and any disputes or difficulties arising are always settled by him. But this custom of living together in communities is dying out, for the peasants are becoming more emancipated and anxious to be independent.

CHAPTER VI. 

THE SERBIAN BOY 

At an early age the average boy in Serbia begins to long for the day when he will be able to go to school. He looks forward to learning to read and write, to taking his place in the classroom and being among boys of his own age.

Although it is not compulsory for children to attend school before they are seven years old, some little chaps start their education even earlier than this. You can imagine how important they feel when they first begin going to school, with a satchel full of books carried on the back. Lessons occupy their thoughts to the extinction of all else, and they are quite happy if only allowed to study. 

In far-away villages in Serbia very little fellows will tramp for miles in order to reach the nearest school. I have often met groups of children in peasant dress, trudging contentedly along, with their lesson books in a bag, hung from the shoulder. They also carry a piece of bread and cheese in their bags, which is their dinner, and in afternoon they will trot all the way home again, thinking nothing of the long road. 

All schools in this country are supported by the State, which means 11

that the children of well-to-do parents, or those of humble birth, all attend the same classes. This encourages a genuinely democratic spirit. 

Games do not enter much into the life of the Serbian schoolboy. 

Up to the present little thought has ever been given to organising games, and it is only recently that football has begun to be introduced. In 

Belgrade there are some very good football teams, but the provinces are still backward in this respect. Cricket and hockey are not known at all. With the first days of spring, you will see boys at every street corner spinning tops and playing with marbles. These are favourite games all the world over. Serbian boys all love bathing in summer, and they will stay in the water for hours at a time, or lie basking in the sunshine on the banks of the rives till they are burned copper colour. 

In winter lads are badly off for exercise and recreation. Perhaps it is because they have so little fun that they so soon grow out of childhood and become serious young men. At the age of seventeen or eighteen they are quite grown up, and in their free time they have othing better to do than meet each other in cafes, where they find most pleasure in discussing politics or current events. 

Cafes and restaurants are the only places where Serbian youths can meet for a chat. They have no cosy "den" or study at home, as in your English homes, and so it is not suprising hat they go to public places.

Boys ussually do their preparation for school under difficulties Fancy how you would feel if you had to study in a room where other people sat and chattered all the time! And yet in winter this is very often what happens, for the only warm room is where the whole family gathers. Of course, before the cold weather sets in, it is comparatively easy to find a peaceful corner somewhere. I know a boy, one of a large family in reduced circumstances, who steals away to the attic when he wants to have a quiet time to do his lessons. 

In the days when my father went to school in Belgrade there were very few books to be had. There was only one bookshop in the whole town. Think of it! Pupils were obliged to copy out volumes of notes necessary for their studies, for there were not nearly enough books to go round. It must have been an arduous task, and made quite a labour for the unfortunate lads. At that time there was no electric light or gas, and work done at night had to be accomplished by the light of a candle. 

The candles were not even such as we have to-day, but tallow dips, which constantly had to be snuffed and shed only a dim yellow light. 

Yet some of those lads who had such a struggle for education were 12

able, later on, to take their place at the Universities of Paris and Vienna and complete with young men who had experienced none of these difficulties.

CHAPTER VII. 

THE SERBIAN GIRL 

A girl's life in Serbia is quite different from that of a boy. For one thing, women are not put on the same level as men, and the education of girls has been sadly neglected. Given no opportunity to better herself, the Serbian woman has up till now resignedly accepted this unfair state of things, but the day of her awakening is not far off. 

Energetic women have founded societies in order to try to obtain emancipation for their sisters in this country. It will no doubt take time before results can be expected, but the work has begun and will surely succeed in the end.

Fortunately schooling is now obligatory for both sexes; however, there are still some parents who evade the law and keep their children at home. How often down in the country one sees litttle girls, and boys too, who, instead of being at school, spend their time in the fields, looking after sheep or cattle. The peasants do not yet realise the importance of education, but the younger generations are clamouring for school.

From their earliest days girls are imbued with the idea of domesticity, and brought up to prepare for marriage as their only aim in life. 

The great necessity for a girl is to be a good housewife-to know how to cook and sew, how to sweep and dust and to be able to do every kind of work in the house. If, including all this, she can paint a little and do embroidery, she has all the qualities that could possibly be wished for. 

While still a child she begins to prepare things for her "bottom drawer," so that when the time comes for marriage her trosseau is ready. 

In Serbia there are no old maids or spinsters. Such a thing is practically unheard of. As soɔn as a girl reaches the age of seventeen or eighteen her parents make it known that they have a daughter ready for marriage. Every girl has a dowry, whether big or small, and prospective husbands are mostly guided in their choice of matrimony by the sum to be bestowed on the bride. A man's earnings are hardly ever enough to keep a wife and family on, therefore he is not to be blamed if he seeks to marry a woman with something of her own. The wife, who has never been accustomed to having anything to do with money, hands her dowry over to her husband. If he proves to be an 13

honest man, all is well, but it sometimes happens that the husband squanders his wife's fortune and she is then left penniless. 

When a girl is willing to wed, some kind friend volunteers to select a suitable husband for her. The matchmaker who undertakes this delicate task is called Provodadjija, and, of course, much depends on the good judgment of this person. If the financial side of the question satisfies both parties, a meeting is arranged between the young couple, and they decide whether they are to each other's liking. This no doubt sounds very unromantic to you, but it would not occur to the average Serbian girl to think so. She is not obliged to marry the first man presented to her; it is only if her intuition tells her the right one is found that the matter is speedily concluded.

The Serbian woman is seldom idle, but she does not know how to lighten labour in her home. All her household duties take her much longer than need be, for she is ignorant of how to save time and energy. 

For instance, she will go back and forward to the pump a dozen times, fetching water, when a canful brought into the house would avoid so many journeys. Instead of providing everything necessary for accomplishing a certain task, she will fetch one thing at a time, and be quite unconscious of the waste of precious moments. All work is done in a primitive sort of way, and one longs to introduce some laboursaving devices into this country. 

At the end of the day the Serbian woman enjoys a good old gossip with her neighbour across the way. She takes her knitting with her, and all the time she is talking her needles fly in and out; socks and stockings must be supplied for all the family, which always keeps mothers and daughters busy.

When they are through with their work, young girls love to tidy themselves up and go for a stroll down the village street. Here they are sure to find friends with whom they can talk over the events of the day. On Sundays and holidays, when everyone is free and looking for recreation, the cafes and restaurants reap a harvest. The strains of a gipsy band always attract the Serb, who loves music. Family parties sit down at the little tables in the Kafana, and in the company of their friends they throw care to the winds and give themselves up to the enjoyment of the hour.

It is by no means easy to make a trip into Old Serbia, and one must be prepared for adventures. Primarily, the question of accommodation is always a difficult problem to solve. Whenever possible, a monastery is the best place to halt at, as the monks are always ready with a kindly welcome for strangers. You can imagine that it is quite exciting travelling in this land, where, in spite of sentinels, you never know if a band of highwaymen will not suddenly dash out from under cover and stop you, shouting, "Your money or your life."

The natives of Macedonia are very superstitious folk. It is, of 16

Tiny babies have a cluster of blue beads fastened to a lock of hair, or they wear a whole string of beads round the neck. Men either have a blue button sewn on to their shirts, or a blue bead worn as an earring. 

The principal thing is for the beads to be in evidence somewhere on the person.

Black water buffaloes, which do most of the hard work in Macedonia, also mules and donkeys, have a string of blue beads slung across their foreheads or fastened on to their harness. 

It does not require a very observant person to note that blue is the predominant colour of the south. The intense blue of the sky, the blue mist on the distant hills, the blue flowers which grow in the subtle, the blue beads worn by the natives, the blue of the shadows in striking contrast to the brilliant sunshine, make a wonderful symphony of colour in varying shades of blue.

CHAPTER IX. 

A SHORT OUTLINE OF THE HISTORY OF CROATIA 

In order to give you an idea of Yugoslavia as a whole, it is necessary to tell you something about each separate state. Although the Croatians are of the same origin as the Serbs, their history is totally different, and this is one of the chief reasons for dissimilarities of character, religion, civilisation, politics and so on. The Croatians belong to the Roman 

Catholic Church, while the Serbs are Orthodox; and, as you know, the development of a nation largely depends upon its religious views. 

About the sixth century the Serbs, Croats and Slovenes migrated from central Russia and peopled the Balkan Penisula. When they first settled in this country, they clung yet awhile to their old faith in pagan gods. They were unwilling to accept Christianity, fearing lest with a change of religion they might also come under foreign rule. Christianity only began to spread when the church services were held in the language of the Slavs. Two brothers of Greek nationality, by name Cyril and 

Methodius, wrote the first Slav alphabet in 863 and translated the Bible into Slav. They then turned their steps towards the Balkans, where they began teaching the Christian religion. German priests greatly op posed the two brothers in their task, and they were obliged to seek the intervention of the Pope. Thereafter they were able to carry on their 17

mission, and it is owing to their devotion to this cause that the growth of Christianity spread throughout the Balkans.

The first record we have of the Serbs, Croats and Slovenes being united into one state in the ninth century, but this combination did not last long. Soon after they separated and divided themselves into three distinct groups.

Croatia became a kingdom in the tenth century, and under the rule of King Tomislav reached the zenith of her power. The Adriatic coastland of Dalmatia was conquered during his reign and the borderlands of Croatia greatly extended, but this time of prosperity was shortlived. Civil wars weakened the kingdom, and in the middle of the eleventh century the Venetians occupied the coast of Dalmatia, and 

Croatia came under the rule of Byzantium.

Internal struggles also took place between the Church and the nobles, and when the national dynasty died out, quarrels arose as to the succession to the throne. Finally, in 1102, a Hungarian king named 

Koloman was chosen, and from that time onward until the Great War the fate of Croatia was bound up with that of Hungary. 

The Hungarians, having once got a footing in Croatia, began to exercise their dominion in ever-increasing measures, and finally the Croatians were obliged to accept a state union with Hungary. And so, during the Middle Ages, it came about that instead of Croatia having a separately crowned king, the King of Hungary ruled both countries. 

The Croatians made several attempts to overthrow the usurpers of their land, but unrest and revolts bore little fruit. Two of the nobles of that period, named Peter Zrinski and Krsto Frankopan, hatched a plot to sever Croatia from the enemy, and in order to bring this about they sought the support of Turkey. They were betrayed, however, and both Zrinski and Frankopan were condemned and put to death in Vienna in 1671. The names of these two men, who sacrificed their lives for the sake of their country, will go down to posterity in the history of Croatia.

In 1493 the Turks invaded northern Croatia, and the plight of the latter was much worse than when under the Hungarians. During all the centuries that the Croatians were subjected to dominion, they were not nearly as alive to their national aspirations as the Serbs in the five hundrer years of their submission to the Turks. This apathy on the part of the Croatians can be best explained on the ground that they had less to put up with than the Serbs. Indeed, in some ways, the Croatians reaped certain advantages, in so far as culture and higher civilisation 18

are concerned. But of course this could not compensate them for the ⚫ loss of their freedom. During the reign of Napoleɔn the Slavs were united into a single province called Illyria, the name by which this region was known in Roman and Byzantine times. This was the first purely Slav state combining a union of the Serbs, Croats and Slovenes since their early alliance in the ninth century. When the Hungarians again took possesion of 

Croatia they objected to the term of Illyria, and forbade the people to call themselves Illyrians, but this short-lived chapter left a definite trace in the history of the Yugoslavs. You see how many different phases this people has been through. 

A Croatian patriot by the name of Ludevit Gaj, who lived in the nineteenth century, did much to rouse the national feelings of his countrymen, who had sunk into an alarming state of indifference. He established the literary language of Croatia on the same basis as the Serbian language, except that the former is written in Latin characters, while the Serbs use the old Slav letters, called Cyrillic. Ludevit Gaj also founded reading-rooms and national theatrical companies, while encouraging the people to preserve Croatian relics of the past. In this way they began to wake out of their stupor, and to realise that, unless they were alert to the danger threatening them, their nationality would be submerged altogether.

For political reasons Croatia was granted certain privileges by Austria in the nineteenth century, and Colonel Jelačić, a Croatian in the Austrian Army, was made governor or Ban of the country. This improved state of affairs did not last long, however, and the old system of arbitrary rule was soon adopted again. Nevertheless the short spell of comparative independence under the governorship of Ban Jelačić was an epoch-making event for Croatia, and helped to reinstate the national pride of the people.

Frequent attempts to impose still greater German and Hungarian authority over the Croatians now only resulted in rousing resentment. 

Instead of meekly submitting to these measures, it had the very opposite effect, stimulating them into active opposition and stirring up feelings of patriotism.

The work begun by Ludevit Gaj was carried on with even greater fervour by Bishop Strossmayer. He devoted his life to developing 

Yugoslav sentiment, to encouraging propaganda of the national literature and language, and maintaining historic traditions. It was owing to his initiative, as well as his financial support, that a Croatian Academy of sciences and a University were built in Zagreb. The future growth 19

of the Yugoslav race was assured through these centres of education, and this achievement cannot be overestimated. Bishop Strossmayer was a saintly man, with no ambition but to serve his country, and his memory is held in love and reverence by all the southern Slavs. Thus we arrive at modern times.

From the very beginning of the war in 1914, the Serbian Government proclaimed that they were not only fighting for their existence, but for the liberation of all the Yugoslavs. Through thick and thin they never lost sight of this cherished aspiration, and after a prolonged and heroic struggle their victory was assured. In December, 1918, the 

Serbs, Croats and Slovenes were united into one kingdom.

ZAGREB 

The capital of Croatia is a fine city, and the inhabitants may well be proud of it. CHAPTER XI. 

"PRIVREDNIK," A CHARITABLE SOCIETY 

I want to tell you something about a benevolent society which goes by the name of Privrednik and is a very remarkable instituti ›n. The aim of the society is to give poor boys and girls a start in life and to enable them to earn a living. Privrednik signifies "to earn a livelihood."

It was founded in Zagreb, thirty years ago, by a Croatian, whose whole life has been devoted to the cause of the Slavs and the welfare of the Privrednik Society. This great-hearted man realised that the youth of his country was in danger of being overwhelmed by Austro- Hungarian influence and losing the sense of their true nationality; he further realised that commerce and industry were likely to be swallowed up by foreigners, who were getting trade more and more into their hands. Therefore, a twofold object was to be accomplished through the Privrednik Society.

In co-operation with Yugoslav business men, this farseeing Croatian benefactor began to gather poor children from out-of-the-way villages and small towns and started them to learn a trade, while at the same time a spirit of patriotism was gradually instilled into them. These were the small beginnings of the society, which steadily increased its activities until it became necessary to establish a home where boys and girls could be given a certain amount of schooling while learning to become apprentices in various branches of arts and crafts. 

When the Austro-Hungarians became aware of the aims of the Privrednik Society, they did all they could to oppose it, and the work had, to a great extent, to be carried on in secrecy. However, nothing could stop the good purpose begun, for the founder of this society saw clearly that only race equipped with sound knowledge of trade and commerce could win independence and keep their nationality. The hope of liberation from foreign dominion and control was the great motive power in the mind of this patriotic citizen of Zagreb, whose vision was ever focused on the future union of the Slavs, and who never doubted his dream would come true. Even in the darkest hours his faith never wavered, and he has lived long enough to see the fulfilment of his aspirations.

During the Great War most of the lads belonging to the Privrednik 22

Society fled the country and joined the Serbian Army as volunteers, and only a very few remained to serve in the Austrian ranks. This is substantial proof that patriotism had become a living factor in the lives of these young men. The Privrednik Society encourages boys and girls to try to save money instead of spending all they earn. Every dinar (about a penny) they can put aside is dɔubled by the society, and think what an incentive that must be! There is also a savings bank in con nection with the society, so that when an apprentice leaves the institution, he can borrow a certain sum of money at a low rate of interest, in order to start business on his own.

During the thirty years of its activity 20,000 girls and boys have been given a start in life and become useful men and women. The headquarters of the Privrednik Society have now been transferred to Belgrade, where the work is being carried on with great success. Of course there are many other admirable humanitarian societies in Yugoslavia, but this one, providing for boys and girls, wil perhaps interest you most.

CHAPTER XII. 

INCIDENTS AND EVENTS IN THE HISTORY OF SLOVENIA 

Slovenia is land composed of mountains and green valleys, forests and fertile meadows, lakes and rivers. Certain parts of it are quite as beautiful as Switzerland, and although the snowy peaks of the high Alps are lacking in Slovenia, nevertheless one or two of her rocky summits are capped with eternal snow. It is an ideal country for spending the holidays in, but before going into that side of the question I want to give you some idea of the back chapters of Slovenian history. 

In the early stages of their existence the Slovenians lived in peace and freedom under the rule of a prince chosen from among their own nationality. Under his leadership, when occasion demanded it, wars were waged against would-be invaders seeking to encroach on their borderlands. Eventually the Slovenians made an alliance with the 

Czechs, and together they formed a powerful state. 

Misfortune befell them in the middle of the eighth century, and they lost their independence. This is how it came about. Exhausted by long years of fighting against hordes of barbarians whɔ threatened to overwhelm them altogether, they at last turned to Germanic tribes, Bavarians, for instance. The Bavarians came to the rescue, but when peace was restored and the danger passed, they refused to leave the country. Despite wars and much bloodshed, the Slovenians were unable to rid themselves of the unwelcome presence of the Bavarians, and 23

in the end they were obliged to submit to becoming a vașsal state. It is not surprising that hatred grew up between the Slovene and Germanic races, for it was owing to the latter that Slovenia lost her independence and was held in bondage from the eight until the beginning of the twentieth century.

Christianity was first heard of in Slovenia at the time of the influx of the Bavarians, who poured into the land in great numbers. The 

Slovenians opposed the warlike spirit in vain. Finally they joined forces with the Germans in order to vanquish pagan lands, and their campaigns were successful.

Once again, at the beginning of the ninth centry, the Slovenians made another desperate attempt to free themselves from German yoke. 

Ludevit Posavski was the originator and leader of the revolt. He gathered together the Slovenians, including numbers of Croatians and Serbs, and united them into one band. This attack against the Germans was nɔt successful, however, and the Franks eventually broke up the Slav armies and crushed the fight for freedom and independence. The insurrection led by Ludevit Posavski is noteworthy on account of it being the first record of the Serbs, Croats and Slovenes having united their forces in an attack against a common fɔe. 

After this defeat the Franks introduced their own system of government in Slovenia, and German nobility came and settled in the country. 

The last trace of Slovenian independence was thus wiped out. 

In spite of the misery and sufferings which the Slovenians endured under Hungarian rule in the tenth century, they succeeded in forming a duchy, calling it Carniola. This soon fell to pieces, however, and when 

Rudolf Hapsburg became German emperor in 1273, the Slovenians were submitted to still greater tyranny and oppression. From the beginning of the fifteenth century the Turks invaded Slovenia, and the Germans left them to defend themselves as best they could. The Slovenians turned to the Croatians for help, and they joined forces in the struggle against the Turks, which lasted several centuries. Naturally this united effort drew the Slovenians into closer ties of brotherhood with the Croatians, at the same time fostering the idea of ultimate union with all the Yugoslavs.

The state of the peasants was steadily growing worse on account of pressure brought to bear on them by the nobility. Just as peasants suffered in other countries from the feudal system, so it was the case in Slovenia; but here their plight was worse, because of the frequent onslaughts of the Turks. In 1515 a peasant revolt took place, but the 24

insurgents were badly armed and unfitted to fight against the wellorganised armies of the nobility, and were thus speedily crushed. The strong ties of friendship which had sprung up between the Slovenians and the Croatians led to the peasants of both countries making yet another attempt to overthrow the German oppressor. 

But in this they were doomed to failure again.

Many Serbs, Bosnians, Herzegovinians and Macedonians sought refuge and settled in Slovenia during the Turkish wars, adopting the national language and customs of the country. This intermingling of the southern Slavs forms a strong link in the chain wich binds them together, and the results of this fusion had a lasting effect. 

The religious movement known as the Reformation began to make itself felt in Slovenia in the sixteenth century. It was a favourable opportunity for the expounders of this new version of the Christian faith to get a hearing, as the people were tyrannised by the Church and the clergy, and consequently quite willing to turn away from Roman Cath ›licism. Books on religious subjects now began to appear, and the foundation-stone of Slovenian literature was laid. The Bible was translated into the native tongue and the first grammar published. But this powerful Protestant movement was arrested by Duke Ferdinand (15901637), who afterward became German emperor. He commanded all

Protestant schools and churches to be closed, drove the teachers and preachers out of the country, and made a bonfire of their books. The 

Roman Catholics, and especially the Jesuits, helped to carry out these orders, and all trace of the Reformation soon disappeared. With the end of this chapter, literature in the language of the Slovenians grew steadily less, and writing in Latin or German became the general rule. 

It was not until the eighteenth century that the native tongue was revived. Then the national spirit of Slovenia, which had lain dormant for so long, awɔke at last, largely owing to the many schools opened during the reign of Maria Theresa and Joseph II. in this century. Although all instruction was in German, the sense of racial consciousness was now thoroughly roused and could not be choked again. 

In the nineteenth century the conquests of Napoleon and French influence were of great significance in the national development of Slovenia, and indeed for the Yugoslavs in general. When Napoleon crushed the Austrian Army in 1809, he formed the kingdom of Illyria, of which I have written something in a previous chapter. Ljubljana was the chosen capital of this new state, and Napoleon stayed here for a time. This occupation by the French was the greatest good fortune for the country. Schools were now taught in the national language and 25

peace and order established everywhere. The Slovenians consider this short period of their history as the happiest time since the loss of their independence. Periodicals and newspapers also began to be published in Slovenian, and this likewise contributed towards reinstating patriotism and awakening a desire for the union of all the Slavs. 

Yet once again the Germans came back to Slovenia and recommenced their reign of tyranny, but it was of short duration. For political reasons, conditions improved in 1882, and the Slovenian language was given the same place as German in administrative offices and in the schools.

At the beginning of the twentieth century a strong movements was set on foot for a joining together of the Slovenians, the Serbs and the Croatians; and in 1912, at the time of the Balkan wars, there was a general demand for the union of all the Slavs. And so when a few years later the situation ripened, and the possibility of massing the Yugoslavs together arose, the Slovenians were ready to respond to the call for union, and glad to combine with the Serbs and Croatians in forming a single kingdom.

CHAPTER XIII. 

LJUBLJANA 

In the minds of a good many people Ljubljana is always associated with the beginning of the end of a holiday. It is the chief starting-point for the Slovenian Alps, as well as being the last place of importance at which one stops when leaving Yugoslavia for abroad. Travellers and tourists are thus tempted to spend a few hours, if not a night or two, in this enchanting old-world city—the capital of Slovenia-without going out of their way to do so.

To those who are anxious to get away from the nervous strain and excitement of life in a metropolis, Ljubljana has much to offer. It is a busy little place, but devoid of unpleasant bustling and hustling, so that one can enjoy lingering in its picturesque streets, and loiter placidly through is thoroughfares.

One of the great charms of Ljubljana lies in the fact that although its architecture seems to belong to the Middle Ages, it combines all the conveniences of modern life. It boasts of one or two comfortable hotels, attractive shops, a theatre and other places of amusement, as well as delightful walks in the immediate neighbourhood of the town. Wandering along some of the old streets, one sometimes gets a peep through an open doorway, which reveals a courtyard of unexpected beauty and rouses one's curiosity to take another look within. On closer inspection 26

When you tire of streets and shop windows, tempting little carriages, drawn by a single horse, are ready to take you anywhere you please. One could spend hours exploring these woods and always find fresh beauties, quite forgetting that Ljubljana is but a stone's-throw away.

CHAPTER XIV. 

HOLIDAYS IN SLOVENIA 

The Slovenians love their mountains. At all times of the year one sees happy bands of boys and girls, and men and women, with nailed boots and bulging knapsacks on their backs, going off for a long tramp among the hills. Old and young enjoy walking and climbing. On 

Sundays and holidays crowds of town dwellers make an early start by train in order to spend a whole day in the mountains. 27

A short journey by rail from Ljubljana brings one right into the heart of the Slovenian highlands, where there is a tremendous choice of tempting excursions to be made. The roads and paths are all marked with signs in different colours, so that there is no difficulty in finding one's way.

Mountain climbers can avail themselves of the Alpine huts provided by the Slovenian Mountaineering Club, where they can spend a night under cover when making a long expedition. It is a delightful experience, even for those who do not aspire to being classified as real climbers, to go as far as one of these huts. After hours of mounting steadily upwards, the sight of a little log cabin, cowering at the foot of sheer rocks, rejoices the heart and is like an oasis in the wilderness. 

Of course huts situated high up in the mountains are very primitive and simple constructions, but you must remember that everything necessary to build them has had to be carried up on men's backs. A hut usually consists of a kitchen and sleeping compartment. There are no proper beds, but just a row of sloping planks, with straw mattresses placed on top, and a few blankets with which to cover oneself. Sometimes another little "cubby hole" is provided just above the chief dormitory, and this can accommodate quite a number of visitors also, but no one ever objects to overcrowding in a hut, and all are prepared to rough it. A guardian is always on duty, and he provides firewood and water, two essential items.

At dawn everyone is astir, and there is a great commotion and clattering of nailed boots while the various parties are getting under way. Good humour prevails, and jokes and laughter echo down the mountain-side, as the climbers hastily prepare themselves a cup of tea or coffee before starting off. If the weather is fine, they saddle their packs with a light heart, and away they go in glad anticipation of conquering some difficult peak.

It was once my good fortune to put up, by chance, at quite a luxurious Alpine hut in Slovenia. Built for Austrian officers during the 

Great War, no trouble was spared to make it a pleasant abode, and it is still a haven of rest to-day. Far removed from any habitation, it nestles under the shadow of giant crags and perpendicular peaks. Real beds are supplied in this hut, with sheets and all, and from the broad verandah which leads off the living-room a striking view of the grandeur of the mountains is obtained. A few days could be spent here very enjoyably, and it would be almost worth while making the trip for the honey alone! The scent of all the flowers in the valley seems cɔncentrated in the delicious thick brown honey served in this hut. 

A chain of mountains called the Triglav offers splendid op- 28

portunities to those who like to scramble among rocks or wish for serious mountaineering. Thick snow, which never melts all year round, lies in great patches near the summit. One can reach the top of the Triglav by many different paths, some of which are comparatively easy, while others can only be tackled by good mountaineers who have a steady head and are sure of foot. The air in these high altitudes is sɔ invigorating that one can walk and climb for hours without feeling weariness or fatigue, but nevertheless to conquer the Triglav requires endurance and determination.

In spring and early summer wonderful flowers grow on the lower slopes of the hills, and the meadows in the valley are carpeted with gay colours. Narcisi and gentians are just as profuse in Slovenia as in 

Switzerland, if not more so. No one can resist bringing back a gorgeous bouquet gathered by the wayside, as a trophy of the day's outing.

For those who prefer to spend the holidays boating and bathing or fishing, there are lakes and rivers where all three pastimes can be indulged in to the heart's content. One of the most fascinating spots, which attracts numbers of people during the summer season, is the Lake of Bled. It is like a beautiful green emerald set in the heart of the mountains. A little island in the middle of the lake looks as if it must have been put there on purpose to add a touch of romance to this masterpiece of nature. On the crest of the island, which is actually a great rock, rising perpendicularly out of the water, a tiny church has been built. Visitors make frequent pilgrimages to the little island to ring the church bell, for there is a legend that wishes come true if, when one pulls the cord, the bell rings three times! All day long one hears the tinkle-tinkle of the distant bell, and it is a sign that believers are putting their wishes to the test.

The King and Queen of Yugoslavia have a castle near the water's edge, and always spend part of the summer here. It is no doubt largely owing to this fact that Bled has become a fashionable resort for all the bigwigs of the land. Diplomats and ministers dwell in secluded villas tucked away in the wooded slopes which encircle the lake, but holidaymakers, who pass the whole day in the water or basking in the sun, are unaware of anything but their own enjoyment. 

Another lake which is equally beautiful, but much more isolated and wilder in aspect, is Bohine. It appeals to people who want a peaceful holiday, without having to bother thinking about clothes. Bathers may find the water colder than at Bled, but it is crystal clear and much more invigorating. One can see all the pebbles at the bottom, even where it is quite deep, and shoals of big trout dart about, almost asking 29

to be caught! But no one is licensed to fish in this lake, which is the 

King's preserve. It must be tantalising to fishermen, but the only consolation is that the hotels frequently serve the most decici ›us trout for dinner.

A favourite resort for climbers and those who fancy all day walks in the mountains is Krainska Gora. This primitive little hamlet is over 

3,000 feet above sea-level, and the grey slate roofs of the houses seem to harmonise with the cruel rocks towering above them. But one can spend a delightful holiday here if satisfied to lead a simple life. The vivifying air will soon tan the palest cheeks and restore vigour to tired anatomy, sweeping away the cobwebs woven in the city. 

In winter, when the snow lies thick on the ground, the Slovenes shoulder their ski and go to the mountains, where there are splendid runs to be made on some of the higher slopes. They certainly know how to take the best possible advantage of their beautiful country at all times of the year.

CHAPTER XV. 

THE VOJVODINA 

The Vojvodina is a strip of land lying north of two rivers, the Save and the Danube. It includes the provinces of Srem, Batchka, Baran'a and Banat, which originally belonged to Austro-Hungary, but was populated almost entirely by Serbs. In one short chapter it is impossible to do more than just touch on some of the most important points concerning the development of the Vojvodina and the history of its inhabitants, which chronicle many stirring events. 

Vojvodina means Duchy. This name arose owing to the fact that during a certain period of their existence the Serbs were granted by Austria the right to elect a national duke, who administered the government of this territory and ruled over it. 

The Serbs of the Vojvodina are usually spoken of as "our people from the other side," for the great rolling plains, cradled in the arms of the two rivers, are separated by this natural boundary. In the history of the Serbs, those from across the river have played a very important part. Indeed, it is largely owing to their loyalty and patriotism that the nation was eventually able to raise triumphant from her ashes. 

When the Slavs came to the Balkan Peninsula in the sixth century, certain clans settled in the plains on the other side of the Save and the Danube. The Magyars, who came later, might have forced the Serbs to cross back over the river, if fresh contigents, emigrating owing to 30

the invasion of the Turks, had not swelled their ranks. After the defeat at the Battle of Kossovo numbers of Serbs migrated to the south of Hungary, and with the disappearance of the independent Serbian states ever increasing multitudes sought refuge here. Soldiers were wanted by the Magyars to defend their frontiers against the enemy, as well as labourers to cultivate the land; therefore the influx of new colonists was encouraged. The Magyars themselves, fearing an incursion from the 

Turks, fled northwards. Thus the Serbs were left to form a line of defence in case of attack, and were free to till the soil. The Hungarian 

King Mattew, depending on Serbian arms to protect his realm, granted them self-rule in 1470, giving the title of Despot to the son of the 

Serbian nobleman George Brankovitch. "Despot" in this case does not mean "tyrant," but was used to designate the last of the Serbian rulers who reigned over a weakened kingdom. 

One may say it is due to the valour of the Serbs as soldiers that the race owes its existence to-day. Had it not been for their bravery under fire and their exploits in times of war, they would never have attained to the position which they held in the Vojvodina. Both the 

Austrians and the Hungarians frequently called on the Serbs to help them fight their battles, and the latter soon earned a reputation for being splendid soldiers. The dual monarchy took sore advantage of the 

Serbs, and whenever they needed assistance in war promises of future privileges were held out as bribes, but were speedily withdrawn as soon as the danger was over.

It was the dawnfall of the Serbian Empire which gave birth to the Vojvodina. When the country gradually became engulfed by the Turks, Serbia's best sons emigrated to "the other side," to the provinces in the south of Hungary. Descendants of the royal house and former leaders, accompanied by bands of faithful followers, deserted their native land in order to save the race from doom. They took with them their national traditions, their Church and political organizations-in fine, all that was left of their State institutions. Much was achieved in those times, in the face of tremendous odds. In spite of being numerically inferior to other settlers in the Vojvodina, they not only preserved their faith and their language, but reached a high standard of national culture, and established the first stepping-stones leading towards modern civilisation for the whole Serbian race. 

It required especially great courage and perseverance to maintain the Orthodox Church against the hostility of the Roman Catholics, but the Serbs would as soon have given up their nationality as their faith, and notwithstanding great opposition, they strengthened their cause by 31

founding numerous monasteries. They were a strong moral force, which was of more importance than actual numbers. 

In leaving their native land, the Serbs' chief object was to work for the deliverance of their country. However, in the sixteenth century new invasions of the Turks dispersed the last traces of the Serbian state, and the refugees across the river were also brought into subjection. In the year 1683 the Turks penetrated beyond Hungary and besieged Vienna, but owing to the timely assistance of Poland, under the leadership of Jan Sobjeisky, they were beaten back, and Europe was saved from further incursions. The Serbs in the Vojvodina were meanwhile, in spite of limitations and difficulties, able to make progress in spheres of learning and culture, while carrying on successful enterprises in trade. 

They were past-masters in the art of inlaying silver and gold in wood and metal, which they used in decorating their churches and ikons, and this work found great favour with the Hungarians. Specimens testifying to this skilful workmanship of long ago are still in existence. It was in the Vojvodina that the first Serbian literature and journalism, painting and music were cultivated and encouraged; in the Vojvodina that the Serbs from the "other side" laid the foundation of the first primitive state and knit the fibres of national consciousness into a fervent sense of patriotism.

A great exodus of Serbs took place in 1690, led by Patriarch Tcharnojevitch. An understanding was previously come to with the 

Hungarians to secure religious liberty and certain privileges for the emigrants, but the compact agreed to was only partly fulfilled. The 

Serbs were willing to recognise the sovereignty of the emperor, but they craved for independence and their own territory and instituti ɔns. 

As time went on the Hungarians tried to frustrate the Serbs' endeavours to preserve their nationality, and sought to crush their cherished hopes of eventually reconstructing the country. The plan of 

Hungary was rather to submerge the Serbs and make them one with the Hungarians. In this, however, they were met with the fiercest opposition.

During the insurrection of Kara-George many Serbs came back to the land of their forefathers, and with their advanced education and culture they opened up new channels of activity in the fight for freedom. 

They introduced modern methods of warfare, of which their brothersin-arms were ignorant, while contributing both moral and material support. Some years later, when Prince Milosh held the reins of govern- 32

ment, the Serbs from "the other side" again came over, bringing with them the results of a century of learning and culture, thus aiding in forming and administrating the way for civilisation. 

Frontiers had not been able to separate this kindred race or prevent them from sharing the same hopes and aspirations through the long period of darkness. The Vojvodina now forms part of Yugoslavia, and the Serbs gratefully acknowledge that it was owing to their countrymen on the other side" that the backbone of the race was saved from annihilation.

CHAPTER XVI. 

A GLIMPSE INTO THE PAST OF BOSNIA AND HERZEGOVINA 

A traveler who had visited Bosnia and Herzegovina forty years ago relates how at that time this land and its people were completely cut off from the rest of Europe and sunk in total oblivion. Customs and manners belonging to the Middle Ages were in full vigour then, as indeed they still are to-day, and the Oriental trappings and garish colours of the East, combined with the natural beauty of the country, offered a picture of rare and fascinating charm. Bosnia and Herzegovina in those days were under the rule of the Turks, swayed by the spirit of Mohammedanism, and so remote and far removed from the interest of Europe, that their very existence was scarcely taken into account. Conditions have considerably changed since then, but these two provinces have lost none of their picturesque charm; neither-fortunately-have the people yielded an inch of ground in favour of modern clothes, but still wear the attractive garments dating from the time of their ancestors. 17 Bosnia and Herzegovina are striking contrasts. The one is fertile, smiling, verdant, like a garden; while the other is austere and arid, a perfect wilderness of rocks and stones. In Herzegovina the inhabitants have a a very hard struggle to make a living, or even to eke out an existence. They try to cultivate patches of soil redeemed from the cruel rocks, but quantities of pebbles and stones must first be sifted from the earth-sometimes great boulders have to be removed out of the way-and in spite of all their efforts the harvests are pitifully scanty. 

Hunger and starvation would often stare these unfortunate people in the face if funds and food were not sent to their rescue. 

Lack of water is also a difficulty to be contended with in Herzegovina. The peasants take advantage of every brook and stream, and near the banks one will find o tobacco plantation as green as an oasis in the dessert, a crop of Indian corn, or some olive trees; but rivers and 33

streams have a way of suddenly disappearing from sight in this barren land. The earth seems suddenly to swallow them up, but they actually continue their course in subterranean passages, far below the surface, to flow out again at unexpected places. 

The monotony and desolation of this great waste of rocks and crags is broken every now and then by Oriental towns and picturesque villages, with mediavel castles perched on perpendicular heights, which in themselves, form natural fortresses. The people of Herzegovina are tall and lithe, like most mountain races, while they have a certain dignity which is the outcome of their national pride. 

Bosnia derives its name from the River Bosna, and is rich in mines and forests, with well-watered valleys and ranges of wooded hills. It is the very opposite of Herzegovina in every respect, and instead of meagre crops, they are plentiful and abundant in fertile Bosnia. 

The Celts are said to have invaded Bosnia four hundred yeas B. C. 

In course of time, after the Romans had taken possesion of the coast of Dalmatia, they conquered the whole of Bosnia and Herzegovina, despite the powerful resistance offered by the Illyrian and Celtic races. 

The Romans were eventually swallowed up by the invasion of the Slavs, but vestiges of Roman remains have been preserved up to the present day and are to be found in many parts of the country. 

Very little is known of the history of Bosnia and Herzegovina between the sixth century, the time when the Slavs came to the Balkan Peninsula, and the eleventh century. Christianity was brought to this part of the world in the ninth century, by the two apostles Cyril and Methodius, who went about teaching and winning converts, here as elsewhere among the Slavs. They also made known to the people the art of reading and writing.

Simultaneously with the introduction of Christianity, there arose a sect of heretics known as Bogomils, and the history of Bosnia an Herzegovina centres, to a very large extent, round this religious movement. 

The founding of the Bosnian state is attributed to the Bogomils, but on them is likewise thrown the responsibility for its downfall. They were a powerful body, and their influence was so far-reaching that it gave impetus to the Reformation in western Europe. The Bogomil Church was based on a return to primitive Christianity in all its simplicity, and their religious practices were chiefly founded on stories and legends, circulated by the early Christians, spread through sacred books, and accepted side by side with the New Testament. 34

Bogomil was said to be the name of the leader of this sect, but there are several versions as to the origin of the world. 

The movement had taken firm room among the Serbs by the end of the tenth cen.ury, and continued to spread with ever-increasing vigour as time went on. It is interesting to learn something about the faith of the 

Bogomils. They believed that the universe was created by an evil power, for according to their way of reasoning nothing imperfect could proceed from infinite good; they also believed in light. Consequently, they considered it a duty to reject everything of material origin and to mortify the flesh. Marriage, eating meat, drinking wine, the wearing of costly apparel, were regarded as temptations of the devil, to be steadfastly avoided. They did not build churches, but held prayer meetings, which was their form of worship, in the open air or in private dwellings. 

The chief doctrine of this sect was tɔ observe a strict asceticism. In the thirteenth century they were at the height of their prosperity, but even before this period they had become so numerous that the Roman Catholic Church in Hungary and Dalmatia became alarmed and was aroused to armed opposition.

The ruler of Bosnia at that time was Ban Kulin.* He was a convert of the Bogomilian faith and defended their cause, offering protection of the persecuted, who willingly took refuge in Bosnia when they were threatened to be exterminated elsewhere. During this reign this great 

Slav Puritan movement reached its culminating point. It spread through 

France and Italy and penetrated even to England. 

From this time on, through several centuries, the history of Bosnia is a long record of crusades against the Bogomils, of wars and of bloodshed. The followers of this unorthodox faith were subjected t› dreadful persecutions, and numbers were put to death or allowed to languish away the years in dark dungeons. Those were days of horrible cruelty done in the name of religion. But, although the most inhuman methods were used to uproot the Bogomils, their tormentors only succeeded in quellig them for a time, and they rose again and again with renewed fervour. Even the horrors of the Inquisition, which was established in 

Bosnia in due course, were not able to vanquish the indomitable spirit of the Bogomils.

The Hungarian kings, who were staunch allies of the Popes, organised rigorous persecution of the Bogomils, in their endeovours to *) To this day when the Serbs speak of anything which can be traced far back, they say: "From the time of Ban Kulin," very much as English people quote from the time of William the Conqueror. 35

crush the heretics. Koloman, who was governor of Croatia and Dalmatia at that period, was sent to Bosnia in 1233 with an armed force of men, and he succeeded in seemingly annihilating the movement. 

However, soon after, he left the country, and it sprang into being again. 

Crusades and persecutions were all in vain. The Bogomils could not be exterminated.

Now we turn to certain historical events which are of importance in connection with the development of Bosnia and Herzegovina. Up to the middle of the twelfth century Bosnia owned allegiance to Serbian rule. In 1350, when Serbia had reached the zenith of her power, Tsar 

Dushan invaded and conquered Bosnia, but it was recaptured by the Hungarians shortly after. There follows a brief interlude of semi-independence for Bosnia.*

Some years later, 1376, Stefan Tvrtko, who was related to the King of Hungary, but also a descendant of the Serbian royal house, was crowned King of Bosnia, Serbia and the coastland, without the knowledge of Tsar Lazar, then reigning over the Serbian Empire. Tvrtko thereupon extended the borders of his realm to a very great extent and aimed at forming a southern Slav kingdom. The Bogomils swore allegiance to him in return for not being persecuted, and his troops were almost entirely composed of heretics. After the disastrous defeat of the Serbs at Kosovo, Tvrtko added Dalmatia and Croatia to his kingdom. His reign was distinguished by wisdom and tolerance, and the country enjoyed a span of progress and prosperity under his rule. At his death the powerful position of the Bogomils rapidly declined, and the country fell into a state of confusion. Hungary once more gained the dominion over Bosnia and Herzegovina, while Croatia and Dalmatia were divided between Italy and Austro-Hungary. 

Meanwhile the Bogomils were still a strong force to contend with, and in the fourteenth century the Hungarians waged war against the heretics. The cruelty and butchery committed through the means of the 

Inquisition, which was then in full swing, forced numbers of Bogomils to fly for their lives. The movement was thus held in check for a few years, only to spring into renewed activity as soon as pressure was removed.

The Roman Catholic monks now tried to win over the Bogomils, and to make converts of them, through the power of so-called miracles. *) Dr. Vladan Georgevitch, a vell-known Serbian politician and historian, has published a book, which runs into three volumes, on the subject of Tsar Dushon. The writer of this work stresses the important role of the Bogomils during the reign of Tsar Dushan, in whose famous code of laws certain restrictions were imposed on the heretics. 36

There is an amusing passage in a book written by Arthur J. Evans, who travelled through Bosnia on foot in the year 1877. He says: "Sometimes the monks condescended to work miracles to forward the work of conversion. One, while addressing a congregation of heretics, stepped,' we are assured, 'into a large fire, and with great hilarity stood in the middle of the flames while he recited the fiftieth Psalm.' We hardly need further assurance that many were turned from the error of their ways by miracles like these, especially when it is remembered that the heretics had the alternative or repenting, or repeating the experiment." 

Further on, this same writer relates how the Bogomils were driven into the mountains and were struck bu celestial fire, which was interpreted by the Catholics as a sign of displeasure from Heaven against the scoffers of their religion. The heretics, however, seem to have been unaffected by these phenomena.

At last, after long-continued persecutions, pursued by fire and sword, the Bogomils turned for deliverance to the Turks, who were threatening invasion, trusting to obtain greater tolerance from the followers of Mohammed than that which they had received at the hands of Catholic kings and monks. This is a great blot to the Christian religion of that day.

Some of the Bogomils took refuge in Herzegovina, where the Duke of St. Sava, who was the governor, favoured their religion. (The name 

Herzegovina is derived from the German word Herzog, which means Duke, and therefore the literal interpretation is Duchy.) Here they were able to withstand the Turks for a time, but eventually they were overwhelmed by the martyrdom of these early Slav Puritans would fill a book, but their leaders have left no record, and their history has only been written by their enemies.

The Turks responded to the call of the Bogomils, and in 1463 the principal fortresses and towns in Bosnia capitulated to the invading armies without offering any resistance. 

Hereafter, for some four centuries, the Turks tyrannised over and exploited the Bosnians and Herzegovinians, levying heavy taxes on the people and hindering their development. Fifty years ago there was not a single bookshop in any of the towns and very few schools were in existence, while all Serbian literature, and even Serbian newspapers, were forbidden.

After the Turkish occupation the Bogomils were little heard of, and are soon lost sight of altogether. They were either wiped out or took refuge in the Mohammedan faith. One thing is certain, that the persecution of the Bogomils was the original cause of the present-day Moham- 37

mecan Slavs. The orthodox population in Bosnia and Herzegovina was hard pressed by the Turks, but nothing could make them give up their religion. They had the same saints and heroes as the Serbs, and considered themselves citizens of one fatherland, which is charasteristic of this nation wherever found.

Finally the Turks' cruel treatment towards the S'av inhabitants roused the attention of Europe, and i was decided at the famous Berlin Congress of 1876 that Austria should step in and restore order and justice. This would have been all very well if selfish motives had not got the uppper hand. The Austrians, finding themselves masters of the situation, in order to secure their position made good roads and erected fine public buildings, and did everything they could to ingratiate themselves with the people. Then, wi hout any warning, in the year 1908 the annexation of Bosnia and Herzegovina by Austria was announced to the world. This illegitimate step crea ed violent indignation in Serbia and nearly brought about war. 

It was Sarajevo, the capital of Bosnia, that some years later a blow was dealt which was o involve the whole of Europe war. A young lad, whose patriotic feelings carried him away, fired a shot at the Archduke Ferdinand of Austria, then visiting the country, and killed him on the spot. The result of his tragic incident was like a match thrown into a powder magazine, causing the most terrible war the world has ever known. Thus, by passing through the fire, Bosnia and Herzegovina won their way to freedom.

CHAPTER XVII. 

SARAJEVO 

It may be imagination, or merely in consequence of a personal predilection for Sarajevo, that the very sound of this word is enough to conjure up a vision of fascinating colours, Oriental costumes, veiled women, Eastern bazaars and a host of indescribably picturesque scenes. 

There is music and rhythm in the name of Sarajevo, and yet again, like the fragrance of some exotic perfume, it ac´s as a magic wand to carry one on the wings of imagination to this far-off spot. A well-known writer refers to Sarajevo as the most beautifully situated town in Europe, and I hardly think this is an overestimate. Ano her writer says it recalls "the pearl and emerald settings of Oriental imagery."

Picture to yourself a chain of hills—some green and wooded, others grim and bare, rising up towards rocky summits crowned by forts-while in the hollow below, as though hugged by these encircling arms and guarded by their heights, nestles the town of Sarajevo. Innumerable 38

mosques and domes stand out against background of uneven roofs and tall green poplar trees; slender columns of the minarets point their white fingers upwards to the sky, and one would need to be of a very stolid temperament to remain insensible and unresponsive to the spirit of the East-or call it what you will-which hovers like an intangible presence above these cupolas and spires.

Sarajevo is divided in two by the River Miljashka, which flows through the middle of the town, but some nine or ten bridges link the one side to the other. The most outstanding and attractive feature of 

Sarajevo is to be found in the Oriental bazaars. Here is the quaintest miniature city imaginable, teeming with interest, and providing such a wealth of colour as to delight the eye of any artist. Wonderful things are created by the inmates of the tiny booths, only a few yards square, which are lined up in rows opposite each other. The proprietor squats in the middle of his minute shop-indeed, in most cases there is nowhere else for him to go-and with a few primitive tools he turns out most charming and useful articles. If a good bargain is concluded with purchasers, it is not unusual for these merchants, who look as if they hay stepped out of the pages of "Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves," to offer their customers a tiny cup of black coffee. All the shopkeepers seem to sip black coffee from morning till night, while tobacco in some form or another is as necessary to them as their daily food. 

One can spend hours in the tortuous, busy streets of bazaars, and never weary of wandering from stall to stall; but, if one is not careful, it is very easy to lose one's way in the maze of narrow alleys. 

The bazaars are divided up into sections, and every trade has its own particular streets, so that, if you want to buy leather goods, for instance, you go to the leather quarther a long row of booths dealing in nothing but leather. In like manner, there is the coppersmiths' quarter, the clothes quarter, the sweetmeat quarter, there are tailors, shoemakers, and so on through a long list of arts and crafts. The booths have nɔ windows, and when the shutters are folded back you could step right into the premises through this opening, which exposes the whole shop to sight; but it is unlikely that there would be room enough for the proprietor and customers.

Veiled women, in shapeless garments which successfully cover up the human form completely, move silently along as though they hardly belonged to this world. Their more emancipated sisters, whose religion allows them to discard the veil, shuffle their way among the crowds on wooden clogs, attired in ungainly full skirts, tied in at the ankle, looking 39

like preposterously baggy trousers. Grey-bearded Mohamedans in turbans, gaily coloured sashes wound round the middle of their portly figures, walk side by side with men whose rank and religion can be correctly estimated by the kind of fez they wear. European clothes are quite the exception, and they do not fit into the picture at all. 

There is no noise of traffic in the bazaars, save the hubbub arising from the throng of buyers and sellers, for vehicle do not circulate in these narrow streets. In the midst of this animated little world, standing within an encircling wall, as though holding aloof from the noise and bustle of the crowds intent on mundane pursuits, there rises the stately dome and tall white minaret of the famous Begova Djamija. It was built by Usref Pasha, conqueror of Bosnia, in the sixteenth century, and is the biggest and finest mosque in Sarajevo. Infidel Christians are not allowed to penetrate within the sacred precints of a Mohammedan temple during the time of worship, while women are excluded on the ground that they have no souls. 

Before entering a mosque the followers of Allah halt to perform extensive ablutions in the enclosure outside the edifice. Water is always to be had for this purpose at the entrance to every mosque. Begova 

Djamija, standing in its large, shady courtyard, has a beautiful fountain in the centre, where water flows ceaselessly from innumerable spouts. 

Cleanliness is a rite with the Mohammedans, who never put foot in their places of worship without first washing, at least, the extremities of their anatomy, and even then they proceed on stockinged soles or barefooted,

Sometimes, through the open door of a mosque, one catch a glimpse of the worshippers at prayer within. Great flexibility of limb and body seems to be the essential qualification for a well-disciplined congregation. To the unenlightened observer, watching a swaying mass of humanity, now kneeling to touch the ground with their foreheads, now pivoting backwards while squating on their heels, or again prostrating themselves face downwards, this form of worship seems a little exhausting, to say the least of it.

Several times a day the hodja-turkish priest-mounts the topmost gallery of his minaret, thence to call the faithful to prayer. He utters most weird and plaintive cries as he goes round the turret, summoning his flock from east, west, north and south. Voices ring out simultaneously from all the minarets, mingling with each other in a strange chorus of varying crescendos, and producing an uncanny impression on foreign ears.

The Mohammedan Lent, called Ramazan, is a time of rigorous fast- 40

ing. All through the day, over a period of several weeks, this cult forbids either eating or drinking. Only after sunset may the faithful partake of food and drink, and consequently those who can afford to be idle sleep through the hours of daylight, and begin to live when darkness sets in. However, the majority of the tradespeople, the workers in the field, likewise school children and teachers, all have their usual tasks to perform in spite of the fast. In the bazaars many of the booths are shut during the day-time, and those who are on duty in the shops are very drowsy, if not actually asleep. In the Mohammedan quarter of the town all the windows in the houses are heavily barred or covered with lattice work. These poor creatures, who dwell in the seclusion of four walls, are very much like prisoners, and their creed does not allow them to go out without wearing an impenetrable black mask over their faces, as well as being completely shrouded from head to foot, so that even the hands are covered. Jealous husbands have nothing to fear as long as this state of things continues. 

The modern part of Sarajevo is a great contrast to the tumble-down artistic houses and streets in the old Turkish quarter. Fine buildings and well-paved thoroughfares distinguish the one side of the picture, while the other is purely Oriental in style. A conspicuous landmark of the modern town is the Orthodox cathedral. It was built many years ago, and its vast proportions seem as though intended to put all the mosques in the shade, and in actual fact it towers above them all. On the banks of the River Miljashka there stands a splendid museum, erected by the Austrians and left by them, perhaps regretfully, as a legacy to Sarajevo. 

The historic collections on exhibition inside are worthy of the fine architecture of the museum's exterior. Another monument of the present day, also due to Austrian enterprise, is the Town Hall, built in purely 

Byzantine style.

A few miles beyond Sarajevo is a charming little watering place called Ilidje, and but a stone's-throw from here bubbling streams of crystal clear water issue out of the ground and form the source of the River Bosna. As far back as the time of the Romans, hot sulphur springs were discovered at Ilidje, and, with their usual zeal for baths, the ancient Romans turned this spa to good account. The sulphur springs still continue to attract devotees to baths, and people who like "to take the cure" can do so at Ilidje to their hearts content. 41

This watering-place is also famous for trout-breeding. Those interested in the various phases of the evolution of this delectable fish can observe them in tanks at all stages of their development. If this interesting study provokes an appetite there is a little restaurant close by where one can sample the trout for lunch. 

I fain would dwell on the charms of Sarajevo and the fair land of Bosnia, but we must proceed on our journey towards the coast of the Adriatic, where the prospect is increasingly alluring.

CHAPTER XIX. 

DALMATIA AND PAST HISTORY 

After being exposed to the pitiless glare of the rocks and stones of Herzegovina, it is a delightful change to reach the Dalmatian coast. 

On these entrancing shores one asks for nothing more than just to let the eyes rest on the tranquil blue waves of the sea, which reflects the glorious blue of heaven in an intensity of colour beyond description, and to abandon one's whole being to revelling in the beauty of the panorama unfolded before the enraptured gaze.

The Rivera of the Adriatic certainly vies with the Cote d'Azur of the Mediterranean. Those who know both coasts may find it difficult to give the preference to one or the other, but one thing is certain, that the beauty of Dalmatia would be hard to beat. It is a masterpiece of nature, adorned by the architectural skill and magnificent sculpture of man.

The Karst Mountains, which constitute the mainland, are of a peculiar limestone formation, almost porous, so that water runs off the surface and penetrates to regions below, where rivers course through subterranean passage.

This is identical with the formation of the Herzegovinian mountains, and streams of water gushing out of the rocks, in the way they do both in Dalmatia and in Herzegovina, can only be explained after this fashion. In the plateau of the Karst regions there are deep hollows and yawning clefts where vegetation grows, and these patches of verdure somewhat relieve the monotony of the otherwise bare limestone. Sometimes the dips in the rocks are large enough to plant a crop of potatoes in, while others of insignificant size are abandoned to weeds and low-growing scrub. The soil in these dingles and crevasses is brought down by rain and deposited wherever these is the slightest depression to receive it. Such pockets of earth are naturally 45

eagerly turned to account by the peasants in this barren land, where every inch of ground is precious.

If we scan the history of Dalmatia, we learn that many different masters have ruled over her. Slavs, Italians, Hungarians, Turks and 

Austrians have each in turn claimed this coastland as their own; but recent events have made it into the Riviera of Yugoslavia. One may well wonder how this strip of territory changed hands so often. I will try to trace in as few words as possible the principal phases in its development.

The most important events in the past history of Dalmatia are concerned with prolonged struggles between opposing forces and races, seeking to obtain dominion. In ancient days the land was inhabited by 

Illyrians, but the Greeks and Romans succeeded in conquering it, and as early as 168 B. C. Illyria was made into a Roman province. A long period of progress and prosperity followed this occurrence. Towards the seventh century, however, barbaric tribes invaded the whole land, driving the Slavs, who had settled on the banks of the Danube in the 

Balkan Peninsula, westward down to the Adriatic. The Slavs eventually took the matter into their own hands, and in their return they rose and expelled the hordes of barbarians. Now, after this the only remaining population was composed of Slavs, and from that time on the chief inhabitants of Dalmatia have always been Slavs.

The struggle for supremacy on these shores fills many a chapter of history and resulted in much strife and bloodshed. In he ninth century the Croatians came into power and ruled the mainland, although the coast towns were subjected to Byzantium, and, as mentioned elsewhere, King Koloman united Croatia and Dalmatia under his sceptre in 1102.

During the succeeding centuries the Venetians and Hungarians contested their rights for the possession of Dalmatia, until the Turks appeared upon the scene in 1537. The Byzantines now took possession and installed themselves as masters in this much debated land, where they held sway for nearly a hundred years. Once again, in the seventeenth century, the Venetians regained their former position in Dalmatia, and remained in supreme command until 1796. Hereafter, according to treaty made with Italy, Dalmatia passed into the hands of Austria. 

But this is not yet the end. The tide of events was now to take a new turn. Dalmatia was destined to fall before Napoleon and his advancing armies, who swept across Europe in the beginning of the nineteenth century. And so this part of the Adriatic came into the hands of the 

French for a short period.

In 1815, however, Dalmatia was united to the Austrian monarchy 46

for the best part of a century, and finally, after the Great War, it was assigned to Yugoslavia. 

CHAPTER XX. 

THE ANCIENT REPUBLIC OF RAGUSA 

Although foreigners are best acquainted with the Italian names for the places on the Dalmatian coast, in order to be consistent we must adopt the Slavonic version. The title of this chapter belies the foregoing remark, but in justice to Ragusa, which is of Roman origin, I shall call it by this name until I can explain how it came to be designated as Dubrovnik.

There is a glamour and romance associated with the medieval town of Ragusa which tempts one to ponder for a while over its glorious past. The story of this brave little republic is full of interest, and those who have any imagination will be captivated by the episɔdes enacted within these buttressed walls. Like a fuse, which when ignited flashes for a second into life, so the inner vision, stirred by the history of Ragusa, leaps up to reach those spheres where noble deeds are born, reconstructing and investing the gorgeous pageantry of other days with all the colour and intensity of reality. 

Ragusa was perhaps one of the smallest republics that ever existed -one could skirt its boundaries in less than an hour-but the proud name she earned centuries ago can never be erased. Let us start with the very earliest chapters of her history. 

The birth of Ragusa took place after the destrutcion of the ancient Greek city of Epidaurus. In the sixth century, when the Slavs were first heard of in the Balkan Peninsula, they traversed the mountainous district behind the coastland and came down upon Epidaurus, now known as old Ragusa or, according to the Slav language, Tzafiat. They did not succeed in conquering it, however, until about a hundred years latter. 

The Roman survivors then fled from Epidaurus and built a city on a rock which juts out into the sea, forming a peninsula, the present site of Ragusa. 1 We have it on good authority that Epidaurus was the home of Aesculapius, the god of healing, to whom a temple was erected. The pagan custom of serpent-worship was also practised here. As everyone knows, the serpent is the emblem of wisdom ascribed to Aesculapius, and this symbol-namely, a serpent-is still used as a sign in medicine. 

Epidaurus was probably a much bigger place than Ragusa. However, violent upheavals caused by earthquakes altered the original site on which the Greek city stood, and some aver that they have seen the 47

walls of ancient houses standing beneath the surface of the sea. This still remains to be proved, but traces of volcanic activity are quite obvious.

The present-day barren mountains which rise up behind Ragusa were once covered with pine trees. In this forest the Slavs took up their abode, and dwelt just outside the rock on which the Roman colony had settled; here, ultimately, the two clans of different race united, and the woods which the Slavs had inhabited were included in the city. The 

Slavs now called Ragusa "Dubrovnik," the forest town, from the word dubrava, meaning forest. Henceforth the Slav language became the adopted tongue.

Dubrovnik was primarily a rock of refuge, an asylum for survivors of shipwreck and a home for fugitives. Never in the annals of her history did she betray anyond who sought sanction and protection, even though it exposed her territory to being ravaged or her walls to being belaguered. She gave shelter to friends and foes alike. If, in their hour of need, kings and potentates turned to her for refuge, it made no difference whether they had previously atacked her defences or laid siege to the city, they were never refused hospitality. She was known as "The 

City of Refuge," a title she well deserved.

An incident concerning Richard Coeur de Lion brings a page out of English history to these shores. On his return from the Holy Land a storm overtook the English king in the Adriatic Sea, off the coast of 

Dalmatia. Fearing shipwreck and sudden death, he vowed that should he reach land in safety, he would, out of gratitude, build a church on the spot where he first stood on terra firma again. After prolonged tossing on the waves the ship at last touched at the little island of Lokrum, just opposite the port of Dubrovnik. The senate of the republic sent over with all haste to conduct Richard Coeur de Lion across the bay, and learning of his intention to build a church, the inhabitan's of Dubrovnik begged him to erect it in their city. A special dispenation had to be obtained from the Pope in order to do this, but his sanction was acquired under the condition that a small church be built on the island of Lokrum as well. The edifice raised by Richard in Dubrovnik was of beauty unrivalled in Illyria, but was completely destroyed by an earthquake some years latter.

The Venetians tried time after time to gain authority over Dubrovnik, but the gallant little republic managed to conduct her own affairs by means of a senate, and thus preserved her freedom. Although dependent on Venice to a certain extent, Dubrovnik never submitted to foreign Powers like other cities on the Dalmatian coast. In the struggle to keep her indenpendence she frequently altered her allegiance from 48

one soverign to another. For instance, in 1346 the republic transferred its suzerainty to Dushan, ruler of the Serbian Empire. Thus, according to necessity, Dubrovnik shifted the balance from Venice to Serbia, from Serbia to Hungary, from Hungary to Turkey and so on, and it was only by dint of the greatest vigilance that her integrity was preserved. 

Dubrovnik had her own laws, her own flag, and struck her own coins with the image of St. Blasius. St. Blasius, it seems, once appeared to an Armenian priest in a dream, warning him that the Venetians were about to attack the city. In consequence of this dream preparations were made in time to defend the republic, which was consequently saved. From henceforth St. Blasius was adopted as the Patron Saint of Dubrovnik.

The natives of Dubrovnik were remarkable for their moderation and sobriety. They also took an active part in preventing slaves being sold, and many philanthropic citizens left substantial legacies to be used in purchasing the freedom of slaves. In the Middle Ages the Ragusan merchants were the great traders of the Near East. Having discovered the wealth of the times to be exploited in Bosnia and elsewhere in the Balkans, commercial relations waxed strong between the neighbouring countries, and they began to export merchandise by sea to distant lands. 

These mighty merchants carried on a tremendous trade with the world, and Oliver Cromwell granted special privileges to them in English ports. 

About the fifteenth century the little republic was one of the most prosperous centres in Europe.

But not only trade flourished in Dubrovnik. The golden age of her civilisation and culture, and of the development of her literature and art in the fourteenth century, is almost unsurpassed in history. It was not for nothing that Dubrovnik was known as the Athens of Illyria. 

Owing to the fusion of the Roman and Slavs, the natives of this city were subject to the influence of both races. To Italian genius was added the poetic inspiration belonging to the Slavs, tinged with their faith in the reality of mythological characters. Living in the very heart of romance, surrounded on all sides by the beauties of nature, those who tuned their lyres in Dubrovnik doubtless caught something of this contagious atmosphere. As if awakening to prenatal influences, in response to the echoes of a glorious past, the soul of the poet was quickened by an ember of divine fire aken from the altar of the Muses, and the Serbian drama was given birth in her native tongue. From the fourteenth century the Serbian language eclipsed the Italian altogether, and all literature was written in Serbian. 

A severe earthquake in the seventeenth century almost entirely de- 49

stroyed the city, and numbers of historic monuments crumbled into ruins. 

Dubrovnik never really recovered from this calamity, although it was rebuilt and restored. In 1816 Napoleon seized the republic, depriving it of its liberties and independence, which it never regained. 

Despite the devastating results of the earthquake, Dubrovnik can still boast many relics of the past. Even in the meanest of her streets one may come across a beautiful archway or a sculptured frieze betoking her former glory. The ancient walls which encircled the town (one can hardly call it a city nowadays) during the time of her prosperity still defiantly stand on guard, even though falling away in parts. 

Gay flowers, planted by an invisible hand, appear in every nook and cranny. Their petals generously throw a luxuriant wealth of colour like billowy foam, or showers of spray reflecting a rainbow, over the cracks in the wall, tenderly covering the ugly scars and gaps made by the ravages of time. Where the waves break against the rocky shores, palms and oleanders grow side by side with cyprus and aloe trees, while, stretching away towards the horizon, the great bosom of the sea heaves and ripples in ceaseless motion, sparkling and twinkling in tones of sapphire and ultramarine.

CHAPTER XXII. 

MONTENEGRO, THE RUGGED HOME OF A WARRIOR RACE 

Montenegro is perched among rocks and barren cliffs, tucked away in the mountains like an eagle's eyrie. A quaint old legend tells how, when God was forming the earth and raising up mountains, he let fall his bag of stones of Montenegro, and blocks of granite and huge boulders of rock rolled about in all directions, covering the whole ground. You may think it is a very childish version, but this race of bold mountaineers has a strain of naive simplicity in its blood, and even the fiercest warriors are not far removed from the child.

The Montenegrins are a stalwart race, purely Serbian in origin, whose ancestors possessed the inborn bravery of the true warrior. This country of the Black Mountains is the smallest, but perhaps the most ruggedly picturesque, territory in the whole of Yugoslavia. Accustomed to be ever on the alert to guard their homes from molestation, the Montenegrin mode of living had to be adapted to circumstances. They were seldom able to lay their rifles aside and turn their thoughts to any kind of occupation. Consequently it was the women who had to bear the brunt of the household cares and do the lion's share of the work. Not only did they have their own duties to fulfil, but those of their husbands as well. The wonder is how the women managed to look after the children, till and sow the soil, tend the flocks and provide clothing for the entire family. Even to this day it is the "weaker sex" who do all the hard work. It would never occur to a 

Montenegrin that there was anything unchivalrous in letting his wife carry a burden balanced on her head, while he walked quietly along empty handed! On the contrary, it seems quite natural, from the Montenegrin's point of view, that women should trive from morning till night, while men spend their time in idleness. It has never been different, and both parties accept this unjust division of labour as the inevitable course of events. The indolence of the Montenegrin man has become proverbial. When the Serb speaks of an idle person, he will usually say, "Soand-so is a regular Montenegrin!" It is a weakness of the Montenegrins to like to be dressed up in all their finery, with glittering weapons fast- *) The great English statesman, W. E. Gladstone, and the former Poet Laureate, Lord

There is a great feeling of equality among the people of this race. 

What concerns one, concerns all, and this principle of solidarity has no doubt been fostered by their patriarchal way of living. Several generations dwell under one roof, obeying the authority of the eldest member of the clan, whose word is law. Unfortunately this rather ideal cooperative system of living is dying out, although it still exists both here and in Serbia.

Montenegro can be approached from the mainland as well as from Boka Kotorska on the Dalmatian coast. A fine road leads from Peć, in southern Serbia, across the mountains to Cetigne. It traverses wild and magnificent tract of country, sometimes mounting up to the very top of steep passes, sometimes descending into the depths of the valley, winding its way through regions which seem to belong to another world. 

There are few towns and villages worthy of the name in this Land of the Black Mountains. The people are scattered over different parts of the country, and live in groups, wherever there is any soil to cultivate. 

Small crops of corn and maize, many of them no bigger than a rug or a carpet, are thus dotted about in this wilderness of rocks and stones, where man has to put his strength against the forces of nature. 

CHAPTER XXIII. 

EXTRACTS FROM THE HISTORY OF MONTENEGRO 

The history of Montenegro is chiefly gathered from the ballads and national songs. In fact, it is only owing to these poems that the early struggles of this country have been preserved. 

With the fall of the Serbian Empire, after the Battle of Kosovo, many Serbs fled for refuge to the Black Mountains, where they took up their abode. Exiled by tragic events from their own land, they were glad to make Montenegro their new home, where they were among their own kindred and could live in freedom. But they were now nothing more than nomads. Thrown back on their own resources, the inhabitants of Montenegro reverted to primitive conditions of existence. Obeying their own sense of justice, following the instinct to preserve life at any cost, some adopted the devious career of the freebooter. The haidouks or brigands of to-day, who still infest certain parts of the Black Mountains, have no odubt inherited some of the reckless tendencies of their early forefathers. 

One of the chief rebels (harembasha) of that period, named Ivo 55

Tzrnoyevitch, played an inmportant role, and to him are ascribed many brave and marvellous deeds. He warded off Turkish assaults many and many a time, and when finally forced to give way before the enemy he did not surrender, but retreated deeper into the heart of the mountains, where he entrenched himself with his faithful followers. Fearless and staunch, these highland chiefs became a powerful force, and their reputation as desperate fighters spread through the land. The exploits and conquests of Ivo Tzrnoyevitch are favourite themes in the ballads. 

When the descendants of Ivo died out, Montenegro was governed by bishops, and wars and skirmishes continued to rack the country, with only short spells of peace to punctuate time. In the early eighteenth century a member of the House of Petrovitch Niegosh was elected bishop and ruler over the little state. He was a descendant of a certain clan of Slavs from Niegosh in Herzegovina, who had emigrated to the Black Mountains and formed a colony there centuries ago. Returning tɔ Montenegro, after having been consecrated by the head of the Serbian Church in Hungary, Danilo Petrovitch Niegosh invoked his subjects to dispatch all the Mohammedans in the land in one night, should they refuse to be baptised. A terrible massacre ensued, which is related at great length in some of the historic lays. 

The House of Petrovitch Niegosh soon acquired supreme power in the country, and the right of government continued to be wielded by his descendants.

Although the Turks virtually took possesion of Montenegro in eighteenth century-at the same time as they occupied the Dalmatian coast-there remained certain inaccessible parts of the mountains which were still held by the highland chieftains. From their ambuscade in the rocks they constantlly attacked the Turks, harassing them also by guerrilla warfare. Finally, after many years, the Montenegrins succeeded in so weakening the enemy that they fled in confusion. 

But there was no question of peace yet a long while. The Turks on the borderland still directed their arms against the Montenegrins, and victory perched now on one side of the opposing banners now on the other. However, failure never daunted the invicible spirit of the fighters in their mountain home. They willingly grappled with other foes when the Turks evacuated these parts, and threw down the gauntlet to the French and the Austrians in return, never surrendering until their last shot was fired. In the nineteenth century they came to terms with 

Austria, but the fighting instincts of the Montenegrins could not be 56

content with a quiet life for long, and there were constant incidents >ccurring on the frontiers.

Under the rule of Peter II. (1830-1851), a member of the House of Petrovitch Niegosh, schools were opened and the first steps made towards establishing educa.ion and civilisation. Peter II. ranks as the greatest Serbian poet, and his work entitled Gorski Vijenatz (The Mountain Crown) has acquired fame, not only in this country, but also abroad. 

Danilo Petrovitch, who succeeded Peter II., took the title of prince. 

During his reign there was sill fighting goin on between the Montenegrins and the Turks. In 1878, however, the small principality was granted independence by the Powers, and her enemies were advised to leave her in peace.

Prince Nicholas, the last ruler of Montenegro, was autɔcratic in his government, like his predecessor; but owing to his practical good sense, he steered the country into peace and prosperity. Nevertheless he was deposed by his people, through the National Assembly, after the war. 

His dream, which was to see the restoration of the Serbian race, was realised in 1918.

National dress is generally worn by the people of Montenegro. 

Both men and women still cling to the picturesque garments of long ago, which are particularly graceful and beautiful in colouring. The caps which the men wear on the back of their heads are partly crimson and partly black: crimson in memory of the blood shed in defence of their land, and black as a sign of mourning for the fatal Battle of Kosovo.

Cetigne is about as unlike a capital as it is possible to imagine. It has no pretensions whatever, and is more of a village than a town. The chief street has a long row of houses on either side, seldom more than one storey high, and such shops as there are, contain nothing but the barest necessities. It was here in Cetigne that King Alexander of Yugoslavia was born, his mother, Princess Zorka, having been a daughter of Prince Nicholas. This strengthens the ties of the past, and binds 

Montenegro to-day still more closely to the kingdom of the Yugoslavia. 57

CHAPTER XXIV. 

THE AMERICAN YUGOSLAVS By IVAN MLADINEO 

The Slavonic family embraces the Eastern Slavs (Russians and 

Ukrainians), the Northern Slavs (Poles, Czechs and Slovaks), and the 

Southern Slavs (Slovenes, Croats, Serbs and Bulgarians). The last group is commonly called JUGOSLAV (from the word "jug" meaning south) with the exception of the Bulgarians, who are presently not included in this appelation. All Slavs today are found in five independent national states, namely, Russia, Poland, Czecho-Slovakia, Yugoslavia and Bulgaria, with the exception of some 600,000 compactly settled Yugoslavs in Italy, and about 100,000 in Austria. 

It is necessary to stress the above facts because of the too frequent confusion in the minds of the average people between Yugoslavia and Czecho-Slovakia.

The Yugoslav group, however, often appears in the public press under the names of its subdivisions, as the Slovenes, the Croats, and the Serbs. Due to their close kinship and their interwoven interests in the 

United States, they are a single group with variations in the characteristics peculiar to each individual subdivision. 

Yugoslavs are found in every State of the Union in numbers sufficiently large to enable them to have organized groups in some 800 towns and cities in 38 States of the Union and in Alaska. Over 5,000 of such local organizations are scattered from Boston to Los Angeles, and from Duluth to New Orleans. Although they are well organized, their first national organizations were formed only forty years ago. 

This fact may denote that they belong to the so-called "newer-immigration". This is true to the extent that the bulk of these people came to the American shores since 1890, as did most of the other foreign races. 

Many had come to America, however, long before the railroads were built and even long before our independent national government was formed.

EARLY IMMIGRATION 

It was often claimed, and historical deductions advanced in support thereof, that among the Columbus' crew were sailors from the Dalmatian coast (Dalmatia is a Province of Yugoslavia, situated along the eastern shore of the Adriatic Sea.) However it is difficult to prove the claim for want of facts in regard to the racial composition of the men who discovered America. 58

This year the American history is celebrating the 350th anniversary of the discovery of Roanoke Island recalling the establishing of the first English settlement in the New World. North Carolina is building a shrine to this first colony within the present confines of the State. The colony was, in fact, twice settled a full score of years before Jamestown and thus takes the Anglo-American history back into the sixteenth century.

Sir Walter Raleigh's second colony (the first being established on 

July 29, 1585) under John White, arrived on July 22, 1586, intending certainly to be a permanent one, for it consisted of ninety five men, seventeen women and nine children. In Augus the "first white child" was born in British Colonial America and named Virginia. Nine days later the child's grandfather, Governor White, sailed away to England, and from that day to this there is no certain word of what happened to the company he left behind him, or which the infant was the youngest member.

Not until spring of 1590 was White able to return to Roanoke. "At the place where they were left in sundry houses (White writes) we found the houses taken down and the place very strongly enclosed with a high palisade of great trees, with curtains and flankers, very fort-like; and one of the chief trees or posts at the entrance had the bark taken off, and five feet from the ground, in fair capital letters, was graven 

CROATAN, without any cross or sign of distress."

This is the first recorded history connecting America with the Yugoslavs or, rather, with the Croatian branch. It is believed that a Croatian ship, stopping at this first permanent settlement in America, left its imprint on the big tree, and, consequently, gave its name to one of the islands of North Carolina—"Croatan".

The first known mass movement of the Yugoslavs to America dates back to the early eighteenth century. After the unsuccessful and bloody uprisings of the peasants in Croatia and Slovenia against their feudal lords in 1573, and of the Reformation movement, ruthlessly crushed by the edict of the Archduke Ferdinand in 1598, many Yugoslavs found refuge in Prussia, having gone there upon the invitation of King Frederick William, who favored the Protestants. A century later their descendants decided to find a haven in New World. During the first half of the eighteenth century they set their sails toward America. One group, compose of 1200 persons, went to Georgia. There they settled on the right bank of the Savannah River at the confluence of a small creek, which they named "Ebenezer". Pastors Gronau and Bolcius led the group.

These early immigrants introduced the cultivation of 59

the silk worm in Georgia, an industry engaged in by many in their original native land before they moved to Prussia. Soon after the Civil 

War the settlement was abandoned and only the cemetery remains as a monument to a once thriving colony of the first Yugoslav settlers in America.

Dalmatian sailors were world renowned for their seamanship, courage and love of adventure. Long before the discovery of America their ships navigated all the known sea routes. Only a few years ago a seamen's guild in Dalmatia celebrated its thousandth anniversary. 

It is recorded that a Dalmatian ship sailed to America by way of India at the beginning of the eighteenth century; they were old-timers in 

California when the first Yankee got there.

A port of call for most of the Yugoslavs sailing to the New World was New Orleans. Until the middle of the nineteenth century this was the chief settlement of the Yugoslavs in America. There many a sailor married and made it his home. Aside from shipping, they were engaged in the oyster industry, controling it for many a decade. From there they wandered throughout the length and breath of the country. When gold was discovered in California they also joined the famous "Gold Rush". 

Most of the Yugoslav "Fortyniners" set out from New Orleans. They advised their relatives and friends in the Old Country of the fortunes made in the "shiny metal" with the result that several ships sailed from their native shores carrying new prospectors to the Golden Gate. California has always had a special attraction for the Dalmatian immigrants, and naturally so. Climatically and topographicaly it reminds them strongly of their own lovely Adriatic coastland. Upon the shores of the Pacific they were unusually successful. 

Yugoslav immigrants are recognized today as the pioneers in apple, grape and fishing industries of California and along the whole Pacific coast. It is recorded that one "Mark Rabasa, apple dealer," a native of 

Dalmatia in Yugoslavia, was the first man engaged in apple business in Watsonville, Pajaro Valley, in the late 70's. This date marks the beginning of this universally known industry. 

Jack London (in his novel "The Valley of the Moon") describes at length and with great admiration the result of the "tenderness and love," which transformed the 12,000 acres of the Pajaro Valley into "one of the most wonderful demonstrations in the United States." He calls it "New Dalmatia" and credits "those first ragged Adriatic Slavs" for making it "Apple Paradise."

Others began to cultivate grapes, and fishing. Large fleet of fishing boats in San Pedro, in Monterey, and on the Columbia River are man- 60

ned and owned by Yugoslav fishermen. A fleet of two hundred modern fishing boats on the Columbia River alone represent an investment of several millions of dollars and are the property of the Yugoslav immigrants. The largest sardine, tuna and mackerel cannery in California belongs to the Yugoslavs. Most famous restaurants in San Francisco, until recently were owned and operated by them. 

Though New Orleans is the oldest existing Yugoslav settlement in the United States, their first organization, however, was formed in San 

Francisco in 1857, as "The Slavonian Mutual and Benevolent Society."

Its purpose was social and beneficial, helping its members in times of illness and distress. The official languages were "English and 

Slavonian." These early settlers called their mother tongue by its general name "Slavonian" (meaning, Slavonic) and even today the Yugoslavs along the Pacific Coast and around the Gulf of Mexico are designated as "Slavonians." Only in 1874 the New Orleans Yugoslavs organized themselves in the "United Slavonian Benevolent Association of New Orleans." The father of the writer of these lines was one of its founders. This organization is this year celebrating its sixtieth anniversary.

The first organization on the Atlantic Coast was founded in New York in 1880, and is now known as "The First Croatian Benefit Society." 

Two years later the Slovenes organized their first association, "The Independent Society of St. Joseph," in Calumet, Mich. Thus the first four organizations were established on the four extreme points of the United States. These pioneer associations, which still exist, can proudly point to five thousand of their offsprings in all sections of the Country. 

CIVILIZATION OF THE NEW CONTINENT 

Other nations of the World have contributed men of courage and zeal to the process of conquest of this continent. Yugoslavs, though one of the smaller nationalities, can also pride themselves with the contributions their co-nationals have rendered to the same goal. 

Among the earliest known Yugoslav arrivals into this part of the world was Baron Ivan Rataj, a Croatian Jesuit, who died in 1640 while a missionary among the New Mexico Indians. He was followed by many others of his countrymen. Most noted among them was Ferdinand 

Konšćak, better known under the Spanish adaption of his name, Gonzales, who came to Mexico in 1730. During many years of his pioneering work he made the first known map of Lower California. Another prominent Croatian missionary was Joseph Kundek, who arrived in 1838. He established several towns in the Middle West, among them 

Ferdinand and Jasper in the State of Indiana. 61

In connection with this missionary work it is notable that it was financed by contribution of the people in Croatia. According to the existing records in the archives of the Archibishopric of Zagreb in Croatia, 57,000 florins were sent from Croatia between 1830 and 1840 for the maintenance of churches and schools in America. 

The Slovene missionaries concentrated their efforts to the North- 

West. Frederick Baraga, the first among them, arrived in 1830. He devoted his whole life to the missionary work among the Indians in northern Michigan and Wisconsin, and in eastern Minnesota. In 1853 he was consecrated a Bishop of the newly established Marquette Diocese. 

State of Michigan honored him by naming one of its counties in his memory. Baraga was also a man of letters. He wrote and published a Grammar and a Dictionary of the Chippewa language. He translated the Bible into this language for his Chippewa flock. He also published a prayer-book in the language of the Ottawa Indians. 

Baraga was followed by several countrymen of his. In 1845 

Ignatius Mrak arrived, becoming a Bishop 24 years later. Then came 

Ivan Vertin in 1863, being consecrated in 1879. These two occupied the See established by their predecessor at Marquette, Mich. The fourth bishop, Jacob Trobec of St. Cloud, Minn., was consecrated in 1897, and came to America in 1864. The last bishop in this remarkable list was Ivan Stariha,' who arrived in 1867, becoming a bishop at Lead, So. 

Dakota in 1902. All the bishops died here in active service, while Stariha died in his native country. One of the most noted missionaries of that period was Francis S. Pirc, who came to America in 1835 and remained in service among the Indians of the North-West until 1873. 

There were many others, some of them even now being engaged in this work.

To the present generation this is ancient history. It is important to recall, however, these facts if for no other reason than to reassert a well known but too often overlooked fact, that America of today-comfortable, secure, great and powerful-is the result of sacrificies of men of every race of which it is composed. In this arduous work the Slavs in general, and the Yugoslavs in particular have undeniably contributed their full share.

LATER IMMIGRATION 

Yugoslav immigration in larger numbers began after 1890. It was chiefly caused by the spread throughout Europe of wonderful tales of easy riches in America. It affected people of all nations of Europe, especially those who were indebted and otherwise in material difficulties. 62

Improvements in the means of transportation facilitated the satisfaction of the urge to financial independence. 

This immigration differed from the earlier one in the fact that it came from the soil, while the earlier immigrants were seafaring people accustomed through generations to face new conditions and dealt in acclimatizing themselves to them. The later immigrants came, generally, with the intention of remaining here a few years until they achieve their object then to return to their old homes. At first they were hired by various industrial concerns and brought over. Later they came to a relative, a friend, or to someone from their village who came earlier to America. Their destination were mosly the industrial centers of the 

East and of the Middle West. 

Unskilled, except in farming which required initial capital and a long period of time before able to realize upon their labor, they preferred hard manual work as laborers in mines, steel and iron works, in quarries, on railroad tracks, as longshoremen, masons and similar. Seeing that the expected fabulous riches did not come their way, they prolonged their stay in America, with it their hopes of success diminished. Anxious to improve their lot they joined labor unions and in some of them they formed a majority in their respective locals. Since the World War many have changed their occupations fitting themselves for different tradesin automotive and other kind of factories, some of them even going to farming. But the majority still cling to their original industrial pursuits. 

With the increase of population the group life intensified. Churches, fraternal and cultural societies sprung up. Each subdivision of the Yugoslav immigrants grouped itself separately. This was caused, primarily, for three reasons: (1) by intense group consciousness developed at the turn of the century; (2) by language differences between the 

Slovenian on one hand and the Serbo-Croatian on the other; and (3) by the difference in religious-the Croats and Slovenes belonging to the Roman Catholic and the Serbs to the Eastern Orthodox church. Catholics were under the jurisdiction of the respective Catholic bishops. The 

Serbian Orthodox Church, until after the world war, was under the jurisdiction of Russian bishops in America. Since then it is organized as a separate diocese with its own bishop for the United States and Canada, residing in Chicago and appointed by the Serbian Orthodox Patriarch of Belgrade, Yugoslavia. The first Yugoslaw church was established in 

Brockway, Minn. by the Slovenian farmers in 1871. Today there are some 70 Roman Catholic and 30 Serbian Orthodox parishes and churches in the United States maintained by the Yugoslavs. 63

With the establishment of the churches, schools were organized. 

Some 38 full time schools with some 12,000 pupils exist today. Half of them belong to the Croatian and half to the Slovenian groups. 

Yugoslav national fraternal and insurance organizations are certainly an achievement considering the fact that they were instituted and administered by men with no education above the fourth grammar grade. The rosters of these organizations list today some 270,000 members, or whom 80,000 are in the Junior Branch, composed chiefly of the American-born boys and girls. The assets of these organizations amount to over $20,000,000 and the total insurance in force to more than $170,000,000. The members or their beneficiaries receive annually about $5,000,000 in various benefits. Aside from these there are several hundred local benefit societies not connected with the national federations. 

Singing, dramatic and physical culture (Sokol) societies came into being in every appreciable settlement. Libraries and even literary circles were formed. Many volumes, some of literary value, were published. 

Several hundred National Homes were established which serve as community centers for all their activities. As the family life grew, Building and Loan Associations were formed.

The press, naturally, was the instrument or encouragement and the recorder of activities and life in every section of the Country. The first 

Yugoslav publication in America appeared in San Francisco, in 1884, named "Slavenska Sloga" (The Slavonic Unity), followed by "Napredak" (The Progress) in Hoboken, N. J., in 18r1, and "Amerikanski

Slovenec" in Chicago, Ill., the same year. Of these only the latter exists. Within the last half century some 200 publications appeared, their life ranging from a single issue to 43 years of existence. Today there are 44 established Yugoslav publications in the United States, varying in circulation from a thousand to 60,000 copies. Of these eight are dailies and most of the rest are weeklies. 

In later years many of the newspapers and magazines introduced English sections and pages devoted primarily to their American born readers. Three publications appear in English only, one of them as the organ of the University students of Yugoslav extraction. 

POLITICAL ACTIVITIES 

Yugoslavs are politically inclined. Their centuries long struggle for national existence, with the races who dominated them, made them keenly political. They brought with them to America, as did other races and peoples, the differences of opinion on many political questions 64

affecting their countrymen in Europe. This was especially accentuated during the World War, when they were called upon to acquaint the World with the political aspirations of their people in the Old Country, who were unable to express themselves.

Though greatly occupied with European problems, they have neglected to interest themselves in the political life of their new Country. 

Their vote was decisive even nationally. It happened in 1916. The 

Yugoslav press unanimously supported Woodrow Wilson for reelection. 

The Yugoslav National Committee, headed by the late Dr. Anthony 

Biankini of Chicago, utilized all its contac's, prestige and organization for the promotion of the chances of the Democratic Party. Yugoslavs in California were traditionally Republican. The vigorous campaign of this Committee and of the racial press, lead by the daily "Hrvatska Zastava" of Chicago (then edited by the writer of this article), converted these standpat Republicans of California to the Democratic Party. 

The vote of California decided the elections. The Yugoslavs justly claim that their ballots elected Woodrow Wils ɔn in 1916. Wilson's plurality in California was only 3,806.

Many a Yugoslav occupies a place of honor and trust in his community in various municipal positions. Several of them are members of State Legislatures and some sit as judges in several States. S› far the National Administration has failed to recognize the support received from this group.

DIVERSIFIED FIELD OF ENDEAVOR 

As industrial workers the Yugoslavs of America have contributed their full share in the developement of this modern giant called the United States of America. There is not the slightest doubt that they have given far more in sweat and brow, in severed limbs, in undermined health and happiness, than they have received in return. Thousands of lives sacrificed at the altar of our industrial progress can not be appraised in material values, as there are no equivalents to human life. 

But if they gave to the United States nothing more than the world renowned wizard, Nikola Tesla, their contribution to the scientific progress of this Country and of the World would be more than adequate. 

Neither the space nor the occasion permit us to list his numerous inventions and discoveries which caused a revolution in our mode of living. 

It will suffice to state that no man has enriched our knowledge in the field of electricity and of its practical possibilities, nor caused the advancement of the world in the electro-technical field, more than Nikola 

Tesla. 

He was first to announce cosmic rays, in the '90s.
