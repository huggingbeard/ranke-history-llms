This report is made and published pursuant to subsection (d) of section 7 of the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951, as amended. 

Investigation No. 51 under section 7 of the Trade Agreements 

Extension Act of 1951, as amended, to which this report relates, was instituted on March 6, 1956, upon application made February 21, 1956, by the Riegel Textile Corp., New York, N. Y., for the purpose of determining whether, as a result in whole or in part of the customs treatment, reflecting concessions granted thereon in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, pillowcases, wholly or in chief value of cotton, provided for in paragraph 911 (b) of the Tariff Act of 1930, are being imported into the United States in such increased quantities, either actual or relative, as to cause or threaten serious injury to the domestic industry producing like or directly competitive products.

Customs Treatment of Imported Articles Covered by the Investigation

Pillowcases, wholly or in chief value of cotton, are provided for in paragraph 911 (b) of the Tariff Act of 1930. The duty originally imposed was 25 percent ad valorem; it was reduced to 20 percent ad valorem pursuant to a concession granted in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, effective April 21, 1948; the duty was further reduced to 12-1/2 percent ad valorem, effective September 10, 1955, as a result of a concession granted in the 1955 negotiations at Geneva involving Japan. The last-mentioned rate of duty also reflects the prevailing concession granted with respect to cotton pillowcases under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade.

The Commission always considers in evidence the views of applicants and other interested parties concerning what coverage of imports and what coverage of domestic production are appropriate in each of its escapeclause investigations, but the Commission has the legal responsibility for making the final determinations. The Commission's views, therefore, may be at variance with those of any or all the interested parties. 

In the instant case, the Commission found that the imports to be considered embrace all imports of unembroidered cotton pillowcases described in the Commission's public notice and that the domestic industry producing articles that are like or directly competitive with such imported pillowcases was coextensive with the domestic production of all unembroidered pillowcases made of cotton cloth, irrespective of the construction of the cloth.

Although some domestic manufacturers of cotton pillowcases also fabricate the cloth from which their cases are made, the Commission does not regard the production of cotton pillowcases as embracing also the production of such cloth. Only a very small part of the domestic production of cotton cloth of the kinds used in making pillowcases is actually used in such production. Many producers, including the applicant in this investigation, purchase from others all the cloth that they use in making pillowcases. 

Nature of import competition

Although all pillowcases are used for the same general purpose, cases made from different fabrics are not equally acceptable to all buyers.

For example, some purchasers have strong preferences for cases made of "muslin"; others have strong preferences for those made of "percale"; and virtually all buyers are influenced in greater or lesser degree by price considerations.

The generic terms that are used to describe the fabrics from which cotton pillowcases are made are more meaningful to cloth manufacturers than to the purchasers of the pillowcases. Few buyers of them at retail (or even at wholesale) are even familiar with the term "printcloth" and few others are always able to distinguish between a muslin and a percale case, except on the basis of manufacturers' markings. Many cotton pillowcases, notably those made of printcloth, bear no manufacturers' reference to the fabric of which they are made; such cases are simply marketed as pillowcases, usually of some specified dimensions and specified number of threads per square inch. 

The cotton fabrics referred to above differ principally in regard to (1) the number of threads per square inch, (2) the diameter of the threads (yarn number), and (3) whether the yarn used is "carded" or "combed." Printcloth used for pillowcases generally has somewhere between 

108 and 180 threads to the square inch, and is made of carded yarn that ranges between 30s and 40s. Muslin used for pillowcases usually has from 112 to 140 threads to the square inch and is made of carded yarn between 188 and 22s. Percale has 180 threads or more to the square inch and is made of yarn of 40s or finer that is either carded or combed. 

From these descriptions, it is clear that, even from a purely technical standpoint, there are no sharp lines of demarcation between the different kinds of cloth used in making cases. From an economic standpoint, the lines are even less sharp.

Some printcloth pillowcases are higher priced than many muslin pillowcases and a small proportion are even higher priced than some percale cases; but the bulk of the printcloth pillowcases--imported and domestic-sell at lower prices than the bulk of the muslin cases and almost all of the percale cases. It should not be inferred from this observation, however, that most imported printcloth pillowcases compete principally with domestic printcloth pillowcases. 

Such is not the fact. Most imported cotton pillowcases offer no more serious competition to the higher priced, domestic printcloth pillowcases than they do to the higher priced, domestic muslin and percale pillowcases. 

Most of the imported printcloth pillowcases are sold in the United States on the basis of their low price and therefore compete most directly with the lowest priced domestic pillowcases. These domestic cases include not only a large part of the domestic production of printcloth cases (notably the bulk of the output of the applicant) but also a very much larger quantity of low-priced domestic cases made principally of muslin (especially types 112 and 128 1/), as well as some made of percale. These muslin and percale cases include "seconds," "imperfects," "special shipments" and the like that are marketed at considerably lower prices than their counterpart of first-grade, nationally advertised brands of pillowcases.

The extent to which individual pillowcase manufacturers have been affected by imports of low-priced printcloth cases has varied roughly with the ratio of their output of low-priced cases to their total output of cases. That ratio has been so small for all producers other than the applicant that none of them have claimed to be seriously injured by the import competition. The applicant's ratio has been high and, consequently, the applicant may well have been injured by such competition. 

If so, the applicant was injured not because the imported cases were made of printcloth, but rather because they were low-priced. 

The "domestic industry"

The legislative history of the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 

1951, as amended, clearly indicates that the Congress did not intend that, in the present investigation, the Commission should consider that the applicant's production of printcloth pillowcases accounts for only an exceedingly small part of its total production of all articles. Nor did the Congress intend that the Commission should take into account that the import competition complained of by the applicant could not have important consequences on the profitability of its overall operations. The congressional intent unquestionably was that the Commission should identify the domestic industry in this instance as being coextensive with the domestic production solely of those articles that are like or directly competitive with the offending imports. 

The escape-clause legislation nevertheless permits the Commission some measure of discretion in determining the scope of the domestic industry. Such a term as "industry" defies precise, all inclusive, definition. In the "escape clause" law, the Congress has established

the limits of inquiry within which the Commission must remain in arriving at a decision. These limits, however, are broad enough to permit of intelligent analyses of the facts obtained in an investigation. The 

Commission is limited by the law to a determination of whether the domestic industry producing articles that are like or directly competitive with imports of unembroidered cotton pillowcases is being injured or threatened with serious injury. Identification of "like or directly competitive" domestic articles calls for careful analyses and the exercise of expert judgment.

In this instance, the Commission could conceivably have identified the "domestic industry" in broader terms than it has, say, "a cotton sheet and pillowcase industry." Similarly, a narrower definition could have been adopted. But, neither the broader nor the narrower conception could be adopted if based upon an arbitrary identification of "industry" unfounded in fact.

The only basis on which the Commission could identify the domestic manufacturers of printcloth pillowcases as constituting an industry, within the meaning of section 7 of the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951, as amended, would be essentially specious. All that such producers appear to have in common is a comfortably ambiguous description of the fabric that they use. As previously observed, domestic pillowcases made from such fabric are no more "like or directly competitive" with imports of cotton pillowcases (most of which are made

of "printcloth") than are the very much larger quantities of domestic pillowcases made of other cotton fabrics, notably muslin. The principal impact of the import competition is on low-priced domestic pillowcases, whether of printcloth, muslin, or even percale. 

The Commission does not find it practicable to identify the domestic industry in question as that which produces only low-priced, unembroidered cotton pillowcases, because most of such cases are produced by the same concerns, in the same plants, on the same kinds of machinery, and, more often than not, by the same workers that are employed in the production of higher priced unembroidered cotton pillowcases. Moreover, while the type and value of fabric used may serve to identify the finished pillowcases for certain purposes, the cutting, sewing, and finishing operations that are involved in making them are essentially the same for all cotton fabrics. The particular fabric used by a manufacturer depends upon its cost, its suitability for the market, and consumers' preferences rather than upon manufacturing considerations.

Commission is therefore content to regard the domestic industry in question as that which converts cotton fabric/irrespective of the name by which it may be known, or its construction, or its value--into unembroidered pillowcases.

Domestic production 

Most of the cotton pillowcases made in the United States are produced in plants in which articles other than pillowcases (notably bed sheets) are made; and most of the larger of such plants are owned by corporations

that operate many other plants in which a wide range of still other textile articles are made. The operations of some of these corporations are integrated vertically, some are integrated horizontally and some, both ways.

Pillowcases are also made by contracting concerns that merely process cloth supplied to them by others, irrespective of whether the cloth is printcloth, muslin, or percale.

The bulk of the production of cotton pillowcases consists of muslin and percale, and many concerns confine their production of cotton pillowcases to one or the other or both of these fabrics. 

Some dozen concerns account for the bulk of the output; numerous small concerns account for the remainder. Of the 48 pillowcase manufacturers (including contractors) who responded to a recent questionnaire prepared by the Commission, 20 produced cases wholly or principally from muslin or percale sheeting, and 28, wholly or principally from printcloth. 2 Ten manufacturers made cases of both sheeting and printcloth. The bulk of the cotton pillowcases produced domestically are made in plants that are located in Maine, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia, Alabama, Oklahoma, and Texas.

Most of the so-called printcloth pillowcases are produced by two large integrated concerns that also make, among other things, some muslin pillowcases. A considerable number of small producers also make printcloth pillowcases but most of them also make other textile products, such as bedspreads, draperies, and cloth novelties. Of the

Notwithstanding, no domestic manufacturer of muslin or percale pillowcases claimed serious injury from import competition.

Imports 

Official United States statistics do not report imports of cotton pillowcases separately. Such imports are reported together with those of cotton sheets. Until 1953, imports of cotton pillowcases were known to be very small; and since then, virtually all of the considerable imports have entered from Japan. Inasmuch as the Japanese Government reports exports of pillowcases and cotton sheets separately, it has been possible to prepare accurate estimates of United States imports of pillowcases in the years commencing with 1954 (see table 10).

The complainant in the present investigation did not regard the level of imports of pillowcases in the years preceding 1954 as causing or threatening serious injury. The available data, therefore, cover the period of principal interest in this investigation.

Imports of cotton pillowcases from Japan in 1953 totaled almost 66 thousand dozen (see table 8), which was more than twice the annual averɛje volume in 1936-40. 1 Imports from Japan rose to 110 thousand dozen in 1954 and to 977 thousand dozen in 1955. In the period from 

January 1953 through June 1956, monthly imports of pillowcases from Japan rose from mid-1954 to the end of 1955 and declined thereafter. 

All indications are that the imports for the whole of 1956 will be very much below those of 1955.

The exceptionally large imports in 1955 were no doubt partly the result of the reduction in the United States duty in that year, but other factors also were important. Low-quality, Japanese pillowcases were available at very attractive prices, and a number of United States importers felt that a quick profit could be realized by importing them in volume and marketing them at low markups, especially through unaccustomed types of outlets (such as cutrate drug stores) that were interested in "traffic builders." In the latter half of 1955 and the first half of 1956, a high and rising proportion of the pillowcases imported from Japan were of printcloth having 128 to 132 threads per square inch. 

Previously, the imports consisted preponderantly of those having 138 to 144 threads per square inch (see table 12). Notwithstanding this deterioration in the quality of the imports, the average foreign unit value of imports rose, reflecting rising production costs in Japan and reduced availability of distress merchandise in that country. These developments coincided with reduced consumer acceptance of very low quality cases in the United States and financial losses to some of the importers who dealt in this merchandise.

The Commission is of the opinion that the combination of circumstances that led to the large imports in 1954 and 1955 no longer exists and that the trend of imports of low-priced pillowcases from Japan is now downward. Exports 

Official statistics of United States exports do not separately report cotton pillowcases. These articles are included in "cotton bed sheets, pillowcases, and shams," exports of which in 1955 totaled more than 207 thousand dozen, valued at $2.5 million. The volume in that year was approximately equal to the average annual exports in the preceding 5 years (see table 18).

Since August 1, 1956, United States exports of cotton textile goods have been subsidized to the extent necessary to counterbalance the competitive advantage enjoyed by foreign mills by virtue of their access to lower priced cotton (including that produced in the United States) than is available to United States mills. Canada, a principal market for the United States exports, has applied a countervailing duty to neutralize the effect of the United States subsidy. Other important buyers of United States pillowcases and sheets are Mexico and a considerable number of countries in South America.

For many years before 1955, United States exports of pillowcases exceeded the imports, not only in volume but more especially in value. 

In only one recent year, 1955, did the volume of imports exceed that of exports. Exports in the first 6 months of 1956 (though less than imports in that period) were greater than those in the first 6 months of 1955, and they were at a higher rate than in any of the immediately preceding years (see table 29).

The domestic industry producing cotton pillowcases obviously enjoys a strong competitive position in world markets, as evidenced by its generally favorable export position. The domestic producers, particularly those who have or hope to have foreign markets, recognize that they cannot expect liberal customs treatment for their products in foreign countries (especially those in which cotton pillowcases are made) if they insist upon a severe limitation on import competition in the United States market. Only the applicant has requested a less liberal customs treatment for imported cotton pillowcases. 

Apparent consumption

Apparent consumption of cotton pillowcases in the United States amounted to 8.5 million dozen in 1953, increased to 9.4 million do zen in 1954, and to 9.9 million dozen in 1955 (see table 29); and consumption will probably be higher in 1956 than it was in 1955. The increase in consumption has been accompanied by important changes in composition of that consumption. The most conspicuous of these have been the higher consumption of both the better and the poorer quality cases and the relatively lower consumption of the middle-quality cases.

For example, shipments of type 180 percale cases rose from 1.1 million dozen in 1953 to 1.2 million dozen in 1954 and 1.4 million dozen in 

1955, and from 552 thousand dozen in the first half of 1955 to 684 thousand dozen in the first half of 1956. Also, shipments of embroidered cases rose from 415 thousand dozen in 1953 to 438 thousand dozen in 1954 and 473 thousand dozen in 1955; and shipments were 70 percent higher in the first half of 1956 than in the first half of 

1955. Most of the increase in shipments of embroidered pillowcases occurred in those made of fabrics other than printcloth--principally percale.

In recent years, most domestic producers of pillowcases, particularly those who have experienced competition from low-priced imports from Japan, have tended to upgrade their output, and the buying public has responded favorably to the higher proportion of superior quality and embroidered cases that have become available. 

The applicant has stated that it has been upgrading its output and shifting toward the production and sale of a larger proportion of embroidered, fancy, and better quality cases. In so doing, it has not only been moving away from an area of competition in which lowpriced imported cases were impinging upon some of its customary sales but has also been following a trend that is general in the pillowcase industry. The need for a successful domestic producer to alter the volume and composition of his output to meet changing market conditions is a generally recognized hazard of private enterprise. The escape clause was not designed to shield domestic producers from that hazard when the opportunity for successful adjustment exists-as would appear to be the case here.

The volume of domestic (apparent) consumption of cotton pillowcases that has been supplied by domestic production was higher in 1954 than in 1953 but was somewhat lover in 1955 than in either of the 2 preceding years (see table 29). The volume of apparent consumption supplied by domestic production appears also to have been at a higher rate in the first 6 months of 1956 than it was for the whole of 1955.

The share of domestic (apparent) consumption supplied by domestic production (in terms of volume) declined from 99.2 percent in 1953 to 98.8 percent in 1954 and to 90.2 percent in 1955. On the basis of incomplete data, it would appear that the corresponding ratio was somewhat higher for the first 6 months of 1956 than for the whole of 1955.

Employment and wages

The Commission sought information on employment and wages from 69 producers of cotton pillowcases but received responses containing usable information on that subject from only 17 producers, of which only 16 were able to report separately on their employment on pillowcases alone. Of that number, 10 make cases principally of printcloth. 

Such a sample is obviously not representative of the industry as a whole. Only about one-fourth of the producers of the printcloth cases responded, but they account for the bulk of the domestic production of such cases. Despite the unrepresentative character of the sample, it is nevertheless revealing, particularly in the light of there being only a single applicant.

The number of man-hours devoted to making pillowcases by the 16 reporting concerns was 3 percent higher in 1954 than in 1953 but was 16 percent lower in 1955 than in 1953 (see table 20). The decline in employment between 1953 and 1955 corresponds roughly to the reduction in inventories that had been accumulated in 1953 and 1954 but which

had begun to decline in the second half of 1955 (see table 17). With inventories reduced in 1956 to more manageable proportions than in the immediately preceding years and with prices of pillowcases generally higher than in those years, employment in the industry began to pick up in 1956.

The accumulation of inventories in 1953 and 1954 cannot be attributed to imports supplying an unexpected share of the market, because imports of pillowcases in those years were, as previously observed, very small. The unexpectedly large imports in 1955, no doubt, increased the difficulty of working off accumulated inventories but the necessary adjustment has since been made. Inasmuch as prices of pillowcases were generally higher in 1956 than in 1954 or 1955, concerns that had high inventories at the end of 1955 may well have reaped a windfall benefit from them.

Twenty-one producers, including some of the largest concerns in the industry, did not respond to the questionnaires. Most of those who responded did not supply the financial information that was requested; and some of those who did supply such information gave it reluctantly and only after repeated requests.

Producers whose output of pillowcases consisted mainly of those made of printcloth were, on the whole, more cooperative than the producers whose output of cases was mainly of other fabrics. Most of the latter took the position, in their correspondence with the Commission, that they should not be expected to furnish the information that was requested. Conclusion 

In the Commission's opinion, the domestic industry producing articles like or directly competitive with imports of unembroidered cotton pillowcases is coextensive with the domestic production of pillowcases of that description. The applicant in this instance accounted for not more than 10 percent of the volume of such production in any recent year, and no other domestic producer of cotton pillowcases has claimed to be seriously injured or threatened with serious injury in consequence of increased imports. On the basis of this consideration alone, the Commission would have no alternative but to deny the application.

The Commission recognizes that the complainant corporation did not contend that it accounted for a substantial part of the domestic production of all cotton pillowcases; it contended merely that it represented a major part of the domestic production of printcloth pillowcases, which, in its view, was coextensive with a domestic

industry in the sense contemplated by section 7 of the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951, as amended. The applicant, however, was unable at the time of the public hearing to give a satisfactory definition of such an industry, though it expressed confidence that it could do so after some study. The description that it subsequently supplied covered pillowcases made not only from the kinds of cloth from which the complained-of imports were made but also cases made from a range of cloth constructions that overlapped into other fabrics, including carded percale, for example. The more serious objection to the definition, however, is that it is based on the erroneous assump-tion that "like or directly competitive" should have reference here to a technical similarity in the fabrics used in imported and domestic pillowcases--rather than to the interplay of economic forces that prevail in the whole market for cotton pillowcases, irrespective of the construction of the cloth from which they are made. 

The Commission had to consider also that some 20 or more other domestic producers of printcloth pillowcases, whose aggregate output accounted for a large share of the domestic production of such cases, did not claim to be part of any distinguishable domestic industry that was being seriously injured or threatened with serious injury by increased imports. But a more important consideration is that the Commission regards the production of pillowcases from "printcloth," however defined, as involving essentially the same operations as are employed in converting other constructions of cotton fabric into pillowcases. The same workers using identical equipment can, and

many of them do, perform virtually identical cutting, sewing, and finishing operations-sometimes in the same plants-without regard to whether they use printcloth, muslin, or percale. The escape-clause legislation does not contemplate, in such circumstances, that the Commission should recognize the existence of a multiplicity of cotton-pillowcase industries fractionated on the basis of more or less arbitrary definitions of the construction of the fabrics they use. 

Even on the untenable assumption of the existence of a distinguishable domestic printcloth-pillowcase industry, evidence is lacking that it is being seriously injured or threatened with serious injury in consequence of increased imports of unembroidered pillowcases made of any or all kinds of cotton fabric. 

In view of the foregoing considerations, the undersigned Commissioners find that no sufficient reason exists for a recommendation to the President under the provisions of section 7 of the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951, as amended.

DISSENTING VIEWS OF COMMISSIONERS BROSSARD AND SCHREIBER 

We dissent from the finding and conclusion of the majority of the Commission in this case. We find that, as a result in part of the customs treatment reflecting the concessions granted thereon in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, cotton printcloth pillowcases dutiable under paragraph 911 (b) of the Tariff Act of 1930 are being imported into the United States in such increased quantities, both actual and relative, as to cause serious injury to the domestic industry producing like or directly competitive products, and that the maximum permissible increase in duty--to 37-1/2 percent ad valorem--is necessary to remedy such injury. Our findings are based principally upon the following 

1. Description of imports. The investigation of the Commission disclosed that virtually all imports of cotton pillowcases under paragraph 911 (b) of the Tariff Act of 1930 consist of printcloth pillowcases. 

Cotton printcloth pillowcases are a distinct type of pillowcase, being distinguished in the trade from "muslin" and "percale" pillowcases.

The intrinsic differences between "printcloth" and the other types of pillowcases lie chiefly in the differences in thread count, yarn numbers, and weight of the cotton fabrics from which they are made. (See table 

3.) These differences, importers acknowledge, "seem to be well known in the trade". (Details regarding these differences are discussed in paragraph 2 below.) 

2. The domestic production of printcloth pillowcases is a separate industry for the purposes of section 7. Since imports covered by the

investigation consist of printcloth pillowcases, it is necessary to identify the "domestic industry producing like or directly competitive products" before a determination can be made whether that industry is being seriously injured or threatened with serious injury by reason of increased imports of cotton printcloth pillowcases. The majority of the 

Commission hold that there is no separate cotton printcloth pillowcase industry in the United States within the meaning of section 7 of the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951, as amended, and that the question of injury to a domestic industry attributable to imports of printcloth pillowcases must be determined on the basis of the impact of the imports upon the domestic production of all cotton pillowcases of the kind described in paragraph 911 ) of the tariff act. It is our considered view that this holding is in error.

Section 7 (e) of the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951 

provides: 

As used in this Act, the terms "domestic industry producing like or directly competitive products" and "domestic industry producing like or directly competitive articles" mean that portion or subdivision of the producing organizations manufacturing, assembling, processing, extracting, growing, or otherwise producing like or directly competitive products or articles in commercial quantities. 

In applying the preceding sentence, the Commission shall (so far as practicable) distinguish or separate the operations of the producing organizations involving the like or directly competitive products or articles referred to in such sentence from the operations of such organizations involving other products or articles. 

Thus, for the purposes of section 7, the domestic industry with respect to which the determination of the question of injury from imports must

be determined is coextensive with the production of articles that are like or directly competitive with the imported products concerned. The determination of the scope of the domestic industry concerned in this investigation turns, therefore, on the question of whether all domestically produced cotton pillowcases of the kind described in paragraph 911 (b) of the tariff act are like or directly competitive with the imported printcloth pillowcases, as held by the majority of the Commission, or whether the domestic production of muslin and percale pillowcases must be excluded from consideration by reason of their not being like or directly competitive with the imported printcloth pillowcases. 

The manufacture of unembroidered or otherwise unornamented cotton pillowcases covered by this investigation consists of a series of rather simple operations. The fabric to be made into pillowcases is first cut (or torn) to the dimensions required to make desired sizes of pillowcases. 

The cut fabric is then doubled over across the shorter dimension and one end and the side with cut edges (the longer dimension) of the doubled fabric are sewed to form a "bag" or "sack". A hem, usually not less than 3 inches deep, is made on the open end; this may be done either before the doubling or after the sewing of the bottom and side seams. 

The hem may also be formed by hemstitching rather than by conventional stitch (pillowcases hemstitched with two or more rows of stitches are not covered by this investigation). Pillowcases intended for embroidery after retail purchase may then have the embroidery outline stamped on the hem or hem and body. The finished pillowcases are then ready for packaging and shipment,

These manufacturing operations are well standardized and can be performed with equal facility and results on different kinds of suitable fabrics. Thus, no significant special quality or characteristic can be attributed to unornamented cotton pillowcases by virtue of the manufacturing processes. However, since these processes are applied to fabrics of different constructions and characteristics, the extent to which pillowcases of certain cotton fabrics constitute separate and distinct articles of commerce would be largely a reflection of the different qualities of these fabrics. 

Virtually all cotton pillowcases sold in the United States are made of muslin, percale, or printcloth fabric. Muslin is the fabric most extensively used for this purpose, but substantial quantities of pillowcases are also made of percale and printcloth fabrics. Information obtained in this investigation on the principal cloth constructions of domestic and imported cotton pillowcases distributed in the United States over the 3-1/2 years 1953-55 and January-June 1956, which is summarized in table 3, provides the basis for the following observations. 

The muslin used in pillowcases distributed in the United States in recent years is a fabric of carded yarns in which the average yarn 1/ number usually does not exceed 20; the material is in several different constructions and thread counts per square inch, of which 128 and 140 appear to be the most common. The percales, or combed-yarn fabrics, are soft textured and have a high thread count; those used in the pillowcases referred to above are made of combed yarns with average yarn numbers 1/ The term average yarn number" refers to the average yarn number as determined by the method described in paragraph 903 (c) of the Tariff 

Act of 1930.

from 31 to 35, and they range in thread counts from 180 to 210 per square inch. 

Printcloths used in pillowcases are mediumweight fabrics made from carded yarns; the average yarn number of the yarns used in these fabrics ranges from 30 to 36 and the thread counts of these fabrics range mainly from 120 to 160 per square inch. In comparing the construction characteristics of the three fabrics, it will be noted that combed-yarn percale has a much higher thread count than either muslin or printcloth, and thiș material is used largely in pillowcases designed for the luxury market. 

Although the thread counts of muslin and printcloth broadly overlap, there is a significant difference in the size of yarn used in each of these materials, and the larger yarn used in muslin results in a heavier textured fabric than printcloth. 

In addition to differences with regard to kind of yarn, size of yarn (average yarn number), and thread count per square inch, there is a further significant physical difference in the weight of the three fabrics. With respect to the principal typical fabrics used in cotton pillowcases distributed in the United States in the past 3-1/2 years, the approximate weight of the muslins has ranged from 4.40 to 5.25 ounces per square yard, the combed-yarn percales from 3.85 to 4.10 ounces per square yard, and the printcloths from 2.50 to 3.55 ounces per square yard. It is thus clear that in general the unit weight of a pillowcase made of printcloth is substantially less than one made of muslin, and somewhat less than one made of combed-yarn percale. 

The factors discussed above relate to technical characteristics that determine quality features of the principal kinds of cotton pillow-

cases sold in the United States market. Size of yarn, thread count, and fabric weight all have important bearing on the quality of a pillowcase in terms of texture, weight, attractiveness, general durability, and ability to withstand laundering. These differences between printcloth pillowcases, on the one hand, and muslin and percale pillowcases, on the other hand, establish the fact that printcloth pillowcases constitute a separate and distinct article of commerce, and are not "like or directly competitive" with "muslin" or "percale" pillowcases.

The views of domestic producers of muslin and percale pillowcases obtained by the Commission in the investigation, by correspondence, and at the hearing, were that muslin and percale pillowcases are entirely different products from printcloth pillowcases and that printcloth pillowcases are not competitive with muslin or percale pillowcases.

Representatives of the applicant in this investigation--the largest domestic producer of printcloth pillowcases--similarly expressed the view, both in testimony at the hearing and in a brief filed with the Commission, that the production of printcloth pillowcases constitutes a domestic industry that is separate and different from the industry producing muslin and percale pillowcases. Taking into account these views of domestic pillowcase producers as well as the quality factors previously reviewed, it is clear that muslin and percale pillowcases are not like or directly competitive with printcloth pillowcases. 

Accordingly, the "domestic industry producing like or directly competitive products", for the purpose of this investigation, is necessarily coextensive with the domestic production of printcloth pillowcases.

Note.--Production figures, all of which are estimates, cover pillowcases made both from print cloth (about 15 percent) and those made from fabrics other than printcloth (about 85 percent). 

Production figures for printcloth pillowcases are based on production reported to the Tariff Commission, assumed to equal 90 percent of total production. Production of pillowcases from other fabrics is estimated on the basis of the amount of cloth used (see table 5). The figures on production refer to unembroidered pillowcases of which approximately 7 to 7.5 percent were embroidered by producers before shipment. Inventory changes are the difference between production and shipments as reported to the Tariff Commission by producers, divided by the ratio of their reported production to the total estimated commercial production for each period. Import data are official statistics, minus the number of dozens of sheets that could be identified by average value per dozen in United States official import statistics or estimated from total value of shipments separately reported in 

Japanese export statistics (see tables 10 and 11). Export figures are estimated on the basis of official statistics (table 18).
