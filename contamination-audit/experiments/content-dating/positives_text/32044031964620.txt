THE PROPOSED NEW FLORIDA CONSTITUTION: 

AN ANALYSIS MANNING J. DAUER Professor of Political Science 

University of Florida

A new constitution for the state of Florida was proposed by the 1957 special session of the state legislature. If the new document is approved by the voters all the articles of the present Constitution except for Article V will be repealed. Article V contains the structure of the judicial system of the state and the Legislature was not able to agree on a proposal to replace the existing judicial article. 

However all the other articles of the Constitution will be on the ballot at the general election in November, 1958, unless one of several suits now in process against the form of the new constitution prevent its being put on the ballot. This matter will be determined by the State Supreme Court of Florida. The main question at issue is the so called "daisy chain" tying the parts of the new Constitution together so that unless all articles of the proposed new constitution are approved by the voters, the entire proposal of the 1957 Legislature will fall. 

The present Constitution of the State of Florida was adopted in 1885. After World War II considerable criticism was heard that the 1885 document was outmoded. A committee of the Florida 

Bar Association proposed a redrafting of the Constitution of the State in 1947. Thereafter other groups began to study the Constitution, including local bar associations, the League of Women Voters, and a special organization known as the Citizens Constitution Committee. The movement for constitutional reform crystalized with the election of the present Governor, one plank of whose platform called for a new constitution for the State. The 

1955 Florida Legislature provided for the establishment of a Constitutional Commission. 1 This 912206

Commission was to study the Constitution of the State, propose a draft of a new document, which would in turn be submitted to the 1957 State Legislature for its consideration. The press of business during the regular session of the 1957 Legislature prevented final consideration of this draft until a special session called in the summer. At that time a committee of the Legislature itself redrafted the proposals of the Constitutional Commission and further changes were introduced on the floor in each house of the Legislature. The resulting document has been certified to go forward to the ballot, unless as already indicated there is a successful suit to prevent its being placed before the voters in the 1958 general election. 

In general it may be stated that the wording of the new document is much more coherent than that of the 1885 Florida Constitution, as amended. In no small part the difficulties with the present document are due to the frequent amendments which have been adopted since 1885. Between 1885 and 1955, some 158 amendments were proposed by sessions of the legislature and some 106 have actually been adopted. As a result, the present 

Constitution of Florida has rather justly been described as a patch work. In contrast, the draftsmanship of the new document is good, and it is much better organized than is the old document with its many amendments. Draftsmanship alone, however, will hardly determine whether the proposed new constitution is approved or rejected. A number of substantive changes have been made in the new document and the main purpose of this pamphlet is to draw attention to some of the more important changes. The voter, before he decides how to mark his ballot, would be well advised, if time permits, to read through both the existing and proposed new constitutions. Both documents are available free upon request by any voter from the Secretary of State, State Capitol, Tallahassee, Florida. 2

THE BILL OF RIGHTS 

As with almost all state constitutions Florida's proposed new one begins with the Bill of Rights. 

Most of the provisions of the present Bill of Rights have simply been redrafted. Section 10 of this 

Article does provide one change regarding condemnation of property for road rights of way. The change permits the Legislature to count benefits from construction of a new road as a charge against the award to a property owner, some of whose land may be taken for the right of way for a new road or for broadening an existing road. A second change is that Section 2 of the 1885 Constitution's Bill of Rights contains a provision that a citizen of the State owes paramount allegiance to the federal government. This provision is omitted in the present constitution. The third principal change from the 

Bill of Rights of the present constitution is in the clause which allows the suspension of the writ of habeas corpus in case of rebellion or invasion. The new constitution, in Article I, Section 13, does not permit suspension of the writ of habeas corpus.

GENERAL PROVISIONS 

The new constitution provides in Article II for general provisions. This article is not found in the old constitution, although the new general article includes a number of provisions found in the 1885 Constitution. These refer to such matters as state boundaries, and a number of other provisions scattered throughout the 1885 Constitution. One 

5 provision which is considerably clarified is Section 8 of this new Article II, which provides a specific time for newly elected or appointed officers to take office. The question is not thoroughly covered in the 1885 Constitution and has led to considerable confusion and litigation. Section 13 of this Article speeds up the process whereby a session of the Florida legislature may consider adoption of an amendment to the United States Constitution. 3

CHANGES IN THE LEGISLATIVE ARTICLE 

In considering changes in the legislative article it should be noted that the treatment in this pamphlet is selective, because of limitations of space. Again it should be emphasized that the voter would have to secure copies of the new constitution in order to study all changes which have been introduced.

The present Constitution contains a rigid provision which fixes the salaries of members of the legislature. The new constitution, in Article III, Section 3, establishes the initial salary of a legislator, but then permits a later legislature to fix the salaries of its membership, but provides the desirable safeguard of an intervening election before any new salaries for members of the legislature would become effective. This change would bring Florida practice more in accord with that of other states. Section 5 contains another desirable provision in that it provides that the legislature shall appoint the auditor, who is responsible for the post-audit system. The present practice in the state of Florida is not standard in other states, in that the auditor is appointed by the Governor although his principal duty is to audit the departments of the Executive Branch of the state government. The new system provides that the auditor be appointed by the legislature itself.

CONSTITUTIONAL PROVISIONS ON APPORTIONMENT 

The single most controversial feature of the proposed new constitution is the apportionment formula for distribution of legislative seats among the counties. Here a distinction must be made between the constitutional provisions which are proposed, and two new statutes which have been enacted in anticipation of the possible passage of the new constitution. However the new statutes would not become effective until after the adoption of the constitution in the 1958 general election.

First we shall consider the constitutional provisions themselves.

The 1885 Constitution provides that the legislature shall reapportion itself every ten years. The question of reapportionment of the Florida Senate has, however, been deadlocked in the Florida legislature since 1955 (the House was reapportioned according to the constitutional formula). Following the failure of the legislature to approve an apportionment act for the Senate in the 1955 regular and special sessions, a constitutional amendment making an attempt at reapportionment of the Senate was proposed but turned down by the voters in the 1956 general election. (This would have provided one senator for each of the sixty-seven counties.)

The present Florida Constitution provides for thirty-eight senatorial districts with these districts "to be as nearly equal in population as practicable”. 

It should be observed however that no mandate on the legislature is self-enforcing and if the legislature does not act then no court order may be issued to make it act. The provision in the proposed constitution for the Senate contains the following statement as to the forty-five senatorial districts, which are created in comparison with the thirty-eight under the old constitution: "Such apportionment shall provide fairness and equity among districts, based upon population and such other pertinent factors as may be determined by the legislature at the time of apportionment;". In summary, this is a mandate which permits the legislature to take less account of population than in the case of the non-enforcing statement of the old constitution. 

Two other provisions of the senate apportionment section also operate to make apportionment by population difficult. One of these provides that no senatorial district shall be composed of more than three counties. The 1885 Constitution under which Florida now operates contains no such restriction as to maximum number of counties in a senatorial district. In view of the population trends in the state this proposed provision of the new

constitution could give increasing difficulty in the future. A second provision which prohibits population representation in the senate is found in both the present and the proposed constitution. This is the provision which declares "no county shall be divided in creating a district." It should be repeated, however, that the present Constitution also contains this same provision. This automatically means that under either the present or the proposed constitution there can never be anything approaching population representation in the state of Florida according to the present distribution of population or what seems to be the trend for the future. Based on a 1957 estimate of the Florida population, today over fifty percent of the State's population lives in five counties. Under the present Constitution this would mean that fifty percent of the voters would have five/thirty-eighths of the senate representing them. Under the new constitution they would have five/forty-fifths of the senate representing them.

PROPOSED APPORTIONMENT UNDER NEW CONSTITUTION 

Turning to House Bill 105X which provides for reapportionment of the Florida Senate under the new constitution, if it is adopted, we find, that based on the 1950 census ten new senatorial districts have been awarded to single counties which by themselves did not have senators before. These counties are: in North Florida, Bay, Okaloosa, Madison, Taylor, and Santa Rosa; in Central Florida, Seminole County; in South Florida, Manatee, Monroe, and Brevard. In summary, the trend (with some exceptions) was to award new senatorial districts to medium-sized counties; and in certain respects, as those counties named are growing counties, some of the senators from them may be expected to have a somewhat more favorable point of view to the problems of the larger cities, despite the fact that the larger cities actually lose proportionate representation in the Senate.

On the other hand, the new constitution and the proposed bill to carry the new senate apportion-

ment into effect definitely decrease the percentage of the state population which may elect a majority of the senate. Under the present apportionment, based on the thirty-eight member senate, 17.7 percent of the state's population may elect the majority of the senate (using the 1950 census). If the new constitution is adopted, then, based on the 1950 census, 16.6 percent of the state's population will elect a majority of the senate; but based on the 

1957 estimates of county population 13.6 percent of the state's population will be all that is necessary to elect fifty-one percent of the senate. In other words the new constitutional provisions do not take cognizance of the areas of the state in which population is growing most rapidly. 

The distribution of senate seats would result in certain changes in favor of the medium-sized counties, and the best argument that might be advanced on behalf of the amendment is that the larger centers of population then might discover more favorable senators from some of these moderate-sized counties. On the other hand the larger counties can not look to the new constitutional provisions for better representation in the senate for themselves.

APPORTIONMENT OF THE FLORIDA HOUSE OF REPRESENTATIVES UNDER THE NEW CONSTITUTION 

The number of members of the Florida House of Representatives is increased from 95 in the present Constitution to 114 in the proposed constitution. An automatic ratio of representatives is created as follows: five representatives are given to the most populous county; four representatives for each of the next two most populous counties; three representatives to each of the next seven most populous counties; two representatives to each of the next twenty-three most populous counties; and one representative for each other county. House

Bill 104X as adopted by the 1957 Legislature pro-

vides for carrying this system into effect. While this slightly increases the proportionate influence of Dade and Duval Counties in the House, it still does not provide anything approaching population representation. Based on the 1950 census 18.7 percent of the state's population will elect a majority of the 114 members of the new House of Representatives. But the growth of the state, according to the 

1957 estimates, shows that only 15.3 percent of the population would elect a majority under the provisions as the populous areas of the state have increased their proportion of the state's population. 

SUMMARY ON APPORTIONMENT 

No American state has a perfectly apportioned legislature. The average for the United States is around thirty-four percent to elect a majority of each house. Thirty years ago this was also the figure for Florida. Florida's present apportionment is inequitable, based on population representation. 

The new constitution does little to change this situation based on percentage figures. The best argument for it is that it would permit a resting point in regard to this matter for a period and with the addition of some new members of the House, plus some from the medium-sized counties in the case of the Senate, a more favorable representation might be secured in the future.

THE GOVERNOR AND APPORTIONMENT 

The powers of the governor in relation to the legislature is somewhat abridged by two provisions of the proposed new constitution of the State of Florida. The present Constitution provides that if the legislature shall not apportion in a regular session the governor shall convene the legislature in special session. The Legislature itself may not adjourn such session until apportionment is completed. Actually, this has proved ineffective in the sessions called by the governor since 1955. However, the new constitution provides that after the

lapse of sixty days from the date on which a reapportionment session is convened by the governor a concurrent resolution of the two houses may adjourn the session. This would prevent the governor holding the legislature in session. A second provision in the present Constitution provides that the governor, in the case of disagreement between the two houses as to adjournment, may himself adjourn a regular or special session of the legislature. The proposed constitution provides that the governor must give notice of such proposed adjournment by at least two legislative days before the adjournment may become effective. 

One other comment may be made on the reapportionment question. Formerly, Florida had a state census midway between the regular federal censuses which are held every ten years, as for example in 1950, in 1960, etc. When there was a state census reapportionment was based on that and came as in 1935, in 1945, etc. The state census was abolished in 1950. The new constitution, however, actually postpones the date of any apportionment until five years after the federal census has been taken. Since the change to the federal census has been made there is little point in delaying for five years a state apportionment based on the federal census. 

THE EXECUTIVE BRANCH One provision of Article IV, the new executive article, strengthens the governorship. This is found in Section 4 where the office of lieutenant governor is created. At present, in the case of the death, removal, or resignation of the governor, the president of the senate succeeds to the governorship until there be another state-wide election. There is an interesting experiment in the provision for the lieutenant governorship in a sentence in Section 4 stating "the legislature shall provide a method and requirements by which in primary and general elections candidates for the offices of governor and lieutenant governor may form a joint candidacy."

This is clearly aimed at having a lieutenant governor who shall be in sympathy with the governor and who may carry on such duties as may be allocated to him by the governor. In view of the growth of the state of Florida, this would be quite a useful provision for some administrative and especially for ceremonial functions. 

THE CABINET 

The governor is assisted in his executive duties by six other elective heads of departments. These are: the secretary of state, the attorney general, the comptroller, the treasurer, the superintendent of education and the commissioner of agriculture. 

Such additional elective officials are usual in most 

American states. 

However, Florida has, by statute, under the 1885 Constitution created a large number of exofficio boards with certain members of the cabinet in addition to the governor constituting these boards. 

Thus for many functions, Florida, unlike other states, has a collegial executive. Moreover, both under the present and also under the proposed constitution the governor may not be re-elected; whereas the cabinet members may be and usually are.

This system of ex-officio boards (with a few exceptions) is not found in the present constitution, although as already stated it permits it by statute. 

In fact, not even the term cabinet appears in the text of the present constitution, although it has been introduced as editorial matter in headings by the statutory revision section of the attorney general's office. The new constitution, however, institutionalizes the cabinet. It also contains in the first sentence of Section 6 this statement: "The legisla ture may authorize any board composed entirely of cabinet members to appoint a director of any department under the supervision of such board."

This could well be a provision of considerable import. The present Constitution provides that all 10

executive officials not elected by the people shall be appointed by the governor. The new constitution, on the other hand, permits the legislature to transfer such executive duties as it may see fit to the collegial executive which includes the governor and the other members of the cabinet. As already indicated the legislature has done this under the Constitution of 1885 but not to the extent of authorizing appointment of officials. One exception is the director of the state department of public safety, but this would probably be unconstitutional if a case were ever raised.

Actually, this raises the question of pros and cons as to Florida's cabinet system.

Pro: On behalf of the cabinet it is argued that the cabinet members, being re-elected, have continuity of experience. Furthermore in their many functions, such as certain members acting as a budget board, they serve as a check upon the governor. 

This prevents a governor from abusing power. 

Another argument on behalf of the cabinet is, that as the cabinet meets in session every Tuesday in open meetings, quite an amount of public business is transacted with the press present and this business is then reported to the people. Finally, the statement may be made that under the present system the governor does have much more influence T than other members of the cabinet and usually is able to secure his program unless it runs directly counter to the direct wishes of a majority of the cabinet.

Con: In opposition to the cabinet system is the fact that the cabinet members ordinarily are elected because of their specific duties. That is to say, the superintendent of public instruction is elected primarily because of his stand on matters affecting education. The same applies to the commissioner of agriculture, and other officers of the cabinet. 

When additional duties are given to them and they function as ex-officio boards this makes more dif- 1 ficult the pinning down of responsibility. In other 11 11

words, from the standpoint of the voter, the governor is the official to whom voters look for general matters of executive policy. If responsibility is divided among seven executives it is difficult for the voter to discover why a given policy is adopted and who is responsible for it. Furthermore, Florida already has a rather long ballot and this dispersal of responsibility creates a number of problems for the voter.

The trend in other states in regard to executive reorganization has been the opposite to that in Florida. In New York, and then in the South beginning in 1929 with the reorganization in the State of Virginia under the then Governor Harry Flood Byrd, the trend has been to make the governor the chief responsible executive under the theory that the voters will then hold one central state official responsible. Furthermore, the executive budget which has been introduced in most states is that recommended by the governor. Budget making has been recognized in most states and in the federal government as an arm of management. 

There is no more effective way to implement policy than to provide funds. Of course in all states the governor only recommends, the legislature still appropriates.

However, in Florida the trend for the last thirty years has been away from giving new authority in this fashion to the chief executive. The proposed constitution would provide the means for carrying this trend even further. As the governor is elected by all the voters of the State, in contested elections, the possible weakening of this officer could also make popular control more difficult.

OTHER EXECUTIVE ARTICLE CHANGES 

The constitution as proposed provides a change in Section 16 of Article IV dealing with the Game and Fresh Water Fish Commission. This agency under the present constitution is appointed by the governor. Under the new constitution it would be 12

appointed by the cabinet. Certain powers which it has to enact game and fish regulations would be transferred to legislative supervision.

One final statement might be made concerning the executive article. At present, Florida has some 

125 agencies either under the governor or under the cabinet. Many revised constitutions in other states have provided for a limitation on the total number of such agencies and require that they be grouped under certain appropriate heads. No such provision is found in the proposed constitution which would require executive reorganization. Of course the legislature would still be just as free under the new constitution to provide for such regrouping under a fewer number of executive departments and officials as it is under the present constitution. 

JUDICIARY 

As already indicated in the introductory statement to this pamphlet, Article V, pertaining to the court system of Florida and the judiciary is not submitted for revision. Actually, this came about not because of a feeling that the present structure of the judicial system should not be revised, but rather because the recommendations of the Judicial

Council of the state could not adequately be considered in time to be presented to the legislature for their consideration.

SUFFRAGE AND ELECTIONS 

Article VI is considerably clarified and the only substantial change is found in Section 2 of this Article which declares that the legislature "may provide for registration of electors outside the territorial limits of the state". This is still subject to the necessary residence qualification of one year in the state and six months in the county, but would permit registration of those in military service who might be stationed outside the state. 13

LOCAL GOVERNMENT 

Article VII of the proposed constitution recognizes the present sixty-seven counties and permits, in Section 2, the creation of additional counties. 

The present county officers are retained with the exception of the elective county surveyor, which is abolished as an elective office. To begin with, county commissioners and school boards are to be elected by the voters of the county at large although they must reside within districts established by the county commission. However, the new constitution has a change whereby if a local law is enacted by the legislature, and approved by the electors of the county, members of the school board and of the county commission may be elected from districts. 

Another change provides that in creating election districts for county commissioners for the county, geography as well as population shall be considered. 

Another optional provision, subject to local referendum, is that by local law affecting a particular county, the elective office of county school superintendent may be abolished and the county superintendent of schools be appointed by the county school board. This is a change which at present is permitted only in several of the larger counties, but it now might be extended to any of the counties. 

Another change in regard to county government is found in Section 6. This would permit county commissioners to enact county ordinances, provided a local law is adopted thus authorizing the commissioners of a particular county. Such ordinances might not conflict with any state general law. An interesting new provision is found in Section 14 whereby cities or counties or local districts may enter into contractual and cooperative agreements with one another, with the state, or with the United States, in the exercise of their appropriate functions, facilities, or services. In view of the growth of urban areas outside city limits, this could provide for a number of interesting agreements to consolidate services, as between city commissions and the 14

county commissioners of that particular county, for example. 

This provision for ordinance power of county commissions is a substitute for constitutional home rule. Constitutional home rule would stop the legislature from enacting local bills affecting a unit of local government such as a county. However under the new constitution the local legislative delegation would retain control. Added is the idea of a required referendum by the voters of the county. 

However, as the legislature retains the initiative, there is considerable similarity with the procedure of the present constitution whereby acts can be adopted in population brackets for individual counties.

Other provisions affecting individual counties are found in Article XIV. These are regrouped from various articles of the 1885 Constitution, with no change of substance. In this article are sections concerning the Dade County Home Rule system, and certain provisions affecting other specific counties which permit consolidation of some functions of government as between the county and city governments of these counties. 5 

TAXATION AND FINANCE 

No substantial changes are found in Article VIII on taxation and finance. A minor change is one authorizing a separate ballot for freeholder elections on bond issues, thus permitting such elections to be held at the time of the general elections and saving some time and expense. Also, in Article X on the public school system the permissible annual interest rate for school capital outlay bonds is raised from four to four and one-half percent. On the other hand the interest rate may run below three percent which would hardly be possible under the present constitution which sets this rate as a minimum. 15

PUBLIC SCHOOLS 

A provision for separate white and colored schools is retained in Section 8 of the new proposed Article X. Section 1 authorizes a system of uniform free public schools and also permits the legislature to provide assistance for "other non-sectarian schools". 

This last section is new and reflects recent concern over federal court decisions affecting segregation. 

Structural changes are provided in that the county trustees, which have been largely a fifth wheel on the wagon for a number of years, are abolished. All school affairs would be managed by the elective county school board. In addition, it should be noted that while initially the county school superintendent would be elected in all but a few large counties; by local bill, and after approval by local referendum he subsequently could be appointed by the county school board. 

STATE MILITIA 

No substantial changes are found in Article XI on the militia. The chief change is that a specified number of generals found in the 1885 Constitution is omitted in the proposed constitution. As before the governor is commander-in-chief of the state militia.

CONSTITUTIONAL AMENDMENTS 

The procedure for adopting constitutional amendments is little changed. Ordinary amendments require that three-fifths of the legislature must approve an amendment, which then must be approved by a majority of the electors voting on it at the next general election. In the case of emergency amendments, which would be submitted to the voters at a special election, a three-fourths vote of the legislature is necessary. Both of these provisions are unchanged from the present constitution. However, there is a more definite procedure outlined for the introduction of amendments where- 16

by notice shall be given, and also the effective date of amendments is more definitely specified.

CONSTITUTIONAL CONVENTION 

The present constitution of the state provides that a constitutional convention may be called by two-thirds vote of each house of the legislature, with the question of whether or not a constitutional convention shall be held then being submitted to the voters at the next general election. The next step would be that the subsequent legislature after the voters had approved a call for the convention would provide the machinery for the convention and the convention would then be elected as the House of Representatives is presently apportioned. 

The present constitution is silent on what would transpire thereafter. However, all three of the constitutions of the state of Florida which have endured for some length of time were submitted for popular ratification by the voters (although the constitutions of 1861 and 1865 were not) and such is the practice of the American states. 

The new constitution provides the same machinery for initiating the call for a constitutional convention. The apportionment of members is slightly changed in that members shall be apportioned among the counties in the same manner as the membership of the Senate and House of Representatives. This would substantially raise the number of members of a state constitutional convention. 

Next, however, a unique provision is found. This is one which is not paralleled in any of the other forty-seven state constitutions. The work of a state constitutional convention would have to come back to the legislature for its consideration under the proposed Florida constitution as outlined in Section 4 of Article XII. If the legislature by a three-fourths vote amends the work of the convention it may be changed by the legislature. Furthermore, the revision of the convention draft, as amended by the legislature requires a three-fifths vote of the mem- 17

bership of each house in order for the work of the convention ever to be submitted to the voters. An alternate method is provided whereby upon vote of three-fourths of the membership of each house of the legislature calling the convention the work of the convention shall be submitted unchanged to the electorate. In either case the proposed constitution provides for a vote of the people on the work of the convention. However, the unique feature is that the legislature itself may be interposed between a constitutional convention and the vote of the people. This probably reflects a general trend of the proposed constitution to strengthen the position of the legislature. 

CONCLUSIONS 

The revised Florida constitution will be approved or rejected by the voters of the State in the general election in November, 1958, unless the Supreme

Court of the State should find that the method whereby the legislature proposed it is not valid. 

But assuming that the constitution does appear on the ballot, and that the system of interlocking the separate articles is also approved by the State Supreme Court, then all of the articles must receive a majority vote or the new constitution will fail.

The voter must make up his or her mind as to whether or not he or she approves the document.

At present the discussion in the press and by those speaking for and against the proposed constitution shows a sufficient number of varied points of view that most voters will have opportunity for hearing considerable pro and con discussion. 

As to this pamphlet, its aim is to present pro and con some of the leading questions. The voter will also be well advised to secure a copy of both the present (1885) Constitution, and the 1957 proposed constitution from the Secretary of State.
