IT WAS the Sunday of the picnic given by the Swastika Social Club, an organization of young women of eighteen or thereabouts, who met each 

Thursday evening at the Settlement House. For what purpose? Who shall say? Nothing definite in their purposes except that of sisterhood, conviviality, but also the important purpose of arranging parties, outings, theatricals and other affairs to which each might bring her young man.

By this means in large cities, a getting-together of the opposite sexes is afforded, so that mating may be hurried along, and once that is accomplished, marriage is not far off. Thus it is that though it has been said, and justly too, that "Man proposes," it is actually woman who arranges for the proposal, accepts or rejects it as the case might be; so it is really woman who proposes as well as disposes. 

Miss Flersheim, the director of the Swastikas, was a young high-school teacher of twenty-five who took

great delight in this directing and spurring onward and forward of her young charges. She had brought with her to the picnic her gentleman friend, Samuel Lexton, with whom she had arranged a program of events that would tend to keep the young people so occupied and entertained that there would be little time for anything but whole-spirited fun, which would keep their thoughts away from the everdangerous, ever-poignant subject of sex. 

Having arrived at the picnic grounds, the young men gingerly deposited the picnic boxes and baskets, which the girls had provided, divested themselves of their hats and coats, which they hung on branches of trees nearby. The girls too were fussing about with their starched and pleated dresses, in vogue in the late nineties, readjusted their bows and collars, gave their straw sailor hats to the boys to hang up, and finally settled down on the grass and formed a large circle.

Mr. Lexton suggested as a starter that they play the game of "Buzz." In this, as you may know, the various numbers are called in rotation, starting with one and going forward to seven, but instead of seven, the word "buzz" is called out. Thus the game started with Mr. Lexton cheerily calling out one, the girl to his right calling two, the next one three, the next four, then five, six, then buzz, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, buzz, fifteen, sixteen, buzz-for 10

"Buzz" had to be substituted for any number which contained seven or was a multiple thereof. 

The game reached its highest pitch of excitement when the seventies were reached and the numbers were called in this manner: Buzz-one, buzz-two, buzzthree, buzz-four, buzz-five, buzz-six, buzz-buzz, and so forth. But ah, there was the rub, and one had to keep one's mind on the game, ever watchful of the multiplication table and listening intently to the numbers as they were rapidly called off before one's turn was reached. Of course, if one missed, he was let out of the game, but the buzzing and multiplying continued merrily on until the contestants dwindled down to three, then two, then one, and the last one received the well-earned prize. 

The game of "Buzz" being ended, Miss Flersheim suggested next a race for the boys, after which was played that good old game of "Last Couple Out."

For the benefit of my young readers who live in this marvelous age of petting and whoopee, permit me to digress a moment and explain this game; 'twill not take long, and if you are not interested, just skip it. I assure you, it has nothing to do with the story, just inserted at this moment, for it must take its place in the regular sequence.

The players are arranged in pairs, with "It" standing with his back turned to the row of couples. When he called: "Last Couple Out," the pair at the ex- 11

treme end of the double column started to run forward-one to the right and the other to the left of the column-in a huge arc, the idea being to get together and touch hands before "It" could catch them.

If one was tagged, that one became "It" and the play continued until each couple had been "Out" and all were quite ready to sit down and rest a bit and cool off.

Now was the logical time to prepare the "spread,” and those who were more animated than the others, got busy delving into baskets and bringing forth the usual picnic refreshments-salads, sandwiches, pickles, olives, hard-boiled eggs, tomatoes, layer cakes, fruit, etc., etc., etc. Mention must also be made of the wooden dishes used at the time, but real silverware was brought along, for the enlightened day of stiff paper utensils had not yet arrived. It was, therefore, with misgivings that the mothers of the girls permitted this pillaging of the cupboard, and promises were exacted to "be sure and count them and bring them back, every one of them."

It was also the age before thermos bottles of any sort and lemons and sugar were brought along in ample quantities, also a large tin bucket for water, which was sure to be found at a spring nearby. Mr. 

Lexton called out for a volunteer to fetch water, and Otto Schmidt answered the call, saying that he 12

was familiar with the country thereabouts and knew exactly where he could locate the spring. 

To Otto then was entrusted the bucket and willingly he started off; but now he noticed that the pretty Irma Metzler had detached herself from the other girls and waved to him, signifying her intention of accompanying him. He smiled at her and together they sauntered toward the spring. 13

OTTO SCHMIDT stood behind the counter of his father's butcher-shop, trimming the fat from a sirloin steak. Nay, start not, gentle reader-butchers too have their place in the sun and, though it is not generally known or written about, they get into very close touch with the housewives whom they wait upon. 

A butcher's is a unique profession, for upon him depends the success of a meal, and, after all, the way to any man's heart is through his stomach. It is he who can instruct the young and inexperienced women just how to cook the various joints of meat, and if he wishes to exert himself, he can be quite artistic in his endeavor to make a particular cut of meat appear most attractive.

Then, too, it is the butcher who, upon being asked as he so often is—-"Is this meat tender?"—can reply, as he leans low over the counter and peers into the face of his inquirer, “As tender as a woman's heart"; and this reply invariably meets with a responsive 20

smile, for each woman who hears the remark thinks he has her in mind.

THE exigencies of married life, with its attendant pregnancy, were not particularly welcome to Irma, for as month followed month she found things most uncomfortable. The distortion of her slim young body was another sore point with her, for she felt that Otto's ardor cooled considerably. Then, too, in the later months, she had to refuse to participate in the conjugal duties, which gave Otto the feeling that in some manner he was being imposed upon. This having of babies was indeed a most cumbersome business, and Irma prayed for the day of her delivery.

It came in due time, and a little daughter was born, whom they decided to call Ella, after Otto's mother who had died several years before. Now descended upon Irma the strenuous work that goes with the care and raising of a baby, work that she was not at all familiar with and which left her most exhausted, for Ella was not the best child in the world. 

To begin with, she cried a great deal and slept 26

The tears sprang into her eyes, as she reached for her bag and gloves, and rushed out into the street, where she hailed a taxicab, and ordered the driver to drive around the park. She knew not which way to turn, or where to go. 

She could not find it in her heart to blame Willie for accepting an invitation for himself; but she knew that this was the beginning of the end; never again would anybody ever invite her, and life 299

They had driven through Central Park and around it several times, when the driver ventured: "Have you decided where you are going, Miss?" 

Gladys looked at the meter, which registered almost six dollars. "Drive to 2078 West End Avenue. That's at 80th Street."

That was Jules' apartment, and she prayed that he might be at home. At least she might talk with him and she was certain that he would not put her out. No, Jules would always find a tiny spot in his home for Gladys. He owed her that much, and he was the logical person to go to now. 

Jules let her in himself. The maid had gone for the afternoon, and Violet, his daughter, had gone up to Larchmont with some friends for a day's drive. "Hello, baby dear, I'm glad to see you. Willie coming, too?"

Gladys hurried in, pulled her tiny hat from her head, took off her squirrel coat, and tossed them both into a chair. She dropped onto the long divan, which stood before the fireplace, where some logs were burning brightly, giving cheer to the room.

Then, the spell over, Gladys perked up her head and looked at Jules out of the corner of her eye, saying, with something of her former roguish manner: "You'll marry me, won't you, Jules, after Vera gets her divorce?"
