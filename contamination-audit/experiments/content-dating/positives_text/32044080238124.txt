A SPIRITED DEFENCE OF A 

LIBRARY NOW LOST 

URING a time of great political unrest and the first stirrings of industrialism in England, there lived a man who, in spite of being friend and adviser to his king and holding the positions of ambassador to foreign courts, Chancellor, Clerk of the

Privy Seal, and finally, Bishop of Durham, nevertheless wrote, "We have resigned all thoughts of other earthly things, and have given ourselves up to a passion for acquiring books." It was the fourteenth century and the man was 

Richard de Bury, patriarch among book collectors and author of Philobiblon, an apologia pro vita sua, written in answer to his compatriots' criticism of what was to them an incomprehensible weakness. Feudalism was breaking up and 

England was on the verge of the Hun-

dred Years' War. "Why," his contemporaries asked impatiently, "should an eminent statesman spend so much time and money collecting manuscripts?"

The bishop builds up a thoroughly convincing justification in his book, a writing so sincerely the expression of a passion for books that six hundred years later it is as alive to his fellow enthusiasts as when it left his pen in 1344. 

Richard's love of books was always paramount in his life. His brilliant work in philosophy and religion at Oxford secured him an appointment as tutor at court to the prince who, when he became Edward III, retained him as adviser; but even important missions to the continent did not take Richard's attention from books. On his errands of state and church he sought out many of his books himself; and his passion for collecting became so well known

that books were frequently offered him as political bribes. He openly acknowledges this in his apologia, where he says: .it was more easy for any man to gain our favour by means of books than of money. 

He adds later, with charming candor: 

And in good will we strove so to forward their affairs that gain accrued to them, while justice suffered no disparagement. Indeed, if we had loved gold and silver goblets, high-bred horses, or no small sums of money, we might in those days have furnished forth a rich treasury. But in truth we wanted manuscripts not moneyscripts; we loved codices more than florins, and preferred slender pamphlets to pampered palfreys. 

The bishop's life as a bibliophile contains no dramatic incidents comparable to St. Columba's copyright battle or to 

Harry Widener's tragic effort to save a first edition. His distinction lies in the

authorship of his book and in the fact of his being "one who loved books so much in an age and country that loved them so little." It was a hundred years before the discovery of printing, and all de Bury's "books" were manuscripts, but his collection was very large, containing more items, it was said, than all the libraries of other English bishops of his day put together. There is a tradition that he bequeathed his library to the Durham Benedictines at Oxford, but the dissolution of Durham College by Henry VIII left neither books nor records. No catalogue of Richard's library is known, but its extent cannot be doubted, Richard's zeal and opportunities as a collector are so apparent to anyone who reads his book, which was completed just before his death. 

Philobiblon survived in as many as twenty-eight Latin manuscripts; it was

printed in both Germany and France from the close of the fifteenth century, and in England from the close of the sixteenth. But the definitive edition, that of E. C. Thomas, Oxford Scholar, published in England in 1888, was a small one. Although Philobiblon is frequently referred to and quoted in books by and about bibliomaniacs, the book itself has always been hard to find. Not until Thomas's translation was reprinted in the present century in the King's 

Classics and the Oxford Medieval Li brary Series did the book become generally available; and even now, because these series are not commonly known in America, the book is rarely seen.

Philobiblon may be divided roughly into four parts. The first three of the very short chapters are introductory, eulogizing learning and wisdom, and expanding the thesis that true wisdom

and happiness are to be found only in books. There follow four chapters written as by the books themselves, voicing their complaints against the "modern neglect and abuse accorded them. In one stirring passage the books cry out in anger against the biped beast...[who], always jealous of the love of us, and never to be appeased, at length seeing us in some corner protected only by the web of some dead spider, with a frown abuses and reviles us with bitter words...and advises that we should speedily be converted into rich caps, sendal and silk and twice-dyed purple...

That is how Richard imagined that the books felt about women! Concluding lamentations, these not at all outdated, are ones occasioned by war. After the complaints come nine chapters setting forth the solid knowledge and clear convictions underlying Richard's en-

thusiasm. He was a collector who read his books with diligence. He delighted in the careful consideration of every word, and his grammatical studies extended as far as Hebrew and Arabic. 

He preferred books of liberal learning to books of law and "did not wholly neglect the fables of the poets" because, like Sir Philip Sidney later, he saw in literature a guide to right action all the more effective for being so entertaining. Closing this section is a chapter entitled "Of Showing Due Propriety in the Custody of Books." Its admo nitions go straight to the heart of every person who feels an affectionate care for his books. The concluding three chapters outline Richard's plan to present his library to Oxford, and specify rules for the use of the books by students. 

The whole book, written in a formal churchly style, breathes a passion and

devotion to books that is as modern as it is ancient. One of the most eloquent expressions of this devotion is his frequent humanizing of the books themselves. He speaks fondly of "the race of books"; he designates translators as "foster fathers", and elsewhere he tells us how "volumes that had slumbered through long ages in their tombs wake up and are astonished." To Richard, books epitomized life. He says: In books I find the dead as if they were alive; in books I foresee things to come; in books warlike affairs are set forth; from books come forth the laws of peace. All things are corrupted and decay in time; Saturn ceases not to devour the children that he generates; all the glory of the world would be buried in oblivion, unless God had provided mortals with the remedy of books. 

Richard de Bury was a loving and sentimental collector, and in his small

volume he expressed six hundred years ago all that has been said since that time about the joys of book collecting. 

Beyond his own delight in his books, there was his thought that his collection would be "a perpetual gift" to the students of Oxford. Although his library was scattered and lost, Philobiblon remains, a fitting memorial. In it the dead is indeed as if alive, and it is a warming experience to meet across the centuries the first English author of a book about books.
