STATE TAX ADMINISTRATION 

HE loss to the state of millions of dollars in uncollected taxes and the unnecessary and costly inconvenience to taxpayers are the chief by-products of the decentralized and uncoordinated system of state tax administration in Michigan. 

Unofficial estimates place the losses of the state government from uncollected taxes in 1938 at approximately $15 million. 

Losses of this sort were attributed by the recent Tax Study Commission to the inadequate administrative provisions of the tax laws.

Although there have been numerous attempts to effect a reorganization of the tax machinery, these efforts have thus far been unsuccessful. In view of the present stringent condition of state finances, however, the possibility of checking these large annual tax losses may soon prove a strong enough incentive to bring about the needed reform. This pamphlet contains a brief review of the development and present status of state tax administration in Michigan, a short summary of administrative reorganization in other states, and an analysis of the various proposals for reorganization that have been advanced in Michigan. 

NATURE AND DEVELOPMENT OF TAX ADMINISTRATION 

Tax administration has been defined by Lutz as "the entire procedure from the initial determination of the tax obligation under any tax law to the enforcement of penalties for a default on that obligation." The term should not be confused with revenue administration which, in general, embraces the entire process of collecting or receiving governmental revenues. Some states have tax commissions or departments that collect other revenues in addition to taxes, while others have revenue agencies whose functions are limited exclusively to tax administration.

Historically, state governments relied mainly upon a state levy under the locally-administered property tax. Defects in both the local administration and in the tax itself, however, soon forced the states to undertake first the supervision and then some of the actual administration of the tax. This change in administration, coupled with the increasing demands for new sources of revenues to finance the ever increasing functions of government, eventually gave rise to state tax administration as it exists today.

Until 1935 the general property tax in Michigan included a levy for state purposes; this was apportioned among the minor political subdivisions in proportion to their total assessed valuations. Many of the units, desiring to avoid their full share of the levy, purposely undervalued the properties on their assessment rolls. To correct this, the state government took its first step towards tax administration through the creation, in 1851, of the State Board of Equalization. The function of this board was to provide an equitable basis for the apportionment of the state levy. It attempted to do this by equalizing the total valuations of the several counties—that is, by raising those which it deemed to be too low and reducing those with comparatively high assessment ratios. 

Varying standards of administration in the hundreds of small, independent tax districts were similarly responsible for an increasing volume of complaints over individual assessments and the manner in which property valuations were determined. 

These and other criticisms-notably the contention of railroads that property in general was grossly underassessed-were responsible for the creation, in 1899, of the State Board of Tax 2

In 1901, after a bitter controversy over the taxation of railroad property, the legislature adopted the special, ad valorem tax on such property in place of the gross receipts tax. Because of the state-wide character of railroad property, state administration of the new tax was deemed preferable to local assessment and collection. The State Board of Assessors, consisting of the governor and the State Board of Tax Commissioners, was created to make the assessment, and the auditorgeneral was designated to collect it. The tax rate is the average of all local property tax rates throughout the state. The revision of the law to include telephone, telegraph, sleeping car, express, and car-loaning companies increased the amount of property subject to this "specific" tax to approximately one-seventh of the total assessed valuation of all property in the state. 

Meanwhile, the increasing diversification in the types of property was steadily bringing about modifications of the property tax. As a result of the inability of local assessors to discover and evaluate all property liable to taxation, and because of the inequity of taxing all properties at the same rate, certain types of property were, from time to time, taken from the general property list and subjected to low-rate "specific" taxes. In some instances, as in the case of the mortgage tax (1911) and the bond tax (1913) administration was vested in local officials. 

Generally, however, because of the greater advantages of uniform assessment and collection, the administration was placed in the hands of some state agency. This is illustrated by the state intangibles tax, which was adopted in 1939 as a substitute for the local property tax on intangible personal property. During recent years the need of the state government for additional revenue has been responsible for the enactment of several new taxes, the administration of which has been scattered among several departments. 3

STATE TAX ADMINISTRATION PRESENT ADMINISTRATIVE ORGANIZATION 

As a result of its unplanned and sporadic development, state tax administration in Michigan is carried on under an extremely decentralized and scattered arrangement. Twelve state and two local agencies participate in the function. Of the state agencies, some are departments under independently elected officials, and others are headed by appointees of the governor. Some are boards or commissions appointed by the governor, others consist entirely of ex-officio members, and still others are made up of both appointed and ex-officio members. To some agencies, tax administration is the chief function, whereas to others the task is of only secondary importance, being overshadowed by other functions.

From a fiscal standpoint, the State Board of Tax Administration is the most important of the state tax agencies. It administers the retail sales and use taxes which, in the fiscal year ending June 30, 1939, yielded a total of $51.8 million. The board is composed ex-officio of four members-the state treasurer, auditor-general, secretary of state, and a managing director, who is appointed by the governor. The managing director is actually the administrative head of the department, although, according to the law, the board, if it so desires, may exercise considerable influence. Thus far, the board has functioned primarily as an appellate and review agency. 

The secretary of state has large responsibility for tax administration. He collects the motor vehicle and gasoline taxes, as well as those on chain stores, malt, aviation gasoline, and aircraft registration. Collections from these sources amounted in 

1939 to $48.9 million. By virtue of an amendment to the sales tax act in 1939 the secretary of state now collects the sales tax from automobile dealers, but the proceeds are transferred to the 

State Board of Tax Administration. 

The auditor-general collects the special property tax from the railroads and other public utilities which is levied by the State Board of Assessors. He also collects the steamship tonnage tax and provides some supervision over local collection of the

inheritance tax. For many years he collected delinquent property taxes, but this duty was placed in the hands of the county treasurers by a revision of the tax law in 1939. Until the adoption of the low-rate tax on intangible personal property, the auditor-general also received from the county treasurers the proceeds of the bond and mortgage taxes, which were replaced by this new tax. Total receipts from these tax sources in 1939 amounted to $15.6 million.

The Michigan Liquor Control Commission is composed of three members appointed by the governor with the secretary of state, or his deputy, and the governor serving as ex-officio members. It administers the beer, wine, and alcohol taxes which amounted to $4.4 million in 1939.

The State Tax Commission consists of three members appointed by the governor. This agency was originally created as the Board of State Tax Commissioners to supervise the local administration of the property tax. In 1929 it was placed in charge of the administration of the severance tax on oil and gas, and in 1939 the intangibles tax was placed under its jurisdiction. This latter tax will increase the commission's importance in the state tax picture, but its chief role is still in connection with the local property tax the levy of which in 1938 amounted to $166 million. Although the state no longer levies a property tax, it must continue to supervise the local administration of the tax in an effort to ensure an equitable and fair distribution of the burden.

The State Board of Assessors, consisting of the Tax Commission and the governor, assesses the property of railroad, sleeping car, car loaning, telephone, telegraph, and other companies paying the special property tax, the proceeds of which, except for the tax on sleeping car companies, go into the primary school interest fund. In actual practice the board is really the 

Tax Commission under a different name, as the governor rarely, if ever, participates in the assessment of the public utilities. 

The Tax Commission, the auditor-general, and the commissioner of agriculture now constitute the State Board of

Equalization. The chief purpose of the state equalization, as already noted, was to provide a fair basis for the apportionment of the state tax levy under the general property tax. With the abandonment of the tax levy on property for state purposes in 1935, the state equalization is now used mainly in connection. with the formula for apportioning special aid to the school districts. 

Of the remaining agencies, the Corporation and Securities

Department is the most important, at least in respect to the amount of taxes collected. The annual corporate privilege tax, which is under its jurisdiction, amounted to approximately $5 million in 1939. The insurance commissioner administers the premium tax on foreign insurance companies, and this amounted to $3.9 million in 1939. The Athletic Board of Control, consisting of five members appointed by the governor, administers the tax upon boxing shows. The chairman, who is designated by the governor, is the executive officer of the board. The racing commissioner collects the license tax levied on the privi lege of conducting horse race meetings. The Public Service 

Commission, which until 1939 was called the Public Utilities Commission, collects the motor transport fees. Not more than 

40 per cent of these fees may be used for administrative expenses, and the balance is credited to the state highway fund. 

THE NEED FOR CENTRALIZATION 

The outstanding characteristic, as well as the chief weakness, of the present organization of tax administration in Michigan is the failure to provide for a co-ordinated administration of all the tax laws. Commenting upon this decentralization, the Commission of Inquiry Into Taxation in 1930 made the following statement: "It must be apparent to all the people of this state that inefficiency, lack of co-ordination, duplication of efforts, multiplicity of ideas having the sanction of official approval, lack of supervision and intelligent direction are all, not to mention many others, breeders of waste. Our present system of ad-

ministration of laws having to do with taxes is so archaic as to suggest a revamping."

Centralization of administration will not solve all of Michigan's taxation problems. It will, however, eliminate many of the defects in the existing administrative organization. Through it, responsibility for the enforcement of all tax laws could be adequately guaranteed; duplication could be avoided; knowledge and information on the workings of the tax system could be readily obtained; inconvenience to taxpayers could be minimized; and, finally, more efficient results and real economies could be achieved.

ADMINISTRATIVE REORGANIZATION IN OTHER STATES 

Although Michigan is not the only state that is operating under a decentralized tax administration machinery, it is one of a steadily decreasing number. By 1938, approximately fifteen states had consolidated practically all taxes under one central agency, while eight others had also improved their machinery through partial concentration. In addition, seven states-Alabama, Colorado, Iowa, Kansas, Minnesota, Ohio, and Wisconsin in 1939 effected reorganizations that were designed to bring about greater centralization of administrative control. 

A recent survey, by the Federation of Tax Administrators in Chicago, of the number of agencies administering the eight most important state taxes reveals that Michigan is lagging behind in the trend toward unification of tax administration. 

The taxes included in the survey were those on property, sales, gasoline, motor vehicles, death, liquor, income, and tobacco. 

Not all states levy all eight taxes; none levy less than five. 

Michigan levies the first six of these eight taxes, and five agencies are engaged in their administration. Among the other forty-seven states, nine have but one agency, ten have two, thirteen have three, eleven have four, three have five, and one has six. For the administration of the taxes included in the sur-

vey, the forty-eight states have a total of 137 agencies, or an average of approximately three for each state. 

THE DEGREE of CentraLIZATION 

Three states-Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, and Utahhave completely centralized all taxes under one agency. Five others-Massachusetts, New York, North Carolina, Oklahoma, and Tennessee—have placed all but one tax under centralized administration. In Massachusetts the motor vehicle tax is administered by the Department of Public Works; in the other four states, the tax on insurance premiums is administered by the insurance department.

Motor vehicle, insurance, and liquor taxes are the three taxes most frequently omitted from centralized administration. 

In most states the regulatory aspects of these taxes are presumably of greater importance than the revenues they yield, although in some states they are treated primarily as revenue measures and administered by tax agencies. Kentucky, in creating its department of revenue in 1936, empowered the commissioner to assign certain taxes to agencies which might be better equipped to handle them. This was done in respect to taxes on insurance companies and motor carriers, although final responsibility for the administration of the taxes rests with the revenue department.

In a few states other functions besides tax administration have been vested in the revenue or finance department. The 

New York Department of Taxation and Finance, for example, performs the treasury function of collection and disbursement of all state funds. Similarly, the Rhode Island Department of 

Co-ordination and Finance administers all fiscal functions of the state. The Massachusetts Department of Corporations and 

Taxation, as the name implies, also regulates and supervises corporate organizations in that state. 

One of the important results of the reorganizations in most states has been the general recognition of the value of and need

for a well-developed, continuous tax research program. Most states which have reorganized their tax machinery have set up divisions of research in the new department to study the workings of the several taxes and to inform the legislature and the public of needed changes and improvements. 

THE TYPE OF AGENCY 

The traditional state agency for tax administration has been the tax commission or board, consisting usually of three members. In Michigan, as in most states, this agency was created for the purpose of supervising local property tax administration. 

Many of the states that have reorganized their tax machinery have made the tax commission the central agency and have transferred the administration of other taxes to its jurisdiction. 

Recently, however, several states have adopted the plan of a tax or revenue department under the direction of a single commissioner rather than a three-member commission. 

There are advantages and disadvantages to each type. The single-headed department is generally viewed as most desirable from an administrative viewpoint because it provides not only a maximum of authority and control, but also definitely fixes responsibility on a single individual. On the other hand, the board or commission type of organization is often favored as a means of preserving continuity in policy, and, further, of preventing arbitrary decisions or abuses of discretionary power. Its chief defect the division of administrative responsibility—is minimized in some states, which provide for an administrative director who is appointed by and answerable to the commission. 

The theory of this latter type of organization is that the commission sets policies and acts as a review and appellate board, while the actual work of administration is carried on by the director.

Where the tax administration machinery has been concentrated under a single-headed agency, several of the states have made provision for a board of appeals and review. In some, 10

this board is interlocked, by means of membership, with the department of revenue or taxation, but in others it is completely independent. 

Iowa, Kansas, Oklahoma, South Carolina, and Utah are among the states which favor the commission type of organization. The first four states have commissions of three members, and Utah has four. The Utah Tax Commission administers all state taxes and supervises the local administration of the property tax. The commission, as a unit, determines policies and acts as a review and appeal body. Each commissioner is separately responsible for the administration of various parts of the work of the commission. 

The Kansas reorganization, effective in 1939, abolished the old State Tax Commission and the Department of Inspections and Registration. In their place, there was created the Revenue and Taxation Commission, with a director of taxation appointed by the commission. The director is in charge of the administration of the income, use, sales, and death taxes formerly administered by the Tax Commission, and the taxes on malt beverages, gasoline, and cigarettes, which were formerly under the Department of Inspections and Registration. The office of motor vehicle commissioner was also abolished, and its functions transferred to the State Highway Department.

New York and Kentucky have combined the plural commission and single commissioner types of organization. The New 

York Department of Finance and Taxation is headed by a commissioner who is also the president of the State Tax Commission which, in turn, has two other members appointed by the governor. The Tax Commission, as the head of the Division of Taxation, administers all state taxes other than those on insurance companies. It also hears appeals and acts as a review body. The 

Kentucky Department of Revenue is headed by a commissioner who, with two associate commissioners, acts as the Tax Commission. The two associate commissioners are in charge of such divisions of the Department of Revenue as the commissioner may designate. 11

At least six states-Louisiana, Massachusetts, Minnesota,

New Jersey, Ohio, and Wisconsin-now have departments headed by a single official. They also have independent boards of appeal and review. Massachusetts has had the longest experience with this plan of organization. The Department of Corporations and Taxation is under the supervision and control of a commissioner. He administers all state taxes except the tax on motor vehicles, and is in charge of the regulation of corporations. In addition, he has broad powers of supervision over local government finances.

Prior to 1930, appeals from the decisions of local assessors or the commissioner of corporations were taken to the Massachusetts courts. Because of lengthy and costly delays in following the regular judicial procedure prescribed for taxation complaints, the Massachusetts legislature, in 1930, created a Board of Tax Appeals and conferred jurisdiction in tax matters to the newly established board. This agency, the name of which lately has been changed to Appellate Tax Board, now has jurisdiction over all matters of appeal and review of protested taxes. 

Minnesota, as part of a general reorganization of its state government, has recently set up a new Department of Taxation. 

The Tax Commission and office of chief oil inspector were both abolished and their functions transferred to the new agency. In addition, the gasoline and cigarette taxes, formerly administered by the agriculture commissioner, and the inheritance and gift taxes, formerly under the attorney general, were transferred to the Department of Taxation. A Board of Tax Appeals of three members was created to hear appeals and review assessments. 

Wisconsin, long cited as an example of good state tax administration, especially in respect to supervision of local property tax administration, also abolished its tax commission in 1939. 

In its place, the legislature created a Department of Taxation headed by a single commissioner, and an independent Board of 

Tax Appeals of five members. 12

PROPOSALS FOR REORGANIZATION IN MICHIGAN 

Within the past twenty years there have been numerous, but unsuccessful, proposals for the reorganization and centralization of Michigan's tax administration machinery. Some of these proposals have been parts of general schemes of state government reorganization, but more often they have been special recommendations applying only to tax administration. To facilitate discussion, the proposals are grouped under three major plans: 

Tax Commission, Tax Department, and Revenue Department. 

THE TAX COMMISSION PLAN 

In 1930, the special Commission of Inquiry Into Taxation recommended that the State Tax Commission be made the basis for centralization and that "all state tax matters now handled by any other department, bureau or division of the state government" be transferred to it. The reorganized commission, it was further recommended, "should be given plenary powers" and "ample authority to make investigations of the correctness and accuracy of tax matters" under its jurisdiction. In his inaugural address to the legislature in 1935, Governor Fitzgerald recommended the creation of a Bureau of Taxation, to be headed by a "non-political body of three members." 

The Bureau, he urged, should be given "all power and authority now vested in.... the State Tax Commission, the State Board of Tax Administration, and . . . . the Secretary of State for the collection of the gasoline tax and the weight tax." In support of his proposal, the governor declared that it would allow the work to "be carried on at less cost and more expeditiously."

Two bills that were almost identical, and which were similar to the governor's proposals, soon appeared in the legislature. 

Senate Bill 133, introduced by Senator Wood, provided a Tax and Revenue Commission of three members "qualified through experience in tax and revenue problems." They were · · • 13

to be appointed by the governor, with the advice and consent of the Senate, for six-year, staggered terms. House Bill 

33, offered by Representative Brown, proposed a similar agency, but stipulated that one member should be an attorney, one a certified public accountant and the third, a qualified tax expert; this bill was not reported out by the committee. 

Under Senator Wood's measure the new commission would have taken over the duties of the tax commission and the State Board of Tax Administration as well as the functions of the secretary of state relative to the gasoline, weight, malt, and chain store taxes, and the inheritance tax from the auditorgeneral. This measure was passed without amendment and sent to the House, where it failed to pass. 

Governor Fitzgerald, in his exaugural message to the 1937 legislature, again urged that centralization be effected. He renewed his support for the commission type of organization by recommending the "creation of a Tax and Revenue Commission of three members to take over the powers and duties of the State Tax Commission, and also to collect the sales tax and the chain store tax, thus centralizing a major portion of the state's revenue-collecting work."

Finally, in the 1939 session, Representative Nichols introduced a bill-House Bill 2-providing for the creation of a tax and revenue commission similar in most respects to those which appeared in the 1935 session, but no action was taken on it. 

THE TAX DEPARTMENT PLAN 

The Committee of Inquiry Into Taxation in 1922 urged that the Board of State Tax Commissioners should be replaced by a separate department of taxation to be headed by a tax commissioner appointed by the governor. The committee, after noting that it was unable to see any justification for having the tax work of the state scattered among several departments, urged that "this work should be centralized" under the proposed department of taxation. A bill to implement the pro- 14

posal-House Bill 448-was introduced in the 1923 legislature but failed to emerge from committee.

THE DEPARTMENT OF REVENUE 

The third type of reorganization plan is based upon a broader concept than either of the other two. Under the name of department of finance or revenue, there have been several proposals for the consolidation of tax administration and other related fiscal functions. Thus, in 1921, the Community Council 

Commission, in proposing general state reorganization, recommended that tax administration be placed under a department of finance to be headed by a director appointed by the governor. 

In 1937, Governor Murphy, in his inaugural address, suggested the "creation of a department of revenue in which would be centralized the task of collecting state revenues now performed by several agencies."

The Tax Study Commission Proposal 

In its report on the tax situation issued early in 1939, the Michigan Tax Study Commission recommended the complete centralization of the administration of all "state taxes, licenses, fees, rents, royalties and income of every description." It proposed that the legislature should create a department of finance to be headed by a director appointed by the governor, with confirmation by the Senate, for a ten-year term. Under the commission's proposal, tax administration would be but a part of the functions assigned to the department. Among its other duties would be the collection of all other state revenues, the registration and regulation of motor vehicles, and supervision of local government finances. 

All state agencies other than the State Tax Commission now engaged in the assessment and collection of state taxes would either be abolished or relieved of such duties. The Tax Commission would devote its full time to the supervision of local assessments, the hearing of appeals on local assessments and 15

taxes, and the assessment of the special property taxes on railroads and other utilities now assessed by the ex-officio State Board of Assessors. It would also perform the state equalization now done by the ex-officio State Board of Equalization. 

The internal organization of the finance department as suggested by the Study Commission, would include the following major divisions: administration, taxation, motor vehicles, finance, and special investigation. 

Senate Bill 273 

In the 1939 legislature, Senator McCallum, a member of the Tax Study Commission, introduced Senate Bill 273 which provided for a reorganization based upon the Study Commission's proposal. Like the Study Commission's suggestion, the bill proposed a department of revenue to be headed by a single commissioner appointed by the governor, with Senate confirmation, for a ten-year term. However, where the Study Commission had recommended that the finance department should collect all state income, Senate Bill 273 provided that the revenue department should collect the following taxes: sales, use, special property (public utilities), inheritance, foreign insurance company, steamship tonnage, gasoline (both regular and aviation), chain store, malt, annual corporate privilege, and severance. On the basis of the tax collections during the year ending 

June 30, 1939, the revenue department as proposed by this bill, would have collected approximately $104 million, or 80 per cent of the total tax revenues. Although the bill was introduced prior to the passage of the intangibles tax, the revenue department would obviously administer this new tax. 

The administration of the automobile registration fee, which accounted for 15 per cent of total tax revenues in 1939, was left with the secretary of state. In this connection, it should be noted that among the states there is no unanimity of opinion as to the proper supervising agency. In New York, the administration is vested in the tax or revenue department; in Massachusetts, 16

it is under the jurisdiction of a special motor vehicle department; while in Kansas the motor vehicle tax is administered by the State Highway Department. The New York Department of Finance and Taxation has indicated its desire to be relieved of the task on the grounds that it is a regulatory function. 

The State Board of Tax Administration was to be abolished under the bill. The Tax Commission was to be limited solely to the functions of "assessment, review, and equalization of values for property tax levies." The bill contained no provision affecting the composition and functions of the State Board of 

Assessors and the State Board of Equalization. 

The bill provided further that the department was to have general supervision over local government finance. Its activities, in this respect, were to include the "administration of all laws relating to the revenues of minor units of government" except those powers exercised by the Tax Commission and the Public Debt Commission. In addition, the department was to make annual reports on the cost of government of all units, including the state, and to prescribe and install uniform accounting systems for all units of local government. The auditor-general would continue to collect delinquent taxes on property sold at the tax sale. Similarly, the Public Service Commission, the racing commissioner, the Athletic Board of Control, and the Liquor Control Commission would continue to administer those taxes now under their respective jurisdictions. As in the case of the automobile registration fee, the regulatory aspect of these several taxes provides a plausible ground for leaving them outside the revenue department.

The internal organization of the department was to be left to the discretion of the commissioner, subject to approval by the governor. Certain features, however, were prescribed in the bill. For example, the commissioner was empowered to appoint "an executive assistant and not more than 5 deputies." Further, the departmental organization was to include the following divisions: administration, business taxation, general revenue, local finance, and special investigation. 17

Both the Study Commission proposal and Senate Bill 273 

contained provisions for broad programs of research and study of the general problems of taxation and its administration. The 

Study Commission pointed out that "the state now.... is without information or data of the kind indicated to be necessary in several recommendations of this report." Senate Bill 273 specifically charged the division of administration with the duty of " . studying and reporting on the operation and effect of the tax laws." The bill was reported out of committee and advanced as far as third reading, where it failed to pass. 

CONCLUSION 

Michigan's present machinery of tax administration, like its tax system, is the result of an unplanned and unco-ordinated growth. Under the present arrangement, the state has one of the most decentralized systems of state tax administration. The need for centralization is indicated by the numerous proposals for administrative consolidation which have been made in recent years, and by the bills which have been introduced into the state legislature. A number of these proposals would have been satisfactory solutions to Michigan's tax administration problems. 

This is especially true of the proposal of the Tax Study Commission and of the reorganization provided in Senate Bill 273. 

Regardless of whether the commission or single-headed type of agency is finally adopted, the centralization of responsibility and authority will make it possible for Michigan to put its tax administration on a sound, efficient, and economical basis. 18
