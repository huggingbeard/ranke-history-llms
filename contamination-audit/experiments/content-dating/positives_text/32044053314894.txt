There were a number of significant developents in U.S. foreign policy during 1958-but nderlying these developments are certain conant features of U.S. policy which are determined oth by the constancy of America's basic goals nd by the existence of long-range trends and connuing conditions in world affairs.

Isic Goals The basic goal of American foreign policy is to 'omote and protect the welfare and interests of e American people. In testimony before the nate Foreign Relations Committee, Secretary of ate John Foster Dulles explained, "The inter- 's of the United States, which our foreign policy ould safeguard and promote, include: the lives d homes of our people, their confidence and ace of mind, their economic well-being, and their eals."

Because the greatest threat to these interests is ir, which today might mean the destruction of civilization itself, the primary objective of U.S. policy is to assure a just and lasting peace. 

But the peace we seek is not peace at any price. 

As Secretary Dulles said: "History has clearly demonstrated that peace cannot be assured by a policy of placating aggressors of 'peace at any price.' The fact is that men will not accept peace at any price. Human beings are so constituted that many would rather fight and die than concede their God-given rights." True peace must be peace with justice a peace which not only protects our lives and our material well-being but also preserves America's ideals and permits them to flourish.

The World Situation 

Policies through which we effectively pursue our goal of a true peace must be based on the realities of the world situation in which they operate. They must take into account the great currents and forces of our time.

Communist Imperialism. President Eisen hower has declared: "The threat to our safety, and to the hope of a peaceful world, can be simply stated. It is Communist imperialism." What makes this Soviet threat unique, the President said, is its all-inclusiveness. "Every human activity," he added, "is pressed into service as a weapon of expansion. Trade, economic development, military power, arts, science, education, the whole world of ideas-all are harnessed to this same chariot of expansion. The Soviets are, in short, waging total cold war." 1

Interdependence. Another basic factor in the world situation is the fact of national interdependence. As Secretary Dulles pointed out, "We cannot be sure of peace, liberty, or well-being unless other nations also have them." Today's world has been so shrunk by the developments of science and technology that events anywhere impinge on men everywhere, he declared.

In the 20th century national isolation is not possible. And despite the vast power and resources of our country, should we attempt to isolate ourselves from the rest of the world, we would be unable to assure even our own national security.

A Changing World. Change is one of the most fundamental and universal characteristics of the world in which we live. Secretary Dulles identified a number of areas where forces for change are conspicuous. "There is change from colonialism to independence," he said, and he pointed out that within the last 15 years 700 million people of 21 countries have won political independence, and this trend is continuing. And, as he said in testimony before the Congress, ". .. these people, as well as the peoples of other less developed nations, are determined that they must and will have economic progress." A second area of change is brought about by the widening horizons of the physical world. 

Studies carried on during the International Geophysical Year have revealed new potentialities in the Arctic and Antarctic for service to mankind.

Great changes are taking place in the world of physical power. As Secretary Dulles noted, "The splitting of the atom has revealed new sources of power so potent and so omnipresent as to imply a new industrial revolution. This new power has already changed the nature of war, and, if atomic warfare does not destroy us first, it will also change the nature of peace."

A fourth area of change is in the opening of outer space by scientific exploration. This fact, the Secretary of State said, has consequences which, "as yet, we sense but dimly but which will surely hold possibilities of vast purport for peace and for war."

Great changes are taking place within the society of nations, Mr. Dulles declared. International communism seeks to unify the world by imposing control on all national governments. 

But a concomitant change is taking place vol tarily within the non-Communist count "They tend to draw together in the face of c mon danger and are more than ever a cohe force in world affairs," Secretary Dulles said. conclusion he stated that this challenge of cha "requires that world society should develop f one of anarchy to one of order. Soviet Economic Offensive 

During 1958 the Soviet Union continued to intensify its economic offensive designed to further Communist influence through trade and economic assistance.

In his message to the Congress recommending the vigorous continuation of the mutual security program, President Eisenhower explained the Government's attitude toward the Soviet bloc policy of offering economic and technical aid to countries of the free world. He stated that, if the purpose of such aid were simply to help other countries overcome economic difficulties without infringing their freedom, such aid could be welcomed as forwarding the free-world purpose of economic growth. "But," he cautioned, "there is nothing in the history of international communism to indicate that this can be the case." Until such evidence is forthcoming, the President said, the United States and other free nations must assume that Soviet bloc aid is a new, subtle, and longrange instrument directed toward the same old purpose of drawing its recipient away from the community of free nations and ultimately into the Communist orbit.

East-West Exchange 

January marked the successful conclusion of negotiations, begun the previous October, for an exchange agreement between the United States and the Soviet Union. The agreement provides for exchanges in such media as radio and television broadcasts and the showing of films, and for wide exchanges in cultural, technical, and educational fields. It also specifies that there are to be exchanges of graduate students, instructors, professors, and scientists. Multiple exchanges are to take place in medicine and agriculture and between athletic teams, and there will be exchanges of outstanding entertainment groups and artists.

Ambassador William S. B. Lacy, Special sistant to the Secretary of State for East-W Exchange and principal U.S. negotiator for agreement, described its significance in a spar on February 12. Threat to the Offshore Islands

In July 1958 the Chinese Communists began intensive military activity in the Taiwan (Formosa) Straits area. Then on August 23 the offshore islands held by the Republic of China, and Quemoy in particular, were suddenly subjected to intensive bombardment by Chinese Communist artillery which lasted for 6 weeks. At the same time the official Peiping. radio repeatedly announced that the purpose of these military operations was to take by armed force Taiwan, as well as the offshore islands. These Chinese Communist objectives were stanchly supported by the Soviet Union.

President declared on May 3. The President at the same time announced that the United States had invited 11 other countries which had engaged in scientific activities in Antarctica during the 

International Geophysical Year to attend a conference to conclude a treaty assuring the continuation of scientific cooperation in the area. 

The kind of treaty proposed by the United States would insure freedom of scientific investigation throughout Antarctica by citizens, organizations, and governments of all countries and a 16 21 continuation of the kind of international scientific cooperation carried on during the IGY which ended December 31, 1958. The treaty would also insure that Antarctica be used for peaceful pur- poses only.

Before the end of 1958 the United States and Australia, Argentina, and New Zealand had agreed to continue their cooperative scientific operations in Antarctica begun during the IGY.

OTHER DEVELOPMENTS Outer Space 

Renewing a previously made proposal to the 

Soviet Union, the President on January 12 called for prompt action looking toward an agreement to insure "that outer space be used only for peaceful purposes."

Soviet Premier Bulganin on February 1 re jected the President's proposal, declaring that the question of insuring the peaceful use of outer space "can be considered only as a part of the general problem of the prohibition of nuclear and rocket weapons. 99 In July the Congress established an agency under civilian control to guide and direct national efforts in the exploration of space. The act contains the following declaration:

The Congress hereby declares that it is the policy of the United States that activities in space be devoted to peaceful purposes for the benefit of all mankind.

The act also includes a statement defining the objective of our national efforts as "cooperation by the United States with other nations and groups of nations in work done pursuant to this act and in the peaceful application of the results thereof." 

The House of Representatives subsequently gave unanimous support to a resolution requesting the President to submit to the United Nations the question of international cooperation in dedicating outer space to peaceful purposes only. 

In September, speaking before the opening session of the 13th U.N. General Assembly on 

September 18, Secretary Dulles declared: "The

United States believes that the United Nations should take immediate steps to prepare for a fruitful program of international cooperation in peaceful uses of outer space." He proposed the estab-

lishment of a representative committee to make necessary preparatory studies and recommendations to this end. 

In November the United States and 19 other nations introduced in the U.N. General Assembly a resolution to promote international cooperation to insure the peaceful use of outer space. In support of the resolution, Senator Lyndon Johnson, representing the United States in Committee I of the General Assembly, called attention to the overwhelming bipartisan support in the U.S. 

Congress for the dedication of outer space to peaceful purposes and "the essential unity of the American people in their support of the goals of the resolution offered now in their name." The resolution, adopted by a vote of 54 to 9 with 18 abstentions, provides for an ad hoc committee to study the means by which the United Nations might facilitate international cooperation in this field. The Soviet Union, which is a member of the ad hoc committee, has stated that it will not participate in the committee's work.

Atomic Energy 

The U.S. Government on January 18 made three new offers of assistance to speed the work of the new International Atomic Energy Agency 

(IAEA) and to insure the future supply of nuclear scientists available to the Agency and its members. The United States offered: (1) to make available to the IAEA on a costfree basis the services of 20 to 30 expert consultants in the field of peaceful uses of atomic energy; (2) to provide about 120 fellowships over the next 2 years for training in nuclear sciences in the 

United States and; (3) to contribute up to $125,000 toward a $250,000 Agency fellowship fund for similar training in member states throughout the world. 

At the Second General Conference of the International Atomic Energy Agency at Vienna in Sep tember, the chairman of the U.S. delegation, John 

A. McCone, emphasized U.S. support for the 

Agency's activities. He declared that, "As time and experience progress, and consistent with our existing obligations, the United States will look to the Agency as the major institutional channel through which international peaceful-use programs of the United States will be carried forward."

Passports 

On June 16 the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that the Department of State regulations placing limitations on the issuance of passports to persons supporting the world Communist movement were m without statutory foundation. This Supreme 

Court decision denied to the Secretary of State the authority, in the absence of statute, to refuse passports to persons who are members of the Communist Party or affiliated with that party. During the proceedings the Government conceded and the Court took cognizance of the fact that the right to travel abroad is a constitutional right, a part of liberty, of which the citizen cannot be deprived without due process of law. 

In a

The administration on July 7 submitted to the Congress a draft bill which would give the Secretary of State the statutory authority to restrict the issuance of passports in certain cases. message to the Congress the President declared it to be essential that the Government today "have power to deny passports where their possession would seriously impair the conduct of the foreign relations of the United States or would be inimical to the security of the United States." The President also asked that the Secretary be given the authority to prevent Americans from using passports for travel to areas where there is no means of protecting them, or where their presence would conflict with U.S. foreign policy objectives or be inimical to the security of the United States. 

Though the House of Representatives subsequently passed a bill giving the Secretary of State the requested authority, the Senate failed to act upon such a measure before the end of the congressional session. 

Increased Availability of Funds

In October, at the New Delhi meeting of the Board of Governors of the International Monetary 

Fund and of the International Bank for Recon struction and Development, the U.S. delegation submitted for the consideration of the members a number of proposals designed to increase the effectiveness of the IMF and the IBRD in promoting economic growth. The U.S. proposals in- 22

The United States also proposed the creation of an International Develi opment Association affiliated with the IBRD which would grant long-term loans for economic development repayable in whole or in part in the currencies of the borrowing countries.

CULTURAL EXCHANGE Exchange of Persons 

The international educational exchange program was extended to 95 countries during 1958, with the exchange of 4,781 foreign and 1,879

American students, teachers, lecturers, research - scholars, and distinguished leaders in govern Iment, the press, labor, the arts, and many other - fields.

A major development during 1958 was the expansion of the exchange program with the other 

American Republics. Altogether, 1,541 exchanges will be carried out with Latin America during the period July 1, 1958, through June 30, 1959.

Ten short-term seminars for undergraduate student leaders from 34 universities in Latin

America are being held in universities in this country. These students attend 4-week intensive courses at their host universities. After a 10-day visit to other parts of the United States, they will return to their campuses for at least a year, where they will be in an influential position to acquaint their fellow students with this country. 

Assistance has been given to three universities in the United States to undertake Junior Year

Abroad projects in Latin America. Although such projects have been successfully carried out for some years in Europe under private auspices, this will be the first time that groups of undergraduate students from the United States will have an opportunity to spend a full year on Latin 

American campuses.

The regular exchange of graduate students for a full year of study is being materially expanded. 

The exchange of university professors, leaders of thought and opinion, and U.S. specialists is likewise being stepped-up, and programs to advance the teaching of English are being intensified through teacher exchanges.

Cultural Presentations The President's Special International Program for Cultural Presentations continued to assist out-t standing American performing artists and athletes with overseas tours so that they might demonstrate American cultural and sports achievements to foreign audiences. The ties which unite us with other nations were strengthened, and distorted views and misconceptions about the United States were at least partially corrected through these first-hand experiences of the nature and quality of contemporary cultural life in the 

United States.

In 1958 the President's program assisted 27 touring units, including 14 musical groups, 4 dance companies, 3 theatrical troupes, and 6 athletic teams. Performances were given in 205 cities in 

72 countries in all areas of the world, including the Soviet Union and other Eastern European countries.

Projecting the American Image Overseas 

A major occupation of USIA was to help build confidence among foreign peoples in the peaceful purposes of the United States by consistently delineating the broad field of American life and culture. 

English language classes at 165 overseas cultural centers and language institutes were crowded. Local teachers of English, whose students numbered more than a million, attended English teaching seminars in many countries. In some places English classes were conducted via radio or television. A TV English course in Mexico achieved the highest audience rating in Mexican television history. 

USIA placed hundreds of thousands of American books in the hands of overseas readers through the operation of 155 libraries, a low-cost book program, a special series of translations in many languages, and other book distribution activities.

Throughout the free world special efforts were made to inform students about the American na- 25

tion and its people. One way of doing this was to encourage classroom instruction in foreign schools in the literature and civilization of the 

United States. 

Peoples in the free-world countries saw USIA motion pictures in their own movie houses or at our libraries or cultural centers. Mobile units brought American movies in many languages to remote areas.

American music was heard increasingly at both recorded and live concerts sponsored by the in formation posts. Exhibits dramatizing U.S. sci ence and technology and the atom in the service of mankind were seen widely, as were presentations of American art. The unique photographic exhibit "The Family of Man" completed 31½ years of showings abroad, with a total audience of al most four million persons.
