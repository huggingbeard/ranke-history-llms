To: 

Report of the 

Virginia Advisory Legislative Council 

Richmond, Virginia 

September 18, 1953

Honorable John S. Battle, Governor of Virginia and 

The General Assembly of Virginia 

During the 1952 regular session of the General Assembly several measures were introduced having as their purpose the curbing of the increasing losses suffered by landowners throughout the State because of stealing of livestock, fruit, vegetables and crops. The General Assembly, feeling that these measures should be given careful study in order to determine their desirability and practicability, adopted the following resolution: 

SENATE JOINT RESOLUTION NO. 12 

Directing the Virginia Advisory Legislative Council to study statutes relating to the protection of landowners. 

Whereas, the stealing of chickens, turkeys and field crops along highways has gotten to be commonplace, the rustling of cattle and other live stock seems to be on the increase; and 

Whereas, it is difficult for the farmer to cope with the situation under present conditions; Now, Therefore, Resolved by the Senate, and House of Delegates concurring that the Virginia Advisory Legislative Council is hereby directed to make a thorough study and report of the statutes designed for the protection of landowners from the presence of unauthorized persons on their property. The Council shall consider appropriate measures which may be necessary to protect farmers from the depredations above referred to and make recommendations deemed appropriate and desirable. The Council shall complete its study and make its report to the Governor and General Assembly not later than October one, nineteen hundred fifty-three. This study and report shall not relate to the statutes concerning trespassing by hunters and fishermen. 

The Virginia Advisory Legislative Council designated Harry B. Davis, of Princess Anne County, to serve as chairman of a committee to make the preliminary study and report to it pursuant to this resolution. Serving with 

Mr. Davis as members of the Committee were the following: Lloyd C. Bird, member of the Senate of Virginia, Chesterfield County; W. R. Blandford, Commonwealth's Attorney, Powhatan County; Parke C. Brinkley, Commissioner of Agriculture and Immigration, Richmond; A. K. Gilmer, Russell 

County; and Paul D. Sanders, Editor, The Southern Planter, Richmond.

Careful study was given the pertinent statute law of Virginia affecting the problem. Information concerning the nature and extent of the practices complained of was sought from law enforcement officials of the State and from others having special knowledge of the problem. After due publicity a public hearing was held by the Committee at which opportunity was given to all interested persons to present their views for the consideration of the Committee.

After mature consideration of all the data before it, the Committee reported to the Council. The Council has reviewed th report of the Committee and submits the following recommendations: 801191

STATUTES FOR PROTECTION OF LANDOWNERS RECOMMENDATIONS 

(1) The section of the Code which provides penalties for larceny of livestock should be amended so as to increase the penalties as they apply to cattle. (2) Provision should be made for the confiscation of motor vehicles used in connection with the theft of livestock, poultry or farm produce of the value of $50.00 or more.

REASONS FOR RECOMMENDATIONS 

Accurate statistical data as to the extent of the damages complained of in Senate Joint Resolution No. 12 proved to be impossible to assemble. There is no central repository from which accurate information as to petty thievery can be ascertained. The only figures are those which deal with apprehension, trial and sentencing of the offenders, which cannot be obtained except from the courts throughout the State. These do not show the many cases of petty pilfering of field crops, fruit and vegetables, perhaps without the knowledge of the victim.

It is evident that cattle "rustling" is, in terms of actual monetary damages to landowners throughout the State, the most serious aspect of the problem. Reports made to the Department of State Police indicate that cattle valued at many thousands of dollars are stolen each year in Virginia. 

In some states in which cattle raising is the major agricultural interest and in which because of geographic reasons and availability of ready markets cattle rustling has been more prevalent than it has in Virginia, elaborate systems to prevent its occurrence have been devised. In some of the western states, widespread use of cattle branding and a system of state registration of brands has been developed. Such a system does not seem appropriate to 

Virginia where, in the absence of open ranges and because of the methods used in the sale of cattle, use of branding is almost unknown. The cost of such a program, and the difficulties of its enforcement, appear to preclude at this time consideration of this as a solution to the problem. 

During the 1952 session of the General Assembly, a bill was introduced which would have required any person transporting cattle by motor vehicle to have in his possession a bill of sale or other documentary evidence of the ownership of the cattle. This suggestion was considered but it appears that due to enforcement difficulties and the harassment of the legitimate cattleman or livestock dealer, the good which might be accomplished by such a statute would be counterbalanced by the disadvantages entailed. It would be especially hard for the small operator to comply with the requirements of such a statute, particularly where the cattle or other livestock which he is handling were raised by himself and are transported to market in his own truck or perhaps commingled with livestock belonging to a neighbor. 

At the 1952 session of the General Assembly, bills were introduced in both houses having for their purpose the protection of landowners from the presence of unauthorized persons on their property. These bills would have created a special type of law enforcement officer called a "land warden", would have permitted any landowner to be made a land warden and thereafter allowed him to carry arms to arrest any trespasser on his premises. 

Evidence has not been produced that the problem under study is so acute in Virginia as to require so drastic a remedy. The laws of the Commonwealth as to trespass, founded on sound common law principles, have been little changed during its entire history and, with the exception of the use of motor vehicles as a means of putting perpetrators of such offenses rapidly beyond the jurisdiction in which the offense is committed, no new conditions have recently developed requiring the creation of what would be in effect an army of private policemen. The adoption of such a measure 2

appears ill advised and would undoubtedly produce more harm than it would cure. 

Another bill introduced at the 1952 session approached the problem by an amendment to the present statute §18-165 to fix a minimum penalty of ten years imprisonment, and a maximum term of twenty years, for larceny of cattle, some farm animals and poultry. This appears too drastic a penalty, particularly as to the minimum set, and it might be that if such were adopted, juries would fail to convict rather than to subject the accused to what they might feel is too great punishment under the circumstances. 

At the same time the increase in the number of cattle thefts and the substantial sums of money involved indicate that some corrective action should be taken. The present statute fixes sufficiently severe penalties for larceny of "a horse, mule or ass" but prescribes a much lesser penalty for larceny of poultry or cows or other livestock. It is felt that this statute in its present form is an anachronism and that an equally high penalty should be imposed for larceny of cattle as is set for the stealing of a horse. It is accordingly recommended that this statute be modernized and that the provisions relating to cattle be placed in that portion of the section under which the higher penalty 3 to 18 years in the penitentiary can be imposed. The use of motor vehicle's has made the stealing of livestock and farm produce more prevalent and more difficult of detection. For instance, it is possible to load stolen cattle on a truck at night and by morning to have them at a market in another state. While no evidence appears that Virginia farmers and cattle raisers have been the victims of any large-scale organized criminal activities in this respect, the use of the motor vehicle in connection with larceny from farms has become more prevalent and for this reason special treatment of these crimes in which motor vehicles are used is justified. 3 It is therefore recommended that a statute be enacted providing for confiscation of any motor vehicle which is used in the commission of any larceny of livestock, poultry or farm produce of the value of $50.00 or more. Confiscation of motor vehicles is permitted under the A. B. C. Act and the statutes relating to protection of fish and game. 

ACKNOWLEDGMENT 

The Council desires to express to the members of the Committee its sincere appreciation for the contribution of their time and effort in the prosecution of this study. It further acknowledges the cooperation which was received from organizations of farmers, cattle raisers and other interested persons as well as the State departments and agencies concerned in this problem. 

Respectfully submitted,

A BILL 

1. 

To amend and reenact §18-165 of the Code of Virginia relating to larceny of certain animals and poultry and penalties therefor 

Be it enacted by the General Assembly of Virginia: 

That §18-165 of the Code of Virginia be amended and reenacted as §18-165. Any person who shall be guilty of the larceny of a horse, mule, cow, steer, bull or * calf shall be punished by confinement in the penitentiary not less than three nor more than eighteen years; and any person who shall be guilty of the larceny of any poultry of the value of five dollars or more, or of a * sheep, lamb, hog, or goat, shall, in the discretion of the jury or court trying the case without a jury, be confined in the penitentiary not less than one nor more than ten years or be confined in jail not exceeding twelve months and fined not exceeding five hundred dollars. 

A BILL 

To amend the Code of Virginia by adding a section numbered 18-165.1, providing for the forfeiture to the Commonwealth of vehicles used in the commission of larceny of certain animals, and how the same shall be enforced.

Be it enacted by the General Assembly of Virginia: 

That the Code of Virginia be amended by adding a section numbered 

18-165.1, as follows: 

§18-165.1. (a) Any vehicle used by the owner thereof or with his knowledge in the transportation of any stolen animal or poultry the larceny of which is punishable under §18-165, or farm produce, when the value of such stolen al, poultry or produce is fifty dollars or more, shall be forfeited to the Commonwealth and shall be seized by any law enforcement officer arresting the operator of such vehicle for larceny of such animals poultry or produce and by him delivered to the sheriff of the county or sergeant of the city in which such arrest is made, and a receipt taken therefor. (b) Thereafter, forfeiture of such vehicle shall be enforced as is provided in § 4-56 of the Code of Virginia as to vehicles used for the transportation of illegally acquired alcoholic beverages, and the provisions of § 4-56 shall apply, mutatis mutandis, to proceedings for the enforcement of such forfeiture.
