CHAPTER I THE PARLOR ROOM of a Chicago mansion in the year 

Nineteen hundred and thirty-nine found the Cartright family, a typical prosperous American family, consisting of John Cartright, the father, May Cartright the mother, a son George seventeen years old, and two charming and beautiful daughters-Jane 15 and Mary 13 years old respectively. Mr. Cartright, a heavy stockholder and an executive officer of an Electrical Manufacturing Co., was not present at the time.

The family gathered in the charmingly furnished parlor about 5 P. M. waiting the arrival of Mr. Cartright who, thirty minutes previously, phoned he had left the office coming home to dinner. Meanwhile

Mrs. Cartright and the three children were feverishly discussing what had taken place at school that day.

The maid, a pleasant-looking young woman, was busily engaged in setting the table for dinner. The meal consisted of fried chicken with all the necessary trimmings. When the butler, with a bottle of champagne in a bucket full of ice, appeared at the door and announced that Mr. Cartright just drove into the garage and was coming up the steps.

Mr. Cartright entering the door scarcely nodding to the butler's greetings. With his newspaper under his arm he walked right into the parlor. Upon en-

tering he noticed a tension prevailed among the members of his family, for all the members were unusually quiet, peering through books and newspapers, with the exception of Mrs. Cartright whose grim expression plainly indicated that she had something of importance to discuss with him. 

John Cartright curiously inquired, "what is wrong with everybody? This is not the way to greet your father on such a hot day. Anything wrong?" "Well John," Mrs. Cartright replied. "I want to speak to you about Mary. That teacher sent her back home from school again this morning. Why, the child has lost four days from school already this week, and she also told Mary as long as she refuses to salute the flag she had better stay home until she makes up her mind to do so. Imagine those teachers how dare they do such a thing, I am so angry I feel like screaming.”

It must be remembered that the Cartright's were among the few Americans who, for reasons best known to themselves, thought it was unnecessary, unpatriotic or unAmerican for them to salute the American flag at school or elsewhere. 

Mr. Cartright being a determined man, characteristic of most business men, had himself told his daughter she did not have to salute the flag. He thundered "by heavens they cannot do that; they have no right or authority to do that, more over it is the schools that are public and not my children. Only the other

day I told them where to get off. If it were not for the pupils those teachers would be out looking for a job. "Since when did the schools and the teachers start running this country? This is a people's government and we have something to say about it." 

Then in a milder tone he asked Mary just what the teacher said to her.

Mary replied: "After all the pupils sang the National Anthem and saluted the flag and because I didn't the teacher took me in to see the principal again. The principal told me I could not come to school any more until I showed more respect for the American flag. It is irreverence for any American to not salute our flag. The flag is the symbol of 

Americanism; it represents the spirit and ideals of our country, etc."

That, of course, did not satisfy the infuriated John 

Cartright, who uttered loudly: "Well you just wait and see. Why should the children or anyone else have to stand and salute a piece of cloth just because it has stars and stripes on it." Inwardly he was thinking --what steps would be necessary to take in order to show them once and for all not to interfere with his constitutional rights, he was determined, if necessary, to take the case to the highest court in the land. 

At this point Mrs. Cartright volunteered the information that she was quite certain the ladies of the

club to which she belonged would take similar steps to help eliminate flag saluting, flag waving and all this war mongering. Moreover they were going to parade down town next week. They did not want their boys plowed under in the battlefields of Europe. 

The butler, a man of about forty years of age, appeared at the door announcing, dinner is served. 

Slowly the family moved into the dining room. Seated around the table while the maid was busy serving the delicious food and coffee, all the members were dining, no one said anything for about ten minutes or so. 

Mr. Cartright, it seemed, had another proposition of great importance on his mind; his firm had offered him an important post in the business abroad and he was pondering over it.

Finally recovering his usual composure he thought this was as good an opportunity as any to break the good news to his wife and children, he looked up and with a smiling expression said: "Well, too bad we had to be so upset at this time just when I was bringing home some unusually good news." 

Every one looked up wondering what this good news could possibly be, after a minute of silence he began. "Our company offered me the agency at Singapore.

I am to be the general manager of all the district of the South Pacific including Hong Kong, parts of

China, the Dutch Indies, Australia, etc. This means a great deal to us, my dear. Mr. Emerson, who was our representative in that district is getting quite old and wants to retire and spend his declining years in a quiet place. The company has given me ten days to decide." 

This bit of news was certainly a great surprise to all the members, especially the children. Their young minds for a few moments were trying to visualize rather happily what fun that would be, adventure, train and boat travel, what a swell vacation and good time we would have. But when they thought of Singapore and Hong Kong they hesitated. To them Singapore and Hong Kong was a jungle infested with wild men. They had seen places like that in the movies. 

They remained silent and expressionless waiting for some one to say something more for they were anxious to ask many, many more questions about a lot of things.

In a few moments that to every one seemed like many hours, Mrs. Cartright replied: "Well, my dear, do you think it will be advisable to leave our friends, home, relatives and everything we possess here and move to a so far away place and start all over again where we do not know anybody? Here we have our family ties, friendly connections for ourselves and our children. It is all quite an adventure, however I have been rather upset this evening, I do not know what to say."

Mr. Cartright abruptly asserted: "The person who would replace me would have to take over our property, in fact the company assured me that we will not suffer any inconvenience or financial loss. Besides, my dear, this most unusual opportunity to a great many people does not come but once in a life time. There are great possibilities for an unlimited market for our merchandise from Shanghai to Australia. "Singapore is a great commercial center, a cosmopolitan city with a polyglot population and that in itself, I think, means a great deal to the children. They will learn the ethics and customs of many people of many races that is quite an education for them. I will admit much of the native population might be poverty-stricken but there are millions who are fabulously wealthy. "The Government Officials, the Dutch, the English, the Germans and French as well as the American colony live in the most exclusive sections; they will be our associates. Besides, we are allowed two months' vacation every year, we can return here or visit other lands if we so desire, so you see it is not as bad as it seems. دو Mrs. Cartright, of course, was thinking about her social connections, her relatives and friends. Being herself a world traveler the only place she had not been was Australia, and now the opportunity presented IO

itself to go more than half way she would surely visit that country within the next year or so. Her enthusiasm was somewhat clouded though because of the uncertain conditions existing in the Orient. 

Rather casually she said: "Well I do not know, whatever you think is best John, but remember there is a war going on between China and Japan. of 

John Cartright assured his wife that the war in Europe would terminate in a very short time for the simple reason; the European countries had no money to fight a long war, and as for the Orient it is not great importance. China and Japan were making business very good with the money they spent by buying rubber, tin, oil, etc. "The natives and everybody in that part of the world have more money now to buy our goods than they ever had before, more over war will never reach the south Pacific," he stated. 

Time was getting late in the evening for the children, they began to disperse and get ready for bed, their young minds churning with the good news of travel. All of them without exception thought the idea was great. Like all youngsters, they were delighted and greatly thrilled with the prospect of travel. They certainly would be disappointed if the trip to Singapore failed to materialize.

Everything that happened in the past month was now forgotten. The following few days Mr. Cartright was discussing the details of his transfer to his I I

new location. The family was also preoccupied with packing, visiting and phoning their friends preparatory to their departure. By Saturday night they were all very tired so they retired early for a good night's sleep. 

Sunday, of course, they slept rather late, breakfast was served about ten A. M. After breakfast was over they moved into the parlor. Mr. Cartright asked for the Sunday paper. He took the sport section and was reading about the Brooklyn Dodgers. Mrs. Cartright was reading the style section and the children were sprawled on the floor looking at the comic section. 

About that time Mr. Phillips, a family friend of long standing, called to visit the Cartright family and see if he could be of some assistance in their preparations for going away. Mr. Phillips, of course, had read the morning's bad news. On the front pages of the newspapers in bold type, appeared the news that Holland and France were invaded; the Germans were near 

Paris. Realizing the seriousness of the times and the effect the war would have for the United States he excitingly walked into the parlor where the Cartright family were oblivious of the situation concerning the momentous events of the day, and despite the sensational news he noticed at a glance that Cartright was reading the sports, Mrs. Cartright the style section, etc. Displaying no emotional interest whatever concerning the war.

I 2

With the usual morning greetings Mr. Phillips asked, "What do you think of the war now?” 

Cartright replied, "What war?" 

Phillips pointed to the front on the floor. page of the newspaper 

Cartright answered, "What are those people fighting for? Can't they live in peace and settle their differences by peaceful means like we are? Anyway that is none of our business. Besides you know the ocean is very long and quite deep-they cannot very well get over here. More over we are going away next week and by the time we settle in Singapore the war will be over," and with this remark he showed quite plainly he would rather dismiss the subject entirely and talk about something else. 

Phillips knew Cartright well enough and did not wish to press the argument any farther, for he felt nothing could convince Cartright. Although he felt indignant he concealed it and changed the subject. 

After a few minutes he departed with a disgusting feeling about his friend's attitude and indifference toward the oncoming global destructiveness, mumbling "How could any one try to pursue business as usual and be blind to the oncoming holocaust; it is unbelievable.”

The next few days final arrangements were completed. The fifth day found the Cartright family on the way to the railway station where their relatives 13

and friends had gathered to bid them bon-voyage and wish them safe landing. San Francisco bound and on a Pacific Liner for Singapore, they were soon on their way.

Thus we find our friends bound for the land of Utopia and riches leaving behind (they thought) a tyrannical United States of America, little realizing that their fate would mark the worst tragedy in 

American history.

Anyway, the weather was fine and everything on the steamer was wonderful and uneventful. After a week or so of smooth sailing they reached their destination, a completely new world to them. They held hopeful visions for the future. John Cartright was hoping for more business and more profits. Mrs. Cartright ambitiously visualizing her outstanding social position, dreaming of princess, Hindu Potentates, English Gentlemen, French Aristocrats and the fabulously rich Dutch and Chinese Merchants. Yes, the upper crust of society with multiple layers of icing where class distinctions were solemnly respected. 

There among the completely polished she would undoubtedly be a social lionness and a great favorite, her unbent dignity and lustre would be of immense advantage to her reputation.

While Mrs. Cartright was hungrily visualizing her future grandeur, her husband came into the stateroom and informed her that the steamer had docked 14

and to be ready with the children to disembark immediately. In a few minutes the family walked down the gang plank and into the Steamship Company's waiting rooms. Telegraphic prearrangements from the home office preceded their arrival thus making it easy for them to clear their papers through the Custom Offices without the usual red tape necessary in such cases. Cartright's local office representatives who had arranged everything were there to receive them.

His Singapore office staff easily located them and after the inquiries and customary representations of each member of the staff the family was escorted to three large waiting automobiles and started on the way to their new home. After driving for about forty-five minutes through the city of Singapore the cars pulled up on the side of a hill and stopped in front of a palatial but oriental looking mansion overlooking the city and surrounded by a thick brick wall. Inside the wall were gardens with a great variety of tropical flowers growing in profusion.

The house had been vacated a short time ago by its previous occupants and was now being remodeled and freshly painted for the new owners. The servants were arranging the furniture around and generally tidying the place, anxious to please their new masters.

The family walked through the house from one room to another. Mrs. Cartright closely scrutinizing 15

the rooms and furniture showing quite plainly she was rather pleased with the whole setup. At last, she remarked to the head servant who was showing them around and who could speak English quite well: "The house is rather nicely furnished for our purpose, but tell me, who are the people living in the immediate neighborhood?" The head steward very politely replied: "Madam this part of Singapore is the most exclusive; only Government Officials, Europeans and 

Americans live here. The Governor General's palace is only a short distance away." "Excellent," she replied.

The following several weeks were devoted to various duties. Mr. Cartright plunged into his office work familiarizing himself with the office personnel and with every agency in his territory, expenditure, merchandise in stock and other details pertaining to the companys business. Being an efficiency expert and a good promoter he would do his best to improve the business much better than his predecessor. 

Mrs. Cartright was also busy arranging a few minor details around the house, making arrangements for the children's school, going to the market and acquainting herself with the servants and other problems so necessary in her new and strange environment. 

A year passed; everything was working out wonderfully. Business doubled due to the fact that a great deal of money was being spent for war material at 16

that time. Cartright, with an expression of satisfaction, remarked to his wife one day: "Well, my dear,

I told you this move we made would be the best move and the greatest opportunity of our lives. Five years of this prosperity and we will not have to worry about anything for the rest of our life. In the mean time things are bound to change in the United States, then, if we like, we can go back."

The Cartrights were opposed to the administration at Washington at that time.

The children, too, liked their new home and their newly acquired young friends. In the exclusive schools they were attending they met many boys and girls whose families belonged in the upper strata of society. 

Jane Cartright, the beautiful seventeen year old daughter met a young man of about twenty years of age at one of the weekly social functions. The boy began paying a great deal of attention to Jane; she also seemed to like the boy immensely and with her mother's approval their friendship grew into true love. Mrs. Cartright secretly wished that some day in the near future her daughter would marry him.

The young man, Victor Stanley, was the son of a titled English Gentleman who was attached to the 

Governor's General Staff. 

In the months that followed business was growing. 

Steamers were arriving loaded with merchandise for distribution to all parts of the South Pacific and de- 17

parting for the United States also heavily loaded with war material. Then one Sunday in December, Nineteen hundred and forty-one the wires flashed, "Pearl 

Harbor being bombed. Japan attacked the United States."

The whole world was stunned. If the Japanese attacked the United States so treacherously they would be guilty of amazing dishonesty and would appropriate to themselves the solace of future oblivion. The 

Japanese Empire will be remembered in terms of anathema for generations to come. More especially at that very moment their representatives or (Judases) Nomura and Kurusu had traveled clear across the Pacific to Washington pretending to settle matters by peaceful means and at the same time had given the United States the double cross. Such a nation must be inhabited by unprincipled tricksters and must be weak and unworthy, and could not sustain an honorable reputation among the family of nations. 

Incredible but true nevertheless. The United States declared war on Japan. More wires, more bad news. 

The Philippines, Midway and Wake Islands were also attacked. All the people of the South Pacific were thrown into confusion, and business was paralyzed over night.

While great excitement existed many still thought the South Pacific would be secure from the ravages of war, concluding that the enemy cannot reach 18

Singapore very easily pointing out to so many formidable fortifications and jungle barriers. Besides the 

American and Dutch navies will soon deal with the enemy quite effectively. But the American navy or what was left of it after the treacherous attack of Pearl Harbor for the time being was unable to stop the Japs, more over many American Battleships were too busy in the Atlantic Ocean convoying war material to Great Britain. So it seemed the enemy had their way at least for the time being.

Manila was now being bombed from the air. As the bad news continued to flash over the wires the people of Singapore became more and more depressed. 

Many were asking, "What happened back home? 

Where are reenforcements?”

As time went on everyone viewed the situation with horror. It couldn't be that the enemy have their own way so completely? But they still were clinging to the hope that Singapore was several thousand miles away and an invasion by land would be impossible. 

Coming like a bombshell, the war stunned the American nation. The struggle started with swift explosive violence, catching the nation off-guard. The 

Japanese treachery and duplicity won for them the first round. Fortunately, America is capable of absorbing such blows without disaster. We might lose the opening battles but win the closing ones that count most. No single enemy or a combination of 1 19

them thriving on perfidity, tyranny and engaged in robbery and murder determined to suppress the liberties of mankind, can conquer the spirit of the United States once it is aroused. Treacherous assault breeds a spirit of vengeance tenfold more deadly than far greater damage in fair fight could do.

While the United States was trying to recover from the treacherous sudden blows, the enemy was swiftly executing its well planned invasion toward the South Pacific, and with unequal intensity and imbued with the lust of conquest occupied in quick succession and in a manner hitherto unheard of, many important and strategic parts in that area. With everything in their favor the gangster criminals, Tokyo's assassins, after lulling their unsuspecting friend for a quick and permanent backstabbing death-blow, hoping to leave their victim either dead or permanently crippled, went on their long, clever and carefully planned secret assignment.

As the weeks went on the enemy penetrated the jungle and were now pounding the surrounding fortifications of the city of Singapore. As the sound of the guns became less and less distinct by the hour everyone knew then that the outer battle lines were crumbling and the city was doomed. 

Finally, in February Nineteen hundred and fortytwo, the obsolete fortification of the city could no longer withstand the enemy's deadly assaults. Singa- 20

pore fell. The days that followed the populationespecialy the English-speaking people-went through ordeals never before experienced by any subjugated people. The enemy seized everything. No one was allowed to carry even a pocket knife. They ordered the people to leave their homes, men, women and children were segregated in separate concentration camps.

Coming back to the Cartright family we find John 

Cartright, the once suave business tycoon, enclosed in the terrible squalor of a concentration camp separated from his family, sharing the same fate with the rest of the subjugated, suffering humanity. His wealth and power, his American citizens' rights could not help him now when he protested. He was brutally informed that he was a defeated American, when he inquired about his family. The little yellow men with a hyena-like laugh replied with a bayonet point to his posterior, and to make matters worse he was compelled to salute both the flag of the rising sun and every Japanese soldier that came before him. 

Many of his associates were removed to better quarters, but Cartright, not being a captured government official or any other nation's representative, was not entitled to the usual diplomatic courtesy accorded them. There he was, a pitiful figure, surrounded by thousands of human derelicts of a polyglot variety, 21

poverty-stricken, disease-ridden people, whose morale had sunk to its lowest ebb.

Yes, John Cartright had a great many things to ponder over now and had plenty of time to do it. The irony of it he mused: "We refused to salute the stars and stripes and here we are forced to salute the Japanese flag." With tears in his red eyes he went on, "Even if I should live through this I shall never find solace in future oblivion for the rest of my life. I would give anything in the world if I could only see the stars and stripes flying in the breeze for just one minute. My brazen bravado has been so much veneer.

I want to shout from the house-tops and tell the world that one of the rottenest creatures on earth is me.

And to think it took a national catastrophe to teach me this lesson. Oh, God, the hammering voice of my conscience is unbearable; the very thought of my touchiness back home concerning our national emblem galls me. I wish I had the opportunity to tell my people to fight for their country while they can and quit living in their favored illusions, and in the belief that some one else will do the fighting for us, or we are too big, too rich or surrounded by vast oceans guaranteeing our future immunity, especially against barbaric fanatics who seem to be instinctively in love with death. "Many of us have been wrapped up in a pride and calm assumption of superiority and for the love of 22

money and lack of common sense would allow ourselves to vary our principles at will. I deserve my predicament," he continued. "I was a fool to listen to those pseudo-Americans, those bogus patriots who used the name American citizen simply as a cloak, because it pays to use it." Thus lecturing himself with such fiery Phillippics, John Cartright fell in a semicoma on the floor exhausted.

But Cartright could not even find peace in his fitful sleep. He would wake up from his deliriousness with a start as if his past was haunting him. He would sit up in the cold, dark, filthy room and while cold chills ran up his spine he was trying to calm himself and collect his thoughts about something he thought he dreamed. "Oh yes," he mumbled this was no dream, but the true story I had read years ago."

His confused mind was now unfolding another drama similar to his. He vaguely remembered the role of the story that was enacted by another man during or after the Civil War. This other man at that time also refused to salute the American flag. Puzzled by his action the authorities consulted the President of the United States. "It is quite simple," President Lincoln replied. "Put that gentleman on a steamer that sails the seven seas and do not allow him to disembark or set foot at any port or any land anywhere in the world."

He did not recall his name, but this person was put 23

on a tramp steamer, and as the story goes he was kept on the boat sailing around the globe for several years, and all these years he never touched land anywhere, worse still, no one on the boat would speak to him, not even the steward who served him his meals in his room. When he asked any questions the boy would walk away without answering him. And when he was an old man and broken-hearted he asked to see the captain and pleaded with him as a last request if the captain would be kind enough to allow him to kiss the

American flag. The captain sensing the end near complied with his wish. Shortly afterwards the man died at sea ending his miserable and lonely life of so many years.

With this alarming thought crashing through his mind plus the fear of barbarities his wife and daughters might be subjected to in the hands of the cruel and savage enemy, was driving Cartright almost insane. His agonized conscience, his mental and physical pain was so excruciating it was too much for any man to bear. In his struggle he would pray God to spare him from further torture. 

To make matters worse every time he fell asleep he visioned Uncle Sam standing before him big as life holding the American flag in one hand and pointing the finger of scorn with the other and in an angry mood saying, "So you thought your only function in this world was to make money. Try and buy your lib- 24

erty now. Your only excuse for living and dying should be your loyalty to your country. As you well know now, if our country is lost everthing is lost. If the rest of the One hundred and thirty million Americans were like you where would the United States be?" 

Cartright would wake up screaming and grabbing through the air for the flag. When the vision disappeared he would suddenly collapse on the floor in painful agony. A few days later he went violently insane. 

His contorted body was carried from the filthy concentration camp and a few hours later he died, a writhing and painful death away from his family and from his country, all alone in oblivion. 25

CHAPTER II 

MRS. CARTRIGHT, the mother, the once proud and socially prominent matron, also found herself in the same precarious situation as her husband. Imagine her sitting all alone in a terrible concentration camp among many poverty-stricken, disease-ridden, suffering human beings, picturing all these things happening, she was panic-stricken concerning her own daughters. The very thought of them being unprotected among savage beasts terrified her beyond human endurance. Every time she tried to complain or frantically inquire about them they replied with the same brutal treatment given to everybody else in the camp. 

Unable to obtain any information concerning her family and without food and sleep for three days, she lapsed into hysteria and in a mumbling voice she was continuously calling the names of her husband and children.

As the days crept on no one would give her any comfort, no one could, for everybody else in the concentration camp was in this same predicament. She became more sick, more hysterical, a pathetic figure helplessly prostrated on the floor mumbling day and night the names of her loved ones. No doctor, no hospital, not even a minister as death was approaching slowly, like a creeping shadow in her last stages of 26

life. As she was sinking gradually away she was begging for a minister to administer spiritual comfort. 

Being a good Christian she was horrified with the thought of dying without receiving the last sacrament. She firmly believed it would purify her soul and give her strength for the death-struggle. Religion of course does good, not only thereafter, but in this world also; indeed the presence of religious faith is a wonderful power. Many a poor, worn-out suffering sick, despondent and broken-hearted wretch, calmed in body and mind by the cheering influence of religion and greatly aided by it becomes willing and even anxious for the hour of departure. 

Her pleading and begging, however, was to no avail, it only added more confusion to an already existing pitiful ordeal. Two days later the death struggle came somewhat painlessly to the unconscious Mrs. 

Cartright. The Great Eternity overshadowed all temporal things and when Angel Gabriel delivered the final summons she died in servility and obscurity. 

The two unusually beautiful daughters, too, were taken captives by the conquerors, Jane, the oldest, was taken ironically enough to her own home where she had lived so happily with her family. First she thought they were taking her there to meet her people, but as they ordered her around as if she were a slave her hopes vanished into fear, and soon after she discovered to her astonishment that on the surface she 27

was supposed to be the maid, but actually the mistress to satisfy the whims and pleasures of the bespectacled and gaudy-looking Japanese General, the present occupant of what used to be her own home.

At first she resented his intentions bitterly, but the second day as he tried to force his brutal and barbaric desires upon her again, she ran to the kitchen and grabbing a butcher knife she avenged her honor by stabbing him through the heart, killing him instantly.

The next day she was executed forthwith. Thus closing the third tragical capter of the Cartright family.

In the preceding pages you will remember we spoke of Jane's fiance, Victor Stanley, who was so desperately in love with Jane. This young Englishman was also held captive in a concentration camp. 

Besides being worried about his own family he was also brooding about Jane, his sweetheart, whom he intended to marry before this terrible thing happened.

Completely unaware of Jane's fate he was contemplating day and night ways to make his escape from the camp and rescue her so he could be with her and share whatever happen together. He was so worried he scarcely could sit still. Each day and night he would go through frightful agony, he would get sick all over and some times suddenly start crying and break out in cold sweat. Other times his mind was reeling and he thought if he didn't soon see Jane he would go insane. He would awake in terror from his fitful 28

sleep in the middle of the night screaming. After a while when he would calm down for a time and in the dark, quietly and in a stupor would try to think; but his mind dwelt upon the same thing over and over again. He decided he was going to do something to help Jane if it meant his death, life in slavery and without Jane was not worth living anyway.

Every morning many prisoners were herded out from the concentration camp in labor gangs to work in various locations constructing airfields, building roads, digging trenches, etc. From these people and through the grapevine route Victor learned that some one saw Jane at home several days before. This bit of information gave Victor new hope. Of course he could not understand why the Cartright family were not molested by the conquerors while everyone else was treated like animals. He decided to go out with the work gang every day and perhaps he could manage to escape and make his way to Jane's home. Every morning after that he would place himself in front of the others so that he would be sure to be sent out. 

At six o'clock every morning five guards counted one hundred prisoners and marched them out to work. 

Victor Stanley was among them.

As it is with all prisoners, always plotting their strategy of escape, so Victor Stanley was trying to plan his escape toward Thailand. Should he take a route through the dark deadly Malayan jungle? Well 29

it did not matter, anything would be better than this barbaric slavery, besides he wanted to see Jane, and was wholly unaware, of course, that Jane met her cruel fate a short time before.

Escape seemed comparatively easy. The Japanese guards were not so watchful of their prisoners. If anybody tried to escape he would certainly be captured and put to death immediately it would be suicide to try. Unmindful of the consequence, escape being the uppermost thought of his mind, Victor Stanley noticed the guards were away, under shelter out of the rain. He thought this was an excellent opportunity to make his get away. The ghastly ordeal, the loneliness of his heart at the prospect of seeing Jane seemed palpable. And perhaps, learn something about his own family.

About four P. M. of this rainy day, while there were no guards near he made his way to the nearby underbrush. He remained there quiet and flat on his stomach for about ten minutes, constantly watching to see if any one saw him or any of the guards came around looking for him, but nothing happened. 

Shortly after he moved slowly a little further and into the thicker brush behind tall trees and stayed there till dark. After dark he quietly kept going toward Jane's home. Being familiar with the district he worked his way around, avoiding meeting any- 30

body that might lead to his probable recapture and his doom.

After several cautious and tedious hours of walking in the dark, feeling miserable and wet, he managed to reach Jane's home. Approaching the house, he noticed lights through the windows upstairs. As he was nearing the front gate he saw a Japanese soldier on duty guarding the gate. Making his way to the rear of the walled house, quietly in the dark, he scaled the wall, jumping into the yard inside. Meanwhile other Japanese soldiers guarding the grounds noticed him creeping towards the house. They waited until he came nearer and then bounced upon him from all sides. Despite his protests they gave him a terrible beating. Being weak from hunger and exhausted from so many sleepless nights he was an easy prey for them. After rendering him almost unconscious they tied his hands and feet throwing him helplessly on the floor of an outer building for the night. 

The following morning when the new general was informed about the prowling captive, he ordered him brought before him for questioning. LAST BUT NOT least we now have George, the youngest and only son of the Cartright family. George happened to be in the downtown section of the city of Singapore when the city was shaken to its foundation. 

It was certainly terrifying for a young boy to be in the midst of such bombarding violence that seemed to him like a gigantic death agony of a great city. He thought the world had come to an end. 

Bewildered and panic stricken George ran towards the harbor. The ships in the harbor seemed to be the only outlet for the city's hundreds of thousands inhabitants who were running berserk seeking safety and shelter from the death dealing explosions blasting all around them.

On his way to the docks, the only avenue of escape, he thought he might meet his parents or manage to go aboard a steamer and sail out to sea and away from this ghastly inferno. With this thought in mind he pushed his way through the great human confusion down to the harbor and along side a big steamer that was loading in a hurry all kinds of heavy war material. He went up and down in all directions looking and searching for his people, but in vain. He could not locate them anywhere.

The prevailing pandemonium was horrible. The wounded were vainly calling for help, while the more fortunate were milling around and frantically running in every direction trying to escape certain death and at the same time locate their loved ones. 

George Cartright found himself in the midst of this cataclysmic volcano. The fear almost paralyzed him. He froze right on the spot of the loading platform of the steamer. Not knowing which way to turn he was blankly watching the sailor care for the wounded while the dead were left where they fell.

He noticed the boat was preparing to pull out.
