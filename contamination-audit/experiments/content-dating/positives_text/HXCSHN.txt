1. Advice. 

There are two foolish persons-the one who gives advice, and the one who does not take it. 

2. The Object of Life. 

Agassiz was asked why a man of so great ability as he was content with so small an income. 

He replied: "I have sufficient for my need. I do not want more. I suppose I could make more money, but I have not time. Life is too short for a man to get rich, and to do his duty to his fellow man too."

6. Going to College. 

There has been much discussion as to the practical value of a college education. Horace 

Greeley said, "Of all horned cattle deliver me from a college graduate." Prof. William Lyon

Phelps, of Yale, writes, "A knowledge of the Bible without a college education is more valuable than a college education without a knowledge of the Bible." President Faunce of Brown, said that half of the students in our colleges should not have gone to college at all. Students who earn their way through college are often praised. Professor Park of Andover, said of one such student, "He sacrificed the benefits of education by earning money to gain it." Rufus Choate advised a stu- 2

dent to borrow money at sixty per cent interest, rather than work for the means to pay his way. 

7. Evidences of Conversion. 

In the Moody revival meetings, Boston, a shop girl was converted. Soon afterward a purchaser at the store where she worked inquired, "Is this real English lace?" "It was, ma'am, before the Moody meetings-now it is imitation." 

8. Patriotism. 

It was Stephen Decatur who gave the toast: "Our Country! In her intercourse with foreign nations may she always be right; but our country, right or wrong." This sentiment has given rise to much heated discussion. A well-known Washington woman some years since, doing the cathedral cities of England, wore a tiny American flag, pinned to the waist of her traveling dress. Some of the English people whom she met, did not understand, or pretended not to understand, what the flag stood for. "You belong to some secret society, I perceive," said an English woman to whom she had been introduced, pointing to the diminutive starry banner. "Yes," responded the 

Washington woman, amiably, "there are seventy million of us.' 3

9. Something for Nothing.

Someone asked a noted divine what original sin was. He replied that he did not know, but he rather thought his was laziness. The lamentable effects of unemployment in England are seen in the willingness, the eagerness, of thousands to accept the meager dole, rather than to work even when work is offered. Henry Ward Beecher writes: "I received a letter from a lad, asking me to find him an easy berth. To this I replied: 'You cannot be an editor; do not try the law; do not think of the ministry; let alone all ships, shops and merchandise; abhor politics; don't practice medicine; be not a farmer, or a mechanic; neither be a soldier, nor a sailor. Don't work. Don't study. Don't think. None of these is easy, Oh, my son. You have come into a hard world. I know of only one easy place in it, and that is the grave.' 

10. Wit and Humor. 

999 

Who can tell the difference between these two? A good matter for consideration. Was the bank-teller a wit or a humorist when he was asked how he caught a cold and replied that all the drafts went through his cage? 

11. Seeing What We Look For. 

It is a habit with many people.

They find

what they expect to find. With some it is temperamental. Mr. Micawber was always looking for something to turn up. A wise woman was once calling at a bridal home, newly furnished, and attractive. As she rose to go the young housekeeper stepped to the porch with her. Seeing some dust, and wishing to excuse herself from carelessness, the bride exclaimed, "Oh, dear! how provoking servants are. I told Mary to sweep this porch, and now look at it!" The friend replied, "Grace, I am an old housekeeper. Let me give you a little advice. Never direct people's attention to defects. 

Unless you do so, they will seldom see them. Now if I had been in your place and noticed the dirt, I should have said, 'How blue the sky is!' Then my caller would look up, as I spoke, and so get safely down the steps, thinking of something beautiful." 

12. Fig Leaves. 

William M. Evarts not only led the 

American bar in his day, but excelled in flashes of wit. When President Hayes banished wines from his table Evarts said that at the White House banquets water flowed like champagne. The chef introduced some kind of confection near the mid- Idle of the course. This Evarts called the life-saying station. One evening he went to the theatre

with a friend. A prominent feature of the play was a ballet with the usual gauzy skirts. Some time near midnight, as the show gave no signs of coming to an end, he told his friend that he must go home and go to bed. "All right," said his friend. "Good night. I guess I will wait for the close." "If you wait for the clothes you won't be home till morning," was Evarts' parting shot. 

13. If I Could Live My Life Over. (cf. 259.) 

Few would wish to do so. All think that they would live differently in many respects, but it is by no means certain that they would. We just begin to know how to live when the end comes. Many people know how other people should live, especially young people. An old man, greatly disturbed by the degeneracy of the rising generation, exclaimed: "What will become of our young people?" "Oh," replied his friend, "they will probably keep right on, grow old, and exclaim, What will become of our young people?"

There are those who lament the evils of the present age. It is worldly, materialistic, frivolous, luxurious, irreligious, and sensual. John Bright was asked the question: "If you were allowed to choose out of all time a period to live in, what would be your choice?" The great English Liberal answered: "I would be born now." There is some-

thing attractive in our age, with its gigantic commerce, its swiftly changing political conditions, its daring experiments in social reform, its splendid scientific researches. Everything suggests bigness —everything except man. They say, that is, President Butler, of Columbia University, says, that in all the world there is not a great man in sight. (cf. 269.) 

14. Other Worldliness. 

A minister preached on recognizing friends in Heaven. A woman came to him after service, and asked him to preach on recognizing friends on earth. "For," said she, "I have been to this church six weeks, and not a soul has spoken to me." Some churches are proverbially frigid. Dr. Smith ministered to such a people.

One Sunday he exchanged with Dr. Jones. The following week Dr. Jones had a bad cold. Friends asked him how it happened. "I caught it from Dr. 

Smith's congregation." There are two sides to this question. There are worshipers who dread the greetings and the handshakings. The first article of their creed is that the church service is a religious, not a social, function. They wish to withdraw in silence, and meditation. They avoid even the minister shaking hands at the door, with the departing congregation. A picture of a

life boat on a rough sea. All around the water is full of drowning men and women. In the bow of the boat stands a sailor reaching out, and taking by the hand, one of the unfortunates. A small boy studies the picture, and then asks, “Is he trying to get that chap into the boat, or is he just shaking hands with him?" Rescuing sinners is more than shaking hands with them. 

15. How Long Should a Man's Legs Be? 

There can be no better answer to this question than that of Honest Abe-"They should be long enough to reach to the ground." 

16. Identity. 

It is said that a dog never says to himself, "I am a dog." Yet he knows that he is alive.

In other words, he has consciousness, but not self-consciousness. We believe that if we survive this life we shall know ourselves in another world. 

Abraham said to Dives, "Son, remember." The daily prints often report people wandering about, unable to tell who they are. It is the old story of 

Ammi Judson becoming confused, and exclaiming "Am I Ammi, or am I not Ammi, and if I am not Ammi, who am I?" It was Pythagoras who traced this sense of identity back into the timeless past, in his doctrine of Pre-existence. The

soul does not begin its life with the birth of the body in this world. It comes from the years beyond the flood. It comes trailing clouds of glory from a far off yesterday. Therefore it knows itself. It is the same self that it was. When Jonathan Edwards, absent minded, like all great thinkers, was walking in the pastures behind his house at Northampton, meditating on foreordination and free will, he met a small lad. "Good afternoon, my boy—and whose son are you?" “I am Amos Whittaker's son, sir." "That's right, glad to know you, be a good boy." Some time later, returning, he met the same lad, at about the same place. "Glad to meet you, sonny-and whose son are you?" Amos was indignant, and snapped out, "I am the same man's son that I was ten minutes ago." I often envy people, but I may be thankful that I am not anybody else. When the schoolboy learns to conjugate his first verb he learns one of the profound truths of philosophy. "I am. Thou art. He is." 

17. Tact.

Common sense is said to be rarer than genius. A man lacking ability may make his way by tactfulness. This quality is a gift, and yet it may be acquired, especially if joined with good nature. No man is better served by tact than the

college professor, or any teacher. College boys will not tolerate a bungler, they applaud adroit good humor. Tutor Beckwith of Yale, a superb instructor, knew how to handle his pupils. One day in recitation a sophomore took out a pocket mirror, as the sun was shining his way, and flashed rays of light on his neighbors. Beckwith saw what was going on, but said nothing until the light was accidentally flashed into his face. 

Then he looked up and said, "The gentleman will please confine his brilliancy to his recitations."

The tables were turned on the student, while the class roared and applauded. Incidents of that kind go a long way toward making the popular professor. Another one of the kind happened at the inauguration of Noah Porter to the presidency of Yale. The students formed a long procession, and moved up Hillhouse Avenue to the president's home, cheering and singing. One of their transparencies, which stopped directly in front of Dr. 

Porter as he came out on the piazza, bore the picture of a large mug of foaming liquor, marked "porter." The president's eye lighted on it. A martinet would have given the boys a lecture on temperance. Dr. Porter's face lighted up with a genial smile, as he pointed to the transparency, and said: "Boys, I advise you to drain that mug dry." 10

There are two elements in tact-thought and action, the intuitive discernment of the right thing to do, and the skilful execution of it at the right time and in the right way, as in the case of the electrician who repaired a mechanism by tapping with a hammer on a certain spot, and who sent in his bill: "Tapping with hammer, $1. Knowing where to tap, $49, $50." The man who knows where to tap may outdistance the more gifted man who taps the wrong spot. 

18. Beating the Devil. (cf. 24.) 

Is there any sin in being tempted? Undoubtedly if our natures were altogether pure they would not feel the appeal of evil. A certain good man said a wise thing: "I cannot help it if the Devil comes up to my door. I cannot help it if he lifts the latch, and walks in. I can help it if I offer him a chair."

Brown University. "Very well," replied President

Senator Sumner, of Massachusetts, was a very able man. He admitted it. President Grant, simple 11

The Bible societies report sales of King James

20. One at a Time. 

Edward Everett Hale said that some people bear three kinds of trouble at once-yesterday's, today's, and tomorrow's : all they have had, all they have and all they expect to have. There are some who bear the world's burden, when all they ought to carry is their share of it. A child, seeing a picture of Atlas stooping under the load of the world on his shoulders, exclaimed, "Mamma, why don't he put it down?" 

21. Red Tape.

The reverence of this age for machinery is monumental. 12

Every time a matter of public interest comes before Congress a committee must be appointed to "study the question." 

Spurgeon said that the best committee is a committee of three, one of whom is sick abed, and one out of town. 

John Sherman cut the knot with his famous epigram, "The way to resume is to resume." 

The reluctance of men to settle a question directly, without palaver, is illustrated by the incident of John and Mary, just married, going out to buy a clock. The first timepiece they saw suited them exactly. "All right," said John, "We'll take this one." "But John," remonstrated Mary, "I just love this one, but—but we haven't done any shopping." "The laws' delays" prompted Hamlet to suicide. Chief Justice Taft has recently dilated on this theme. See Jarndyce vs. Jarndyce.

It is the same in medicine. "The regular practitioner would rather that a patient should die according to rule than be cured by a quack." Garibaldi was once on a vessel when a man was lost overboard. Great and hurried preparations were made to rescue him. While officers and crew were getting the life-saving tackle ready Garibaldi leaped into the sea and brought the man back to the ship. 13

The church has undying faith in machinery, organizations, societies, committees, et cetera. A missionary society sent a gasoline engine to Africa. Soon after it arrived the natives were seen bowing down and worshiping it. Machine idolatry. And someone said, "Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men." 

22. Self-respect. 

One of the best things that President Garfield said: "I do not much care what others think and say about me, but there is one man's opinion about me which I very much value-that is the opinion of James Garfield. Others I need not think about. I can get away from them but I have to be with him all the time. He is with me when 

I rise up and when I lie down, when I eat and talk, when I go out and come in. It makes a great difference whether he thinks well of me or not."

24. Satan's Occupation. (cf. 18.)

Two men laid a wager. It had to do with a neighbor who never spoke ill of anyone, however bad. One man bet that he could make this neighbor admit that a certain person was wicked. 

They went to the soft-spoken man and said to him, "You certainly can't say anything good of the Devil." "Well," was the reply, "we all know that he is very industrious." (cf. 236.) Some think him very interesting also, and the opinion of the critic is that the real hero of Paradise Lost is Satan.

Another fact is that he is often found in good company. The old legend has it that some angels set out one Sunday to find evil spirits. They went to the slums of the city and found few. They flew to the homes of rich and poor, visited streets and alleys, everywhere to little purpose. So they gave it up, and decided, before going back to heaven to join the people at worship in church. 

There they found the imps in great numbers, hovering about the pious, most numerous around 15

the pulpit. "The sons of God came to present themselves before the Lord, and Satan came also."-Job 2:1. 

Dr. Talmage says: "It is foolish to think that

Satan wastes his time on the poorest spiritual natures—he seeks the richest prizes. A pirate goes out on the sea, and one bright morning he puts the glass to his eye, and looks off, and sees an empty vessel floating about. He says, 'Never mind-that's no prize for us.' But the same morning he looks through the glass, and sights a vessel from the Indies, laden with spices and gold, and shouts, "That's our prize, bear down on it." " 

25. Procrastination. 

The emperor of Brazil said: "The difference between the people of the United States and our people is that they say 'Today'—we say 'Manana.' 999 Procrastination is the thief of something as important as time-the habit of using time, the power to use it. 

26. What the Raven Said. 

It is customary in England to ask tourists to write their names in a visitors' book on leaving, also a sentiment if they are so disposed. Two women stopped at a wayside inn, where the fare 16

and the service were so poor that they decided that that visit should be their last. On departing they wrote their names, and underneath, "Quoth the raven."

The innkeeper is still puzzled as to their meaning. 

27. Presidential Eloquence. 

Few, if any, of our Presidents may be listed in the first rank of orators. Compared with the great prime ministers of England, say Chatham, Pitt, Fox, Gladstone, they are pale. Some of them are remembered for striking expressions, as Washington for "entangling alliances," Jackson for "to the victors belong the spoils," (first uttered by Senator William L. Marcy in a speech in the Senate), "it is His way," McKinley assassinated, "the strenuous life," Roosevelt, "Safe for democracy," Wilson, "I do not choose," Coolidge.

None of these has been more noted than Cleveland's "innocuous desuetude." Cleveland, like all positive, aggressive men aroused both enthusiastic devotion, and bitter opposition. As a speaker he was heavy, and labored, often didactic. It was said that he compiled his speeches from books. At one time he hesitated, and stumbled, in his address, when a listener shouted, "Never mind, Grover, we will look it up in the Encyclopedia." 17

28. Transfigured Faults. 

There was an unsightly rock in the land.

They were making a park. The landscape gardener, instead of tearing out the stone, made a bed of flowers around it, and trained over it an ivy vine.

Goldsmith's village preacher was so good that "Even his failings leaned to Virtue's side." 

A fault may be the basis of a virtue. Anger may be a vice-directed against evil it becomes moral indignation. A good hater may hate his neighbor, or his neighbor's sin. The same element of character may become gold in one man, dross in another. "What is obstinacy in a mule is firmness in a king."

A woman showed Mr. Ruskin a beautiful and costly handkerchief on which a careless hand had dropped some ink. She said that it was of no more use. Ruskin put it in his pocket and carried it away. In a few days he brought it back to her, having on it a beautiful picture in India ink, with the blot as its basis. Blots may become adornments. 

29. City Pride.

Chicago and St. Louis can hardly be considered rivals as to size, but there is a friendly emulation between the two. Chicago plumes itself 18

40. Theodore Dwight Wolsey. 

President of Yale-a learned authority on international law, but withal a very humble man, illustrating Ignatius Loyola's motto, "Humbly to conceal humility, and to shun the praise of being humble." He had a keen sense of humor.

When Dr. McCosh, of Princeton, was expected to visit at his home he requested Mrs. Wolsey not to have squash for dinner, explaining that if he were to say, "Dr. McCosh, will you have some squash?" he would collapse. Yale men will not soon forget his memorable sermon, "The need of the spirit of meditation in this age.' 99 During his presidency the chair of Hebrew was held by Professor Day who had a study in Divinity Hall where he prepared his lectures. One morning Day came down from his home to his study, unlocked the door, left the key in the lock, and entered the room. A theological student, who, like all of us, "could resist everything except temptation," saw his opportunity, softly turned the key, and tiptoed away. The clock struck eleven, the Professor rose from his desk, and, gathering up his papers, essayed to go out. To his astonishment he could not open the door, then 24

remembered the reason why, and pounded upon the door until the janitor came and let him out. 

His class that day were surprised at his perturbation, as he was usually a calm and quiet man. That afternoon he sought the President's office, and indignantly told his tale. Wolsey heard him through, restraining laughter with difficulty. At the end the President asked, "Am I to understand that you went into your room, and left the key on the outside?" "Yes, I usually take it in, but this time I forgot." "And do you mean to say that a passing student locked you in?" "He surely did." "Well," said the President, "I should think he would." That was all the satisfaction the Professor ever received.

63. A Comforting Way to Look at Crime. 

Since the World War civilization seems to have degenerated. Evil deeds have increased, evil men have multiplied. We may look at it from 

Macaulay's point of view. "That there are ten thousand thieves in London is a melancholy fact. But looked at in one point of view it is reason for exultation. For what other city could maintain ten thousand thieves?

What must be the mass of wealth, when the fragments, gained by lawless pilfering, rise to so large an amount? St. Kilda would not support a single pickpocket. The quantity of theft is, to a certain extent, an index of the quantity of useful industry, and judicious speculation."

Also the amount of vice suggests the far greater amount of virtue. For every criminal there are hundreds of good citizens. How strong must be our civilization when it can carry without faltering such a load of iniquity.

71. Charities.

72. Punctuation.

In social correspondence few writers pay much attention to punctuation. They are like the author who wrote a book with no marks in the L text, and at the end filled several pages with commas, semicolons, colons, periods, interroga- 41

tion and interjection marks, directing the reader to distribute them to suit himself.

Punctuation is important. Often it seriously affects the sense.

In the days of our fathers it was a custom in the church for worshipers to send notes to the pulpit requesting prayers for friends, etc. One

Sunday, at a church in a coast town in Maine, the wife of a sea captain, who was about to set sail, sent up such a request. "A man going to sea, his wife asks the prayers of the congregation for his safe return." The minister ignored the comma, and the congregation was startled on hearing, “A man, going to see his wife, asks the prayers of the congregation for his safe return." 

An excellent practice at school is declamation. 

Perhaps there would never have been a Patrick

Henry, a Webster, but for this drill. In a little 

Red Schoolhouse a lad was declaiming Horatius at the Bridge. At the end was the customary period, then the name of the author. The declaimer, not understanding, fervently brought Horatius to the climax: "For Romans, in Rome's quarrels, Spared neither land, nor gold,

Nor son, nor wife, nor limb, nor life, In the brave days of old Macaulay!" 42

Many a statement seems false, till rightly punctuated. As for instance: "Every lady in this land 

Hath twenty nails upon each hand; 

Five and twenty on hands and feet, And this true, without deceit."

Puns are the lowest form of literature. The worse a pun is, the better it is.

A merchant, wishing his agent to send some coal from a distant city, wrote a semicolon upon a sheet of paper, and mailed it. The agent understood, and read, "See my coal on." He at once shipped the coal, wrote a colon upon a sheet of paper, and mailed it to the merchant. 

How would you punctuate the sentence, "I saw a $5 bill on the sidewalk"? Make a dash after the $5 bill.

89. Extempore Preaching. 

A hundred years ago ministers read their sermons from manuscript. If a parson ventured upon impromptu speech he was considered super- 51

ficial. Dr. Nathanael Emmons, of Franklin, 

Mass., the Nestor of Congregational preachers, announced ex cathedra that the prerequisites of extempore preaching were "ignorance, impudence, and presumption." The Wesleys and

A peculiar experience of Francis W. Perkins of 

Southport, Me., is suggestive.

He went directly to his pastorate from the divinity school, and each week carefully prepared two written discourses for Sunday morning and afternoon. He did not dare to try the extempore. 

One Sunday afternoon, after he had been there a short time, he entered the pulpit, conducted the opening exercises, laid his manuscript on the open Bible before him, and was about to begin. 

To his surprise and consternation he discovered that he had brought the morning sermon, which he had already preached to the waiting congregation, having forgotten to change manuscripts. 

He hesitated, looked at the people, and decided to make a clean breast of his dilemma. "I have 52

made a mistake, and brought the sermon which you have already heard this morning. I must do one of three things-I must preach this sermon again, which you would not like to have me do. 

Or I must go home and get the other sermon, but that would be unpleasant to us all. Or I must preach all I can remember of the afternoon discourse." Nobody said anything, and, after a pause, he decided on the third way. Fortunately he remembered the theme and the text. Accoutered as he was he plunged in, and to his surprise found it exhilarating. The ideas of the sermon came back to him, also new thoughts. The people were aroused. No hearer slept in church that afternoon, and at the close of the services, his parishioners flocked around him, and told him that it was the best sermon he had ever preached. 

That was Perkins's conversion to the extempore, and he had a long pastorate.

105. Does Anything Exist?

A matter of dispute through the ages. 

The ancient philosophers discussed the question thoroughly. The pundits of India have demonstrated that only God is. The Germans are at odds as to the ding an sich. Christian Scientists multiply rapidly on the doctrine of Unreality.

127. The Soft Answer. 

In early times there was a certain college president who was famous for his iron hand in a velvet glove. His way was pleasant, and he usually had it. When students called on him and stayed too long, perhaps from shyness not knowing how to get away, he helped them out of their embarrassment by genially inquiring, "Well, young gentlemen, will you go now, or will you sit longer?" They were never in doubt as to which alternative to choose.

The college was in the country, and its head cultivated an acre or two of land, kept a horse, cow and a hennery. At one time his chickens began to disappear. Somebody was robbing his roost, not regularly, but at intervals. He suspected some colored people who lived in the village, and decided to watch for them. After dark he secreted himself behind some barrels in a corner of the coop, and awaited developments. It was a moonlight night so that he had a fair view of his surroundings. He had not long to wait. Stealthy footsteps approached, and low voices were heard.

He peered out cautiously, and what by the aid of the moon, the incautious conversation of the intruders, and their clothing, he discovered, to his amazement, that they were two students, members of his senior class. One of them carefully 70

took a fat rooster from his perch, and passed him to his companion, saying, "Here is Mr. Prexy."

He then reached for a plump hen, passed her on with, "Here is Mrs. Prexy." They departed, and 

Mr. Prexy came forth, shut the hen-house door, and went to bed. For some strange reason there was a broad smile on his classical countenance. 

It was the custom for the head of the college to invite members of the senior class to dinner once during their last year, five or six at a time, until all had been entertained. After a social hour in the parlor, they adjourned to the dining-room, and were seated at one long table. Grace was said, the door from the kitchen opened, and the maid came in, bearing a large roasted fowl upon a platter, which she set down in front of the president. She went back into the kitchen, and returned with a second fowl, which she deposited beside its mate. The master deliberately sharpened the knife, deftly sliced portions of both birds, and turning to one of the culprits pleasantly asked, "Mr. Jones, would you like a leg of

160. Lawyers and Clients. (cf. 280.)

A delicate question of casuistry arises in a lawyer's practice. Is it right for him to advocate a cause which he suspects, or knows to be wrong? 

May he, for instance defend a man accused of theft if he believes him to be guilty? It was said of a certain Bay State lawyer that he would prove a thief innocent even if the money were found in his boots. What shall be said of an advocate who puts on a sham sympathy for a culprit, stirs the emotions of the jury falsely, and tries to win his case by cajoling, and deception?

The doctrine of the legal profession is that every man, and every cause, has a right to the benefit of the law of the land. Every accused person has the right of being defended according to the laws, and of having all said that can justly be said on his behalf. No matter what his seeming guilt may be he must be tried in accordance with the strict laws of evidence. 

Some authorities hold that a barrister should not think or know anything as to right or wrong of his client's case, only what can be said legitimately, legally for him. The theory is that upon the whole, in the long run, more guilt will be punished, and more innocence saved, by the efforts of counsel pulling in opposite directions, keeping themselves within the rules of legal evi- 88

dence, and rigorous argumentation, than in any other way. For it must be borne in mind that no one on earth knows certainly who is innocent, and who is guilty. Even the confession of guilt does not always prove guilt.

Whatever may be the merits and defects of the jury system, the legal fraternity do not favor trial by newspapers, nor by popular clamor, nor do they regard with approbation a popular custom of showing sympathy with murderers by saying it with flowers.

Two shibboleths have been accredited in the courts of Christendom: 

"Better that six guilty should escape, than that one innocent should be adjudged guilty." "Slow justice is preferable to swift injustice."

175. Dr. Cuyler's Parishioner. 

Dr. Theodore L. Cuyler, of Brooklyn, was one of the popular preachers of his day. In the height of his success it was difficult to get a sitting in his church, and there was usually a waiting list. 

A newcomer to the city applied for a pew to accommodate himself and family. He was told that he should have one when his turn came. 

He waited some weeks and called on Dr.

For some Sundays he and his family were regular attendants at church service. Then they began to fail. One and another were absent, and sometimes the whole family.

The good doctor was puzzled. He was no less faithful as a pastor than as a preacher, and soon made a pastoral call at the home of his new parishioner.

Skilfully paving his approach to the delicate subject, he pleasantly expressed surprise that the family, which had been so eager to worship at his 96

church, should so soon seem to tire of the services. 

Not at all embarrassed the father replied: "We don't wonder that you are puzzled, Doctor. It does seem strange, we admit, but the situation is this. We sit in the back pew. The whole congregation is in front of us. You preach the best sermons that are preached in Brooklyn, and as they come down toward us the great congregation eagerly appropriates them, each one taking a part, until by the time they should get to our pew there is nothing left for us."

Dr. Cuyler laughed heartily, and did not press his investigation further. He was wont to relate this incident with much amusement, but he never knew why the family did not come to church.

181. Apocryphal?

The administration of President Kirk land at Harvard extended from 1810 to 1828. 

By his wise and capable management the college was developed into a university. The students numbered about three hundred in his day, mostly from the New England states. 

Discipline was paternal, the relations between pupils and professors being friendly and intimate. 

About 1825 there were rumors of excessive drinking among the young men, which disturbed the faculty, and the president decided to investigate.

The reports concerned especially a certain road house, a mile or more from the college, where, so it was said, the under classmen spent too much time and money.

Choosing an hour when students would be busy with studies and recitations Dr. Kirkland walked out to the tavern, and found nobody there but the barkeeper, who looked much surprised and somewhat alarmed when he saw his unusual caller. 100

"I understand that you dispense a mild tipple called flip in this place." "Yes, sir-we has flip." "Will you give me some of it?" "Certainly, sir-my treat."

The man mixed the drink, and passed it over the bar to the president.

He looked at it curiously, smelled of it curiously, put the mug to his lips, and took a small tentative sip. 

His face beamed with pleasure. "Is it intoxicating?" "Not unless you drink too much of it, sir.”

The doctor made a second trial, larger than the first, and smacked his lips. "Is this a favorite drink with the students ?" "Well, sir, they takes more of it than anything else." "They must like it then," reflected the doctor, looking thoughtfully into the mug. 209. Experts.

The value of expert testimony is denied by some learned judges, while others, equally learned, vigorously defend it. Down this inclined plane the victim slides, still blindfolded, and lands in a large tub of water, his face splashed by sponges, in the hands of attentive sophs.

Various other pleasantries follow, and the climax comes when the freshman is bundled into a large box, and the cover shut down. To this box at the four corners is attached a stout rope which passes up through a pulley fastened to the ceiling, the end of the rope dangling down to the floor. "Yank him up," cries the master of ceremonies, and up goes box, freshie and all. It seems a hundred miles up, and the inmate of the box wonders what next. The bottom of the box is on hinges, with a ratchet catch on the opposite side, controlled by a dangling cord, so that when the cord is pulled the ratchet slips, the bottom of the box falls, and by Newton's law of gravitation down goes whatever or whoever may be in the box. calls the captain. That descent seems two hundred miles to the descender. He thinks of all the sins he ever committed. He sees in a vision the man with the broken leg. 225. Are Large Families Desirable? 

There lives today (1929) in the hills of Maryland a man who has thirty-three children, one B. C. Tranum, 112 years old, reads without glasses, enjoys comfortable health. If Mr. 

Tranum had lived in Old Testament times, when a numerous progeny was taken to be a mark of divine favor, his name would have come down in holy writ along with Job, David, Solomon, and the rest.

The ancient theory that a rapid increase of population is a blessing, long accepted by statesmen and the general public as an axiom, is now challenged not only by political economists, but by churchmen, biologists, and students of eugenics. It is vigorously maintained that the highest interest of society is not to stimulate but to restrain increase of population, to diminish the number of marriages and children.

If the movement toward world peace continues and strengthens one motive for producing large 122

families will be weakened, perhaps removed, because war has called for large armies, and the fathers and mothers have been urged to show their patriotism by providing many sons to fill the ranks.

Has the high cost of living deterred many from entering the married state? Long ago Thackeray wrote, "If people only made prudent marriages what a stop to population there would be!" Many avenues to labor have been opened to woman of late, which give her the opportunity of self support, and make her independent of male protection and care. This enlargement of the spheres of female industry has already affected domestic life, and the career of woman, in a measure, is transferred from the home to the place of business. Along with the revolution which is taking place in the relations of the sexes, to say nothing of the demoralization which accompanies a luxurious civilization, these elements of change cause the moralist and the philanthropist to pause and ponder. 

And we must not overlook Malthus and his teachings, Thomas Robert Malthus, English economist of a century or so ago, who gave to the world an essay on the Principle of Population as it Affects the Future Improvement of Society, the main thesis of which was that population out- 123

runs subsistence. There are too many mouths to feed. Means of subsistence increases in arithmetical, population in geometrical, progression. This essay suggested to Darwin his Struggle for Existence, and the Survival of the Fittest. Shall we rejoice when the census man tells us that the population of the land is multiplying fast! Do the people of famine-stricken China, Russia, India, sing for joy when millions of children are born in their homes?

In his History of Philosophy Lewes dilates thus: 1 "There are men who bear poverty meekly.

231. History. 

Mr. Ford expresses the opinion that "history is bunk." He does not explain what he means, or why he takes this view of history, but 128

we may assume that he thinks that history is unreliable, that the record of the past as given in the volumes of historians, is not accurate and trustworthy.

He is not alone in this judgment. So high an authority as Sir Robert Walpole in his Walpoliana uses the expression, "Anything but history, for history must be false."

In his Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire Gibbon defines history as "little more than the register of the crimes, follies, and misfortunes of mankind," an idea which may have been in the mind of Ford, or he may have had a dim conception of Fichte's dictum that "man is not saved by the historical, but by the metaphysical.' 99 Being a practical man of affairs Mr. Ford more likely had in mind the popular verdict expressed in the saying that Froude's history is his story, Hume's history is his story, Parkman's history is his story, etc.-that is to say, every historical writer takes his own personal, particular, and possibly prejudiced view of past events and characters, has a bias toward certain opinions, and colors his narrative, and his interpretation of facts, with tints which please his fancy, does not intentionally misrepresent, but selects, perhaps unconsciously, material which favors his personal views. It would be unlikely, for example, that a 129

whig historian would present tory politics and administrations in the same light as a tory writer, or that a Roman Catholic author would write up the Reformation led by Luther in a way to please Protestants.

History is one of the most stimulating and profitable of the disciplines.

In his Essay on Studies Bacon writes: "Histories make men wise; poets, witty; the mathematics, subtle; natural philosophy, deep; moral, grave; logic and rhetoric, able to contend."

Bolingbroke quotes the ancient saying that "History is philosophy teaching by examples." 

Carlyle says that "History is the essence of innumerable biographies." 

Thackeray writes to his nephew, "Read a tremendous lot of history."

Of all the cultural studies History is first and best.

243. Conscience. 

That there is such a faculty of the human soul has been denied. Right and wrong are simply matters of custom. What is called right today may be wrong tomorrow and vice versa. 

Wordsworth calls it, "Stern daughter of the 

Voice of God."

The popular belief is that it is the recognition and acceptance of principles of conduct as binding. 

It does not tell what is right and what is wrong, but when the judgment or ethical sense has perceived certain actions to be good and others to be evil, Conscience commands that the former be chosen and the latter eschewed. Conscience says, "You ought," "You ought not," "Thou shalt," "Thou shalt not."

Conscience bestows approval or disapproval according to the obedience or disobedience of its subjects. 281. Yea and Nay Minds. (cf. 70.)

Some minds are so constituted that they fix firmly upon certain facts and truths. 

A question once decided, they have no doubts. "This rock shall fly from its firm base as soon as 

I." They may be called affirmative minds.

Tertullian said, "I believe because it is impossible." Anselm, "I believe in order that I may know." The world's great leaders have had minds of this cast.

There are minds which find it impossible to reach settled convictions. They may deplore this inability, or exult in it as a mark of superiority, but they are like the sea gulls, always on the wing, ever hovering, sweeping down as if about to alight and darting away, sometimes alighting but only for a short stay.

The English churchman who declared, "The apostle Paul says, and I partly agree with him," may have been a mind of this variety. 

Dr. Popkin, of Newburyport, awoke every morning under the impression that everything was an open question. He rather thought that two and two are four; was not sure of it. 174

Dr. Bartol, Unitarian, Boston, once ended a sermon: "Such are my sentiments today-what I shall think next Sunday heaven only knows."

Erasmus, learned scholar, a yea and nay mind, was pictured as eternally hovering between the upper and the lower world, undecided which way to go.

283. Overdoing It. (cf. 53.)

Talleyrand's last admonition to commissioners, about to go to a foreign court: "Above all things, gentlemen, not too much zeal." (cf. 170.)

He foresaw that eagerness on their part would be interpreted as a sign of weakness. Their rivals would take advantage of it. Coolness and apparent indifference are good diplomacy. 

Solomon gives us this warning: "Be not righteous over much; neither make thyself over wise.". "Be not over much wicked." (Eccles. 7:16, 17.)

The ancient Romans applied this principle to the pleasures of life: Ne quid nimis-Nothing too much. 175

If you would get the deepest satisfaction out of your enjoyments be moderate; overindulgence results in satiety, ennui.

Such was the doctrine of the Epicureans.

286. Are Automobiles a Blessing? YES. 

They have given employment to untold thousands. In preparing the material of which they are made, in manufacturing and repairing them, in transporting them to market by rail, in selling them, an army of workmen, mechanics, railroad men, salesmen and their helpers have gained a livelihood. They have called for new roads, 176

stretching all over the country, whose construction and maintenance have furnished occupation for another great army. Building garages, carrying on filling stations, putting up great houses for the storage of new cars, have also to be added to the count. Then there is the multitude of men who make a living by driving cars, chauffeurs, taxicab men, bus operators, etc. Adding up all these beneficiaries, with their families, to whom the auto means bread and butter, we easily run into six figures.

Another aspect of the commercial value of automobiles is seen in their usefulness in business. 

Transportation of merchandise has been revolutionized since the days of horses and wagons. 

Motor trucks carry loads impossible a few decades ago, and in a fraction of the time. To the innumerable caravan that moves the motor car is a welcome convenience, carrying household goods from place to place with a single shipping and unshipping. Here, too, is to be reckoned in the thousands of drivers employed by business houses, and the costs of transportation to the merchant have been materially reduced, while the delivery of store goods to the householder has become more prompt and expeditious.

Automobiles have added greatly to the pleasure and health of many. They have allured people 177

out into the open, giving them fresh air and a good time. Whole families go on pleasure drives and picnics who would never have thought of owning horses and carriages. Friends and acquaintances make excursions, and reach distant points, see new scenes, think new thoughts. The business man gets away from his office, and sees the country.

The benefit to invalids is on the credit side of the auto. They are often refreshed and revived by a drive, as good for the mind as for the body. The quickness with which a patient can be taken to a hospital in an emergency case has been known to save life, as also the promptness with which a doctor can respond to a sick call. 

That country people appreciate the auto is proved by the increasing number of cars called for by villagers and farmers. They use them for work and for pleasure. They travel about far more than formerly, go visiting oftener, take trips to the city. Here, too, appears the medical advantage. The country doctor reaches his patients quickly, and the city doctor consents to go to the country because he can get there soon. In general it may be said not only of country people, but of city people as well, that the auto has done much to relieve the monotony and toil of life, to enlarge people's horizons, to get them interested 178

in other people and places, and so to broaden the mind, and cheer the spirit. 

287. Are Automobiles a Blessing?

NO. 

Many people say that they are a curse. 

They have been bought by many who could not well afford the expense, who have mortgaged their property in order to buy them, leaving their other bills unpaid, cheating their creditors out of their just dues. The injury to business from this cause has been incalculable. The auto thus fosters selfishness, and ambition to keep up with the neighbors.

Reckless driving, and indifference to the rights of others, lying and deception as to licenses, stealing of cars, a certain lawlessness in people otherwise upright.

Church attendance has been falling off due to joy rides on the Sabbath day. The beaches and other popular resorts are crowded on Sunday by hilarious multitudes who hold high carnival on the Lord's day. Can the nation afford to secularize the Sabbath? (cf. 118.)

The deplorable increase in crime may be charged in large measure to the auto. 

It is easy to hold up pedestrians and messengers with pay rolls, easy to plunder houses by night, 179

easy to rob cash registers and shut up the owner in the cellar, easy to pillage banks at the point of the pistol, easy to enter churches and pilfer from the charity boxes, easy to practice all manner of thievery, because the automobile has made it easy, easy to the criminal in his approach to his victim, and in his getaway. The auto is the ally of the bandit, of the bootlegger, of every violator of the law of the land.

Automobiles are the ruin of many girls and young men. They afford opportunity for petting parties, and petting parties lead to something worse. Girls are taken to road houses to spend the night. Autos figure frequently in divorce cases, as they furnish opportunity for statutory offences. Fast living, the pace that kills, the wages of sin! 

Men and women neglect exercise, ride instead of walk, to the injury of their health. The auto is a temptation to the lazy man. A temptation not only to neglect exercise but to waste time, and a temptation to rush about the country with no chance to view and appreciate the beauties of Nature.

The deaths charged to the account of the auto in the United States number about 33,000 per annum, with five to ten times as many people maimed, more or less seriously. It has been well 180

said that if this loss of life, with the injuries, were caused by war, the nation would be in mourning. 

Many drivers seem to be indifferent as to how much pain and injury they inflict, while the public is becoming hardened. A majority of the victims are pedestrians, many of them children. 

Traffic rules are multiplied, and the police and the courts are busy, but the evil increases from year to year because the nation is auto mad.
