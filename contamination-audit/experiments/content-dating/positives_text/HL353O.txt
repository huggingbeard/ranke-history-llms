HIGHLIGHTS OF REPORT ON PERSONS UNDER THE SUPERVISION OF THE FEDERAL PROBATION SYSTEM FISCAL YEAR 1963 

This is the second report covering a full year's work of the Federal Probation System. Unlike the one for 1962, this report is far more comprehensive in accounting for probationers received and removed from supervision. Also, it includes persons received as parolees or mandatory releasees from Federal institutions operated by the United States Bureau of Prisons and for military parolees released by the Department of Defense. 

Part I of the report deals almost exclusively with the 88 United States district courts with a special section on the United States District Court for the District of Columbia. 

Part II deals with all of the Federal Probation System, the 88 United States district courts, and the district courts in the District of Columbia, Guam and the Virgin Islands, which provide supervision for persons released on parole or mandatory release. Such persons coming under the jurisdiction of the Federal Probation System have been released from confinement for Federal crimes and for crimes against the Uniform Code of Military Justice. 

Introductory Data 

1. During fiscal year 1963, there were 24,965 convictions in the United States district courts, exclusive of immigration violations and other violators of special acts: 

Agricultural, Antitrust, Fair Labor Standards, Food and Drug, Migratory Bird and Motor Carrier. One half (50.1 percent) of the defendants were placed on probation, 45.2 percent were sentenced to imprisonment and 4.7 percent received fines. 

(Table 1.) 

2. On June 30, 1963 there were 38,551 persons under the supervision of the Federal Probation System. This was an increase of 5.1 percent over July 1, 1962. A total of 28,240, or 73.3 percent, were on probation. The balance were on parole (18.7 percent), mandatory release (5.8 percent), deferred prosecution (1.3 percent), United States commissioner probation (0.5 percent) and military parole (0.4 percent). 

(Table 2.) 

3. The Court may place persons on probation immediately, or delay probation by giving a split sentence, a mixed sentence or probation following military service, local jail

sentence, hospitalization or similar delay. In fiscal year 

1963, the courts immediately placed 83.5 percent of the defendants on probation. Delayed probation was used for 

13.2 percent.

Another 3.3 percent were reinstated to proba tion following a violation. (Table 4.)

Women tended to have somewhat shorter terms of probation. (Tables 5 and 6.)

An examination by offense shows that males had a ratio of 30 to one for violation of the Internal Revenue liquor laws. Theft, other than postal, showed a ratio of 29 to one. Proportionately, there were twice as many women as men who had had their marriage broken by divorce, separation or by death of the spouse. 

About three out of ten placed on probation had completed high school. About 20 percent reported no schooling or attendance at an elementary school, only. (Tables 10, 11 and 12.)

PERSONS UNDER SUPERVISION OF THE FEDERAL PROBATION SYSTEM, FISCAL YEAR 1963 Introduction 

Beginning with 1963 the probation officers of the Federal System furnished to the Administrative Office substantially more information relating to Federal offenders than had been available previously. For the first time information was obtained on the individual characteristics of offenders who had received presentence investigations by the probation officers but had not subsequently been placed under supervision. Previously, such information had been reported only on those to be supervised. By receiving this data for all persons who were given presentence investigations, it was possible to analyze the sentencing practices of the Federal courts with great detail for 1963. A special report on this, entitled 

Federal Offenders in the United States District Courts, 1963, was prepared and issued in February 1964. In this it was pointed out that a subsequent analysis of persons under supervision would be prepared and issued.

A special report, similar to the 1964 one on Federal offenders, was issued in May 1963 covering data for 1962. There, it was stated that little information on probation violations was available, but that during 1963 there would be accumulated knowledge of the kinds of violations that occurred and that this would make possible an examination of them, together with an analysis of their relationship to the original offense, sentence, and personal characteristics of the offenders.

This report furnishes considerably more detail than has ever been available before concerning the kinds of persons under supervision, their status, the lengths of their supervisory periods and the reasons for termination of supervision.

In making available this expanded information acknowledgement must be made of the interest and cooperation received from all the probation officers and their staffs throughout the Federal Probation System in supplying the data and in smoothing out the difficulties which are always inherent in the first year of operation of any changed system of reporting. 

This report will be presented in two parts with eight major divisions. Briefly, the six divisions of Part I involve the following:

PART I 

PROBATION I. USE OF PROBATION 

Before examining the processes of supervision and termination with regard to persons placed under probation supervision by the United States district courts, it is of interest to ascertain the relative use of probation in contrast to imprisonment by the district courts and by the kinds of offenses involved. In the recent report on sentencing these data were presented for 1963 and are again summarized in this report.

In 1963 there is included among those granted probation defendants given a "split" sentence, i.e., a term of imprisonment not to exceed six months to be followed by probation. 

It appears that in such cases the major intent of the court is to place such persons under probation supervision. In 'mixed" sentences of imprisonment, i.e., more than six months to be followed by probation, the assumption is made that the 11. 

· 2.

main objective is imprisonment and these are included with the imprisonment group as contrasted with the probation group. In most such instances of mixed sentences the imprisonment ranges from one to five years, and it appears that the major emphasis of the courts in pronouncing such sentences is on a term of imprisonment. In nearly all of the latter cases the defendants are released from imprisonment by the United States Board of Parole in the status of parole or mandatory release for a period of supervision before the probation term starts to run.

In studying the relative use of probation and imprisonment by the courts certain offenses were excluded as they were the types that usually resulted in sentences of a fine or deportation. These exclusions included immigration violations for which deportation is the most usual type of disposition and violations of certain special acts: Agriculture, Antitrust, Fair Labor Standards, Food and Drug,

Migratory Bird and Motor Carrier, for which fines are usually imposed.

In presenting first the use of probation by type of offense the results in 88 district courts were shown, omitting the District of Columbia, and the territorial districts of the Virgin Islands, Canal Zone and Guam. This is necessary simply because the type of offenses handled in these courts are not the usual Federal offenses that occur in the other 88 district courts but are almost entirely local offenses-violations of criminal codes comparable to criminal cases handled by state and local courts but differing materially from the offenses in the 88 district courts. 

The distribution of sentences showing the number and proportion that are given imprisonment and placed on probation is presented in Table 1 by offense group. The 19 offense groups are arranged in order of the use of probation from the highest to the lowest. Some of these groups need further explanation. It was found for instance that fraud cases fell into two separate patterns in the use of probation. Fraud, Group A, which leads the list in Table 1, includes frauds under the Federal Housing, Veterans, Railroad Retirement and Social 

Security Acts. Fraud, Group B, are all other frauds and are shown in three subdivisions in Table 1, income tax, postal and other fraud.

The robbery cases which show the least use of probation are made up predominately of persons sentenced after conviction of bank robbery.

Probation granted under provisions of Title 18, Section 3651. 

Title 18, Section 3401, provides that the U. S. Commissioners have the power to grant probation for petty offenses. Persons placed on probation are supervised by Federal Probation System. 

United States attorney with the consent of the person accused or his parent defers prosecution and refers the person to the 

Federal Probation office for supervision for a specified period.

Original probation refers to persons who have been on continuous supervision in contrast to those reinstated to probation who were previously removed to inactive status. 

Probation supervision begins immediately following sentence.

Title 18, Section 3651 provides that when the maximum sentence for an offense is more than six months, the court may impose a sentence of which up to six months can be served in a jail-type or treatment institution. The balance of the sentence is suspended and the defendant placed on probation. Example: Defendant received a three year sentence of which the first six months be served in a jail and the balance (30 months) on probation.

Minor and Major Violation

Prior to 1963 information as it related to the kind of violation or criminal offense a probationer was involved in had never been available. Through the efforts of the probation officers, in 1963 details were reported about all persons who came into a violator situation. This permitted "minor a distinction to be made between what is termed a violation" and a "major violation." While a distinction between these is necessarily an arbitrary one, it nevertheless establishes a difference which is most helpful in studying the outcome of probation. By definition a major violation is one in which a person has been arrested and held under felony charges or has been convicted and committed to imprisonment for a period of 90 days or more or placed on probation for over one year on a new offense, or has absconded from supervision with felony charges outstanding against him. All other situations are defined as minor. These include technical violations, convictions, and sentences where the period of imprisonment is less than 90 days or does not exceed one year where probation is granted on the new offense. 

One of the basic reasons for making such a distinction is the fact that there is a great deal of variation between the attitude of courts, probation officers and United States commissioners with respect to the lesser types of violation. 

On the other hand, it is felt that those that fall into the major category used in this report are much more evenly reported in all jurisdictions. It is, therefore, suggested that the major violation proportions or rates offer the sounder basis for comparisons of district groups, etc. 

One further word about violation rates. The proportion of all probationers whose supervision is terminated who fell in the violator class as shown in Table 13 was 20 percent and for those who fell in the major violation class, 12.2 percent. Obviously, the persons whose supervision terminated during the year are not a common group who commenced their probation at the same time. Some of these people have been on probation for five years, some for a few months. The only justification for using the proportion of the total removals as a violate rate rests on the fact that this covers a year's operation, a large sample, and presumably reflects fairly well the true rate which would otherwise be computed on different terms. - 21 

---

As time goes on it will be possible to develop true violation rates based on follow-ups for a specified group of persons placed on probation in a given year. In other words, 1963 being the first year that this information is available, it will be possible to study the people placed on probation in 1963 and in succeeding years from now on and establish true violation rates based on the developing experience of each annual class or cohort. Presumably, it will take about five years to have complete information on a year's probationers as all persons placed on probation during a given year should no longer be on probation at the close of a five-year period. While this will be a more satisfactory method of establishing rates, until sufficient time has passed, the only other alternative is to use the violations of a given year in proportion to the terminations of that year which, as has been already pointed out, is a large sample and probably reflects violation rates fairly accurately.

VI. DEFERRED PROSECUTION 

A practice has developed in the Federal prosecution system which has no statutory authority but which has been accepted by the Department of Justice as a matter of policy which is described by the term "deferred prosecution. This procedure was introduced for the express purpose of handling certain juvenile cases in which it did not seem desirable to prosecute the juvenile for a Federal offense, and the authorities were opposed to dismissing the matter. Under this policy the United States attorney, having determined not to prosecute a given person for the offense, refers or turns over the offender with his consent or that of his parent to the United States probation office for supervision for a stated period of time. During this period of supervision, presumably the probation office renders the same service to - 43 -

the defendant as would be given to a probationer committed to his supervision by the courts.

Violation Data

It is of considerable interest to examine the portion of removals who were called "violators" out of the total number removed from supervision. These data are shown for each class of case in Table 43. As has been described in the section on probation, violators have been classified into two separate groups, minor and major. The same distinction as was made in the case of probation violations is made in accounting for violations by parolees and mandatory releasees. A major violation denotes either a new conviction with a sentence of 90 days or more, an arrest resulting in a holding on a felony charge, or the absconding of a person with felony charges outstanding against him. All other violations, most of them based on warrants issued by the Parole Board, are classified as minor. 

As will be seen in Table 43 the major violation rate for first paroles of Federal cases was 21.5 percent.

In the case of mandatory release there were excluded from the total on which the violation rate is based, 176 releasees whose term of supervision was 15 days or less. 

It would appear that where supervision is so brief, although they have to be accounted for statistically as official mandatory releases, such cases should not be made a part of those that are being studied to determine the outcome of supervision. It will be seen that the first mandatory release group showed a major violation rate of 17.2 percent. 

Military cases, whether they were released from the Federal prison or military custody, showed relatively low violation rates.
