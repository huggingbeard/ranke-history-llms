FOREWORD 

A portion of the material in this volume had been previously prepared by the author and presented in lesson leaf form. A few brief statements have been selected from other existing sources. No attempt has been made to narrate consecutive Quaker history. 

There is abundant source material readily available to those who wish to study our beginnings and progress. The purpose of the present small volume is to provide Overseers and First-day School classes in particular and Friends generally, with a ready reference book covering the religious profession, social concern, educational interest and the organization of Friends. Members are often perplexed when asked about our belief in certain tenets usually held by Christian groups. It is hoped that the statements of our attitude on these questions will be helpful. The book does not attempt to speak with authority on matters of opinion: since our religious profession is based on experience, with the doctrine of the Inner Light as its center, no one is commissioned to speak for the entire membership except with historical statements. The fact that we have no creed often troubles some Friends who would find it convenient to have formulated statements of our profession. The lack of such statements is an essential corollary to any non-authoritative religion. We make positive affirmations concerning the Inner Light because 5

FOREWORD 

Fox's explanation of this guide has been verified in the experience of many. We do not deny any doctrine not in accord with our own experience, but we do not accept formulated historical creeds which, in general, do not accord with the experience of Friends, and often are outdated by modern reverent scholarship. 

We base our religious profession on a direct and first-hand knowledge of God and its expression in conduct, commonly called "a way of life."

The presentation is designed for the use of the group which has its headquarters at Fifteenth and Race Streets, this being the only one with which the writer is very closely in contact. Attention has been given to the increasing unity among all branches. 

They are all better supplied with literary interpretation than the one for whose use this brief volume is especially intended.

The plan includes treatment of the doctrinal position of our group, our social background and present concerns, and our institutional practices, such as Meetings for Worship and for Business, and cites trends toward the elimination of branches as we all understand each other better.

The Appendix contains suggested reference material. It is hoped many classes will want to do their own research work and not depend upon the written presentation of any one person. It also contains some suggestions to teachers who wish to help classes become familiar with the Society of which they are members.

Do not attempt to cover each chapter in one lesson period. The chapters are arranged by subjects without regard to the length of class periods.

CHAPTER I THE ATTITUDE OF FRIENDS TOWARD THEOLOGY 

Note-This applies only to so-called Hicksite Friends. 

A person who talks seriously with various members of the Society of Friends concerning our beliefs will be impressed at once with the individual variations that are expressed. 

With no creed nor confession of faith, and no theological test for membership, uniformity of opinion is impossible. The basis of unity is an acceptance of the Light Within, and an earnest endeavor to follow its guidance. 

Quakerism is essentially mystical rather than rational. We have many clear rational thinkers, and we rejoice in their contribution, which mainly is that the way any of us think about theological or metaphysical matters is non-essential. Unity of spirit, not unity of opinion, is the tie which holds us. We do not object to our members holding to varying theological views; we do object to the effort of any one group of thinkers to impose their

opinions on another group whose reasoning or experience has led them to different conclusions.

VIEWS OF EARLY FRIENDS 

The practice of religion took such a vigorous hold upon the early Friends that it occupied their attention more than the elaboration of any scheme of theology. At the same time, they were well aware that thinking and doing go hand in hand, and cannot be sundered.

Every act performed is the result of some form of thinking. In their early experience as a people they were called upon to defend their faith, as a result of which we have a body of religious expression which is quite akin to theology. They laid, in their speech and writings, great emphasis upon the confession of Peter, "Thou art the Christ, the son of the living God," and the reply of Jesus, "On this rock will I build my church." They were persuaded that the "rock" was the revelation of God the universal Father, to the souls of all his children. They held that a new life is born in man by personal contact with God. This inward birth, according to Barclay, was the spirit born of the Christ within, and good works were the fruit of this spirit. They re-

9 vived the doctrine of Paul that regeneration does not come from good works and is not something earned by the worker, but is due to that which Paul calls "free Grace," and Fox called the "Inner Light." Both bear testimony to the transforming power of the Spirit of God working within the soul of man. They wrote voluminously concerning Justification, Sanctification, the Scriptures, and the common ordinances of the Church. But every doctrinal exposition refers back to the same inner principle of guidance, and individuals differed as widely as we do now concerning doctrinal points.

VARIETIES OF THEOLOGY 

The natural result of organizing a religious society without a creed (an intellectual formulation of belief) or a confession of faith (a formal statement of the things upon which our religious life depends) is great diversity of intellectual belief and varied profession of faith. The intellectual end of religious life and practice depends upon individual psychology, environment, training and experience. We are the "heirs of all the ages" and the creatures of the influences around us. The religion of experience needs no formulation

and no defense. We know it is true and lifesaving to us, though all the legalism and dialectics of the earth are turned upon it to prove it unsound. It may be possible to prove that there is no such thing as fear to those who have never been afraid (if there be any), or to establish the fact that love is a myth in the minds of those whose hearts have never felt its movings.

All Friends hold some kind of theological views. We hope these views are enlarging and broadening as we become more concerned to live the best lives possible, and learn to think more clearly and logically. Uniformity of views seems to us not only impossible but unprofitable. Pennington and Barclay would not have enriched the early thought of Friends so greatly if they had put identical interpretations upon the Scriptures and their own experiences. Written creeds tend either to stultify or arrest the thinking processes, or to induce people to profess to believe what they do not. Both these conditions are opposed to the fundamental Quaker position of walking in the Light. While Friends feel that authoritative statements have no vital connection with the individual religious life of man, they recognize the powerful influence

11 which authority has exerted upon religion, and commend a study of its formulated statements.

INDIVIDUAL FREEDOM 

The fact that no assent to certain beliefs is used as a test for admission into membership in the Society of Friends does not mean that our members may think carelessly or not at all concerning the great questions of the relation of the children of God to Our Father. 

Membership implies a seeking spirit, a turning toward the Light. If that mind is in us "which was also in Christ Jesus," even in a limited measure, we cannot go far astray. A life God-centered does not need a creed, and men and women who have not yet found God within will hardly be helped in the search by metaphysical and legalistic statements. The freedom of the Friend is the freedom of the Light, not the freedom of self. We are cautioned not to deny what has seemed true to others, even though we have had no perception of its truth; but we need to be equally careful not to revert to what the early Friends called "notional religion" because it is easier to accept the findings of others than to seek

to develop understanding minds and hearts of our own.

THE DIVISIVE EFFECTS OF THEOLOGY 

There are said to be several hundred different Protestant denominations in the 

United States. All are striving to serve the same God, all depend largely upon the Bible as a religious textbook, and all have similar concerns for the advancement of the Kingdom. The divisions are due to disagreement in theological statements or to difference as to the proper forms of worship and methods of Church government. While there are advantages in approaching the great problems of religion from various angles, no one doubts that the Christian Church has been greatly weakened by division. The Society of Friends fell a victim to disagreements about theology. As a result of the painful days following the Separation of 1827, our own branch has, perhaps, given too little attention to seeking and stating the original grounds of its existence. Much careless thinking and many partially considered statements have characterized a portion of our members.

CHAPTER II BAPTISM 

Protestants apply the word sacrament to baptism and the Lord's Supper, especially the latter. When the Society of Friends arose they found the numerous denominations engaged in the bitterest controversy over the value and efficacy of the sacraments. The early Friends came constantly in contact with this, and their history cannot be comprehended without some understanding of their attitude toward baptism. 

From the earliest period of the exercise of this rite it has been closely connected with regeneration and conversion. Baptism was at first a Jewish custom, applied to the converted Gentile, who must be washed in the sacred waters of the Jordan before he could become one of God's chosen. When Jesus first encountered it, but little of its primitive meaning had been lost. It stood for the change from darkness to light, from evil to good. 14

BAPTISM 

15 Among the Jewish practices of the ancient law he found sacrifices, offerings, sacred vestments, sacred trumpets, scapegoat, tabernacle and temple; he turned from these and others better known, and suffered himself to be baptized. He denounced all rites that partook of hypocrisy, and strove to lead those that embodied sincerity into the reality for which the symbol stood.

Down through the centuries of the Christian Church the rite assumed a strong objective character, much of which has since passed away.

EFFICACY OF BAPTISM 

That it served as a magic charm and acted upon the persons that received it without any consent or intention on their part, was once firmly believed; this gave rise to the idea that children and other irresponsible persons derived lasting benefit from the touch of the sacred water. It was also held that the rite wiped away all sins, however long they had been accumulating, or how late it was administered. For this reason the Emperor Constantine had the matter postponed until within a few hours of his last mortal illness.

In the fourth century it was the common

belief that the water was changed into the blood of Christ. Then came the belief that no one could be saved unless baptized. "They perish everlastingly who will not be baptized when they may," said Vossius. Hence, infants dying before baptism were lost. 

Up to the thirteenth century the rite was generally administered by immersion. It slowly gave way to sprinkling, which is still practiced by churches except the Baptists and a few minor sects. Much bitter controversy took place between the advocates of immersion and those who preferred sprinkling. The Scriptures were searched for the defense of each position, and each side was satisfied with the support which its devotees found for their particular practice. The Baptist sees in "One Lord, one Faith, one 

Baptism" a defense of immersion; the Friend sees in it only the baptism of the Spirit. The advocates of sprinkling see in the baptism of a large number of converts in a single day a certainty that the form used was sprinkling, because of the physical impossibility of immersion; Friends see in the pouring out of the Spirit on the day of Pentecost, a type of the only baptism that avails.

BAPTISM OF ADULTS 

Protestant Churches insist that a profession of faith must precede the administration of the rite. Most churches still hold that there is a special saving and protecting power in the ordinance, although many members of all churches recognize it only as a form symbolic of the new life in Christ, which those who accept it publicly profess. The ceremony at its best is impressive and probably helpful to the persons who accept it as a type of closer spiritual union between God and man. 

It unfortunately is often administered as an outward form, sometimes with spectacular features that detract from its serious purpose. It is so easy to fix our minds upon the sign rather than the thing signified, that 

Friends decline the ordinance. Howard M.

Jenkins says, "Both ceremonials (Baptism and the Supper) seem to them outward and tending, like all ritual observances, to the worship of the symbol instead of the thing symbolized. So far as either of them has virtue or value it must be spiritual, not external or formal, and it is to the spiritual baptism and communion that Friends aspire. 

If it be demanded whether these ceremonies

are not enjoined by Jesus, they think not, and they point to the fact that such injunction as he may appear to have given concerning them, is far less definite and positive than his language in relation to the washing of one another's feet-a ceremonial which the churches with almost complete unanimity decline."

THE BAPTISM OF INFANTS 

Luther favored the baptism of infants, and insisted that faith may be exercised in infancy. Calvin favored election among infants. Zwingli looked upon infant baptism as a consecration of children to God by their parents. The Anglican Church refers to it as "a mystical washing away of sin." George

Fox and his followers did not found birthright membership upon the idea of Zwingli.

They felt that such membership has its foundations deep in the Christian feeling that belonging to a Christian household consecrates every member of it. Infant baptism is now limited to the christening service which many people practice, either because they feel that in some inexplicable way it may be a protection to the child, or because it seems like a suitable ceremonial in connection with

BAPTISM 

19 giving a child a distinguishing name. It in no case takes the place of a later formal connection with the church. Friends naturally do not consider that such a ceremony has any potential influence.

How OTHERS VIEW FRIENDS' POSITION 

Members of churches which practice the rite often feel that Friends cannot be truly consecrated without this form, and our younger members are often called upon to explain why we claim to be Christians when we have not submitted to the symbolic regenerative washing. It may be a satisfaction to know that some great religious thinkers have recognized the validity of the Quaker position. Wall, a great Anglican writer on infant baptism, says, "Quakers may be regarded as baptized, because they have the substance of that of which baptism is the sign." Dean 

Stanley says the Quakers were "justified by this maxim of the early Church—'the martyrdom of the unbaptized is itself baptism." 

Later John Watson (Ian Maclaren) wrote, "The Sacraments are faithfully observed by every branch of the Church except the Society of Friends, who faithfully keep in the spirit what they do not observe in the letter." 999

CHAPTER III COMMUNION 

The most widespread of all the ordinances of the Christian Church is the sacrament known as the Lord's Supper, or the Communion.

During the apostolic age, this was not connected with formal worship. They followed the example of Jesus and broke the bread and partook of the cup at the close of the daily meal. Often, after their missionary efforts, they came together as brothers and as they partook of the brotherhood meal, they consecrated themselves anew to the service of God. It was accompanied by two different ideas. That of John, which was life; as the bread sustained the physical life, they believed that as they fed on Christ, in them eternal life would be produced. The other idea is Pauline, and includes the idea of escaping from the dominion of sin. The bread and wine were visible reminders that, as we are sustained in the flesh by food, so as we 21

die to the flesh, we must be sustained in our spiritual bodies by the spiritual body and blood of the risen Christ, who had shown us the way to escape from sin.

Following the first century of this usage, there is a marked increase of the literal, and loss of spiritual significance. In order to impress the rite upon a superstitious people in a superstitious age, its formal and literal nature was emphasized. The Roman Church taught that the substance of the bread and wine were transubstantiated into the very substance of the body of him who died on Calvary, ceasing after their consecration to be any longer bread and wine.

LUTHER AND EARLY REFORMERS 

Luther denied the transubstantiation by retaining the substance of the bread, but insisted that the body is in, and with, and under, the bread, so that the substance of the bread and the body are there together. This coexistence of the two substances is not permanent, but ceases with the sacramental action. Luther called it "a supernatural, incomprehensible union." He says, "the earthly elements remain unchanged and distinct, but they become the divinely appointed media for

COMMUNION 

23 communicating the heavenly substance of the body and blood of Christ." The consecration was not the result of the action of a priest, but came by the power of God.

Zwingli turned back toward the simplicity of the apostolic age. He denied the corporeal presence, but advocated the spiritual presence. To Calvin, the sacrament was more than a beautiful symbol. He looked upon it as a means whereby the grace of Christ is conveyed directly and truly to the believer's soul. 

While denying the corporeal presence, he insisted that the body and blood were really and sacramentally received by the faithful, but how the spirit really unites things that are totally separated, the human understanding cannot grasp. In this sense he held that "pure souls are fed by the flesh and blood of Christ, just as our corporeal life is preserved by bread and wine."

THE EARLY FRIENDS' VIEW 

The Friends went back to the simplicity of the apostolic practice of the communion. It was in remembrance, and it was life. Communion and fellowship with God through Christ was the central feature of early Quakerism. To be partakers of his spiritual

body was to have fellowship with the Father and the Son. Life is the central thought in the following, condensed from Barclay. "The 

Light that reveals the wrongness in thee is that body thou must partake of, and feed upon. This is thy communion, unless thou wilt commune with darkness; hence as thou sufferest that small seed of righteousness to bring forth a new birth in thee, then this new and substantial life in thee will feed upon, and be nourished by this spiritual body,this inward child is fed by the flesh and blood of Christ."

Friends have never practiced the formal observance of the Eucharist. To them, a merely mechanical theory is of no value, and the ease with which people substitute the symbol for the thing symbolized, the letter for the spirit, has influenced them to decline a form which seems to them, rather a hindrance than a help to the more exalted theory of communion which is the basis of their faith.

CHAPTER IV THE DIVINITY OF JESUS 

Perhaps no single question has given rise to so much difference of opinion as the problem of identity of Jesus with Christ, with the resultant disposition to consider such identity as dependent upon acceptance of the virgin birth.

THE POSITION OF FRIENDS 

The attitude of Friends toward theology, as indicated in a previous lesson, naturally puts a definite and uniform profession of belief concerning a question like this, in the realm of non-essentials. Some individuals find strength and comfort in accepting the idea of a complete metaphysical union between Jesus and the Eternal Word which was in the beginning. Others with rationalistic tendencies lay emphasis upon the complete separation of the two, and hold that Christ in the heart is a measure of God in us, and is the 26

THE DIVINITY OF JESUS 

27 same in kind and nature as the complete measure of God's spirit that was expressed in Jesus. As a Society we affirm the moral and spiritual divinity of Jesus but make no demand on our members for acceptance of metaphysical ideas concerning divinity. So little is it considered, that our own young people frequently are obliged to inquire, when the question comes home to them, what our belief on the subject is. 

The early Friends had their thoughts and interests so centered upon the new religious message, which makes us all partakers of God's nature and assistants in his work in this world, that they gave little attention to the consideration of abstract ideas. Many of them accepted the current ideas concerning Jesus and Christ insofar as they did not come into conflict with the Inner Light. They freely used the phraseology of the time and attempted to express new meanings through the same verbiage that was commonly used in conveying religious ideas. They invested the formalism of profession about Christ with the fruits of a living experience. They thought that the humanity of Jesus and his record left in history, could not be separated from the divinity of Christ and the witness

thereof, left by the "Seed of God" in their hearts. 

Pennington says, "The Friends distinguish between that which is called the Christ and the bodily garment which he took. The one was flesh, the other, spirit. The flesh profiteth nothing, the spirit quickeneth." "Spirit only is vital, its garment of flesh and blood, and its historical record were of no avail." George 

Fox apparently adhered closely to the unity of the outer and the inner Christ. It is possible to edit, arrange and infer from his writings, either one position or the other concerning what we commonly call the divinity of Christ. 

To gather the spirit of his meaning, requires careful reading and a full understanding of his sense of ultimate values in religion. Certain it is, that Fox and his followers so expressed themselves concerning the divinity of Jesus, that they were scorned and persecuted by the literalists of the time. Fox constantly makes use of figurative language like the following: "It is an easy matter to make an outward profession of Christ's death, but it is another matter to come to die with Christ, and suffer with Him, which they must do if they will come to live and be glorified with Him."

MODERN CHRISTOLOGY 

Within a few years, great changes have taken place in the minds of reverent students, concerning the divinity of Jesus. Scholars have called attention to the fact that Mark, the earliest gospel, makes no mention of the virgin birth; that John, the great interpreter of the spiritual life of Jesus, does not base his argument upon a miraculous birth, and that the tables of genealogy in Matthew and Luke do not agree, though both trace the descent through the line of Joseph's ancestors.

It is pointed out as probable that the current tendency to ascribe miraculous origin to great men had its effect upon the tradition of the time, and that Matthew and Luke wrote into their Gospels the current belief without attempt at verifying the things they had heard.

It is contrary to the principles of conservative scholars and thinkers to deny a generally accepted fact without convincing evidence of its error, so that most students of the Bible and contemporary records are not prepared to announce conclusions. There is no doubt that the prevailing trend of carefully weighed judgment is to attach little importance to the birth stories, or the idea that Jesus was divine

in any other sense than is possible to all men in a state of perfect obedience and trust. The 

Sonship of Jesus was unique, in that he was "full of grace and truth," divine in all his attributes while human in his feelings and instincts, "tempted in all ways" as we are, "yet without sin."

JESUS' OWN TEACHING 

Jesus always asserts his complete union. with the Father-"I and my Father are one"-but repeatedly makes it plain that this is a unity of spirit-"Of myself, I can do nothing, I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me." He does not separate himself from men. "Greater things shall ye do because I go to my Father." He stated his mission in the synagogue at Nazareth in the following words: "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he hath annointed me to preach the Gospel to the poor, He hath sent me to heal the broken-hearted; to bring deliverance to the captives and recovering of sight to the blind; to set at liberty them that are bruised; to preach the acceptable year of the Lord." The real divinity is forever upon him because he carried out the divine mission, as stated above. He called other men into their

THE DIVINITY OF JESUS 

31 heritage of Sonship, but never referred to the position afterward assigned to him as the second person in the Trinity.

PRESENT-DAY VIEWS OF FRIENDS 

Like our forbears, we Friends of today attach little importance to dogmatic statements of belief concerning the divinity of Jesus. Our concern is to be like him, insofar as in us lies, "To follow with reverent steps the great example," to emulate his patient forbearance, his long-suffering patience and infinite tenderness, his perennial hope and deathless devotion, to become partakers of the vigor, the heroism, and the benevolence which dwelt in his heart of love. Our individual views differ, and we are content to leave each mind free to rest in whatever view appears most helpful. We are out of sympathy with attempts to force assent to any intellectual proposition on the subject.

We encourage our members to have minds open to new developments of truth, whether these come through the establishment of historical verities, or in response to revealings to individual souls. While we set the revelation to any soul above all other sources of wisdom, we must bear in mind the fact that such

openings are not convincing to other minds in matters of historic facts. 

The Society also feels that faithfulness to the central principle of Inner Guidance, may lead us to very different convictions from many of those held by Fox, and historic citations concerning intellectual conclusions, while profitable for consideration, may not suitably be used to impose his theological views upon later generations. The relation between the past and the present is always significant in helping us trace the spiritual struggle of mankind to come nearer the Father. Temporal expressions of spiritual conditions which are vital and sustaining in one stage of their existence, may become dead and lifeless, in another.

CHAPTER V THE ATONEMENT 

The doctrine of the atonement is the centre of prevalent Christian theology. Its foundations are both historical and psychological. 

No purely historic tenet can continue to hold such sway in the minds and hearts of men unless some confirmation of experience vitalizes it. The statements of the doctrine are usually unintelligible, and this idea perhaps preeminently has suffered from the continued expression of its vital meaning through language with which a legal and formal interpretation had long been associated. 

THE OLD TESTAMENT IDEA 

The religion of the Hebrews provided for an elaborate system of escape from the consequences of sin. The culminating sacrificial rite was on the Day of Atonement, when the High Priest, at the close of solemn ceremonies, sent forth a ram into the wilderness as a sin offering for the people. Sacrifice is a fundamental idea in religious history. Since 33

men began to formulate doctrines into words they have expressed a deep sense of the reality and the contagion of sin. Among the 

Hebrews, especially, blood was regarded as the universal medium of purification. Blood covenants, the sprinkling of blood, and the pouring out of blood vicariously, were their highest expression of fealty and consecration.

As civilization advances, there is a tendency to mitigate the rigor of sacrifice, to accept lesser offerings from the poor and to substitute bloodless rites, such as the mass. of the Roman Church. On the other hand, with the evolution of higher ethical ideals, a contrary tendency demanded that sacrifices should be made of the best. This second tendency culminated in the death of Jesus on the cross as a sacrificial offering for sin. No greater sacrifice could be conceived; hence the idea of further sacrificial offerings came to an end, and the great doctrine of "Free Grace" pointed the religious world to the way of escape from the consequence of sin.

THE NEW TESTAMENT IDEAS The New Testament teaching concerning the escape from sin bears the traces both of

THE ATONEMENT 

35 the influence of Ezekiel's teaching (Ezekiel xviii), that the sinner is a party to his own redemption; and the idea of Isaiah (Isaiah liii), that the righteous must suffer for the guilty and make intercession for them. Constant expressions like the following characterize the Epistles, especially those of Paul: "He bore our sins."-"Christ died for our sins."-"He reconciled us to God in the body of his flesh through death."-"He was our ransom." We trace clearly in all these expressions, the use of figures borrowed from the older dispensations, through which the teachers of the time sought to apply a common principle of pedagogy, by proceeding from the known to the unknown. The interpretation of the great spiritual regeneration which Paul preached with the fervid intensity of a man "dead to the flesh" while he yet lived clad in its garb, by the literal standards of an earlier age, is one of the greatest limitations ever placed upon the spread of vital religion. The writings of Paul read as a whole, without undue emphasis upon a few passages selected for proof purposes, express something much more profound than the unethical idea of a purely substitutionary sacrifice. He taught everywhere a complete shar-

ing of the life and death of Christ. We must die to the flesh, i. e., to the dominion of the flesh, and live in the spirit, making complete the at-onement with God. (Romans vi: 8-11; vii: 1-10.) 

THE POSITION TAKEN BY FRIENDS 

The early Friends refused to formulate a dogma on the subject, though they wrote profusely, and not always clearly, about it. They held that no atonement was complete until 

Christ had brought forth in them, not for them, the same righteousness which he wrought. Without any accepted and formulated dogma, it is as difficult to characterize the ideas of atonement held by the early Friends as it is to speak authoritatively for our own generation. The differences felt and expressed on this point furnished a portion of the causes of discord that led to the separation. It is a fair general statement to say that no Friend accepted the death of Jesus as an exemption from sin and sacrifice on the part of the individual, unless accompanied by a sensible experience of the "begotten of God" in the heart, and a fellowship with his death.

Our own branch of the Society of Friends finds no value in the legal view of an outward, substitutionary atoning sacrifice. CHAPTER VI THE TRINITY 

The use of the word Trinity dates from the second century. It is an expansion of the doctrine of the Incarnation, and has its basis in the claim of Jesus that he and his Father were one.

Tertullian, a forerunner of Augustine, (220-160 A. D.) first taught that the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost are of one substance; that they are susceptible of number, but not division. The Father projected the

Son as the root the tree, but there is no separation. The Spirit is third from God and the 

Son as the fruit out of the tree, is third from the root. Later writers interpreted in various ways the triplicity of persons in the unity of substance, many of the views being dangerously near tritheism. Church councils were called to settle the difference between the views of churchmen, and the finally accepted doctrine of the Trinity was, as is the case with other doctrines, determined by human author- 39

ity arbitrarily exercised in an age of intolerance.

OPPOSITION TO THE IDEA 

The most serious early questioning of the 

Trinity was led by Socinus (1525-1562). He gathered a considerable sect who asserted that the Trinity was inconceivable and selfcontradictory. God is an individual, not a composite of persons. They denied that either the life or the death of Jesus brought any satisfaction for sin. They first gave a corporate body to the movement which was later known as Unitarianism.

Heated controversies arose over the question of the Godhead. The Scriptures were searched and abundant citations made by both sides, to establish either Unity or Trinity. The Trinitarians were the profounder scholars and the weight of the Churchmen was with them. A careful correlation, coordination and synthesis of passages in the Bible was made and the resulting doctrine of the Trinity as officially stated says that when 

God expresses Himself as Creator or Ruler, He is God, the Father; when He expresses 

Himself as Redeemer, He is God the Son; and when He personally applies to each soul

Holy Spirit.

STRENGTH OF THE TRINITARIAN POSITION 

With the exception of a few minor sects including our branch of the Society of 

Friends, the world has remained Trinitarian.

The Congregational Churches of New Eng land revived the old controversy not many years ago, and the organized Unitarian 

Church resulted. Perhaps the reason why this doctrine, which seems on the face of it like a reversion to polytheism, has had such hold upon the Church is because in human experience the three ideas noted above comprehend about what we know of God experimentally.

Successive generations of Christians have felt the loving Fatherhood of God, and realized through the operation of the Holy Spirit the possibilities of Divine Sonship which our 

Elder Brother attained in its fulness, so that the idea of Trinity is held as a fact of experience by those to whom the inexplicable subtleties of the interrelations of the three persons, who are one, do not appeal.

THE VIEW OF FRIENDS 

The early Friends, of choice, made no statements about the Trinity. Such formu-

lation belonged to what they called "notional religion," which interested them very little. 

Their inner experiences planted them on a ground that was neither trinitarian nor unitarian, as expressed in that day by the ardent advocates of either. As an expression of what we know of God, the early Friends looked more toward the idea of unity, while the churchmen of their day placed the emphasis on the diversity. Pennington says, "And thus they believe their being to be one, their life one, their light one, their wisdom one, their power one; and he that knoweth and seeth any one of them knoweth and seeth them all.” 

Penn insisted that there could not be three distinct persons in the Godhead. "God," he said, "cannot be divided, but is one pure, entire, eternal Being. Mistake me not, we have never disowned a Father, Word and Spirit which are one; but we disown man's doctrine built on this."

When Friends passed out of the period of their enthusiastic beginnings, and began to formulate dogma as did the sects with whom they came in contact, divisions of opinions at once arose. When they began to account more the holding of correct views than being vitalized by living experiences, the seeds of the

CHAPTER VII THE INNER LIGHT 

The central profession of the Society of Friends which distinguishes it clearly from all other religious denominations, is its profession of dependence upon the Inner Light. 

We are accustomed to the idea of this Light, but it is liable to mean no more to many of our members than any other dogmatic statement about religion. As a credal statement it has no particular value. It is only when it becomes a matter of experience with each individual, that we realize the unique power which it holds as a controlling center for religious life.

Briefly stated, we hold that God endows every human being with a portion of His spirit, which if given opportunity to grow in strength and power is sufficient to open direct avenues of contact between ourselves and the 

Giver of this source of light. George Fox first gave this doctrine definite form as the result of "Openings" which came to him 45

when he failed to find satisfaction in any religious contacts which he tried. 

Fox was not the first to experience this direct communion but was the first to make it the center of his religious faith. Augustine wrote, "It is the inward master that teacheth," and Luther said, "No man can rightly understand God or the word of God unless he immediately receive it from the Holy Spirit." 

John Calvin said, "Unless we feel the Spirit residing in us, what hope have we of the blessed resurrection?" With these leaders the idea of direct contact between God and man is subordinated to dominant theological propositions, and it was left to Fox to make it effective in practice.

PRACTICAL OPERATION 

Our meetings for worship gathered under the covering of silence are based upon the belief that a recognition of Divine Presence is more likely to be experienced when all intellectual stimuli are absent; but it should be borne in mind that the guiding light in the soul of man is not limited to the formal period of worship, but is an ever-present guide. "Walking in the Light" meant for early 

Friends not only personal guidance, but a new

47 social order. It was not something to be talked about and preached about; it was a practical guide to action.

We are more inclined to think of the Inner Light as a mystic communion with God than as a light to lead us into the world's service. 

The early Friends left us no more valuable heritage than the example of an entire people working under divine concern for the service of men. An important conception of the Light is an inner urge to direct us in fair dealing, brotherly kindness and the fulfillment of social obligations. The monastic system provided that men should be religious by keeping away from the world. The method was not a success. Friends have, sometimes in their history, come dangerously near this attitude of believing the Light would shine more clearly for individuals and groups largely separated from the world. Christianity has grown strong only when it has tried to lift the cares and burdens of the world. 

Quakerism receives new strength when it engages in altruistic service.

WHAT THE LIGHT DOES 

The philosophic-minded have been inclined to try to analyze and define the Light. Inter-

est has been felt in determining the relation of conscience and the Inner Light. Some hold that they are identical, others that conscience is the medium through which the Light works, and still others see no essential connection between the two, one being classified as a psychological reaction of the mind, while the latter is a revelation of God. For practicalminded people, such distinctions hold little interest. We care more about how the Light works than how it should be defined. The most remarkable characteristic of the Light, as Fox conceived of it, is its progressive ability to serve successive generations. It can never become an outgrown formula as may easily be the case with most doctrines. It stands for perpetual progress and is capable of infinite development and varied application. It is the religion of the unfolding life. 

Its application means that whatever limits or hampers life must go; whatever enlarges and liberates has a place.

Sometimes we fall into the error of trying to gather from history and apply to our own needs what the Light probably revealed to 

Fox and other early Friends, instead of seeking for and trusting in our own revelation.

CHAPTER VIII WORSHIP 

Worship appears to be a primary instinct of the race. The superstitious savage, the cave-dwellers, the men of the stone age shared in common with the twentieth century Christian thinkers, the desire to get into contact with, and obtain help from, some power higher than themselves.

Men have worshiped sometimes according to their individual movings, but more frequently according to the customs which their environment brought forth. They have paid homage to the spirits of their ancestors, worshiped the great forces of nature, paid adoration to fire, hewn out idols and worshiped them, have gone into solitude and endured fasting in their search for God, and have with elaborate congregate services signified their dependence upon the Most High.

The most elaborate ceremonial worship ever devised was the Temple Service of the 

Hebrews under their latest law and covenant. 50

51 Christianity, which was so simply taught by Jesus as he moved about among men, mingling with them socially, talking with them incidentally and exercising his marvelous gifts. for their help and healing, was the heir to Jewish tradition. Many of the fasts and feasts of the Hebrews were carried over to the early Christian Church with new meaning attached to them. Ceremonial observances, at first entirely lacking, have shown a tendency to come back into Christian worship. Praise, Adoration, Prayer, Exhortation, and Exposition of the Scriptures constitute the usual salient features of worship. Every great religious leader has called people away from a dependence upon mere formalism, and emphasized in some particular way the necessity of individual heartfelt worship. The vehicles of expression of the feeling of worship vary somewhat in different churches, but all prescribe some form which has been found profitable to somebody's experience, to be observed during the time devoted to public worship. 

INDIVIDUAL AND PUBLIC WORSHIP 

We recognize a sharply drawn distinction between the individual communion of the soul. with God, which most people have felt most

clearly in some hour of anguished need, and formal public worship. The former is probably practically universal although its variations are so curious that it is impossible to define it or to classify heart yearnings, longings and strivings as worshipful or worldly. 

Public worship has always been considered a helpful and reasonable requirement. Two diverse ideas characterize it; one that it is something demanded by God and the act gives Him His due, the other that the universal human need for help can best be served by setting aside special times and seasons to consider 

God and to strive to approach nearer Him.

FRIENDS' VIEW OF WORSHIP 

The early Friends recognized public worship as a twofold act, one of receiving and one of giving, and as such it was participated in by the entire congregation. In this we see the period of preparation and the time of participation which was either a silent or a vocal ministry. They did not make the preparation the sole act of worship, though they regarded it as very important, nor did they—as some branches of the Society have done—make the giving through prayer, song or preaching the sole act of worship. The perfect and harmoni-

This is the threshold of worship. This is what Fox meant when he said, "All men and women must come to the true Light of Christ and the spirit and the grace in themselves, if they be true worshipers of 

God." Following the period of preparation was the giving to God, either silently or vocally, that which the gift of His Spirit has enabled us to make our own. Friends looked upon the vocal exercises of a meeting as a result of the attitude of worship experienced by the assembly. George Fox recognized worship as the highest possible act of fellowship which can only be accomplished in the will of God. "It can be accomplished," says Barclay, "only by waiting until the gift of the Spirit shall reign supreme within; then we may have a meeting together not only outwardly in one place, but inwardly in one

CHAPTER IX THE MINISTRY 

All church organizations from the most primitive to the most complex have been dependent upon some sort of spiritual leadership, which has always expressed the highest conception of the religion held by the people of the time. This leadership has usually taken the form of preaching or exhortation. 

The old Covenant recognized kings, prophets and priests as a superior class, set apart to rule the people religiously as well as socially. The new Covenant recognized the religious democracy of all people. True worshipers were all possible leaders, preachers and teachers of righteousness. The curtain which had shut out the Holy of Holies from all but a chosen few was to be no more; and all, from the least to the greatest, were to be free partakers of God's love, and to have free access to His light and leading. With the passing of the old Covenant, fear, servitude to a ruling class and offering of sacrifices to 57

a remote God passed away; and a religion capable of infinite development through its freedom and personal recognition of the worth of each individual, took its place. 

PRIESTLY ANd Pastoral SYSTEMS 

The Christian church could not, however, completely change the ideas and practices of the people among whom it was established. 

The hereditary priesthood was abolished, but an especially ordained priesthood took its place. The theory held was that the true minister can only be fitted by the endowment of special grace, conferred by a particular officer of the church. The grace thus conferred at ordination was indelible and could not be affected by either the character or conduct of the recipient. It endowed him with authority to administer the sacraments, to remit sins, and made him the channel through which the 

Christian must approach the Father. The

Priestly system of the New Testament inherited much from the Old Testament system that preceded it. It became increasingly formal and legalistic until the time of the 

Reformation. 

The Swiss reformers introduced a very different idea of the ministry, which we call the

THE MINISTRY 

59 pastoral. The theory of the pastor is that all are not capable of partaking immediately of the Lord's table; that some must have a schoolmaster to bring them to Christ.

FRIENDS' CONCEPTION OF MINISTRY 

One of the great contributions which Friends made to religious thought and usage was the complete obliteration of lines of distinction between the clergy and the laity. The early Friends attacked with equal vigor the priestly and pastoral systems. The real sympathy which Friends might have felt for some features of the pastoral system was probably shut out by the fact that the early adherents of the system seemed unable to state their position as clearly as modern thinkers have done, and in practice the functions performed by priests and pastors seemed so much alike. 

The early Friends felt that the flock could have but one Shepherd, and He was their Pastor. They all knew His voice, yet when any one of the flock heard His voice more readily than the others, they willingly followed the leader.

PRACTICAL CONDITIONS IN THE SOCIETY 

Probably no object has given the Society more concern than the decline, and in some

places, the complete extinction of a gospel ministry. We have not succeeded either in justly estimating the causes for this condition, nor in effectively remedying it. Certain branches of the Society have openly confessed the practical failure of our ideal ministry and reverted to a pastoral system. In some places a passive silence characterizes our meetings; in others attempts are encouraged to present any good thoughts which arise during the silent waiting. None of these plans seem to be justified by beneficent results. 

Undoubtedly the fundamental cause was the abatement of spiritual fervor which led to the deplorable condition, when a Separation over matters of opinion was possible. A 

Society engaged in internal dissensions could hardly at the same time, continue to live in that close fellowship with God and with one another which is the condition favorable for vocal ministry. CHAPTER X SYMBOLISM AND RITUAL 

The Reformation repudiated much of the symbolism and ritual which characterized the church of the sixteenth century. Puritanism still more vigorously opposed images, decorated churches and a formal ceremonial service. George Fox carried the reaction against externals in worship still further. He desired to, and believed he succeeded, in banishing all formalism and ritual from religious meetings. 

THE MEANING OF Fox's PROTEST 

Fox knew that if men associated symbolically decorated, stained-glass windows with religion they were liable to consider them an essential adjunct of religion; he knew that if they repeated formal prayers and responses, they were liable to substitute this lip service for the real spirit of prayer and the expression of loyalty to God's leading. He wished to take away all transitory, casual and man- 66

SYMBOLISM AND RITUAL 

67 devised forms, and let the human spirit go out to meet the Divine Spirit in worship, without the intervention of mediaries of any kind. 

The Friends' meeting house was merely a quiet shelter where men might seek introversion of spirit without external distraction. 

There was no minister and no choir, no formal reading of the Scriptures, because such things would distract the individual search for a sense of God's presence. He would leave no opportunity for people to substitute worship of the container for the thing contained, the sign for the thing signified. No sincere student of religious history discounts the greatness of his contribution, though many question the possibility of realizing fully his ideal through the method of banishment and limitation. 

The thing that Fox demonstrated was that simplicity of symbol and ritual was for many people a better medium for spiritual worship than elaborate form. It is obvious that if something is to be contained, a container of some kind is necessary, and symbolism is the essential way in which all ideas and emotions take on tangible expression in a world of sense impressions. The idea of a clock is valueless until it is worked out in materials, love is a

profitless abstraction until it extends toward some reality and finds expression. Religious worship must have some kind of external environment, and religion must find a means of expression in a material world. The elaborately decorated churches gave way in the Society of Friends to a type of architecturally plain meeting houses, whose similarity of structure and arrangement makes them markedly symbolic of simplicity. The form of silence is the essential basic environment of a Friends' meeting. It early became common to recognize especially those with a gift for ministry and to set apart others to advise them as to soundness of word and doctrine. 

Standards of conduct were regulated by formal queries and answers. The Society of 

Friends soon developed a highly specialized and very formal simplicity of democratic procedure. The adherence to certain cuts of dress and certain forms of address, developed a symbolism of the most pronounced type. The idea of no forms, no symbols, no ritual proved to be a dreamer's abstract idea. 

The contribution of Fox was to substitute a very simple, direct and democratic type of formalism in worship, for the highly elaborate ceremonial observances which character-

69 ized the church of his day and which are still largely apparent in Christian worship. 

WAS THE SUBSTITUTION WORTH WHILE? 

Undoubtedly the lessening of complexity tends to more individuality in worship. Placing a particular kind of externals in the realm of non-essentials is a step forward. Release from rising, genuflexion, verbal response, or standing at intellectual attention, opens the opportunity for individual search which is the heart of all religion. Diminishing the number of external distractions makes introversion easier. The essentials of religious worship relieved of the non-essentials may function with less loss of energy. 

The rectitude of life expected of those who wore the plain garb and used the peculiar language, gained respect and often served as a protection against solicitation to do evil, and against exploitation. It was also a restrainer to those whose dress and speech proclaimed their allegiance to a sect which laid such emphasis upon clean living. The constructive work undertaken by individuals and corporate groups of Friends, was recognized on account of the very peculiarities which prevented their actions from passing unnoticed.

The tendency has for many years been away from the use of symbolic dress and language. Minor innovations have changed the austere simplicity of some of our meeting houses. Clearly, an insistence on special forms and symbols leads us back toward the very condition from which George Fox called his people away. We must not place our religious trust in externals. Our very peculiarities may so obscure the vital elements of our religion that people are repelled from rather than attracted to us. People do not like to be peculiar for no sufficient reason. The ones who claim to admire our plain speech and "picturesque" Quaker bonnet do not themselves wish to be identified with us. Our external peculiarities apparently tend to increase the difficulties of extending our vital religious message among people outside our ranks. 

Sometimes the rejection of a specified kind of content to religious meetings has resulted in little content of any kind, and many of our members have sought other religious homes because they failed to find in our freedom of opportunity sufficient stimulus to use it. 

WHAT SHAll We Do ABOUT IT? 

We are in a state of transition. We know we must have vehicles for expression more or

71 less symbolic and formal. We have left behind us the rigid age of non-conformity. We have not yet clearly defined which of our forms and symbols are of constant value and which are used for traditional reasons only. 

It is a time when we need to weigh values, to feel with intense zeal and fervor the importance of spreading what is vital and perpetually helpful in Quakerism, and to consider what we shall let go because it hinders our central purpose, and what we shall continue to hold fast because experience has tried it and found it valuable. It is not a time to try careless and destructive experiments. It is rather a time for earnest search, for far-seeing judgment; for clearness of vision as to the purpose which the Society of Friends should serve, and determination to keep it effectively in line for these ideals, no matter what traditions it must leave behind. It is a good rule never to change for change sake, but always to change in response to adequate and carefully considered reasons why we should do so.

A book of discipline is necessary to make our testimonies clear and to insure the orderly transaction of business. It is desirable for it to contain a sufficient background of Quaker development to make it clear why certain professions and practices were established. The use of the word "Discipline" is objectionable to many, as it does not truly express the purpose of the book. English Friends have met the objection by issuing separate volumes to cover historic development of certain professions and rules of business procedure. Up to the present writing, our Discipline contains three distinct divisions, Historical Statement, Basic Religious Conceptions and Testimonies, and Business Organization and Methods. 

All are combined in one small volume.

ADVISORY OR MANDATORY? 

The tendency in recent revisions is to make the major portion of the book advisory only. 72

OUR BOOK OF DISCIPLINE 

73 In earlier Disciplines, the mandatory sections predominated. In a society where uniformity of individual belief is not enjoined and in which the greatest freedom must be extended for freedom of conscience in acting upon our social testimonies, it is reasonable to exact conformity only to a sufficient extent to maintain orderly procedure and the upholding of essential testimonies. The unwritten law of the Society is often more in evidence than the limitations formally stated. What we have been in the habit of doing, we incline to continue without regard to Disciplinary regulation.

OUR DISTINCTIVE FAITH 

The membership of a society without a creed must hold certain important testimonies in common, which should be mutually understood in order to provide the necessary coherence to be called a distinctive Religious Society. Our adherence to a central truth expressed as "The Inner Light," our democracy of worship and ministry, mark us as decidedly different from other Christian sects, though we hold many essential Christian testimonies in common with them. We also make many important affirmations concerning several

testimonies which are adjuncts rather than essentials to the profession of churches. This body of affirmations presented to the membership by the Discipline as the ideal of Friends, constitutes the most important, but often not the best observed, part of the book. 

A society that hopes to maintain vigor and influence must rely upon the constant practical application of these testimonies in the lives of its members. A consistent opposition to war, intemperance, gambling, race prejudice and slavery of any kind is expected of every concerned Friend. We do not drop members who do not fully support the affirmations of the Discipline on all of these subjects, if they are in general sympathy with our profession and methods, but we hope always they will see the light and strengthen our testimonies.

CHANGING THE DISCIPLINE 

Making changes is wholly within the juris diction of each Yearly Meeting. The General 

Conference maintains a Committee on Discipline to act as a clearing-house among the Yearly Meetings when revisions are proposed, but is without authority to direct or prevent changes in the rules of any particular

With a developing tendency to make frequent minor changes, our machinery is too complicated to achieve uniform results without undue delay. We have fortunately emerged from the period when we felt that it was desecration to change the language and structure of the book in order to adapt it better to existing needs. Use furnishes the means for evaluating the book, showing its adequacy in guidance or its failures to stimulate us to our best accomplishment. In general it is a sound principle that changes should not be made merely for the sake of change or experiment, but should never be delayed when it is clear that revised or additional statements will help the life of the Society.

CHAPTER XII BUSINESS MEETINGS 

Friends' method of conducting business meetings is quite unique. It is based on the idea of friendly interchange of views and arrival at decision by common agreement. 

When a subject is before the Meeting, all who desire to express a judgment may do so. When there is decided difference of opinion which is not readily reconciled, no action is taken. 

Members are, however, urged in the Discipline not to insist unduly upon pushing their own views if most of the members do not agree with them. This method seems often to permit a minority to control the decision. In all history we find that minorities have quite as often been right as majorities. We are concerned with what we call "the weight" of the

Meeting. It is obvious that upon some subjects certain members have a measure of knowledge and experience back of them which is not possessed by all. Their views are therefore of greater value to a meeting than the 78

BUSINESS MEETINGS 

79 opinions of the uninformed or less concerned members.

Our attempt is to base decisions upon the most reasonable and helpful views expressed rather than to resort to the common method of counting pros and cons. Our procedure has been tried by successive generations of Friends with general satisfaction. We talk things over and agree upon decisions without being divided into opposing groups. The formality of parliamentary procedure would fit very poorly into our plan to move only in unity of spirit in the midst of personal preferences.

DUTIES OF THE CLERK 

Our business method vests peculiar responsibility in the Clerk. He is not only a presiding officer, but a planner of business and a judge and recorder of decisions. His duty is to place business before a meeting in an orderly manner and see to it that the Meeting understands matters brought up for decision. 

When consideration by the Meeting has reached a point where there seems to be general agreement, he is expected to record and read for immediate approval by the Meeting what seems to him to be the Meeting's judg-

ment. The Meeting may reject, accept or amend his minute when read. After it has been put into form acceptable to those present, it records the official decision of the Meeting. The reading of the Clerk's minute as soon as the matter is settled is important in our method of conducting business, in order that all may know what decision has been made. A Clerk introduces most of the business directly from the desk, though there should always be opportunity for the introduction of concerns from the body of the meeting.

The position of Clerk is a difficult one to fill successfully. Such an appointee is both more and less than a presiding officer. A meeting looks to a Clerk for leadership but not for direction. It is the function of the Clerk to record the decisions of the Meeting whether they are in accord with his own judgment or not. In this respect he is the servant of the 

Meeting. When he guides a Meeting into an intelligent understanding of matters before it, he becomes an interpretative leader. The 

Meeting looks to its Clerk to see that all business matters are handled with as much dispatch as is consistent with sound judgment,

VOLUNTEER COMMITTEE WORK 

The ideal of Friends is that all members will give a reasonable measure of whatever kind of service they are best able to carry on.

The special genius of the Society is its dependence upon the general service of its members for preaching, teaching and social effort. 

In order to be effective, members are set apart on committees charged with varied responsibilities, who generally work without the aid of financially compensated leaders. (In sporadic instances, some assistance is given by paid secretaries.) This widespread committee organization is depended upon to keep the Society dynamic. It does not work with a hundred per cent effectiveness.

QUERIES AND TESTIMONIES 

Early in our history, certain testimonies upon which the members were agreed were set forth in minutes and letters. Later they were gathered together in the Book of Discipline. In order to encourage members to live up to our profession and to develop care

on the part of Meetings to keep regular minutes and to see that their affairs were at all times carefully looked after, a series of questions called "Queries" were framed and all regular Meetings were expected to answer them. The method of answering these searching questions and forwarding the summarized replies to superior Meetings was the means adopted to survey "the state of the Society." These queries have from time to time been revised and many of them made exempt from answers. Some Friends believe their usefulness has been outgrown, while others consider them an important means of keeping our standards before the members. 

At this writing (1936), many places are substituting reports on the state of their Meetings for summarized answers to queries. 

There is no doubt of the value to Meetings of reporting quite fully upon their own progress and hearing in turn what other Meetings are doing. It is too soon to judge whether queries regularly answered and reported are still the best means of establishing close ties of interest among the Meetings. At this time they do not hold the place of primary importance which once belonged to them. Our great committee system, with an inclination for com-

The unit of membership is the Monthly Meeting, which thereby becomes in some respects superior to the "Superior Meetings" to which it reports. Under our present Discipline, members of a Monthly Meeting are automatically members of the Quarterly and

CHAPTER XIII MEMBERSHIP 

Persons are admitted to the Society of Friends without any formal rite such as baptism. Careful inquiry is made to ascertain whether applicants for membership are really in sympathy with the profession of Friends. 

If their requests appear to be based on a sincere desire to become affiliated with us, after a period of waiting for committees' or Overseers' recommendation, they are recorded as members. We have never had any means, such as revival services, to stimulate applications for membership, and until quite recent time have had little care to invite those who seemed interested into membership. Naturally our membership has not increased. We are often regarded as exclusive and not desirous of having new recruits who are not descended from old Quaker families.

In recent years this inertia has been very largely overcome, and we cordially welcome 86

BIRTHRIGHT MEMBERSHIP 

The Society has had its ranks recruited. during its entire history very largely through birthright membership. This institution rests. upon the supposition that we are a big family and children born into the homes of individual Friends are also born into the larger family which comprises our membership. Since we do not recognize any particular profession of faith nor any fixed ceremony for admittance into the Society, it seems logical that children surrounded by and participating in the institutions and practices of Friends should be considered as members unless, after they have arrived at years of discretion, they prefer to withdraw and elect a different affiliation. Certain problems have arisen in connection with the system. Some birthright members fail to receive adequate instruction and are thus denied the opportunity to learn the value of their birthright. Some when they arrive at maturity feel pride in their birthright but fail to accept its obligations. This has resulted in a considerable fringe of nominal members who assume no responsibility in

the Society and feel no concern to uphold its testimonies. The situation presents a problem. Propositions arise, especially from younger Friends, either to do away with birthright membership or to consider a Junior

Membership only which must be confirmed by the expressed desire of these members when they reach maturity if they wish to remain with us.

THOSE WITH ONE PAREnt a MembER 

A considerable number of Friends unite in marriage with those not our members. The children of these marriages have a divided interest. They may be recorded by us as members if both parents approve this course. We believe at present reasonable care is taken to do so. 

In almost every community of Friends we find one or more adults, only one of whose parents was a Friend, who are not recorded with us. It should be an immediate duty to seek out such and invite them into membership if they are not already on the lists of some other religious body.

Note This lesson makes no effort to enter into the many contributory causes which led to the separation. 

While it is not the purpose of this lesson to lead into the complications of the history of the Separation of 1827, this unhappy event has so important a bearing upon both our present and future that it seems necessary to make a brief statement concerning it. 

CAUSE OF THE SEPARATION 

The underlying cause was apparently a waning of spiritual vigor in the Society as a whole. In England as well as in America, the clear message of a religion of experience which should supplant a religion based on formulated tenets had been unable to continue to draw recruits and vitalize the Meetings as it had done in its days of greatest fervor. 

Possibly the Society was saved from extinction by a great evangelistic movement that swept English Quakerism. Adherence to cer- 92

THE SEPARATION 

93 tain definite theological doctrines assumed a place unthought of in the days of Fox. The movement was brought into America largely through the visit of a concerned English Friend who, in his zeal for the spread of his own convictions, did not reckon with that portion of the forces of American Quakerism some of whose members were totally unprepared to make the doctrines in question a test of sound Quakerism.

In its days of greatest spiritual vigor, it would have been impossible for disagreement in doctrinal matters to have divided a closely knit brotherhood. It was because Quakerism had become somewhat decadent that it fell an easy victim to the passionate feelings engendered by the controversy which arose as to which doctrines were sound (orthodox) and obligatory upon all true Friends. 

The leader of the revolt against the imposition of doctrine was Elias Hicks, of Long Island. He was a man of real ability, a clear thinker and a determined opponent of the views which did not appeal to him. The body of Friends who agreed with his position were dubbed "Hicksites," a name which has abided with us and is often used as a distinguishing epithet even though not officially recognized.

The contest of opinions deepened and involved the control of the officers and the property holdings of Meetings. The central profession of Friends-that we should follow revealed Light and its almost equally important testimonies concerning non-resistance and a spirit of love for one another-were submerged in heated and unchristian controversy. Incidents disgraceful in the annals of any religious body took place, and when the storm subsided, American Quakersim had been broken into two main divisions, with further minor divisions in prospect. It is fairly evident that neither side can be adjudged either right or wrong. Disputes can prosper only as they produce division. There are two sides to every controversy. Both sides were originally sincere in their positions, but undue desire to prevail over the opposition led them to actions and bitterness of feeling most unbecoming to Friends and calculated to weaken their influence in the world.

A final alignment did not take place in England nor in the New England Yearly Meeting.

The Meetings now included in Friends General Conference were the only ones which adhered as a group to the position taken by

In the final settlement into separate units, the Orthodox group was unable to remain intact. The greater number turned toward a system of regular pastors, and as migrations spread westward many meetings under the pastoral system were settled from Indiana to California. Arch Street Meeting assumed an independent, non-pastoral position. The largest body of Friends in America belongs to the so-called Five Years Meeting group. THE PRESENT SITUATION 

For many years after the unfortunate failure of true religion in 1827, the branch called "Hicksite", (they sometimes rather inappropriately call themselves the "Liberal" branch) were the black sheep of the flock. No Yearly 

Meeting, even those where no separation had taken place, would correspond with them or in any other way acknowledge that they were Friends. Occasionally through the years,

some concerned Friend would intimate that forgiveness and reconciliation awaited those who would return to the true fold; but the chasm which the conflict of opinion had opened was wide and deep and only in recent years has progress been made toward a united 

Society. A separate lesson deals with trends. in that direction.

The bitterness of the struggle has disappeared. A recognition of the folly of the past is quite general. Our testimonies are the same; our methods of worship and of conducting business are identical; we share a common history up to 1827. The importance of correct theological views takes a minor place in the minds of Friends who are throwing their lives into great altruistic movements for human betterment. We shall probably never all agree upon a theological profession. 

If subjected to the test now, a new alignment would have to take place. We need each other: each branch has something to contribute. It is always difficult to readjust any organization which is running in different established grooves. There are many minor as well as some major adjustments to be made. In that stupendous religious struggle known as "The Reformation," men of all schools of thought sought for an infallible guide. The Romanists held to the idea of an infallible Pope, while the scholarly Protestants placed final authority in the Scriptures. 

These writings were used as the sole weapon of the early reformers for separating authentic Christianity from the traditions and vagaries of contending apologists. This was the situation that confronted early Friends. They found men proving all things from the Bible, from the wildest vagaries to the most profound truth. They found disunity and discord thriving by reason of the contradictions gathered from the Scriptures by the selective ingenuity of man.

109 the Bible. They must find the confirmation of the Scriptures in the convictions of their own souls. All early Friends spent much effort in opposing the exaltation of the letter above the spirit, and this opposition was so marked that they seemed to be opposing the Bible itself rather than the perversion of its use. 

These ideas imperfectly transmitted to their posterity, together with the principle that "God teaches his people himself," developed a condition in middle-age Quakerism when the Bible was little used, and ignorance of its content was not considered any reproach to a religious society. The progress made by reverent Christian scholars during the latter part of the nineteenth century came to the relief of puzzled Friends who had begun to feel their ignorance of the Bible as a handicap, but yet found little enlightenment and satisfaction in reading it.

HIGHER CRITICISM AND THE BIBLE 

With the great impulse which was given to earnest and diligent study of the Scriptures, great changes took place in the views of 

Christian people toward it. Reverent scholarship has stripped from the Bible the environing husks of tradition, and furnished us with

an intelligent method of using the vast treasures of spiritual strength and comfort which have been handed down to us through fragments of history, scraps of prophecy, outbursts of fervid poetry and apocalyptic visions. At first the so-called Higher Criticism of the Bible was met with a storm of opposition from churchmen; but gradually it has dawned upon us that whatever is vital and of perpetual value in the Bible cannot be injured by the most searching examination; and wherever gold and dross are curiously mixed, as we often find them, it is of the greatest value to Christianity to have an intelligent separation made. To all open-minded people, the traditional view of the Scriptures, which rests upon verbal inspiration, has passed away. The destructive criticism of the eighteenth and early part of the nineteenth centuries has ceased to be interesting, while constructive students and interpeters are bringing the Bible into a place of interest and helpfulness to us which it has never held. The place assigned to the Bible by the great present-day students of its history and content opens it to large usefulness by the Society of Friends.

CHAPTER XVIII EDUCATION AND SCHOOLS 

From the earliest times Friends have had an interest in education, probably because a society which had repudiated a ministerial class, and based its profession on a brotherhood of democracy, felt the need of a trained membership from which leaders might arise.

CHAPTER XIX OUR TESTIMONY FOR SIMPLICITY 

One of the distinctive professions which Friends have always held is that simplicity in outward observances is favorable for the development of spiritual vigor. The early expression of this testimony was shown by avoiding changing fashions. The usual form of dress used by the rank and file of the generation which brought forth Quakerism was retained while the world at large followed changing forms. In addressing common people "thee" and "thou" were used, while the plural "you" was used only to persons of rank or especial importance. Friends' idea of democracy and fellowship led them to avoid distinctions in manner of dress, so they adhered to the forms in everyday use. They saw in the names of the days of the week and of the months a relationship with paganism, since these names were derived from the names of heathen gods, so avoided their use. 131

SIMPLICITY BECAME FORMALISM 

The forms which later generations called "plainness of speech and dress" were maintained long after the reasons which brought them into being had been forgotten. Plain dress became a distinctive garb, making Friends unlike other people instead of denoting a sense of equality with everybody else. 

Our peculiar forms of speech have been to some extent retained long after any association with simplicity and plainness has passed away. In business a peculiar form lacks plainness, since the average man or woman does not quickly understand it. The mention of "January" no longer brings up images of 

Janus opening the gates for war and closing them in times of peace. Within the last few years we have rapidly shaken off these exterior peculiarities. They no longer represent to us the testimonies they were originally meant to advance. Our forbears were sincere, vigorous, and faithful to the Light as they saw it. We honor the means through which they expressed their testimony. We should express our convictions in a way that will be of greatest benefit to the age in which we live. It seems to most of us that such a course

OUR TESTIMONY FOR SIMPLICITY 

133 will not lead to the preservation of formalism that has lost its meaning.

The original testimony once expressed by certain forms of dress and language is as vital to Quakerism as it ever was. Queries and advices have carried out the idea that extravagance in dress, house furnishings, showy accompaniments at weddings and funerals should be avoided.

Language which is clear, well chosen and free from unnecessary expletives, exaggeration and inaccuracies of statement best meets today our testimony for simplicity in speech. 

Clothing which is neat, inconspicuous and adapted to the occasion for which it is provided is more suitable than peculiar garb often expensive and difficult to obtain. Ugliness is not necessarily plainer than beauty. 

In this age special attention needs to be given to frugality in expenditure and to the avoidance of becoming the slaves of changing fashions. When a form is abandoned, there is always danger that the principle which it embodied will go with it. Simplicity of home furnishing and habits of living is a Quaker testimony of perennial value. It is a part of the stability of a Society which hopes to make a sustained contribution to human progress,

that its members devote time and effort to altruistic purposes rather than to the pursuit of pleasure and a dependence upon personal luxuries.

CHAPTER XX SOCIAL CONCERN I THE TESTIMONIES OF EARLY FRIENDS 

The history of Quakerism shows that coincident with the missionary zeal which proclaimed that God teaches His people Himself without the intervention of priests or ministers, the early Friends showed in practical ways their acceptance of the belief in the brotherhood of man. They taught that in the eyes of God there is no class distinction and accepted as a part of their mission an effort to strengthen the world's sense of social responsibility. Since they never sought recognition for their efforts to improve society, there are few reliable records of the early centuries and it is difficult to trace, with any degree of accuracy, their efforts (sometimes successful and sometimes unsuccessful) to bring about conditions in society which would insure every man a fair chance and a square deal. 135

Among the causes to which Friends have testified for or against are the following. 

Many of them are summarized in Penn's Holy Experiment. Among them we find: 

An abatement of the evils of poverty. The possession of wealth is a stewardship and should be so administered.

Fair and honest administration of business, fair dealing and justice in the payment of debts.

Care of public health, especially treatment of the insane. 

Reform of prisons. Prisoners are erring people who need reclamation if possible. 

Condemnation of capital punishment. 

Opposition to slavery and recognition of the rights of all races. All mankind are the children of God.

Efforts to abolish the use of alcohol, a proven enemy of man's best development and usefulness.

Adherence to the idea of the equality of women under the law.

Opposition to war as unchristian and ineffective.

Opposition to gambling which develops a fundamentally unsound social attitude.

THE DEVELOPMENT OF SOCIAL EFFORTS 

In nearly all of the concerns enumerated here, Friends were pioneers. Some of the subjects have been taken up vigorously by other groups and carried forward until they are generally recognized as essential movements toward well-ordered society. A single one equality of women under the law-has been largely accomplished, though certain inequalities with regard to property and business rights still exist in many states. 

Fair dealing in business and just payment of debts have always been fairly well observed among Friends, and theoretical public ethics are in entire accord with these principles. Unhappily, subterfuges and legal technicalities are often invoked to screen moral practices not in accordance with just business practices.

Abatement of the evils of poverty has made little headway. It is the foremost problem of our generation.

Public conscience and the efforts that it has motivated have accomplished marvelous changes in the care of the insane. Of course

some of our public institutions devoted to this purpose are far from what they should be, but there are others which are conducted on the basis of conservation and cure through kindly and intelligent treatment.

To a large measure, early concern for the Indians has been taken over by the Government. Arch Street and Five Years Meetings continue work with Indian schools.

All other fields in which Friends pioneered have been taken up in a large way by organized agencies, and headway has been made with most of them. Some prisons are better, most of them are apparently still in great need of pioneer spirits to fight their battles.

CHAPTER XXI SOCIAL CONCERN II 

A normal accompaniment of the profession of Friends that religion leads to a way of life is widespread interest in bettering human conditions. A Friend is expected to be personally upright and fair in all business transactions and concerned for the welfare of others, especially those needing protection, encouragement and friendly help.

REALIZED TESTIMONIES. 

The practical expression of our social testimony led to consistent opposition to human slavery until that institution was relegated to history. Advocacy of equal suffrage was a logical result of our recognition of men and women equally in business meetings. To this viewpoint the Society as a whole adhered.

Probably the most persistent and consistently upheld social testimony of the entire Society of Friends is the one against war. 

While an increasing number of people in America deplore war as a national calamity, many are influenced largely by economic reasons. The financial cost of war is devastating. 

While, of course, Friends with others are interested in this phase of the situation, the basic testimony does not rest upon any material line of reasoning. To the Friend, war is wrong. To take life is not a proper prerogative of men nor nations. We stand for the peaceable settlement of difficulties because that is the only way which is consistent with a belief that all men are children of God, even though some of them have strayed far from

the Father's house. Taking life may be justified by the laws of men, but the law of God written in terms of the brotherhood of man cannot be annulled. Friends thus make a unique and consistent protest against war and capital punishment. CHAPTER XXII WEALTH AND BUSINESS 

Friends appear to have had an earnest concern on the subject of wealth at a very early day. Such light as we have upon their attitude does not suggest that they were concerned about limiting wealth. It was rather to be sure that it was acquired by the practice of ethical standards in business and rightly used by its possessors. John Bellers, an early exponent of social concern, was very much interested in relief for the unemployed. (How modern this sounds.) The basis of his somewhat elaborate relief plans seems to rest on two main theses: (1) "Profit by the rich as all would be profited thereby." (2) "Abundance to meet the needs of the poor." The chief interest which inheres for us in his proposition is not in the nature of his plan, but in the fact that Friends so early felt a deep interest in the problem of adequate care for all. William Allen, another Friend writing in the last years of the eighteenth cen- 146

WEALTH AND BUSINESS 

147 tury, believed that the withdrawal of the population from the country into the cities. was the chief cause of poverty. He actually started a farm project associated with various workshops and laborers' cottages. He met with no great measure of success, but the idea has persisted, though not a special interest of Friends.

BASIS OF TESTIMONY ABOUT WEALTH 

The early opposition to accumulating wealth seems to have been based principally on the thought that wealth increases luxury and tends to impair our testimony in favor of simplicity. Friends were encouraged to consider intrinsic surplus as a trust and to use it for the benefit of others. As often happens, one good thing often offsets the results of another good thing, unless their joint effect is carefully weighed. Friends laid emphasis on thrift and frugality. They are good testimonies, but when practiced to an extreme degree, they may lead to the accumulation of unnecessary wealth, and the habits of great care in spending may prevent a wise distribution after surplus has been gathered. 

There is an omnipresent danger that if too high a regard is felt for thrift, we may dis-

regard the effects of our own thrift on others dependent upon us for business arrangements.

BUSINESS ETHICS 

According to Jorns, Friends were the first to use practically the one-price standard for everybody in business. Prior to their day, business was transacted on the bargaining principle of selling as high and buying as low as possible.

The principles upon which many Friends built up successful business were the observance of the strictest honesty, which gave rise to the familiar saying, "A Quaker's word is as good as his bond"; the avoidance of all speculative adventures; and insistence on the principle of promptly meeting all obligations. 

The latter concern was expressed for many years in the familiar Query, "Are Friends punctual to their promises and just in the payment of their debts?"

These guiding principles have undoubtedly been of great service to many generations of 

Friends. Individual lapses occur and corporate bodies often pay little or no heed to upholding these principles among their mem-

WEALTH AND BUSINESS 

149 bers. Business and its obligations have become more complex and the competitive spirit creates a great strain upon those who desire to maintain high standards and still must provide the material resources necessary for the support of families.

DIRECT AND INDIRECT STANDARDS 

We are less likely to fail in the support of visible and immediate standards. We do not forge checks nor steal chickens. The vast economic structure of which we are parts, makes it easy to fix fictitious and inflated values, to exploit the ignorant by buying from them at a price below potential value because they know no better, to advertise too colorfully, to take risks involving the money of other people, to invest in securities not calculated to help society, to be all things to all men in the world of trade, to be careless about the prompt and scrupulous payment of debts, to borrow without knowing how payment can be made, and to push aside others to make opportunity for ourselves. Friends in business need to measure the indirect implications and associations by the same four square standards that they use in returning

an accidentally lost article to its owner. We can be happy together, rich and poor, if we can really trust each other.

VARIETY OF GIFTS 

In business as in everything else, we find men endowed with different abilities. This does not matter if the spirit is the same. A recent writer has compared our mixed life to a chess board. Certainly business often resembles a game of chess and we ought to be able to play it as fairly, with due regard to the rules. Nobody expects a knight to make the move of a bishop, and a pawn is not despised because it cannot move like a queen.

But every piece on the board is entitled to its own move, and none must try to substitute a different move nor interfere with the defined rights of each piece. If we are only pawns in the business game, there are a great many of us, and if we play the game well, within our rights, we are not subjects for reproach because we are not kings, neither are the kings in the wrong if they exercise their larger privileges according to the rules. 

He who disobeys the rules and tries to usurp another's place is an outlaw. The Friend who fails to use every ounce of conscience which

WEALTH AND BUSINESS 

151 he has, and who does not try to follow the clearest light shed upon him by past experience and present search in conducting his business, is helping to lower the standards which our fathers set for their Quaker descendants to support and lift higher. 

THE ABOLITION OF POVERTY 

Our forbears were deeply concerned that poverty should be banished by fair and equable opportunities, just compensation and an attitude of brotherhood toward all men. 

It all sounds very easy as an academic proposition, but neither Friends nor anybody else has made much headway toward actual realization of this Utopian state. Perhaps its best hope is in the last-named condition, viz., an attitude of brotherhood toward all men. This makes it distinctly a religious, rather than a purely economic, problem. Economists propose various theories, generally having something to recommend them, but lacking some of the elements of successful operation. Legislative action is constantly trying to reduce poverty and unemployment by law. Perhaps it can be helped in this way, but before leaning too much on legislative regulation, we ought to examine the fundamental structure 1

of society and see whether the things we are trying to regulate are in themselves capable of regulation.

Some earnest people see in State Socialism our only solution; others equally concerned see in this course the making of both business and the people engaged in any form of it, a football for politics.

It is fairly apparent that, old as the recognition of the questions of wealth, poverty and unemployment are, the methods used thus far to mitigate their evils have had no great measure of success. The concern should be with Friends today.

CHAPTER XXIII FRIENDS' GENERAL CONFERENCE 

This organization is an evolution and combination of Friends' agencies which arose to meet the need of a close relation of interests among our scattered members.

In 1857 the First-day School interests of the Seven Yearly Meetings found it advantageous to form a central organization to approve lesson material and to exchange ideas. This Conference met biennially during the remainder of the century, its place of meeting being fixed by a revolving schedule including places in each Yearly Meeting. 

Among the promoters of this bit of machinery were Eli M. Lamb of Baltimore, Louisa J. 

Roberts and Lydia H. Hall of Philadelphia, Joseph A. Bogardus and John W. Hutchinson of New York (afterward especially identified with philanthropy), Elizabeth H. and

Edward Coale of Illinois.

OVER-ORGANIZATION 

From that time until 1900 we began to suffer under the weight of the machinery we had created. We went to the place of meeting, organized, presented a program and had an appropriate closing session. As soon as the first Conference was over, we met and organized, conducted and adjourned another. 

This process continued until all four had finished their business. By the time it was over, we had an impression of being machine-made. 

There were four sets of officers to choose, four sets of plans to be made for perpetuation, four kinds of topics, strictly kept within their own compartments, to be considered. 

The same people attended them all. 

THE GENERAL CONFERENCE 

The idea of centralization of interest naturally resulted, and in 1900 the meetings at Chatauqua all approved of a merger, governed in business matters by a committee of one hundred members allocated to the Yearly

Meetings according to their size. All special interests were naturally somewhat apprehensive that they would lose by the change. The situation was met by creating subdivisions in the Central Committee, charged with particular concerns. This method has continued in satisfactory operation since that time, with minor changes and some added concerns. The most important of these was the creation of a

PURPOSES SERVED BY THE CONFERENCE 

Perhaps its most important function is to unify the interests of Friends and to give Friends living in places rather remote from other Friends a wider vision of the Society and its possibilities. CHAPTER XXIV THE AMERICAN FRIENDS SERVICE COMMITTEE 

Out of evil good sometimes seems to arise.

This sequence has happened so many times that people often seriously query whether we are justified in doing wrong things in order to accomplish some greater good. We can hardly support such a proposition, though we are ready to agree that, amid the devastation, suffering and wrong of the great World 

War, one lasting benefit accrued to American

Friends. The common demand upon a Society, however divided, to show its willingness to do some kind of self-sacrificing public service, since its testimonies were against participating in military service, resulted in the formation of the American Friends Service

Committee. Those who called this organization into being builded better than they knew. 

They visioned the organization as an emergency creation to administer relief in the war-torn zones of Europe. It has proved that 159

people always need relief and that the fellowship of all Friends in a great movement for human help has been a potential agent in revitalizing a Quakerism that was suffering from ingrowing tendencies.

ORGANIZING the Work 

The call to service met with ready response both from men and women. The tremendous task of organizing necessary financial support, of mobilizing volunteers, making plans for the kind of assistance most needed, negotiating with nations at war for permission to feed their children, care for their sick, rebuild their homes, etc., was headed by 

Wilbur K. Thomas, the first secretary of the 

Committee. For ten turbulent and difficult years he was the executive leader in a herculean task. The vision, spirit and enthusiasm of many others, especially Rufus M. Jones, the Chairman, contributed their full measure of devotion, but the executive detail fell upon the Secretary. The service he rendered should be held in grateful remembrance by Friends.

The need first was for relief; later, when hostilities ceased, for reconstruction. The story of funds poured out from sources outside of Friends, of the willingness of tech-

AMERICAN FRIENDS SERVICE 

161 nically enemy countries to receive our workers, of the request of the Government for Friends to administer their relief funds, is a familiar story. It has probably been told in nearly every Friends' Meeting House. 

WIDENING OPPORTUNITIES 

The spiritual results of material service are in the class of uncovenanted mercies. The people of the Central Powers began to ask, "Who are these people?" "Why do they feed and nurse us when we are at war with their country?" "Can it be that they take seriously the injunction 'Love your enemies'?" etc. As a result, centers were established in various parts of Europe to answer such questions and provide a means of understanding fellowship. Some of these were finally closed from lack of financial support. It is easier to get money for the necessities of life than for intangibles. At this writing, centers at 

Geneva, Vienna, Paris and Berlin are maintained.

With the closing out of most of the organized work in foreign fields, many Friends thought the Service Committee, having finished its mission, should disband. Or if not, what should it do? The answer came in the

shape of the appeal for help in the distressed soft-coal areas, where thousands were dropped from an employment in which they would never be needed again. Under the leadership of Clarence E. Pickett, the postwar Secretary, relief and slow rehabilitation in certain parts of West Virginia, Pennsylvania, Kentucky and Tennessee were organized. Its story is briefly written in the reports of the Service Committee made each year to the Yearly Meetings. 

SECTIONAL WORK 

On the intellectual side, a Peace Section has been indefatigable in organizing Peace Institutes, attended by thousands of students, and otherwise extending the testimony. A 

Social Order Section develops active service in regions where living conditions are on a low level. A Fellowship Council, designed to bring all Friends in the world into closer sympathy and understanding, is a by-product of the Committee which assumes increasing importance.

APPENDIX TEACHING QUAKERISM 

Many of our teachers are sincerely desirous to instruct their classes in the principles of Friends. Most of them feel their inability to do so satisfactorily and believe that if readyto-use material for each grade were handed out to them the need could be met. Such a plan is too ambitious for us and would fail in many points if tried. Webster says of oratory, “It must exist in the man, in the subject and in the occasion." His observation, with appropriate adaptation, explains why formal prepared material is not enough. 

Quakerism must exist in the teacher in mobile and active enough form to be used. It can generally be best applied when real situations arise which can be helped by seeing how Friends feel about such things. The subject talked about may not be labeled as "Quakerism." A religion which finds its chief expression as a way of life must be gradually 166

APPENDIX 

167 absorbed by growing life. It cannot be taught like a lesson in mathematics or history. 

Of course there is also a place for imparting knowledge of Friends' history, testimonies and present activities in a formal way. 

The objective must always be to make the history and incidents vivid and real. A development of real loyalty to Quakerism is important. It cannot be done by academic instruction, but by developing real interest in both past and present. Baltimore has an organization for quite young Friends known by the unusual name of "Quaker Vagabonds."

For dealing with adolescents, plans for getting together, and calling for active effort on their part outside of the class periods, are desirable. Teachers should be mindful of the fact that they cannot give what they do not have. Knowledge of Quakerism in all its phases and enthusiasm for its future are assets essential to leading pupils in interest and loyalty. Certain suggestions to teachers wishing to do this follow. 

I. At what age shall instruction concern ing Friends begin? Beginners and primary classes will be found very responsive to the "voice within" as a natural experience. The foundation of Quaker concepts may very

properly be laid here in a simple way. Incidentally, primary pupils may have their attention called to the simplicity of meeting houses and the fact that we worship in silence so that we may hear God speak to us. 

2. When shall we give formal instruction? The junior or early intermediate age is probably suited to getting the exterior setting of Quakerism before classes. Show pictures and, if possible, have members visit several meeting houses. Point out the ones with particular historic value, e. g., Jordan's in England, where William Penn was buried; 

Birmingham, Pennsylvania, used as a hospital by Revolutionary soldiers; Jericho, Long 

Island, built by Elias Hicks; Easton, Maryland, preserved as an historic shrine. Teachers will add others of special local interest. 

Pictures of well-known Friends, present as well as past, may help to interest boys and girls. Stories and anecdotes from the lives of 

Isaac T. Hopper, Lucretia Mott, etc., are often available. Pictures of Conference groups are interesting.
