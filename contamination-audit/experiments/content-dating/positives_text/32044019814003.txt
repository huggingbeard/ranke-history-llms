THE WEEPING STATUE AND DANTE'S GRAN VEGLIO BY H. THEODORE SILVERSTEIN 

IN the fourteenth canto of the Inferno Vergil explains to Dante the origin of the rivers of Hell. On Mt Ida in Crete stands un gran veglio.

La sua testa è di fino oro formata, e puro argento son le braccia e il petto, poi è di rame infino a la forcata; da indi in giuso è tutto ferro eletto, salvo che'l destro piede è terra cotta; e sta'n su quel, più che'n su l'altro, eretto. 

Ciascuna parte, fuor che l'oro, è rotta d'una fessura che lagrime goccia, le quali, accolte, foran quella grotta. 

Lor corso in questa valle si diroccia: fanno Acheronte, Stige e Flegetonta; poi sen van giù per questa stretta doccia infin là ove piu non si dismonta: fanno Cocito. . . . 

Concerning the sources and background of the gran veglio, commentators have had little essential to add to the first suggestions of Benvenuto da Imola and of Boccaccio. They are generally agreed in naming Nebuchadnezzar's dream in the book of Daniel as the principal source; to which source, giving it shape as a symbol of human history, has been added Ovid's description of the four ages of man.¹ The common origin of the rivers of Hell in tears dripping from the statue has elicited, with the exception of two recent suggestions, only an elaboration of Boccaccio's remark that the tears represent the miseries and sufferings of mankind.?

The central significance of the origin of the infernal rivers in tears for an understanding of the entire symbol was first recognized by Vittorio Capetti, who pointed out several legends, European and oriental, of weeping statues.¹ Independently of Capetti, Asín Palacios has noted briefly Islamic tales in which the Nile, the Euphrates, and the 

Tigris originate from brass domes or statues.2 But neither Capetti nor

Asín has succeeded in developing his materials beyond the stage of parallel and illustration. Nor has Asín, in attempting to suggest 

Arabic influence, taken into account important Christian literature, or pertinent classical myths, which, in union with the statue in the book of Daniel, might well bave suggested the complete symbol to Dante. 

It is the purpose of this paper to consider first the earlier lore to which Dante's symbol is related as a type. Some of this lore Dante certainly knew; much of the rest was current in his times and may well have reached him. Then we shall turn our attention to a large body of contemporary Christian literature in which the themes of the weeping statue and the rivers of tears are a central feature. Finally we shall consider a passage in Vergil's Georgics, hitherto unnoticed from this point of view, which, together with the Christian lore and the statue in Daniel, probably contributed to Dante's conception of the powerful symbol of human history in the fourteenth canto of the Inferno. In ancient lore we find numerous legends of weeping statues and rivers of tears, of both classical and oriental provenience, which throw striking light upon Dante's gran veglio. Ovid's Metamorphoses contains four such legends in the stories of Acis, Marsyas, Niobe, and Phaeton.

In his More Nevouchim the twelfth-century Jewish philosopher, Maimonides, relates a weeping statue legend. Tammuz, a prophet, invited a king to worship the seven planets and the twelve signs of the 

Zodiac. Tammuz was put to death, and on the night of his execution all the idols of the various countries of the earth gathered together in the Temple of Babylon, before the great golden statue of the sun which was hung between heaven and earth. The statue made a funeral oration for Tammuz, and the idols wept and groaned all night.¹

But more important is another legend of tears, going back to the first centuries of the Christian Era and current among the Jews of the Middle Ages as a part of their Talmudic lore. Centred upon what was to the Jews their greatest disaster, the destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem, the legend is a moving figurative representation of national calamity. There were, then, numerous legends of weeping statues and rivers of tears of various origins known in Dante's time.¹ Ultimately based, perhaps, on ancient nature myths in which bodies of water were transformed into anthropomorphic gods and rivers were frequently represented as flowing from an urn held by a male or female divinity, the legend of the river of tears is a variation commonly associated with some great misfortune or calamity. And it is with respect to this motive that the legend of the river of tears resembles the other type of legend, that of the weeping (or sweating) statue. The two legends taken together, to whatever extent Dante knew and used them, must have enlarged his conception of the statue in the book of Daniel, and in their light the gran veglio of the Inferno assumes a new aspect. 

II 

But mediaeval Christianity had a legend of tears of its own, which, developing from the eighth century, reached its florescence in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The Legend of the Wood of the 

Cross, treating of the great calamity of human history, Adam's fall, and its greatest triumph, the redemption through the tears of Christ, presents the rivers of tears and the weeping statue legends with an elaboration in art and literature far surpassing that of similar non- Christian legends. Since Dante met the legend, and the related symbolism, at every turn in the painting, the sculpture, and the literature of his age, and since its conception of history is related to that which lies behind the symbolism of the statue in Nebuchadnezzar's dream in Daniel, this Christian legend is without doubt the chief source for 

Dante's own legend of tears in the Inferno.

But it is only with the development of the Legend of the Wood of the Cross that the legend of the rivers of tears becomes centrally important in Christian lore. To this legend and its connection with Dante we must now turn.

Under the influence of the danger of Tartar invasions and the popular identification of the Tartars with the Ishmaelites of the Old Testament, thirteenth-century Europe took a renewed interest in the Revelations of the Pseudo-Methodius.³ These prophecies, attributed to 

Methodius, fourth-century Bishop of Olympus, purport to describe the imminent end of the world. Beginning with Nebuchadnezzar's dream and the description of the four great kingdoms in the book of Daniel, and supplemented by numerous other eschatological passages in the Old and New Testaments, these writings trace the history of mankind from Adam and Eve, through the six millennia of history and

But the Revelations had already previously achieved a widespread popularity, which reaches its climax in numerous literary treatments, beginning in the twelfth century with Peter Comestor and Godfrey of Viterbo and continuing through the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, until examples are found in the literatures of England, Germany, France, and Italy.2 3 From the very beginning, the use in literature of the Pseudo- Methodius material links its treatment of history with the episode of Seth's visit to the four rivers of the Earthly Paradise in that version of the Legend of the Wood of the Cross which became popular during the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. In that version of the legend, Seth, at the behest of his father Adam, goes to Paradise to obtain the "oil of mercy." In the centre of Paradise he sees a large and beautiful tree, the roots of which reach down to Hell. The tree is barren of leaves, because, as Seth learns, of the sin of his parents. From its base flows a fountain of water, which, dividing, forms the Gihon, the Phison, the Tigris, and the Euphrates, the four rivers of Paradise. Seth does not obtain the "oil of mercy." Instead, he receives three seeds, from which, planted in Adam's mouth after his death, spring the trees that are to furnish the wood for the Cross of Christ, whose crucifixion in the sixth is to atone for the sin of Adam in the first millennium.¹ 

Nor is this connection of the history of the world and the Legend of the Wood of the Cross new to the twelfth century. For the episode in the apocryphal Gospel of Nicodemus, which is the direct source for the Legend of the Wood of the Cross in the Middle Ages," itself reflects the Christian interpretation of human history as a progressive series of passing confusions between the great initial fact of man's fall and the greater culminating fact of his salvation. III 

Vergil's description in the Georgics of the signs foretelling the death of Caesar has, in its connection with Dante's great symbol of history, escaped the notice of commentators. Nor, taken alone, is its relationship to the Dante figure at once perceptible. But, examined in the light of the large body of weeping statue legends, especially the Christian legends, the passage takes on a striking suggestiveness. For it is apocalyptic in character; and, true to type, it centres about a great national calamity, and at its climax is a weeping statue legend. The description occurs in Georgics, I, 463-483.

IV 

Dante was thus the inheritor, both from ancient times and from his own age, of a rich body of tradition concerning weeping statues and rivers of tears. The Christian lore, related naturally to the historical symbolism of the statue in Nebuchadnezzar's dream in Daniel, and elaborately treated in the literature of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, furnished abundant suggestion for Dante's symbol. Nor must we overlook the existence of the same symbolism in Christian art. 

For, parallel with the literary development, was a development of the figure of the Crucified Christ in painting and sculpture that makes it truly a weeping statue. Examples are numerous. The cover of a tenthcentury Gospel Book which belonged to Otto II shows typically the figure of the Crucified Christ, carved in relief, as the source of the Gospels, which in turn are represented by the four rivers of Paradise,³

But it is to the paintings of the Crucifixion in Italy and to the works of Florentine contemporaries of the poet that we must turn for influence on Dante. The Crucifixion of Giovanni Cimabue, the first great painter of Florence, who was personally known to Dante, bears a curious resemblance in its details to that account of the Legend of the Wood of the Cross which is of Slavic origin. After relating the history of the Tree from the time of Seth onward, this Slavic version concludes: 

It became the Cross of the Saviour: When Christ was crucified on it, His blood fell through the rock upon the head lying beneath, thus freeing Adam from sin, and redeeming him.³

Cimabue's painting shows Christ on the Cross, with blood streaming from his wounds. Two angels are catching in chalices some of the blood from his hands and from his side. The rest of the blood streams down over the feet into the mound on which the cross is set. The mound is shown in cross-section, and within it is seen a skull (Adam's), on which the blood falls. Identical in these details with the Crucifixion of Cimabue is that attributed to another contemporary Florentine painter, Bernardo Daddi.5
