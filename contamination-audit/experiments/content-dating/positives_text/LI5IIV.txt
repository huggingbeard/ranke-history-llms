FOREWORD 

Payments for personal service constitutes the largest single item in the operating budget of most cities. How to attract and retain qualified personnel for careers in municipal government is therefore one of the most important aspects of city administration. 

Formal salary plans have been developed in an increasing number of cities. The need for maintaining municipal salaries on a competitive basis with salaries of other employers in the community and the increased value of experienced employes have both been recognized in these plans. 

In recent years fringe benefits have also become recognized as an important part of employe remuneration. To an increasing extent various fringe benefits are regarded as a form of wages to which the worker is entitled. Paid vacations, sick leave, retirement and insurance benefits are thus becoming an increasingly significant part of the compensation offered. Tax considerations have no doubt been one factor contributing to the greater use of fringe benefits in private employment, and this has necessarily had an important influence on the compensation policies of city government. 

Information presented in this bulletin indicates the present policies and practices of Oregon cities with reference to the salaries and fringe benefits of city officials and employes. The information compiled here has been provided by 169 cities and the cooperation of these cities is gratefully acknowledged. 

The survey forms were developed with the assistance of Robert R. 

Johnson, staff consultant for the League of Oregon Cities. The tables were compiled by Robert E. Moulton and Mrs. Katherine Lueck. 

Mrs. Shirley Mikkelsen typed the master sheets and was in charge of production.

SALARY RATES AND FRINGE BENEFITS IN MUNICIPAL EMPLOYMENT Summary 

The adjustment of salaries and the addition of certain fringe benefits for municipal employes during the past year are reported by a number of Oregon cities in replying to a recent questionnaire which was completed by 169 cities.

Several factors have apparently influenced city action. First, the cost of living index rose sharply in 1956-57. Also, carpensation rates in private employment and in other governmental services increased during that period and there has been an expansion of fringe benefits. Finally, competition for qualified personnel particularly in specialized fields remained keen despite recent over-all reductions in employment and economic activity. The questionnaire indicates that city officials are to an increasing extent making formal surveys of employment conditions and practices in their respective communities as a guide for municipal policies and are seeking more actively to compete for well qualified personnel.

Salaries. Some 30 of the 169 Oregon cities which provided information reported blanket salary increases during the past year. Many of the other cities made salary adjustments for individual positions. instances additional fringe benefits such as city-paid insurance premiums were provided in lieu of salary increases. 

At least 10 Oregon cities have now adopted formalized position classification and pay plans covering all city employes. Several other cities are considering similar action and at least three small cities report the establishment of formal salary schedules providing for salary adjustments based on merit and tenure. The staff of the Oregon State Civil 

Service Commission has been employed to assist several cities in developing classification and pay plans.

The salary rates of officials and employes in 169 Oregon cities are reported in Table I starting on page 8 of this bulletin. Titles most commonly used have been applied to various offices and positions. In making comparisons between cities it should be remembered that the responsibilities and duties of persons occupying positions with similar titles may vary considerably in different cities. This is especially true in the smaller cities. For those cities which have position classification and pay plans, existing ranges have been shown for each position in which more than one person is employed. The tables have been footnoted to indicate instances in which an official or employe acts in two or more capacities to the extent reported.

Increased appropriations for state personnel services made by the 1957 Oregon Legislature provided salary increases averaging about 7 per cent for state employes effective July 1. Also, a basic minimum of $200 per month was established for state employment. New salary ranges for various types of employment were then established by the State Civil Service Commission. The ranges in effect in December, 1957 for various state positions which are common to cities are reported on the following page. 

The Consumer Frice Index of the U. S. Bureau of Labor Statistics fluctuated within a narrow range from 1952 until the middle of 1956 and then rose sharply to reach a new all-time high in 1957. The salaries of some private employes are directly related to this index and, of course, price levels exert a general influence on all salary rates. Hours of Work. A 40 hour work week has been adopted by a majority of the cities for most of their personnel, although there are still many variations. The principal city offices are closed on Saturday mornings in most of the larger cities, but public works employes work regularly on Saturday mornings in some of these cities. Where city offices are open on Saturday mornings, some cities maintain a full staff, others have a skeleton staff with employes working alternate Saturdays, while others grant a 40 hour week to all employes except department heads and emer gency personnel. 

The police departments of the larger cities maintain someone on duty 24 hours a day and a police officer is on call at all times in most of the smaller cities. Some 12 cities have established a 40 hour week for policemen and 6 cities report a work week in excess of 48 hours. 

Fire department service is, of course, maintained on a 24 hour basis in all cities with some firemen on duty at all times in paid departments. -2-

The work week of firemen was reduced to 72 hours by state legislation adopted in 1949 and a majority of the cities with paid firemen follow this schedule. However, the work week of firemen has been reduced to 

56 hours in several cities and the schedule is similar to that of other city employes in a few of the smaller cities.

Overtime. A majority of cities have developed standard policies for payment of authorized overtime work. The nature of reimbursement varies from compensatory time off at straight time or time and one-half to cash payment at straight time, time and one-half, or double time. Some cities add accumulated overtime to vacation allowances. Often the employe has some choice as to the type of credit or reimbursement. A number of cities have standardized overtime pay plans to apply equally to all employes including police and fire personnel. However, cash payment for overtime is more common for public works employes than for employes of other departments. 

While employes of the State of Oregon are entitled to either compensatory time off or additional compensation, both at the rate of time and one-half, the state discourages cash payment for overtime as a matter of policy and makes such payment only when a department can not reasonably grant compensatory time off. Also, state employes in the higher salary brackets are not paid for overtime which their positions may require. 

Holidays. All but five of the 92 cities over 1,000 population that reported information in this survey indicate that a definite schedule of paid holidays has been adopted. New Year's Day, Memorial Day, Independence Day, Labor Day, Thanksgiving Day and Christmas are each listed as a paid holiday in all but three cities. Pendleton grants a "round-up" time holiday for all except emergency personnel.

The state legislature has established the following legal holidays for the state: New Year's Day, Lincoln's Birthday, Washington's Birthday, 

Memorial Day, Independence Day, Labor Day, Veterans' Day, Thanksgiving,

Christmas, statewide election days, and any day that may be proclaimed a holiday by the Governor of Oregon or by the President of the United States.

Some cities do not give the same number of holidays to all groups of employes. Forty-one cities report that they provide compensatory time off or pay for employes who must work on a holiday. The method of payment varies considerably from city to city and also between departments within individual cities. Also, some type of compensation is granted to employes of 23 cities when a holiday falls on a regular day off. 

Vacation Leave. Standards for granting paid vacations vary from city to city with some cities computing vacation leave on a calendar day basis and others on a working day basis. An effort was made to clarify -4-

these differences in Table III, page 46, but there may be minor discrepancies.

It is becoming the general practice among reporting cities to grant a two week vacation to employes after one year of service and additional vacation time after five, ten or fifteen years. A few cities grant only one week of vacation the first year. Proportionate vacation time is usually allowed part time employes. 

Sixteen cities permit employes to accumulate credit for unused vacation.

The maximum accumulation allowed is one month. 

The majority of cities pay employes for unused vacation at the time of separation although such payment may be denied when an employe is discharged or leaves without notice. 

State employes receive one week's vacation for each six months of service. The minimum requirement for eligibility is six month's service. 

Employes receive two and one-half weeks of vacation after five years of service and three weeks of vacation after ten years.

Sick Leave. A formalized sick leave policy is reported in 13 cities over 10,000 population and in 37 smaller cities. Allowances range from 10 to 15 days per year with six months to one year the minimum service requirement. Many of the cities that have not adopted a formal sick leave policy do grant such leave on a discretionary basis. 

Sick leave credit may be accumulated beyond a year in 39 of the cities reporting a formal sick leave policy. Accumulation allowances range from 15 to 90 days.

Approximately half of the larger cities grant a sick leave to regular part time employes but most of the smaller cities do not. Oakridge pays health insurance premiums for employes in lieu of sick leave. 

State employes earn sick leave credit at the rate of one day per month with a 90-day maximum accumulation privilege.

Leave of Absence to Attend Meetings. All of the larger cities and most of the smaller cities grant their officials and employes paid leaves of absence for attending training programs, professional meetings, and conferences related to city affairs. About half of the larger cities and a few of the smaller cities pay their officials and employes while on leave to attend conferences and conventions of professional organizations not related primarily to city affairs. Α few cities grant paid leave of absence for attendance at civic, fraternal or union conventions, the policy of almost all cities being to deduct such absence from vacation time. Policies with reference to payment for travel expense for attendance at meetings devoted to municipal affairs vary considerably among cities, with most cities -5-

paying all actual expenses while some cities pay a part of the expenses or a per diem allowance.

Accident Insurance. 

As required by law, state industrial accident insurance is provided for all city employes engaged in hazardous occupations. 

In some of the smaller cities, department heads are not covered and clerical workers are not included in others. Accident insurance other than state industrial is available to employes of five cities over 10,000 population and to employes of 17 cities under 10,000 population. Although only selected groups of employes are covered in most cities, all Canby city employes are covered at the city's expense and all Medford employes are covered with the city contributing one-half of the cost. 

In The Dalles, police reserves and volunteer firemen are covered at the city's expense.

The majority of plans offered in the smaller cities apply primarily to volunteer firemen with the city paying at least a part of the cost. 

Coverage for other city employes is optional with the employe paying the entire cost.

City participation in industrial accident insurance and private insurance plans is shown in Table IV, page 51. 

Hospitalization, Medical and Surgical Benefits. A comparison of the 1953 

and 1958 fringe benefit surveys indicates a trend toward more complete hospital and surgical protection for municipal employes in Oregon. Sixtyfour cities over 2,500 population and 27 smaller cities report group hospital, medical, or hospital-surgical plans. Employe participation is optional in most cities. A few cities pay the full amount of the premiums or contribute to the cost, but most employe coverage and all family coverage is at the employe's expense. The amount of premium is withheld from the employe's paycheck in all but one city where a plan is in effect. The number of participating employes ranges from 

25 to 100 per cent with the majority of cities indicating a range of 

50 to 75 per cent.

Group Life Insurance. Seven cities over 5,000 population now provide optional group insurance plans for employes. The Salem group plan applies only to police department personnel. Five cities share the cost with the employe benefits varying according to the plan selected. 

In the Coos Bay and Eugene plans, benefits and costs are determined by salary level. Other plans provide fixed amounts of insurance coverage.

Tillamook is the only one of the smaller cities that provides a life insurance plan. The Tillamook plan is optional and provides a $2,000, double indemnity policy at the employe's expense. 

Retirement and Social Security. All but four of the 169 cities pro viding information in this survey indicate that their employes are -6-

covered by some type of retirement plan. The employes of almost all 

Oregon cities are now covered by the Old Age and Survivors Insurance features of the federal social security program. Employes of a number of cities are covered by the Public Employes Retirement System as well as social security. Most city employes who are members of the PERS are also covered by social security under the program under which the two systems were integrated in 1953. Police and fire personnel of 

Portland and Salem are members of special pension funds. 

Uniform Allowances. Most cities provide some allowance for the purchase of uniforms or work clothes of firemen and for the purchase of uniforms and equipment for policemen. Allowances are usually in the form of direct purchase by the city of uniforms or equipment although in a few instances, allowances are made in cash. A few cities provide equipment or work clothing such as rainwear, boots, gloves and coveralls for street and water department employes. -7-
