MORANA CHAPTER I 

Ralph Rayburn was a rather mysterious character to the people of Reynoldsville. He had little to say and was very reticent in reference to his past life. Nobody knew where he came from or anything pertaining to his antecedents, consequently, he was more or less interesting to those of inquisitive minds, and there were people of this kind in Reynoldsville. 

However, he was a likeable fellow and conducted himself in such a manner that no one could say aught against him.

He had just arrived in the town a short time before and was impressed with the beauty of the place. 

After strolling about the town on a tour of inspection, he found a rustic seat on a small knoll on the side of a hill. Someone had erected the seat there for the reason that it was a good point for observation. It was in the month of October and as he gazed out upon the scene, he saw at his feet, a village surrounded by hills and covered with trees. There was the maple tree with its leaves ranging from orange to yellow, the gumtree with its maroon red, the water oaks, with their crimson leaves and there were pine trees with their green foliage mingled among the others which

gave variety to the scene. On the side of the hill at different places, were bunches of sumac in their variegated colors of red, yellow and green. 

As he gazed out upon this beautiful scene, he felt the thrill that might well do credit to an artist who paints pictures; in fact his artistic nature, and he was endowed with one, was rampant with the beauty of the scene. He was aroused from his revery by the sound of a bell as the chimes rang out from the belfry of the church on the side of the hill, playing an old familiar tune. He arose and stood looking down upon the village where he saw a street which led to the south where it ran into the hills. On the other end, it turned the corner into a valley. It was the artery that led from the country to the town and formed the main street, in the center of which were the business houses. 

Small streets, like laterals ran out from it on which were located the homes and residences of the citizens. 

Here and there on the side of the hill would be a large commodious and beautiful home. These were the homes of those who had acquired wealth and affluence. 

The village was situated in a valley about a mile wide. 

It was a beautiful morning and the sun was shining and its rays were invigorating yet mild and pleasant. 

So thrilled was he with the scene that then and there he made up his mind that this should be his homethis town of Reynoldsville.

For a long time he drank in the beauty of the scene but at last aroused himself, though loathe to leave. 

He descended to the village and was soon located in a nice boarding house.

Then death claimed Ralph Rayburn and Edith was left alone with her child and the responsibility of operating the farm. Her experience in this respect was very limited, as Ralph, during his lifetime, was the practical farmer and she but the housewife. She had no relatives. She was practically alone in the world. 

There was no friend to advise her or to assist her, and the responsibility, therefore, was great and difficult. She struggled along the best she could, but it was too much for her strength and it told upon her health.

Now she lay upon the bed exhausted and sick. Her neighbors had been kind to her and had occasionally assisted her, but since she was confined to her bed, she had to depend largely on her little boy. He chopped the wood and attended to the chores as best he could and often he would be very tired. 

Tommy was nine years old, nearly ten. Edith called him 'Tommy, honey'. This was her favorite

MORANA CHAPTER II 

Angus McKenzie's farm was about a mile from Reynoldsville on the brow of a hill. Its high elevation afforded a fine view. Angus McKenzie had lived there for twenty years. He was a hard-working and penurious individual who had accumulated considerable wealth. In fact, he was considered one of the richest men in that neighborhood. 

When Angus McKenzie arrived in Reynoldsville, he was a young, robust, healthy Scotchman who was fairly good looking. He was energetic and thrifty and soon gained the respect of the people in that neighborhood. It was not long after his arrival that he met 

Grace Anason. It was thought to be 'love at first sight' for both of them. Grace Anason was a girl about twenty years of age and she was pretty and well educated. She came from one of the best families in the neighborhood . . . the Anason's on her father's side and the Morton's on her mother's side. 

It was with a good deal of satisfaction and elation that Angus McKenzie received the consent to marry her. She was wealthy, good looking and he was in love with her. 

That old adage that 'love never runs smooth' was prevalent. There was opposition to their marriage on the part of Grace's parents but when they found she was determined to marry Angus 'come weel or

19 come woe', they finally consented and Angus and Grace were married.

Angus rented one of the Anason's farms and with their assistance started off as a farmer. Before the year had gone by, the crash came! Mr. Anason had been involved in the bank and had signed some notes and when the bank failed, it took him down with it and he was practically bankrupt. The blow was more than he could stand and in a few months, he passed away, to be followed shortly after by his wife. This was a terrific blow to both Grace and Angus. Angus had looked forward to some day inheriting the wealth of the Anasons. So much did he take it to heart that he became morose and taciturn and soon his love seemed to have subsided and he treated Grace, his wife, with a coldness which nearly broke her heart. 

She grieved silently and many a night her pillow was wet with tears because of the actions of her husband, but never did she complain.
