INTRODUCTION IN THE FOLLOWING pages, Miss Mary M. Crawford of Lapwai gives an interesting account of her experiences of forty-one years among the Nez Perces Indians of Idaho. She knows whereof she writes, for she is a niece of Miss Sue L. McBeth, who was one year at Lapwai before the death in 

1874 of the Rev. Henry Harmon Spalding, pioneer missionary. Thus she succeeded to leadership of the Mission at Lapwai directly on the death of its founder. Six years after Miss Sue McBeth's arrival her sister, Miss Kate

C. McBeth, joined her and in 1895 their niece Mary, known to her friends as Miss Mazie Crawford, began her remarkable work. 

Miss Crawford has written these recollections as a contribution to the 

Centennial Celebration of the colorful arrival of Mr. and Mrs. Spalding at

Lapwai in 1836. I am glad to write an Introduction connecting the story with its thrilling past. On November 29, 1936 it will be one hundred years since the Rev. Henry Harmon Spalding and his wife, Eliza Hart Spalding, together with W. H. Gray, guided by a group of one hundred and twenty-five 

Nez Perces Indians, arrived in the Lapwai Valley. The three missionaries were in Dr. Marcus Whitman's party and had gone first to Dr. John Mc- 

Loughlin's settlement in Fort Vancouver on the Columbia River, arriving September 12th after a journey of seven months. Dr. Whitman located his home, planning to do his work five miles west of the present site of Walla Walla, Washington, about one hundred and twenty miles west of Lapwai, and then accompanied Spalding to the Clearwater River, where at the junction of Lapwai Creek he had selected his site on October 12th. The historymaking journey of Whitman and Spalding was the indirect result of the arrival in St. Louis in the winter of 1831 and 1832 of the four Nez Perces in search of Lewis and Clark whom they believed, when requested, would send teachers to the Nez Perces people.

Miss Crawford has lived forty-one years among the Indians in Idaho. 

At first she was employed by the government, then commissioned as a missionary by the former Woman's Board of Home Missions, later by the Board of National Missions. Yet the sparkle in her dark brown eyes is [ 4 ]

undimmed, the corners of her mouth irresistibly turn up, the brightest smile fairly transfigures her face and a lively interest in clothes and hairdressing keeps her deliciously human! She completely captured the hearts of those whom she met on a recent visit here. One could but wonder at the combination of child-like confidence and a trusting heart with the mature woman's head and seasoned judgment. She can do well the greatest variety of unrelated work, meet seemingly impossible situations, disagree with white people and Indians alike and yet hold the admiration and respect of all. The Nez Perces are intelligent, strong willed, logically reasoning men and women. There is no weakness nor indolent thinking among them! Miss Kate McBeth was emphasizing these characteristics to me one day when I finally said, “Well, Miss Kate, it is your stiff Calvinistic teaching that has deepened these inherent qualities in the people!" "Aha," replied she, "aha - maybe so - maybe so -."

Miss Crawford will allow these Indian people to talk to her as long as they wish. Seemingly she has unlimited time for each one needing her, for she always lets each talk the difficulty entirely out. Then she talks; they pray, and then each talks more and and eventually they see eye to eye.

The 

Nez Perces love and revere their women missionaries and trust them.

Walla Walla Presbytery, part of the present Presbytery of Northern 

Idaho, has been liberal in electing Indian pastors and elders as commissioners to the Presbyterian General Assembly. While automobiles were still in the experimental stage and none had as yet been seen in Northern Idaho, one of the Nez Perces preachers was a commissioner to the Assembly. In Chicago he was met by an elder friend with his car to take him to his home. Later my Nez Perces friend told me about his General Assembly experiences. He said that as he stepped into the car, he raised his heart in silent prayer to his Heavenly Father that, "if it be Thy will for me to die this way it is all right.” That man has been driving his own car now for many years!

An elder friend in a great eastern city who was entertaining one of the Indian pastors at another General Assembly meeting invited some friends in for dinner. Later, one of the guests said to the Nez Perces visitor that he was deeply interested in languages and would like to hear the Indian repeat the Lord's prayer in his own tongue. Whereupon the Nez 

Perces promptly said, “Oh, no— - no, I cannot pray for a show at all, at all; but if we could have a season of prayer and all worship our Heavenly 

Father, I would be glad to pray in my own tongue." [ 5 ]

My friend concluded his reminiscences by saying that before that group of dignified Presbyterian elders quite realized what they were doing they were all down on their knees worshiping their Father in Heaven. We were sitting in the room where the incident had occurred and my friend feelingly added that it had been for him a holy place ever since. Which reminds me of an experience I had with the Reverend James Hayes. Years later he was made a Doctor of Divinity, the first Indian pastor to be so honored in our church. He was describing the sign language saying that it was generally understood by all the Rocky Mountain and Coast Indians. "Yes," I agreed, "I realize the sign language is widely understood for bartering and trading, but is it sufficiently developed to convey spiritual truth?" "Yes it is," he added. Whereupon I asked him if he would be willing to interpret the Twenty-Third Psalm in the sign language. "Yes," he replied, and immediately stood up. With unforgettable grace and beauty he gestured so vividly and so convincingly that before I realized what I was doing I was quietly repeating to myself the Psalm line by line. 

He added that he had not found Indians who failed to understand the sign language.

The Indians are most devoted parents. Nothing is too good for the baby! They simply cannot deny babies anything. I was a guest in an 

Indian home, when at supper the baby boy cruelly burnt his mouth by grabbing his mother's cup of hot tea and drinking it. All that night, as I lay in the comfortable bed in the upstairs room, I could hear that father walking back and forth, back and forth carrying the little one in his strong arms, gently singing to him, comforting him in every possible way to ease the pain of a suffering baby. That man was a farmer who had worked all day in his fields.

At one of the first camp meetings I attended I wanted to ride. While petting my mount, the pony caught a good square look at me and with no warning whatever, reared, pulled the bridle so suddenly that he dragged me to the ground and galloped off. I picked myself up and asked the reason from an Indian nearby. With an absolutely grave face he replied, "Indian pony not like Boston hat; Boston hat scare Indian pony."

I was wearing a broad brimmed black straw hat in striking contrast to the gaily colored scarfs and shawls generally worn at that time by the 

Indian women. Everything not Nez Perces was "Boston" a hand-down from the old days of the Hudson Bay Company. I realized that the Indian 

[ 6 ]

-- was valiantly keeping an absolutely sober face, but when I laughed heartily at my own fall, he and the nearby group of half a dozen Indians joined me enthusiastically. Later at the great fourth of July banquet all manner of delicacies would be offered me, each with an amusing little speech connecting the food with the “wise” (?) Indian pony the "Boston hat" the "one who could laugh when she tumbled down". The variations on the theme were simply endless and would stand comparison with our keenest wit. Never believe that Indians are glum, dour, or long faced. You do not know them if you think so. 

Miss Kate McBeth, for thirty-six years a missionary among the Nez

Perces Indians, wrote a summary of the speech she made at the dedication of the new High School at Lapwai, not long before her death in 1914. In this address she tells such an interesting story of the early days that I reproduce part of it here: "In 1805 Lewis and Clark with their party of explorers came from Montana, over the Lolo trail and down to the place where Orofino now stands. There they dug their canoes from the cedar trees, left their horses with Chief Twisted Hair and floated down the Clearwater into the Snake River, then into the Columbia and on to the sea. The next Spring they returned and were guided by some Nez Perces up over Nez Perces prairie and down into the Kamiah Valley and spent about a month, until the snows melted from the Bitter Root mountains and allowed them to go on their way. During the time in Kamiah, they and the Nez Perces became great friends. Not long afterward the Nez Perces heard something about God and then they at once began to grope for the truth, but each year grew more and more perplexed and would say, 'If we could only find the trail of Lewis and Clark they would tell us the truth about God.' "Twenty-five years after Lewis and Clark had been with them, they sent out a delegation of four Nez Perces to seek for them. They followed the American Fur Company's trail and reached St. Louis where they found 

Clark, but Lewis had died several years before. Clark failed to give them the information they sought, but some other persons interested in them promised that a man would be sent to them with the White Man's Book, to teach them how to worship the true God. Only one of them lived to return to the Buffalo Country, Montana, where he met some of his own people who were there hunting, to whom he gave the message that a teacher would come, and so firmly did the Nez Perces believe this that each year they went out on the mountains expecting the promised one. Five [ 7 ]

years later, at the Green River rendezvous on the Rocky Mountains in Wyoming, they were overjoyed to meet Dr. and Mrs. Marcus Whitman, the Rev. Henry Harmon Spalding and his wife, Eliza Hart Spalding, and

Mr. W. H. Gray. Dr. Whitman established his mission at the mouth of the Mill Creek near Walla Walla, Washington, among the Cayuse Indians, while Mr. Spalding came on up the river 120 miles and established the mission at Lapwai. "The first of December 1836 a company of Indian men and women, on tired ponies, came up over Thunder Hill guiding their three white friends, Mr. and Mrs. Spalding and Mr. Gray down into the Lapwai Valley, a Nez Perces word, meaning 'the place where the butterfly dwells.' 

The Indians were wise as to location so their first camp was at the foot of Thunder Hill. Old Mrs. Lawyer took great pride in telling me about 

Mrs. Spalding, for she was one of the company who guided them and used to say, 'Mrs. Spalding was soon so wise about our language and had such a quiet heart.' "The Buffalo tent was at once put up and twenty-five days after a little log house was built. The logs were carried by men from the Clearwater 

River, taking twelve men to a log and there were four relays. As soon as possible Mr. Spalding began to give the people instruction in planting seeds and making their gardens. Here we see the first agricultural school of 

Idaho. The Indians were called from other parts of the reservation and 

Billy Williams, a young man from Kamiah, was there one day when Mr. 

Spalding pared a potato while he talked and then handed Billy a piece on the end of the knife. Billy tasted it and pronounced it 'taats' meaning 'good'. The next year Billy's garden was the talk of the whole tribe! It was wonderful! This caused a Potlatch girl who had rejected Billy the year before to change her mind and follow him up to Kamiah and take him for her husband. "The Spaldings were only one year or a little more at Thunder Hill when it was thought best to have the mission located on the Clearwater and they then moved down and built a larger house. While Mr. Spalding was teaching agriculture, Mrs. Spalding was giving the women instruction in what you would call domestic science, for she had her spinning wheel and knitting needles with her and she also taught them how to cook. "The school on the Clearwater was near where James Moses' house now stands, near the old gnarled apple trees. The school flourished and sometimes there were as many as two hundred pupils, men, women, and [ 8 ]

children. A lesson was put on the board for them and they would print it and recite it and all study aloud. Think of what a school that must have been! "In 1839 they had a pleasant surprise when a train of pack ponies drew up to the door bearing a printing press, a gift from the mission in Honolulu, and a native from that mission came with it to show them how to use it. How proud they were when primers printed on that press were handed around among the pupils. And this was the first printing press used on the Pacific coast. It is now in the Oregon Historical rooms in Portland."

I have used the portion of Miss Kate's address relating to events occurring prior to 1842. It was embellished with many a story. Miss Kate was a prince among story tellers one of the best I ever knew. She had her information in the Nez Perces language, largely from Billy Williams, who was considered the best historian of the tribe. 

It is little enough recognition to record here the names of the four whose quest resulted in the coming of Whitman and Spalding to the Pacific Northwest. I copy from Miss McBeth's diary: "Lapwai, Idaho, July 7th, 1894. Elder Billy Williams today gave me the following statement about the Four who went to St. Louis in search of the Bible, or Light.

First "Tip-ya-lah-na-jeh-nin” (Black or Speaking Eagle) was chief. He died in St. Louis. He was Kip-ka-pel-i-kan's grandfather or Pak-al-is. They went out by the Lolo Trail, the same road that Lewis and Clark came and went. 

Second-"Ka-ou-pu" (Man of the Morning or Daylight) was one of the two older ones. His mother was a Flathead, his father a Nez Perces. He died in or near St. Louis. 

Third-"Hi-youts-tohan" (Rabbit Skin Leggings) of the White Bird

Band, part Palouse Indian, was Speaking Eagle's brother's son. Yellow Bull is from the same band. This was one of the two young men. He was the only one to live to return to the Buffalo Country, Montana, where he met his own people, the Nez Perces. He told all about his visit and that the promised one would be sent with the Book. He never came back to his people in his own land. It is not known where he went. 

Fourth-"Ta-wis-sis-sim-nim" (No Horns on His Head, or little horns like an old buffalo) died on the road home, perhaps in Yellowstone Park. 

He was about twenty years old when he started. He was a doubter of old [9]

beliefs. Billy remembers him well. Billy was near ten years old then."

Miss Kate told me that there were descendants of the four connected with some one of the churches. Billy was one of the party which accompanied the four a few days' march on their journey.

In her book, "The Nez Perces Since Lewis and Clark," Miss Kate Mc- Beth states that the first Presbyterian church in the Oregon Territory was organized August 18, 1838, at Waiilatpu (place of rye grass), Dr. Whitman's station among the Cayuse Indians, about seven miles from where the present city of Walla Walla is located. The pastor of this church in the wilderness was Rev. H. H. Spalding and the elder chosen was Dr. Marcus 

Whitman. It was Resolved, "That this church be governed on the Congregational plan, but attached to the Bath Presbytery of New York." 

The Indians crowded into the Whitman house and watched every particular most intently Miss Kate says, "What a strange looking company filled that room! Leggings, moccasins and the cover-all blanket or skin of some animal; shells in their ears; hatless, their heavy braids falling in front. 

So were the men. The women had their skin slips on, fringed with the same around the bottom; few ornament for them. The baby boards were covered with beads, shells and bangles. The consecrated missionaries had no eyes for these queer things, but were thinking of the time when the Spirit would plow up the soil and give life to the seed. We love to look back through the years, some of them dark, and trace the blessings which have come from the doings of that day. And still there is more to follow. "What a visit that must have been to Mrs. Spalding, who had not seen the face of a white woman for nearly two years, when they went down to see the Whitmans. How those two dear friends, Mrs. Whitman and Mrs.

Spalding, who had travelled for months together, would talk as they worked for sure I am that Mrs. Spalding did not sit with folded hands in that busy home at Waiilatpu. They strengthened each other's hearts by telling over God's care for them, and of His presence with them in their homes and in their work. Their discouragements were many, but back of them His promises. All too soon the time came when the Spaldings were obliged to return to Lapwai."

Dr. Clifford M. Drury, pastor of the Presbyterian Church of Moscow, Idaho, in his excellent book "Henry Harmon Spalding - Pioneer of Old

Oregon" (published by the Caxton Press of Caldwell, Idaho) has told the story of those thrilling pioneer days. He shows how Spalding introduced agriculture in his efforts to settle the Indians on the land, growing the first [ 10 ]

apple tree, building the first grist mill and persisting with devoted zeal in his evangelistic labors. Whitman's famous winter ride he feels was undertaken primarily to change the decision of the American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions which would have closed the station. The story of Dr. Whitman's interview with the statesmen in Washington is recounted. Cayuse Indians massacred Marcus and Narcissa Whitman on 

November 29, 1847, together with eleven others. Spalding narrowly escaped with his life.

The Spaldings later settled in the Willamette Valley. Then for a generation the darkness of Indian war spread over the whole country. When peace finally came and Mr. Spalding, whose heart had always been in the 

Nez Perces country, returned as a missionary in 1871, he found the Christians had continued to sing the old hymns and read the gospel of Matthew, translated by him years before and printed on the old press. But during the period from 1847-1871, when they had no regular missionary, their Christianity became mixed with old heathenism and the Nez Perces say it was like beginning all over again. 

But by this time Mr. Spalding was an old man, with a strong spirit but enfeebled body. He welcomed Miss Sue L. McBeth who, at his death in 

1874, succeeded him and went to Kamiah to live. 

Miss Sue L. McBeth was an exceptional Latin and Greek scholar, a successful college teacher, and later a missionary among the Choctaws. In intellectual acumen she was a giant; in Christian experience, faith and trust she was as a little child busy about her Heavenly Father's business. 

Miss Kate joined her sister in 1879 and worked among the women until after Miss McBeth's death, when she assumed the leadership of the training class. Miss Kate then had for her assistants her nieces, Miss Mary and Miss 

Elizabeth Crawford, who developed and greatly enlarged the work among the women and young people. After Miss Kate's death, Miss Mazie conducted the training class to the satisfaction of the people and the Presbytery of Walla Walla, and her sister, Miss Elizabeth, spent her time in the homes and among the young people and children. The training class was closed in 

1932 and since then Miss Mazie Crawford has been working as hard as but spending more time among the young people and children at the great government sanitorium for Indian boys and girls from all tribes. ever,

Miss Alice C. Fletcher, who was allotting agent under the government, at the time that she taught ethnology in Harvard, in her introduction to Kate McBeth's "The Nez Perces Since Lewis and Clark" published by the [11]

Fleming H. Revell Co. in 1908 testified to the kind of citizenship training the McBeth sisters had given the Nez Perces long before they were citizens and paints this realistic picture of their work.-"When in 1889 I went to the 

Nez Perces reservation, charged by the government with the duty of carrying out the provisions of the 'Severalty Act' and to give to each man, woman and child a share in their inherited tribal lands, I had the privilege of knowing the Misses McBeth in the field where they had so unselfishly labored. Much could be written about the wise way in which they had builded, for they had sought to make the individual strong, not only through his personal belief and hopes, but through his family life and his responsibility to the community. He had been taught Christian living as well as Christian thinking, and that his life must be one in thought, in speech and deed. He had been instructed also in the history and laws of our country. So when the talk of allotting the land in severalty began, it became evident that only among those who had been under the teaching of the Misses McBeth could the law be intelligently explained and accepted. The sisters had prepared their pupils to understand the importance of citizenship to the Christian 

Indians. . . . (Note-The italics are mine.-JULIA FRASER.) 

... "In their cabin, the Misses McBeth had lived for years bereft of comfort, exposed to cold and heat, forgetting themselves and ministering to others. 

Here the elder sister battled with disease and physical weakness and wrought her work within the hearts of men that widens with years in its far-reaching influence. . . . "I hear again from a camp of Indians halted for the night on their way to the mines, their ponies laden with produce to sell to the miners, the sound of family prayer and song wafted over the hills as the shadows fall and again the sounds of the early morning worship before they resume their journey over the mountain trails, float over to where I am listening. Nor can I forget the courtly courtesies of Indian men and women, nor their generous help and cordial support in many a difficult experience. "The reasonableness of the Nez Perces, their willingness to look upon a new aspect of a subject, their teachableness, and their patience were evidenced during the four years I was among them. They have gifts of mind and heart which cannot fail to make welcome citizens of our common country."

For more than sixty years four women of the McBeth family heroically and successfully have carried on a definite policy of intensive training of preachers, of throwing prominence and leadership upon them, and particu- [12]

larly of holding each absolutely responsible for his own work and yet themselves seldom if ever speaking to the Indian congregations. The amount of work these women have accomplished is incredible. Words utterly fail to picture realities as I know them. 

One of Miss Sue McBeth's early students and the first Nez Perces preacher, Robert Williams, was ordained in 1879.

The records reveal there have been eighteen trained Indian pastors-sixteen licensed and ordained by the Presbytery of Walla Walla, one by the Presbytery of Seattle, one by the Presbytery of Spokane and one now ready for ordination. No statistics were kept of the number trained for church officers. But elders, trustees, Sunday School teachers, officers of the Women's Missionary Societies, of Christian Endeavor and the Temperance groups had regular times for intensive normal instruction for the duties for which they were responsible.

Another great asset, not only for the student class but for the entire Christian work among the tribe has been the remarkable singing voice of Miss Crawford. A high, clear and absolutely true soprano, it could readily be distinguished in all chorus work. Miss Kate and I would smile to each other as we would recognize Mazie's voice. Often Miss Kate would nod and say, "Ah-but our Nez Perces have fairly sung themselves into the 

Kingdom-and my Mazie has taught them how to sing!"

The remarkable bond of Christian friendship which has always existed between the supporting constituency and the Nez Perces Mission is a joy to remember. Not only the members of the McBeth family and in recent years other missionaries who have been associated with the mission, but the Indian pastors themselves have always been most welcome guests in our homes from the Pacific as far eastward as they ever travelled. The McBeths not only taught theology, but correct table manners as well. Frequently I have been a guest in Nez Perces homes and never had more considerate or courteous hosts. My personal contacts with the mission extend over a period of more than thirty-five years.

The Synod of Idaho, the Presbytery of Walla Walla and now the Presbytery of Northern Idaho have always been most sympathetic and helpful to the Nez Perces. Without their strong support and moral backing, results might not have been as constructive and permanent. Friends of the Nez 

Perces most gratefully appreciate this Christian courtesy and cooperation.

Chapter I FORTY-ONE YEARS IN RETROSPECT 

WHEN I GO OVER MY forty-one years among the Nez Perces Indians, the time seems very short. Yet, when I recall some of the details, remembering the struggles that tested my mettle but were good for me, I would almost shrink from going through it again. How worth while the struggles have been! How glorious the results!

One time when we were going through a local option election, working hard and pulling some righteous wires in a righteous way in order to outwit the enemy, and losing some sleep over it, too, I came in one evening all tired out and said to my sister and niece, "My, but I will be glad when this is all over." They both laughed and one of them said to me, “Oh, now you know that you love a scrap." This relieved the tension and I laughed with them. 

Aunt Kate McBeth used to tell me this story. When she was about to start for Idaho in 1879, to come to this mission field, much farther away than it seems now, her home church in Wellsville, Ohio, arranged a farewell meeting in the church. The ministers from the surrounding region were invited. Several of them, in their talks, spoke very feelingly of the sacrifice that she was making to go to such a far away land to work among Indians. 

Near the last, Dr. Beatty of Steubenville, Ohio, an old man, rose and was almost ready to stamp his foot as he said, “Brethren, what are you talking about when you say sacrifice, sacrifice! She is not making any sacrifice for you well know that the Lord is going to make up to her far more than she can ever give up and will bless her in it." My aunt used to say very reverently, "And so He has."

Sometimes when people, particularly in the east, say with a look of amazement, "And you live among Indians!" I reply, "Why yes, that is the best place in the world to live." The next question usually is, "Aren't you afraid?" to which I answer, "No, not half so much afraid of the wildest 

Indian as I am of some white people."

Sometimes they talk to me, too, of sacrifice and with sorrowful faces you have left your home and all that you hold dear. How could say, “But [14]

you do it?” I am always glad to tell them, with a smile on my face, “I am not deserving of any sympathy, for while I love my own folks just as much as ever, I have only enlarged my family circle, which has added much to the richness of my life, so that the gain is mine after all." 

I have learned much of value from the Nez Perces. Instead of wanting to leave I rejoice to stay on with them, having lived a happy life among them.

Some people think that all missionaries have to do is to go and tell the beautiful gospel story so that the Indians will repent at the first invitation and live happily ever after. I know that much of a missionary's life consists of doing everyday things and trying to do them well. But life among the Nez Perces offers a continued mental test to those who work among them. There is plenty of excitement, not only from Indians but because of their contact with the outside world and the government. They are in closer touch with Washington and know the commissioner of Indian Affairs and some political leaders better than some white people know the President. They read the press and know what is going on in the world. 

Unless the missionary is up-to-date and able to teach the Indians what they want to know or what will be of use to them, he or she will surely be found in the rear of the procession. 

Nez Perces men, women and even children have been taught to take charge of and conduct their own meetings. Older women who have had no English education can conduct a meeting for organization and do it perfectly according to Hoyle, or rather Roberts' Rules of Order. 

One time the government sent around a woman to organize Mothers' Clubs. She went into Kamiah expecting to conduct the business meeting and effect the organization, but some of our women stepped to the front, appointed a temporary chairman and organized it without any help from outside. Not long ago when an extension worker from the University of Idaho came here to organize a cooking club she said, "I know you Nez Perces women can do your own organizing so you may now take charge." Our women proceeded to elect officers and carry it on and were just as much at home as if they were in their own kitchens. I have heard them criticize white people who are careless about parliamentary rules. One day they asked to be allowed to make up the menu of roots, and served five different kinds. 

How the extension worker enjoyed it! They were roots used for food in the olden days.

Nez Perces Indian women are proud of the fact that the Lapwai Mis- [15]

sionary Society was the first to be organized in the State of Idaho, 1891, for almost as long they have had missionary organizations in all the other five churches. What fine workers they have always been, taking all responsi bility for carrying on the activities! I have been astonished at their ability to get up and give the best talks with but little or no preparation beforehand. 

One time after my aunt had been giving them a lesson about the Holy Spirit three of the older women, one right after the other, followed, carrying out the thought and enlarging upon it, keeping close to the text. They love 

Bible missionary stories better than any others, and apply them to mission fields in our own and in other lands. 

They always send delegates to presbyterials and take their places on the honor roll for meeting their apportionments. They have written reports and greatly enjoy the meetings.

Since the closing of the training class, I have had a group of men who study during the winter from nine to twelve each morning five days a week, not to prepare for the ministry, but for sheer desire to know more of the Word. I am supposed to be the leader, but sometimes I feel that I am being led in spiritual things.

The Indians have a simple faith in God and His Word. When they ask questions it is never in the way of criticism. On a certain occasion one chose the book of Revelation for study and translation. Nez Perces enjoy this book. 

I will often find them reading it when they are sick. It doesn't seem so hard. to them. One day when we had been reading it, we came to the word "para dise." The question was asked: "Where is paradise?" I had always been careful to explain our idea of the word so that they would not think that after death there was an "in between" place. I was startled for a minute, but before I could answer he said, "My people, when they are dying, sometimes see beautiful things just before the breath leaves them: it may be an open door, or angels, or a beautiful city. All do not see them, but some do."

When another was reading Revelation and came to the verse, “And they sung as it were a new song, and no one could learn that song but the redeemed," he stopped and said, "I want to tell you something. You know I was very sick a few years ago, and for a while they thought I could not get well. One night when I was very low I had a dream, or a vision, and thought the Lord Jesus came to me. I could not see Him clearly for the light seemed dim, but He said 'Come with me.' He took me on a journey and we came to a place where I heard such beautiful singing as I never heard before. The 

Lord said to me, 'You lead the song.' I at first told Him that I could not, That now & itir [16]

but He said, 'Do this for Me,' and He helped me. It must have been the song that 'no one could learn but the redeemed'."

One day we had been studying about the Dead Sea, how it was so salty that nothing could live in its waters. Then we talked about the freshness of the Sea of Galilee. When I told them that it was full of fish, one of the men asked, "How did the fish get there?"

I was startled at first for I hadn't thought much about it. But I said, "The Jordan river is fresh from its source, as fresh water is always coming from above." But he said, "Fish do not come from the source, but from the mouth of the streams. We know how the fish fight their way up stream over every obstacle; they do not come from above. If they can't come from the Dead Sea, where did they come from?" He quickly answered his query by saying, "God must have planted them there long ago." He was perfectly satisfied and so was I!

Indians from other tribes like to join the Nez Perces in their camp meeting at Talmaks. One year nine tribes were represented, speaking almost as many languages. The address of some would have to go through two or three interpreters.

When the Rev. James Hayes went as an evangelist to other tribes, he would preach each evening, but through the day he would go among the old people and preach the Gospel to them in the Indian sign language, the only speech which they had in common. [17]

Chapter II ARRIVAL AND EARLY EXPERIENCES IT WAS A BEAUTIFUL DAY, March 16, 1895, when I stepped from the Snake 

River boat at Lewiston, Idaho, named after Lewis of the Lewis and Clark Expedition in 1805. It was almost the end of my journey from East Liverpool, Ohio, my girlhood home, to this land of the northwest.

I had been travelling four days on the Union Pacific railroad, from Chicago to Riparia, followed by a seventy-five mile trip up the Snake River, which forms the boundary between Idaho on the east and Oregon and Washington on the west. However, the time had not seemed long. The latter part of the journey we had the company of the Idaho supreme court justices and an ex-governor on board. We realized the spirit of friendliness and hospitality that is typical of the West. 

The best experience of the journey came when I stepped from the gangplank and met my sister, Elizabeth, who had spent a year and a half with my aunt Kate at Lapwai and Mount Idaho. We walked up through Lewiston with its one street of small buildings, the roofs of which were mostly moss covered. The team and hack from the government school met me and we were soon out on the rolling prairie, driving down Soldiers' Canyon, and out into the beautiful Lapwai valley, perhaps a half mile wide. At the other end the government Indian school buildings were gleaming white in the distance.

My aunt Kate welcomed me to her home, McBeth Mission, built two years before by Mrs. William Thaw of Pittsburgh, who gave the deed to the 

Presbyterian Board of Home Missions with the understanding that my aunt was to live in it as long as she chose whether employed by the Board or not. 

After my aunt's death, Mrs. Thaw was to make the same provision for me. 

How independent it made her feel! She used to smile and say, "I feel better than if I owned it for I will never have any trouble about whom I shall leave it to."

Being among Indians was a new experience to me. My childhood had been spent where once powerful tribes roamed the river hills of the Ohio, [18]

and floated down the slowly moving streams. Now the only traces left of them were the hieroglyphics cut in the rocks along the river banks. Nez 

Perces later thought pictures of them were the sign of the "wy-ya-kin” or attending spirit, connected with their pagan worship. 

It was not long till I was quite familiar, not only with the Indian faces, which at first all looked alike to me, but the Indian boys and girls and workers in the government school for Nez Perces, which joined the mission grounds.

I was constantly under the eyes of the Nez Perces ministers and elders, who were often coming to my aunt's home. Indians are great readers of character, oftentimes keeping their opinions to themselves. I was quite relieved when, after due deliberation, one of the elders later said, "Miss Kate, she has a friendly face."

I went to church with my aunt and heard the Indian minister preach so eloquently and fluently in his own language that I bowed before their reverence for the things for the spirit. Although I did not understand a single word, the gestures were expressive to me. As most of the singing was in the native tongue my mouth had to be closed, but I could make myself useful in helping to lead in the English hymns for there was then no organ in the church. As a girl I remembered how my aunt Kate, after she knew that she was to work among Indian people in Idaho, came to our home, which was quite musical. Sitting at the organ and patiently picking out the notes, she learned to play hymns with one hand. She knew that she would have to lead the Nez Perces in their song worship. As she was not musical it was little wonder that she was glad when I came.

I soon fitted into the life at the mission and the nearby government school. My aunt had organized the Christian Endeavor, almost the first society in the Northwest, among the young Indian men and women who were in the government boarding school. A few years ago a "Community 

Hall" was opened at the mission. One night when they were having a 

Christian Endeavor meeting the name of the hall was to be chosen. One of the men rose and said, “I would like to have it named 'McBeth Hall' for Miss Kate, for she organized the first Christian Endeavor among us when we were in the government school. We young folks were the officers and 

I was the first president. Let us call it 'McBeth Hall'." The vote was unanimous. The name was very fitting for the organ belonged to my aunt and the stove, tables, chairs and the Bible were hers also. The organ was a 

Mason & Hamlin which she brought with her from Ohio in 1879. [19]

My aunt handed over to my care the government school Christian Endeavor on Sabbath night as well as the Bible story hour on Wednesday eve ning. There have been few evenings missed, either Christian Endeavor or story hour, in all these forty years at the government school. It was first a school for two hundred Nez Perces, then later a sanitorium for tubercular Indian children from many tribes. At one time sixty-five tribes were represented. When Mr. McConville, the government superintendent, offered me a position in the school I worked among the Nez Perces children, closely associated with them for four years. In 1899 I left the government service to become a missionary under the Presbyterian Board of Home Missions. 

As my aunt was then training a class of Indian men for the ministry, I took over her duties of going into the homes and working in the churches. 

I spent most of the time in the Lapwai church because that one was nearest the mission. The other five churches were scattered over a territory fifty by seventy-five miles. I could not go to them very often for in those days we travelled by wagon. It would take us two days hard travelling, camping overnight, to go from the mission in Lapwai. climbing the mountain and journeying over thirty-five miles of prairie before going down the long, winding hill into Kamiah. Some years later we thought it very fine when the railroad was built up the river to Kamiah and we could go in about four hours. Now with good highways we can reach by automobile our farthest away church at Stites, seventy-five miles distant, in less than three hours and have the car to use there. The automobiles save us much time and energy but when we have blow-outs or punctures the Indians laugh when I say, “I wish that I had a horse."

I visited the homes and soon was acquainted with the families, always finding a welcome, for was I not "Miss Katenim eighth?" In their language "nim" is the sign of the possessive, "eighth" (we spell by sound), is niece.

With my aunt I visited the sick. I listened as she read the Bible in their own tongue, heard her start the Nez Perce hymns, "Holy Spirit, Faithful

Guide" being one of her favorites. However ill the patient was, there would always be a movement of smoothing the hair or some getting ready for worship, then a joining in the hymn, even if the voices were weak. Sometimes it would be only a whisper.

On one occasion when I was attending evangelistic meetings in the church at Stites one of the elders, an old man, was sick in his home a few miles up the river. An Indian man took me to see him. He was very ill. As it was hard to keep warm he had a rather heavy woolen cap on his head. Nez [20]

Perces are always careful to remove the head covering when entering a house or in worship. He lay there seemingly unconscious but when the man who went with me said, “Now we will have worship," up went elder Jim's poor swollen hand, so swollen you could hardly tell that it was a hand, and he managed somehow to remove his cap. To him it was God's house. 

I was learning many things in those days. Once when I was walking with aunt Kate to make a visit I asked as we walked along, "What makes the white men marry Indian women?" She was never sentimental about

Indians and believed marriages with their own people were best for both races. She drew herself up with dignity and said, "Oh, sometimes the 

Indian women are superior." I never asked again.

Occasionally now when there is a mixed marriage white people are inclined to bewail the fact that a white girl has married an Indian. Although 

I do not favor it, I am apt to say, "You need not be so troubled about that, for the Indians are just as much opposed to it as you are." 

My aunt might not have been so frank, but one summer evening we were sitting on the porch when a man riding by used awful oaths. She knew that it was a white man and said to me privately, "Aren't Indians superior? There are no oaths in their language. If an Indian swears, and very few of them do, they must do it in a borrowed language." 

I was able to help the Indian young people with their music, although I had nothing beyond an ordinary musical education. My father loved music and understood it. He used to be the precentor in our country church and started the hymns with a tuning fork. He taught "singing schools" in the country during the winter at different schoolhouses. Musical people were welcome visitors in our home. How we used to enjoy the visits of Will L. 

Thompson, who was publishing his own songs and hymns at the time, such as "Come Where the Lilies Bloom," "Drifting With the Tide," "Softly and Tenderly," and many other of his compositions which Indians love. I used to attend all the singing schools near us. Not thinking of the benefit it might be later to me, I went more for the fun and the "recess" but I absorbed some of its values. Later I had more instruction, not much. I know of no part of my training that has been more helpful, and am always insisting that our Boards give every missionary as much musical training as possible.

I do not know what I would have done on the mission field without music. Indians love to sing and most of them have very good voices. In their churches they sing more than white people do; they sing in their [21]

homes and at family worship: they sing everywhere. They sing by the hour and never seem to tire. Once when they had been having evangelistic meetings one of the young men told my aunt that after they had sung all evening in the service they went out and gathered in a home and sang their own native songs a couple of hours longer. She said to him, “David, you should not do that. You will wear your throats out and weaken your lungs." His face clouded up and he said very sorrowfully, "Now, Miss Kate, you are against the worship!"

Before they became Christians and were taught the songs of the Gospel their singing was a weird chant in a minor key. The song usually began in a higher key and gradually came down the scale a few notes to the lower tone and then went up again. They had songs of war, and songs or sorrow, but there was no joy in anything they sang. It was little wonder that the joyous songs of redemption and freedom charmed them.

When Mr. Spalding brought them the Gospel in 1836 he not only translated the Gospel of Matthew into their own language but translated some hymns into their own tongue. He taught the Indians to sing them, always being careful that every hymn was of Jesus the Savior. He wrote hymns for special occasions such as Christmas and Easter and for the burial of a child or adult.

Later when my aunt Sue McBeth came she added to these hymns and improved their singing somewhat. As the ministers were trained they, too, added hymns to the collection. Then younger men began to translate hymns, which was a difficult task, sometimes taking days. They would sing to music just as correctly as any white. But when they began to sing a new song in church, where they do not use books, the stronger voices of the older people in almost every case would shade the hymn off into a minor key until, to us, it could hardly be recognized. 

Mr. Spalding translated a hymn for Christmas, "O Bethlehem," which to this day is used in their Christmas programs. As the old people left us one by one there were fewer voices to join in this old hymn. A few years ago our church choir of young people, who sing mostly in English, decided to learn Mr. Spalding's old Christmas hymn. When "O Bethlehem” was announced, the few old people began to sing it, a voice here and there, till the young people's choir lifted their voices. The faces of the old people brightened to think that the younger ones were learning the old hymns in their own language and would continue to sing them in the days and years to come. [22]

Much has been made of the singing among the Nez Perces in Sunday schools. They would literally wear a song book out in about three years, singing almost every song in it. They thought that money could not be spent in any better way than by buying new song books. As a result they have bought many of the song books which have come out in later years. 

The "Sing" on Tuesday night long ago became an institution at Mc- Beth Mission, when the students and their wives and the young Indian folks of the neighborhood came in to sing. They may sing new songs from their hymn book or practice music for special occasions. This year they practiced Handel's "The Hallelujah Chorus" for their Christmas program. 

Because of this special work in music the Nez Perce Indian high school students were chosen to give the music at the baccalaureate service a few years ago in our local high school, as well as at commencement, with an 

Indian boy as pianist.

The great time of the year is at the camp meeting, when the six churches gather on one of their timber reserves and camp together for two weeks. White people have called it “a great Chautauqua." The Nez Perces secure and pay for a musical director who trains the choirs of the six churches in union work. They give a sacred cantata quite creditably on the closing night. In this way they are developing the older voices and each year training new ones.

As the people sang their own native hymns in their homes, or in church, of course many who were not Christians would learn them and themselves begin to sing. The hymns carried the message to heathen as well as Christian and men were led to believe on Him through the ministry of music. [23]

Chapter III CAMP MEETINGS, PAST AND PRESENT 

Taken in part from "The Nez Perces Since Lewis and Clark" by Miss Kate C. McBeth (Fleming H. Revell Company, 1908).

THEY SAY IT WAS King George's men (Hudson Bay Fur Traders) who taught the Nez Perces about the Fourth of July camps. During the time 

Mr. Spalding was away, from 1847 to 1871, they gathered to celebrate the 

Fourth, not for one day but for two weeks, and it was a time of drinking, gambling, horse racing and changing of wives.

It was into such a camp in Kamiah, in June, 1870, that the Lord sent four Yakima Indian ministers to preach His word and through His power the camp became a place of repenting and turning to Him. It is still called the "Place of Weeping." It was near the ground upon which Lewis and 

Clark camped in 1806 and not far from where later the Northern Pacific Railway depot was built. Was there such power in these Yakimas? Were they so gifted? No, acquaintance with the leading one in after years convinced me that the Lord had used a very feeble instrument to accomplish a great work. In the Lord's time he fulfilled his promise, "My word shall not return unto me void." Later Mr. Spalding was sent back under the

Presbyterian Board of Foreign Missions to gather in the sheaves, and baptized several hundred people.

After Christianity came among them they forsook the heathen camp; they had to, and the Christians of each church either camped beside their own worshiping place or had union services with some of the other churches.

In 1888 there was a Boston agent in Lapwai who loved the spectacular, wanted to see the Nez Perces gathered together in one camp, invited the Christians to camp with the heathen and make one grand display. He said to the Christian, “You can camp across the creek and still have your wor ship there, but it must not interfere with the gambling, horse-racing and the dancing on this side, of those who do not believe as you do, that it is all sin." It was a most severe testing time for they had been brought up to obey the agent. They came to my aunt with troubled hearts saying, “What [24]

will we do?" She, in her stern Scotch way said, "I do not know what you will do, but as for me, I will not set foot in that camp ground; not even on the Sabbath day when they have the Lord's Supper, for it will be like trampling the Lord's body under the feet of the racehorses." But the elders said, "We must go with our people to keep them from going down into the sins of old heathenism." So at the appointed time they pitched their tents according to the agent's direction. My aunt sat still in her house for she had told them she would not go. They could call her before the session if they wanted to.

She stayed out alone for several years; the elders, Sunday school superintendent and Christians were all in there. It took them a year or two to see what that vile camp was doing to their church. The elders would tell her of the awfulness of the sin in the camp and how the Christians were being gradually sucked down into it. She would say, "Get out of that, every one of you, or you will all go down to death in that sin and the church will be destroyed." However, it took several years for them to realize it and then to see how to break away from it. Finally the elders and Sunday school superintendent stayed out with my aunt. So dearly did they love the camping and coming together for a good time that at the Fourth of July they would come and camp beside my aunt's house, not only for company but for the strength and courage which she gave them to stand up against the things that had been so attractive to them in their old life. 

Finally, in 1897, their hearts were so greatly troubled that the elders went to presbytery to ask the white brethren to help them make a separation. The church at Lapwai, or Spalding was then without a minister so the responsibility must fall upon the three good elders. The men of the presbytery encouraged them and Dr. Gunn, Synodical Missionary, wrote a letter to the church which closed with the text: "Wherefore come out from among them, and be ye separate, saith the Lord, and touch not the unclean thing, and I will receive you. I will be a Father unto you, and ye shall be My sons and daughters saith the Lord almighty.” (2 Corinthians 

6: 17, 18.)

Since the church was without a pastor at the time, Reverend James Hayes was called from his church in Kamiah to read the letter from the presbytery to the congregation. After an appropriate sermon the letter was read and the people heard it in silence. An elder rose and said, “We are going to have our own Christian camp, and we expect every Christian to be there, but if you choose to go to the heathen camp stay there; there [25]

must be no crossing over, not even to the Lord's Supper on Sabbath day. 

But when the service was over and they were on the outside there was much talk both for and against, among the people. One of the leaders of the wild ones mounted his pony and rode around the church announcing that all the Christians were invited to the heathen camp for there would be worship service there and closed by saying, "No one has any right to divide the people; the Lord will do that when He comes." The same 

Yakima minister who preached the gospel to them in that old time heathen camp in Kamiah had weakened in the intervening years and he came back to hold worship with the heathen. 

But the battle was on. I remember James Hayes riding up to my aunt's gate, the morning after the announcement that there would be a Christian camp and saying, "Miss Kate, surely the Lord's coming must be near; I have never seen anything like this" Feeling was running high; it was not only dividing the tribe into heathen and Christian bands, but it was running the line right through families where there were both Christian and heathen. It was almost separating husbands and wives, for some of the unbelieving husbands said to their wives, "You may go to the Christian camp if you want to and I will get another wife.” For the next two weeks, until time to go into camp, the strife was so fierce that it seemed as though it was a hand to hand conflict with the devil. Just who, and how many of the Christians would be able to stand the test, which was the hardest one I ever saw any people put to. Imagine running that line through. white churches! But at the appointed time the elders and the committee, which had been appointed, pitched their tents and began preparations by hauling wood and tent poles for the camp from the mountains twenty miles away, for the visiting brethren. A great worship tent was put up and nicely seated and lighted, with chairs and lamps from the government school, and the worship began. 

How happy the people were when one day a long procession of Nez Perces people on ponies, with pack trains, came into camp. It was the ministers and people from the other churches. What a load was lifted from our hearts. It seemed as though we were living in Nehemiah's time and that these faithful servants had heard the sound of the trumpet at the weak point, and heard the cry, "Resort ye hither unto us." The other churches were not greatly troubled with the heathenism, but Lapwai was the stronghold of evil, having not only the evils of the Indians but that of the incoming civilization of the white people as well. [26]

On the fourth of July there was great excitement in the heathen camp. 

They were to have a sham battle and a parade. They were all morning getting ready, bedecking themselves and their horses with paint, donning feathers, war bonnets and beads. It was a most gorgeous display; their recent payments making this possible. Just about noon the word was carried to the Christian camp that, as an insult to the Christians, they were to lead the procession, which meant all their old heathenism, in and through the Christian camp. The Christians received the news in silence. 

The procession was already in sight; the lane was full from fence to fence with yelling, painted, almost naked Indians riding horseback. 

Just at that time seven Christian men rode out from the Christian camp and turned their horses heads and thus formed a line across the road, facing the oncoming multitude. When they came up to the seven 

Christian men they halted them and said, "You will go no further to insult God's people." The leaders of that procession were determined to pass. There were angry speeches from the balked riders and firm but decided words from the seven men, who never wavered, till finally the whole multitude turned and rode the other way. My aunt, who had been in the house on her knees, rose and said, "Surely the day of miracles is not yet past."

That winter the Lord poured out such a blessing on the Lapwai church as it had never seen before, and many were added to it. It had been weighed in the balance and not found wanting. From this testing time the Spalding church has steadily grown in strength and is one of the best working churches among the Nez Perces today. 

The next year the same battle, Christianity against heathenism, was to be fought over again, the lines clearly drawn. The mission ground was just between the two camps. We could hear the beat of the tom toms, the war drums and dancing on one side and the hymns of the worshipers on the other. At the end of the second year many of the Christians not strong enough to pitch their tents with the Christians the first year had grown stronger and were in their places with the Christians the second year. It was decided that now the Spalding church was able to stand alone, either to stay at home and stay out of the heathen camp or go with the Christians gathering at some one of the other churches. The campmeeting then went the rounds of the churches, all Christians gathering in one place and having their worship and good times away from temptation. [27]

In they were camped with the Second Kamiah church. In recent years the reservation had been thrown open; little towns of white people had sprung up among them, many of them godless, caring neither for the worship or the Sabbath day, always putting up some ungodly counter attraction on Sabbath, sometimes as close as possible to the Christians. Then much of the land had been fenced up and it was difficult to get pasture for the hundreds of horses belonging to the Christians. At the camping time each year it was very hot in the valleys. Dr. Ghormley, our synodical missionary, and I were invited to elder Felix Corbett's home for Sabbath day dinner and it was while there that Dr. Ghormley suggested that they search for a camping place on their mountain timber reserves, away from "wild whites", where it would be cool with abundant pasture. 

It was suggested to the united session. They at once appointed a committee, a minister and elder from each of the six churches, to go the next week and select a place. They took their wives with them, making it a camping party. They were gone all week, visiting one place after another but there was the drawback of insufficient water. The springs were unsuitable or the ground was rough. But when they came to Talmaks, which means "A Butte on the Prairie", their search was ended. It was a beautiful place having an altitude of about four thousand feet. There were groves of great pines with grass and wild flowers growing up to the roots of the trees, then groves of smaller pines and great open spaces, like fields. Best of all was a large, never failing spring of the coldest of water. When the location was announced in their churches the next Sabbath one old elder said, "Oh, I'm glad it is at Talmaks for there are such sweet smelling things; pines, flowers and shrubbery there."

The Nez Perces organized a Campmeeting Board with twenty-four members. They made the posts, secured the wire and enclosed six hundred and forty acres which provided abundant pasture for the four or five hundred horses. It took several weeks' work before it was finished. They made out a program, secured a Bible teacher, and musical director, paying them from their own funds, and Talmaks was formally opened in 1910. They borrowed a big worship tent that year and later bought a new one which would seat several hundred people. They have carried on the camp ever since, taking the whole responsibility for the program, also the responsibility for the order and safety of the camp. It is carefully guarded at night to keep out bootleggers or drunken men. The expense is paid by the Nez Perces. [28]

The services begin with a sunrise prayermeeting. There is a children's service from eight to ten o'clock which is followed by the Bible class. We have had some of the best Bible teachers of our church. At one o'clock the musical director meets the singers in the tent to practice the sacred cantata. 

The campmeeting owns its own piano and we use the little folding organ for special practices. The practice lasts till two-thirty o'clock and some special service or prayermeeting follows. From four till seven o'clock the young people have recreations: volley ball, basket ball, croquet and play many of their own games. Song service is at seven o'clock followed by a sermon, Christian Endeavor or temperance meeting. They have an oration on the morning of July fourth, a children's and young people's patriotic program in the afternoon and later games out on the playground. 

On one day they have a great dinner, all eating together in the big dining room. A committee is appointed and everything is so carefully planned that it goes off like clock-work. Most of the food has been prepared on the big range, nine feet long with two fire boxes and two ovens, which was given to them by the government agent, Mr. Lipps, a few years ago. Their food room is screened in and the shelves are loaded with pies and cakes and there are large tables from which to serve other food. The dining room seats about one hundred fifty people, twelve at a table. The young people serve as waiters. The Indian men and women are capable; they can prepare and serve three or four hundred with the greatest of ease. 

One of the high points was in 1925, when the Indians entertained the Synod of Washington for four or five days. The dining room was built for that occasion and they transported tents, bedsteads, bedding, furnishings and provisions from twelve to thirty-five miles. It was quite an undertaking to prepare beds and board for one hundred and fifty men and a few women for that time.

They say it was the most spiritual meeting of synod that they ever held, for they were shut off from distractions, but the Nez Perces think it was because the men of the synod were all there for the sunrise prayer meeting. They had the communion on Sabbath. One of the young Nez 

Perces ministers, who had been recently ordained, was chosen by the synod to preside and administer the bread, and a young negro minister from Seattle to administer the wine, one on each side of the table. I thought of the Bible verse "With God there is no Greek nor Jew, bond or free, but all one in Christ Jesus." The Indian elders distributed the elements. It was a great day. It had meant work to get ready. We thought that they [29]

would not want to undertake it again soon, but even while they were there 

I overheard them talking and one said, "We'll wait five years and invite synod again and we will do it better next time." 

The campmeeting closes each year with the sacred cantata of seventyfive or eighty pages, given the last night by the Nez Perces Indian choir. 

The next day the camp is put in order, tent and seats placed in the storehouse, the piano taken to one of the Indian homes not far away, the electric light plant locked securely, and the people hurry home to their harvest fields and gardens. The campmeeting at Talmaks is entirely in the hands of the Nez Perces people and grows in interest each year. What has become of that heathen band which so bitterly opposed Christianity when they separated in 1897 at Lapwai? The next year there was a division in their ranks over the question of leadership. Each succeeding year it grew weaker until there was just a handful of tents and finally they had not the courage to make the attempt. Surely the Lord has blessed the Christians and led them on, enabling them to stand up against every fierce test that has come to them. [30]

Chapter IV ALLOTMENT, CITIZENSHIP AND TEMPERANCE 

The Nez Perces were allotted land in severalty many years ago. Severalty is defined in the dictionary as the system of ownership by individuals as distinguished from ownership or occupancy in common. The allotting agent was a woman, Miss Alice C. Fletcher, who worked during the summers of 1889-1893. Each man, woman and child was given eighty acres of land. Given their choice, they, of course, chose the well watered parts. 

Sometimes they would take poorer land in preference to good land with insufficient water or poor springs; good water comes first with them. 

After the allotment was finished, three commissioners from Washington D. C. came to treat with the Nez Perces for the acquisition of the surplus land on the reservation. The purpose was to throw this open for white settlers.

If these commissioners expected to sway the "childlike Indians" with a few words of wisdom and in a short time secure their signatures to the treaty they soon found that they were mistaken. They met with the Nez 

Perces gathered from the reservation in a great council around the agency. 

The commissioners used their persuasive powers in presenting arguments as to why the reservation should be opened but the Nez Perces always met them with the one objection, "As soon as the reservation is opened the white man will bring in his saloon among our people to destroy us." 

Attractive inducements were offered but a whole week passed without making the least progress. The Nez Perces saw only the evils of the liquor traffic and were unmoved by any monetary bait dangled before their eyes.

Not until a prohibitory clause was inserted in the treaty could the commissioners secure even one signature.

A year and a half later when the reservation was thrown open, white settlers soon took up the vacant land. Some years passed, the government protecting the Nez Perces in the best possible way, making it an offense to sell liquor to an Indian, or bring it upon what had been the reservation. [31]

We had the bootlegger, of course, but he was soon made to lead an uncomfortable existence. Yet, in spite of that, we often heard the timeworn statement, "Oh, the well regulated saloon would be better than the low-down bootlegger."

About the year 1905 a man by the name of Dick was arrested, tried and convicted for introducing liquor into the Indian country. The case was sent to the Circuit Court of Appeals in San Francisco which reversed the decision of the lower court. The decision held that as the Nez Perces were now citizens, and not wards of the government, consequently the prohibitory clause in the treaty could not be made to apply to them. This meant that the right to have the saloon in their midst was to be forced upon them. At once, more than thirty saloons sprang up on different parts of the reservation. If any one doubts whether the so-called "regulated saloon" is better than the bootlegger I hope he will not have to go through the experience of the Nez Perces to be convinced. Drunkenness and wickedness increased, but it was no worse among Indians than white people. 

We would often see processions of white men, as many as ten in a row, going up to the saloon. White settlers would come in with loaded wagons, tie their horses to the fence and spend the day and perhaps the night in the saloon. One team stood there for twenty-four hours. 

The Dick case was carried to the United States Supreme Court and after consuming two and one-half years' time, during which the saloon keeper was allowed to ply his trade, a decision upholding the original treaty was reached. Our good Superintendent, O. H. Lipps, was told to order all violators off the reservation and prosecute all offenders thereafter. 

When some of them telephoned asking how much time they would be allowed to settle up and make their exit the answer was "No time at all; get out at once or you will be arrested." But it was hard to break up the nests that had been so well established. The bootlegging which followed seemed to be working over time. 

A few months later some United States detectives, under W. E. (Pussy Foot) Johnson, came to the agency, presumably to buy land. They spent several weeks looking over the land. They were getting acquainted with the situation. One night, with the aid of Mr. Lipps and the Indian police, they made simultaneous raids in seven different places on the reservation, caught large numbers of offenders in their net and seized and confiscated many gallons of whiskey. After the years, we still have the bootlegger to some extent, but with the strong temperance sentiment in our [32]

Indian churches and with Nez Perce county dry, “King Alcohol" finds his power almost a thing of the past.

Nez Perce county was a great stretch of territory with its miles and miles of the richest grain-producing soil, timbered mountains and mining regions. During the last ten years the northern and eastern parts fifty or sixty miles distant had been asking for county division. Lewiston is situated in the extreme northwestern corner of the county and only the Snake River separates it from the state of Washington. However, just as often as the subject of county division was brought up in the legislature, just as promptly Lewiston sent men to Boise to fight the measure.

It came up year after year with the same result until after the county voted dry. Then the liquor men of Lewiston saw that their only hope of ever getting control again lay in county division. So they sent their lobbyists to Boise to put through the measure which they had been fighting for years. It was announced in a short time that Nez Perce county had been divided and a thirty-five mile stretch of the finest grain raising prairie had been given to the new Lewis county, while Clearwater county was constituted of the timber and mining land, leaving Nez Perce with the smallest possible territory beyond the borders of Lewiston. Almost the same expense would be necessary in keeping it up, with its reduced population. There was quite a stir among the white farmers when they found the large increase in tax rates. You can imagine their indignation when they looked up the tax records and found that there was little or no increase, but sometimes a reduction in the taxes of the large property holders in Lewiston, with the wets getting all the favors and the drys, mostly white farmers, left to foot the bills. 

The liquor men, feeling sure of their ground, began to agitate for another local option election. The drys fought this not only because they hated liquor but because of the added expense to the county, and they were not quite sure of results. The commissioners were wet sympathizers and called the election for March 12, 1912, appointing wet registrars and, as far as possible, wet election boards, and it looked as though they would have everything their own way.

The drys saved their ammunition for the last ten days and during that [33]

time fought fierce and fast. The white farmers had "their war paint on", although it was a busy season when they were plowing for the spring sowing. Some of them turned out as speakers, others rode the county to get every voter out to register, and the women did good work. Lewiston had not counted on the Indian votes, but the Nez Perces have all the rights of citizens. When it comes to fighting the saloons they are only too glad to avail themselves of the privilege. One does not have to engage in argument with a Nez Perces to make him see that whiskey is an evil. Even the heathen undertsands the danger of "fire water" and the drinker wants it put beyond his reach. All they ask is to know how and when to act. 

Church committees were appointed to look after certain districts and get voters men and women out to register. They came in by wagon loads, through mud and rain, to "write their names." One day my aunt had a missionary meeting in her home and after it was over she led nine Nez Perces women to the village to register. She could pronounce their long Indian names and sometimes would have to spell them for the wet registrar. He tried to bluff those Indian women, for he knew that every one meant a dry vote, but he did not succeed. 

Election day dawned bright and clear and the battle of ballots began. 

We are within reach of two precincts; we vote in Sweetwater, but the church with a large percentage of Indian voters is located in Spalding precinct. 

The wets had worded the ballot to confuse the minds of those not on the alert, hoping to gain twenty percent of the voters by mistake. We had sample ballots. In church gatherings and at every other opportunity, the 

Nez Perces who could not read were told just where to put the mark. One young Nez Perces had made ballots and put four old women through the form of voting so often that one of them said afterward, "I wasn't a bit troubled when I went in to vote; I think I could have done it in my sleep." 

We went to Sweetwater in the forenoon and stayed until most of the Indians had cast their ballots. After I voted four or five Indian women were going in and I unthinkingly tried to help the election board out by pronouncing some of their long Indian names but a "wet" soon called out saying, "I object to Miss Crawford acting as interpreter." I laughed and went out before the constable could get after me.

A little later one of our good old women with the Indian name of He-yo-mak-a-ma-lats went in with her granddaughter, both wanting to vote. The old woman gave her name, which of course was hard to under- [34]

stand and when the young woman, who spoke English, tried to make it plain the wet man objected again and the young woman was put out. 

Still the judges kept searching the register to find the difficult name. We on the outside were quite amused and I said, “That woman is legally registered and is going to vote. If they refuse to have an interpreter that is their loss; it is up to them to find the name." Inside the search went on. 

At last some one came out and had me write it down and a little later called me in to identify the woman and the name. 

Another old woman voted who had been well drilled on the ballot. 

As she was coming out she met another woman who asked her for some information when up rose the objector again. The woman who knew was hustled out, but while still within hearing called back in the Nez Perces language, "Put the mark on the left side." The thing was done and all the Indians enjoyed a good laugh at the way Im-na-wa-kin-ma had outwitted the wet man.

In the afternoon we went to Spalding where they were holding prayermeeting in the church. All day we had seen wagon load after wagon load of good white farmer folks, old men and young men, old women and mothers with babies and children, walking up to the polls to register their protest against the saloon. It encouraged one to believe more in humanity and be more hopeful for the county, state and nation. By nine o'clock that night the returns were all in and Lewiston's wet majority of six hundred and sixty-one was overcome with a county majority of about two hundred in favor of the drys. The Lewiston paper accounted for the defeat by saying that it had been done by women and Indians, adding that the Indians should not have the right of suffrage. There was talk of contesting the election on that ground but it was only the wail of the fallen enemy. Nez Perce county is still dry. 

I quote from another letter which I wrote after the wet and dry election of 1914 "We seem to have something exciting almost all the time in Lapwai but this election beat anything I have ever seen. Dr. Alley, our good government physician, laughs and says that he has seen hot southern elections, and has seen Tammany ballot boxes stuffed but this surpassed them for excitement.

The wets had organized the election board, putting on it only wets, some of them hard drinkers. They marched up to the rink where the election was to be held and prepared to do business. The drys insisted on having some dry men on the election board. They wrangled for quite a [ 35 ]

while and one of the wet men said, “Let's have an attorney" and mentioned one who was over at the hotel. They had him there on purpose, hoping to overawe whites and Indians with his self-given authority. The lawyer soon made his appearance and began to sputter but our good sensible white business men and farmers soon settled him and put two dry men on the board.

Then the word went around that they were going to turn down the Indian vote for they counted them all dry and they had the balance of power. The law is that Indians who have severed their tribal relations are citizens of the United States, although the government holds most of the patents for their land and it is untaxable as yet. They are subject to taxation on their personal property but have never been assessed. 

The drys sent up three representative Indian men whose votes were challenged. They were denied permission to vote. Every vote was challenged and when they refused to swear them in, the Nez Perces walked out. 

Then things began to hum. All Indians were told to go in and compel refusal in every case. The Nez Perces were not slow in lining up. A line of about forty reached back to the door, showing a good deal of the old fighting spirit in their faces. The agency clerk was there to take down the names of those who were refused. 

The rink is a big place and it was packed with the better class of white people wanting to see the Indian ger justice. They were two and three deep, some standing on benches. I'll admit that they were saying a good deal to the men of the election board who wanted to rule their actions by their own opinions. They were refusing the Indians but grilling them with all sorts of unheard of questions, such as, “Are you a tax payer?” “Are you under the agent?" I happened to be near one of the young women when the old drunken judge was questioning her. Although she could talk English she knew that she was not getting fair treatment from him so she very quietly answered him in her own tongue which made him angry. There stood the long line of Indians; first, the young woman with her husband back of her, then Mr. Sharp, the agent, with Indian men and women back to the door. On all sides were excited clamoring crowds of white people. Things were getting hotter all the time till they were about to pitch that election board out into the street. 

All at once, in the midst of the excitement, after several ineffectual attempts, Dr. Alley managed to get their attention, stepped inside the railing and announced that he had a message from the prosecuting attorney [36]

and asked, "Will the election board hear it?" The sheet shook in the 

Doctor's hand, he was so indignant. Then he read that it was the prosecutor's decision that all Nez Perces Indians over twenty-one years of age were citizens of the United States and if properly registered had the right to vote. Then turning to the board he said, "Will the election board accept this decision?" The faces of the wets went white and they sat there in blank silence for a few minutes till the dry men got them together and laid down the law to them. They knew they were caught and the election was allowed to proceed after the applause that shook the rafters quieted down.

They at once called for the three Indian men who had been refused first and they walked up with a smile of triumph on their faces. Then they brought back the whole line and they voted without further trouble. 

Some of the white people thought when there was such a dispute at the polls the Indians would be frightened away but the Nez Perces are not that kind; every man and woman stood right there to see the thing through. 

I think they were rather enjoying the fight!

My Aunt Kate happened to be down in the middle of the muddle but was so tired she came home, got her dinner and pretty soon we saw her coming back. My sister Elizabeth came home, got dinner and brought mine down to me for I went at eight-thirty in the morning and never returned till after seven in the evening.

They had the same warm time in the Spalding precinct where there are a good many Indian voters. The wets from Lewiston sent a wet attorney from Asotin, Washington to challenge votes. The drys just stopped all proceeding till the man was put out. When the wet constable was told to do it he refused, so the only dry man on the board took a hand and he had to leave.

I added to this letter this later postscript: "The drys won by a small majority and the wets from Lewiston, without legal authority, came and openly opened our ballot box; they did the same thing in first ward, Lewiston, and counted out enough dry ballots so they now claim a victory of twenty-two. The drys contested it but a wet judge decided in their favor and the saloons were allowed to open for business in Lewiston. They say it is not safe to walk the streets because there are so many drunken men." 

The years have rolled away and the Nez Perces helped to put Idaho dry. At every election they worked to elect dry men to office, sometimes having to go on to three tickets to get them. They are still fighting against [ 37 ]

whiskey and all the evils that follow in its train. There has not been an 

Indian vote challenged since that exciting day back in 1914.

During the prohibition years the Nez Perces never slacked up in their fight for a dry country. I often hear white people say with long faces, willing to admit and take all the blame for the evils that came in in spite of prohibition, not because of it, “Oh, we were to blame; we let down in our fight, ceased to educate, etc." But I never heard a Nez Perces say it or admit treason to the cause. They had a temperance meeting in the church every other Sabbath before prohibition and kept it up all through the years. They have it yet and it is a most interesting service. The Indian does not give up. [ 38 ]

Chapter V TRAINING A NATIVE LEADERSHIP 

AFTER MR. SPALDING'S DEATH in 1874, my aunt Sue McBeth left her teaching in the government Indian school and went to Kamiah to begin the work of training a native leadership among the Nez Perces. There has been one continuous policy of training Indians for their own work ever since. 

It has been well that it has been continuous, for Indians do not take kindly to many changes. My aunt Kate used to speak of the work of other tribes and say, "They have had almost as much missionary work done among them as have the Nez Perces, but the many changes of missionaries and the changes of policies pursued have made so many breaks that they are far behind in their advancement along religious lines." 

I quote from General O. O. Howard who visited my aunt Sue in her little home, about a month before the Joseph war broke out, in the spring of 1877. "In a small house of two or three rooms I found Miss McBeth living by herself. She was such an invalid from partial paralysis that she could not walk from house to house, so I was sure to find her at home. 

The candle gave us a dim light so that I could scarcely make out how she looked as she gave me her hand and welcomed me to Kamiah. The next time I saw her was by daylight, which showed me a pale intellectual face above a slight frame. How could that face and frame seek this far-off region! Little by little the mystery is solved. Her soul has been fully conse crated to Christ, and He, as she fully believes, sent her upon a mission to the Indians.

Her work seems simple; just like the Master's in some respects. She gathers her disciples about her, a few at a time, and, having herself learned their language so as to speak it passably (Editor's note this was in the early part of her work) she instructs them and makes them teachers. There is the lounge and chair; there the cook stove and table, and there, in another room, is the little cabinet organ and a few benches. So was everything about this little teacher-the simplest in style and work." [ 39 ]

She gathered about her not only men who wanted to study for the ministry, but elders, Sunday School superintendents, and teachers for the church that was about to be organized, so it would have trained leaders. Because she was so frail she seldom went out among the people. When she went to church she would have her chair taken over and sit near the door for in the crowded church it was hard for her to breathe. 

She was teaching the leaders and they were going out to give the gospel in their own fine language; there was no misunderstanding that. They were fearless preachers of the gospel. Once I heard an Indian man speaking, not unkindly, of one of the Nez Perces ministers and saying, "He has the sword in his words and it goes straight to my sinful heart." She planned for and mothered the churches that later grew up about her: "Besides those things that are without, that which cometh upon me daily, the care of all the churches," sometimes gave her heavy burdens.

She trained ten men for the ministry, all being ordained by the Walla Walla Presbytery. Besides these she taught many who became leaders in church work. As her health failed near the last she would say, "Oh, if I were only young again, how I would like to go out into some wilder tribe and do the same work the Lord has helped me do here, over again," so firmly did she believe in Indian leaders for their own people. 

My aunt, Kate McBeth, came to the Nez Perces in 1879. Her work was out more among the people, visiting in their homes, teaching the women to sew, cook and take care of their homes and children. It was the beginning of the change in their lives from the old to the new. She was trying to break the old custom of the women doing most of the heavy work. 

It was strange teaching for them and hard to understand. They always called her "Miss Kate" to distinguish her from aunt Sue who was always "Miss McBeth" to them. One day one of them said to her, "Now, Miss 

Kate, wouldn't you be ashamed to see Peter making the wood." They were apt students and it was not long till the men were "making the wood," going out and catching the horse, harnessing it and driving around with the buggy or hack for the wife and children.

Both my aunts used the Nez Perces language and taught in it in their school work. They loved its smooth flowing expressive phrases. They taught from English books at first, translating the teaching into the Indian tongue until their students could master enough English to read the Bible and translate it for themselves. Either of my aunts could have translated the 

Bible for them, but they were far-seeing and knew that the time was coming [ 40 ]

when the Indians would need the English. In later years I could see how wise they were for it was a mental test the Indians would not have in any other way. the year

My aunt Kate gave the Nez Perces their first Sunday school in 1879, of her coming among them. It has gone on in the six churches uninterruptedly ever since. The singing of hymns is in English; the lesson is read in English and translated by the superintendent. There is an English speaking class for young people but all the others are taught in Nez Perce, and by Nez Perces.

Aunt Kate gave the people their first Christmas tree and it has always been a wonderful commemoration to them. Each of the six churches plans for and carries out its own program. Aunt Kate moved from Kamiah in 

1886, to Lapwai (now Spalding), the stronghold of old heathenism, and most courageously challenged the wild ones to a battle for supremacy. One of the many things they accused her of was what they considered a great sin, “dividing the hearts of the people." They would often say, “Oh, if we could only get rid of Miss Kate we would all have one heart,” and well they knew it would be a heathen heart.

How often, when she was going through the struggle and was having many black looks from the opposition, she would smile and say, "Won't they be glad when my old bones are laid away in the grave?" She was often asked, when the struggle was fiercest, "Aren't you afraid, Miss Kate?" and would always answer, "No, not so long as I can look up to God and say, "I'm right'," or "If they ever had any notion of harming us, they well knew we had the backing of a strong band of Christians, and they would not like to incur the displeasure of their own people." 

After my aunt Sue died in 1893, Aunt Kate continued her work, the training of a native ministry. The mission was now in Lapwai and she had student cottages built on mission ground so her pupils with their families gathered around her and studied during the winter and worked in the summer on their farms and thus paid all their expenses. They were independent; no Board or Presbytery ever paid a cent for the training of any Nez 

Perces minister. All it cost was the missionaries' salaries, besides half her time was given to general missionary work. Aunt Kate trained four more men for the ministry, ordained by the Walla Walla presbytery. 

When my aunt Kate began teaching the class of students I had the general work outside, visiting in the homes among the people and in the churches. During the winter it has long been the custom among the Nez [ 41 ]

Perces to hold a week's evangelistic meetings in each of the six churches. 

One church would entertain and all the ministers, and as many of the people as cared to come, were invited. Since they had little else to do in the winter they would go in quite large numbers. 

I believe all Indian people are very hospitable. They enjoy the greater social fellowship of their own people. The entertaining church would prepare everything beforehand. They would move into their little "church houses" built on church ground, comfortably furnished, and be ready to entertain all the way from half a dozen to twelve or fifteen. One time I asked a woman how many guests she had and she looked disappointed and said, "Melats"—just a few, only seven.

In these meetings they would carry on a regular program. I would have a Bible class at 9 o'clock with ministers, elders, Sunday School Superintendents and young people. There would be a sermon right afterward at ten o'clock. At one we would have a choir practice working on the sacred cantata to be given at camp meeting at Talmaks the following summer. 

Another service followed at two-thirty, a sermon, women's missionary meeting or prayer service. Then in the evening there would be a preliminary song service, sermon, Christian Endeavor or temperance meeting. No worship service is ever short among the Nez Perces. Years have passed and the services are about the same today, having the same enthusiastic interest. 

After my aunt Kate left us for the glory land in 1915, I inherited the training class and a rich privilege it has been. Then my sister, Elizabeth, took my place among the people. She had been with us for three years before, but not officially connected with the mission. However, she was doing the best kind of missionary work for she loved the Nez Perces and they loved her so she served most satisfactorily for twelve years. On July 22nd, 1925, the Lord took her home and we laid her away beside Aunt Sue and Aunt Kate in the cemetery at First Church in beautiful Kamiah. 

My sister's work was out among the people in their homes and among the children. When they were sick she had a way of rubbing their sore spots. 

Old women who did not like to take doctor's medicine liked to have her smooth away their aches and pains. They liked mustard plasters, too, and preparing them was easy for her. In the first years of the flu epidemic, she went to the church to see some sick people, and a little later telephoned me that she would not come home soon for there were twenty-five cases. In a few days the number had increased to forty. Whole families were ill. She did not get home for three weeks. There were two or three deaths, which al- [ 42 ]

most broke her heart. She only had time to go around, take temperatures for the doctor, and see that they were sufficiently fed. She would take a kettle of soup to a house and deal it out to them, give medicine and wash dishes once a day. Finally the Red Cross sent a man to help her, who took care of the patients at night. She helped them in sickness so much that some would laughingly call her Te-wa-ta-at, the "medicine woman."

How she enjoyed her work among the children! I think of her oftenest at the Children's Hour at Talmaks. She loved the bright-eyed babies. At first they were afraid of her, for she seemed so different from their mothers. 

Taking one in her arms, the baby began to cry. When my aunt took the child at once it quieted down. Elizabeth said to the mother, "Why does the baby not cry when Aunt Kate holds it?" She answered with a smile, “Oh, she isn't white."

Then I had assistants, Miss Leah Thompson, Miss Ora Haft, Miss 

Janette Taylor, Miss Irma Fremd, and Miss Pauline Nute, all doing good work over the reservation and among the young people. For the last three years Miss Vivian Purves has been doing good work in Kamiah and in the surrounding churches.

My class of students was somewhat different from my aunt's, for they had only Nez Perces Indians while mine had representatives from three tribes: a Spokane, a Makah, from Neah Bay, Washington, and four Nez

Perces, only two of them completing the entire course. Later another Makah man studied, was licensed and ready to be ordained. But because of the depression Indian work, for economy's sake, was combined and there have been no vacancies since, nor any money to start new work, although there is a tribe of four hundred Indians within fifty miles from his home who have never had any religious training, nor had the gospel preached to them. 

Since we have had students from three tribes we have had three languages, or four, with English. The students all use English, except the translation, for they have had some English foundation. At the beginning of the term each man selects a book of the Bible from which to translate, choosing a chapter or a few verses each day. I had one student who translated the whole of the New Testament in this way during his four year course.

First we would all read the chapter in English. I am no authority on one Indian language, let alone three, so my part was to make the English meaning just as plain as possible, then they would carefully translate it, each in his own tongue. [ 43 ]

On one occasion we had gone to evangelistic meetings at the North Fork church on Friday and stayed over Sabbath. The train made it possible for us to come home Monday morning and by taking a taxi from Spalding we would be here in time to begin the class work at nine o'clock. On this day we were soon hard at work. The student who was to begin had for his lesson Hebrews 11 and he began with, “Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen." He shook his head and tried again but was not satisfied. There is no similarity in the Indian languages, but each of the other men suggested a solution. There was more shaking of heads. Sometimes the different expression would shed a little light. They consulted the New Century, Weymouth and Moffatt translations, Aunt Sue's dictionary, and a Catholic translation. Finally they had to transcribe it. 

We spent the whole time allotted for translation, one hour and a half, on that one particular verse. When they speak of that day they laugh and say, "That was the time we had all the books down." 

In 1925 three of my students were ordained to the ministry by their respective Presbyteries. The Spokane was ordained by the Spokane Presbytery—the Makah Indian by the Seattle Presbytery—and the Nez Perces by the Walla Walla Presbytery. After being ordained these three went to Winona Lake, Indiana, and had a summer course in the Bible School of Theology, and that fall took charge of churches either among their own people or other tribes.

I had another Nez Perces ordained in 1928 and ever since he has been in charge of the Fort Hall Mission among the Bannock and Shoshone Indians in Southern Idaho.

The training class was closed in 1932 for lack of funds. It was a great sorrow to Indian people for our own line of native ministers was running low. Each minister has to take two churches and one of them is soon to be retired. Unless we can borrow an Indian minister from another tribe, two 

Nez Perces churches must have a white minister, which will be humiliating to them, for they have been independent since this first minister was ordained in 1879 to preach in the language all understood. Students from other tribes were beginning to come for training and we have had several applications since.

McBeth Mission has never trained men who could take a college course. 

We have taken the English foundation they had and built a substantial structure upon it. We never received men who could take higher education and 

I do not think a Nez Perces would ever ask it. [44]

The Nez Perces have not been selfish with the Book which has been so much to them but have gone out to give it to others. They have been trained for it; none but trained leaders could do it. They have been a missionary tribe from Mr. Spalding's time. He would take them with him while going from one tribe to another and they would be most helpful to each other, for Indians know Indians as no one else can. Then in my aunt Sue's time they would go out in parties, largely at their own expense, to preach the gospel to wilder tribes.

The early ministers would take long journeys to the Shoshones, their old time enemies. They would ride their ponies, using pack horses for their baggage, spending about two weeks on the way over mountain and desert. 

For one place it was necessary to carry enough water to last for twenty-four hours. The Nez Perces name for the Shoshones was "Te-wel-kin” or the enemy. At first they were rejected but the Nez Perces kept on going till finally the Shoshones opened their homes to them and later their hearts to the story of salvation.

Nothing has so interested the Nez Perces as telling the Good News to their red brethren.

Indian tribes have been calling for these Indian evangelists. White missionaries have been asking for them. There is a general saying among 

Indians that “The white man's religion is only for the white man. The Indians have their own religion, which was made for Indians and is better for them," by which is meant the old heathen religion of superstition with its trust in a "Wy-ya-kin" or attending spirit. the But when real Indians preach the same gospel that the white missionaries preach, their opposition is weathered. A few years ago when the Nez 

Perces had calls from eleven tribes by dividing their forces they reached nine of them, travelling over five states and working with four different denominations. Reverend James Hayes, D. D., the most prominent Nez Perces evangelist, had at the time of his death reached twenty-five tribes from the Canadian border to Arizona, going not only once but again and again, so anxious was he for the salvation of the souls of the Indian people. All Mr. 

Hayes' expenses were paid by a friend in the east. The Nez Perces are still reaching out to the tribes of the northwest in a most helpful way. [ 45 ]

Chapter VI WHAT THE GOVERNMENT HAS DONE 

IF WE WERE TO BELIEVE everything that we read in the papers and magazines or the stories told about the government's dealing with the Indians, we might come to the conclusion that those who carry out the wishes of the administration are thieves and criminals. 

One administration discredits another by telling what it has or has not done. Those of us on the inside seem to hear them say, "No one ever understood or helped the Indian till we arrived. All wisdom came in with us and will die off when we are gone." One must admit that mistakes have been made by the government as in any other great organization. I am really surprised that there have been so few, for the offices of Secretary of the Interior and Commissioner of Indians Affairs are both filled by political appointment. They are apt to be selected not so much for their efficiency as for their ability to swing votes. They enter these positions with but little knowledge of Indians and the work among them, and with far less experience. 

The only wonder to me is that there have not been more blunders to record. 

I know no remedy unless it be civil service, through which, by a series of promotions after years of training, experienced men would reach positions of importance to the Indian people. 

The present Commissioner, and Secretary of the Interior arrived on the scene with the same attitude toward Indian work and past administrations. 

They were ready to be interviewed since they had their minds made up about the great things that they were going to do for the Indian. The Commissioner presented his views soon after going into office and had much to say about how the Indian had been robbed of “birthright of land and stream, robbed by force and legal statute. The white man and the white man's government have despoiled the Indian of things far more precious than vast prairies teeming with game, of mighty rivers filled with fish or vast areas of trackless forests, of fair valleys or pleasant meadows. The white man has stolen the Indian's self-respect, his self-reliance, his faith and hope and pride. Almost - but not quite the white man filched the Indian's soul." 

The statement that the Indian was being robbed of his self respect [46]

brought a smile to my face, for as long as I have lived among Indians I have never yet found one, however low down in the scale, who had lost his self respect or failed to think that he was just as good as the white man or any one else.

As I grew up in my home in southeastern Ohio, about us were the signs of the Indian tribes that roamed the hills and fished in the streams. 

They had left behind them pictures and hieroglyphics cut in the rocks along the Ohio river, together with Indian mounds. Rather recently a whole 

Indian village was uncovered and restored. Where are the Indians today? 

Certainly not there. The white race was dominant and slave-minded in those days too. Why did not the white man place them in servitude? The

Indian is not the material out of which slaves are made, for he refuses servile obedience. The Indian will die, but will never bow to a slave master. 

When a monument to Marcus Whitman was dedicated near Walla Walla, Washington, my aunt, Sue McBeth, was there. Reverend James 

Hayes, then a young Nez Perces Indian minister, made an address. He began by saying, "We stand beside a martyr's grave, but my grief is greater than yours because it was a red hand that made it." He spoke with such perfect ease that white people were astonished and asked my aunt how she accounted for such poise in a man of a primitive race. She said, "Oh, that's easy. No Indian ever feels that he is inferior." I am sure the first Americans never had an inferiority complex, nor have I ever found the least sign of it in any of their descendants today. That is what makes the Indian worth working for.

What has the government done for the Indian in the last fifty years? 

It has done much for him. Through schools which taught the English language he has been enabled to take his place as a citizen beside the white man. This administration scorns the idea of compelling Indian children to speak our language but I am sure that if they had not been compelled, not unkindly, they never would have learned English. The old government schools, both reservation and non-reservation, even with the most meagre equipment and many handicaps, were a means of grace to the Indian boys and girls. Our men and women who were trained there will rise quickly to defend these schools the minute they hear them unjustly criticised.

Since the allotment these schools have decreased, not because of defects, but because they have served their purpose. Indian boys and girls are now in public schools which is the natural result of allotment. These "special [ 47 ]

denaturing plants", as the present commissioner is pleased to call them, have taught the Indian youth trades, and with other tribes, we have carpenters, harness makers, shoemakers, foresters, farmers, clerks, teachers, seamstresses, gardeners, chicken raisers, cattle raisers and homemakers who are able to make their own living, raise their families and be useful, independent citizens.

The Nez Perces have a native Christian leadership. They have six churches, with native pastors, elders, Sunday School superintendents, and all organizations in the church are manned by their own people. The Mission has trained them, but always with the hearty moral support of government agents, superintendents, doctors, teachers and other employees. Government and mission have worked together to train native leadership. 

The government gave the Indian his land in severalty and advanced him along agricultural lines. It taught him how to shoulder his own responsibility for his home and family. The allotment of land has been condemned. I have lived here more than forty years since the allotment and feel that despite some disadvantages, it has on the whole worked for the advancement of the Indians of the Northwest. The Nez Perces generally live on their own land, and farm enough to keep their families. They are splendid gardeners and raise all kinds of vegetables. Indian women are thrifty and their cellars are filled with canned fruit. Since the depression they have gone out and dug quantities of nourishing roots which they used to live on in the early days. Since the allotment they eat "the white people's food" just as we do.

The Indians take good care of their families and send their children to public schools. It is true that some have disposed of their land. Part of it has gone for taxes since the price of grain and all other products has gone down, but while they had it they reaped a good income. When selling was necessary they did it of their own accord and for a good price, and they enjoyed spending the money in their own way. 

It was not men and women of the generation that was allotted who sold their land, for many of them have it today. However, in the second generation heirs began to multiply and often it was difficult to determine the heirs, and in the third generation many thought best to sell their holdings. We have a saying that, "it is only three generations from shirt sleeves to shirt sleeves". One generation works hard and acquires property or money, the second uses or spends it, the third is back to shirt sleeves again. This happened to some of the Nez Perces and to other allotted [48]

tribes, not because they were Indians, but because it seems to be the natural law. How many white people have their old family homesteads after the third generation? Mine is in the hands of foreigners, but it is not because of mortgage or taxes. It would not have been sensible to keep the home for sentimental reasons when we could neither make use of it nor enjoy it. 

It is always hard for white people to become used to the way in which the Indian spends his money, for we are apt to think we could do it so much better. A considerable white population lives on what was the Nez 

Perces reservation. The government gave them their land free, as it did the 

Indians. I cannot see that they have made any better use of it, and they have sold it far more quickly. With the depression the white farmer, with few exceptions, found himself down and out. The white settlers who took up land were too poor to improve it, the next improved the land and when they got a good price sold it and moved on. Now it is the third set that is living on the land. Very few who took up land still have it today. 

After all the Nez Perces were allotted, the government bought the surplus land, offering them a good price, and making per capita payments. 

While some of the money was spent foolishly, most built new houses. The men would buy a team of American horses, the women new stoves, sewing machines and then added simple furniture to their homes. A number lost a part of their money in dishonest banks but in the main what they received was well spent. Later if they wanted to do with plainer furniture or with fewer conveniences so that they might buy a good car that was their own affair. I am glad that the Nez Perces and men of other tribes whom 

I know did not spend their returns wickedly, and go down in debauchery. 

The Nez Perces are willing to work. They are not complaining because they must. If in these depression years they find nothing to do, they would scorn the bread line. They would not know what it meant! When 

I was telling an Indian how hard a time white people were having these days to earn a living he said, “I think Indians are just the same but they do not whine." Even in their poverty before the allotment, the Nez Perces were too proud to accept rations from the government. They managed in some way to make a living, and often went hungry. Our Indians do not need insane asylums, for few go insane, nor county poorhouses, nor do they accept old age pensions. The north Idaho asylum located on the former reservation is crowded with white people. If one says, "It takes a smart man to go crazy," that proves nothing. I am sure that white people would be unwilling to admit that is the reason why they are on the outside! [ 49 ]

Chapter VII COOPERATING WITH GOVERNMENT WORKERS 

I IMAGINE that what I now say may seem strange to some. I am rising to the defense of that much maligned, misunderstood, and misrepresented part of the government of the United States which deals with Indian work. 

Often I hear criticisms of the government. It is easy for some to speak in a general way and say, "Oh, the government is to blame for this or that" without stopping to offer proof for their statements, because they feel that they will not be called to account. Our government is too big to stoop to the methods of the petty politician, and answer the charges brought by ignorant people. A few years ago a series of articles in one of our higher class magazines was written by a woman who seemed to know little about Indian affairs. Moving from one reservation to another, staying a day or two in each place, gathering her information from malcontents, it was of course unreliable.

This writer attempted to discredit government agents, teachers, superintendents, saying that they were ignorant. She called some doctors "derelicts". She told how Indian children in government schools were sometimes starved and mistreated, giving an instance of how they grabbed for their food in a school dining room because they were nearly starved. My reply was, "I think it was lack of manners instead of lack of food." 

When I asked a Nez Perces man if in the government school he ever went hungry, he laughed and said, "No, not unless I gambled off the best of my food for the other fellows' marbles. Sometimes he would be eating my pie and cake for two or three days while I was paying my marble debts."

When I was visiting in Ohio a friend who had spent the winter in Florida told me of hearing a certain Indian lecturer. Ready to shed tears over the report of the wrongs which the government had committed against the Indians she said, "Isn't it just awful the way the government has treated the Indians?" I surprised her by saying, "Save your tears. That Indian was probably making his living by working on the sympathies of an uninformed public." Once in the East I heard one lecture and when I made [ 50 ]

myself known and inquired about some Indian friends whom he had mentioned he lost interest at once, and would not even tell me his English name. With a profound bow all that he said was, "My name is Chief 

Tecumsah." with the Indians

Poor old government! It has suffered from political propagandists, would-be friends, silly sentimentalists, as well as mercenary exploiters. 

For forty-one years I have had the privilege of living beside a government agency, and the Indian school for Nez Perces which was turned into a government sanatorium for tubercular Indian children twenty-five years ago. During these years I have regularly gone in and out of these government institutions on an average of three times a week and at irregular intervals, visiting the boys and girls in dining room, living rooms, dormitories or wherever they were. Never in all this time have I seen any sign of mistreatment. It is not the Indian who is making these complaints. He is perfectly capable of doing it in an intelligent way if he sees the need, but it is the white man who gets worked up over the situation. Sometimes he has an axe to grind.

I am rising also to the defense of the fine people who have been ´employed by the government. I believe that I can speak for the majority of them. Most of them are college trained, or have had other special training. They have common sense, big hearts, and they know the Indian. 

While my experience has been limited to this one place, I have kept in touch with what is going on in nearly every other tribe in the Northwest. We have a native ministry which has gone out, singly and in parties, to preach the gospel to every tribe from the Canadian border to Arizona, time and again. Being Indians they get into the heart and life of other 

Indian people. Never once have I heard them mention mistreatment of

Indians by the government and news travels fast among Indians. 

When people will say to me, “Oh, but it is different among the Nez 

Perces," I ask, "Why is it different?" Through these forty-one years we have had the same system of transfers that all the other agencies and schools have had. I have seen many government employees come and go. Workers of a high class have come from other places under the government. By a system of promotion they have gone on to other places. If we have them, other places also have had them, and get them when they leave here. The Nez Perces have not been singled out for any special favors. 

We have had an occasional misfit of course, but the government has been ready to right any serious wrong as speedily as possible. [51]

The government people made the best neighbors and finest friends. 

We missionaries were taken into their fellowship just as if we belonged, to meet visitors, or for special entertainments. These government people have given us their heartiest moral support, and without them we could never have done our best work, nor been so happy in doing it. 

Almost a year has rolled around since the new departure of carrying on a Sunday School in the hospital of the government sanatorium. This is a government institution for tubercular Indian children of any tribe in the United States. It has a capacity of 150 and is always full. The children are from six to eighteen years. At one time there were sixty-five tribes represented, from many states. 

The missionaries have always had the privilege of carrying on religious work among those fine boys and girls-four services each week. They have a fine Sunday School which is carried on in the chapel by two good Christian teachers. We are free to go in and out at any time as long as we do not interfere with their school, or rest hours. In the chapel we have carried on the work through a Christian Endeavor on Sabbath evenings which the young folks plan for themselves. There is a song service and Bible story young folks plan for and carry on themselves. There is a song service and 

Bible story hour on Wednesday night. I visit in the boys' and girls' buildings any time through the week, besides visiting those who are more seriously ill in the hospital.

Last year the doctors decided to keep about fifty in the hospital all the time for special care and rest. They do not accept advanced cases but some become more ill after entering.

This meant that those in the hospital would be kept in bed most of the time, with their meals served on trays. They would, accordingly, not be allowed to leave the building to attend the chapel services as they had done before. I offered to have a Sunday School and Bible class on Thursday for the hospital boys and girls. The doctors and nurses were glad to have them cared for religiously in that way. They set the hour for nine o'clock Sabbath morning and we began last November.

I had twenty-six Protestant children. The Catholics took care of their own. We met in the hospital dining room. There were fourteen boys and girls from fourteen to sixteen years of age. The rest were smaller boys and girls and most interesting. A number of them were from tribes where no religious work had been done; they knew nothing about finding places in the Bible or what was in it. [ 52 ]

We began by asking one of them to lead, or take charge of the Sunday School service. We made out the first program which they followed, sometimes with minor changes. A little later they could lead a meeting with ease. A goodly number of them had become Christians when the Nez Perces ministers held evangelistic meetings there. One would lead, another draw a picture on the blackboard to illustrate the lesson, and another would tell the lesson story. Different children would take these parts each Sabbath. 

Each one was glad to volunteer. It was their Sunday School, and they were proud to carry it on themselves. The first eight Sabbaths there were leaders from seven tribes. Sioux, Yakimas, Umatillas, Warm Springs, Western Shoshones, Shoshones and Nez Perces. We took them just as they came from many tribes and states. 

They are always so interested in the picture that is put on the board. 

They like to draw, and Indians are talented along that line. One day a boy from Wyoming was to "make the picture," the lesson being about "The Prodigal Son." As it was the first time he had tried, he was afraid, and said, "Miss Crawford, when I get up there to tell the story my eyes just get bleary and I can hardly see." I told him to read the story and see what he could find to put in the picture. On Sabbath he had put at the top of the board-Three Lost Things. The first was The Lost Sheep. He had drawn a crooked line to a woolly sheep, to show how it had become lost. 

I had always thought of sheep getting lost among rocks and bushes, but this one sheep was out on the prairie alone. Without anything in sight to tell it which way it was to go, it had given up and was ready to die. Gene knows how hard it is to find the way on a trackless prairie. 

Second was-The Lost Money. A crooked line showed how a piece of money rolled down. The dollar sign was on it.

Third was- -The Lost Boy. He drew another crooked trail to a pig trough where a big mother pig was on one said with three little ones that had eaten the food and were scrambling across to the other. Off a little way was the Lost Boy, sitting on a big stone,-his head in his hands-lonesome and homesick. Below it were the words, "I will arise and go to my father." 

Sometimes a little crippled girl makes the picture. She gets out of bed and goes in a wheel chair. The blackboard is taken to her bed. She does it so well. I have a hospital Bible class each Thursday afternoon when we have more time to study the Bible and use maps. They are interested to find that the Bible land is only across an ocean and we could really see it if only [53]

we could go there. They have been interested in the war between Italy and 

Ethiopia and can find the places on the Bible map. 

Last Decoration Day was on Thursday. When I went up the flags were flying and everything quite gay. I told them they could celebrate the holiday, in which case we would bring the little folding organ out in the hall and sing patriotic songs with all joining in or else they could have the regular Bible class. Gene spoke up up and said, “I would rather have the regular service." When I said, "All right, then, ring the bell," he did so, and they all trooped in with their Bibles. 

Last fall the doctor told me that as some of the boys and girls were not doing so well he would have to keep them in bed so that they could not come to the Sunday School. He thought that I could reach them by bedside visits. Then for a while, I would make out the work for the week for those in bed, using carbon, printing the title, the golden text, and daily readings with some helps. They would study in bed and we would count them present. It is the only class which I have ever had which studied the lesson beforehand. Finally we sent for student quarterlies for all those who read. 

This makes my work easier, and they get much more out of it. It has been a pleasure to see them grow. Boys and girls who never had a Bible in their hands before now can find places and tell the stories and understand the teaching.

Once a year the Nez Perces ministers come and preach to them and call for decisions and baptize those who have not been baptized before. Almost all who are old enough have become Christians. I have the great privilege of leading them on into a well rounded Christian life. On a Sabbath afternoon two groups, one in the chapel and another in the hospital, are given the Lord's Supper. How I love these children's communions, with fifty or sixty from many tribes sitting at His table. 

Sometimes in a group of ten or twelve communicants at the hospital we have had eight or nine tribes represented, so that you can readily see how far-reaching and important the work is. Unless we do our work quickly and show them the WAY they may go back to heathen tribes and homes, and never find it. [54]

Chapter VIII BACK TO THE BLANKET? 

HOW OFTEN I HEAR white people say, “Oh, there is no use to educate the Indian for he will go back to the blanket." Then they shrug their shoulders and shift all the responsibility and consider that this ends it. Nevertheless, some have been fair enough to ask if it were true. 

I have been told pitiful stories of Indian boys and girls, educated in government or mission schools. They are said to go back to the old home and find themselves out of touch or sympathy with their own people and surroundings. The pull is downward, and finally they are sucked down into the whirlpool of the old heathenism to which is added the vices of civilization.

Quite recently we made a survey of our Nez Perces boys and girls, who have in years gone by, attended the non-reservation Indian school at Carlisle, Pennsylvania. This survey was taken after twenty-five years, giving ample time to judge results. Only one out of the hundred whom we took went back to the blanket and lived in heathenism until his death a few years ago. Ninety-five percent of the one hundred Nez Perces boys and girls made good, raising fine families and making good use of the training which the government, through Captain Pratt and Carlisle, gave them.

Why is it that such a large percentage have turned out well? Were our 

Nez Perces boys and girls better than those of other tribes? I think not. 

Was it because the missionaries put on any wonderful program for the returned student? It was because they had Christian homes and churches to come back to. Christianity means progress and it gave to those young people the high standards of living that they needed. They kept busy in the work of the churches, in making their homes and families Christian, and were a contented happy company of young people. 

I can imagine how different it is for others who go back from Carlisle, or mission schools to tribes still in the depths of heathenism, to pagan degradation with the evils of so-called civilization added. I can readily believe all the stories of loneliness and misery that are told of them. [55]

Is there no remedy? Let us not just criticise them, or pity them and let it go at that. If we give these fine young people Christian homes and churches to go back to results will be just as great in other tribes as they have been among the Nez Perces.

How can we do it? If we put a missionary into every tribe to give them the gospel of Jesus Christ the home will be made Christian. Christian families will make the church, and the church will develop a native leadership to carry on. Give these fine young returned students Christian homes to help them battle against sin and the old heathenism among their own people. I often hear Christian people say, "We will educate the young people and they will go back and reform their own people." But you are giving them an impossible task unless they have some support from their homes.

When are we going to do this? We should have begun fifty years ago for a Christian home is no mushroom growth. The government has done its work well. It has given the young people good practical training, and expected the church of Jesus Christ to Christianize them, to do for them what it could not do for we live in a country where church and state are separate. We, as a church, have failed. Make the home Christian and most of the distressing conditions among the Indians of today will be blotted out. 

Our Nez Perces boys and girls have been for twenty years in public schools, taking their places beside young white people. We have an ever increasing number who have been graduated from high schools on and off the reservation. Some of them are in universities and colleges. 

After a visit in the South and East for a year, I conclude that there is less race prejudice here than in any place where I have ever been. At 

Lapwai, just across from McBeth Mission, there is a fine grade and high school, with eleven teachers. The Indian takes his place in classes, athletics, and all entertainments on an equality with white boys and girls. The Indian has not been discriminated against, nor have I heard complaints from either white or Indian people, which speaks well for both races. The freedom from it here is because most of our boys and girls come from Christian homes and they fit in better with the public school life. It speaks well for the Indian when he can take his place in white schools, colleges, and universities. He is only about two generations out of the old tribal life with the handicap of having to use a strange language, yet he keeps up with white people who have hundreds of years of civilization and Christian training behind them. [56]

A letter that I wrote to Rev. Ward W. Sullivan, Ph. D. of Whitworth

College, Spokane, Washington, after one of our young men had been taken under the care of the North Idaho presbytery may be of interest. I include it as it tells of one boy who demonstrates the response which the Indian is capable of giving to the right training. "My dear Dr. Sullivan: 

Lapwai, Idaho, October 20, 1933.

No one could be happier than I was when Angus Wilson decided to study for the ministry and was taken under the care of the presbytery last Tuesday. He is the first Nez Perces to choose the ministry who has had the advantage of an English foundation on which to build his college and seminary course. The men who were trained in McBeth Mission did not have it, were underprivileged in their early years, with only a limited knowledge of English. Since they were intelligent men they responded to it in a fine way. The twenty men trained here, of whom eighteen were ordained, were broadminded pastors, thoroughly evangelistic and laid good solid foundations in the hearts and lives of their own people. 

I wonder if the presbytery and college realized that they were giving so much to Angus Wilson when they so kindly proposed to see after his education. I know people often look upon the Indian as a child in his thinking, education and actions, and are inclined to class him among the ignorant, stoical and unresponsive, when that very Indian is speaking a language which is perfect and he is speaking it far more perfectly than we speak English. He may look stoical to the white man but at the same time he is weighing him, forming his own opinions and often wise enough to keep them to himself.

The Nez Perces have a real language, not a jargon. When my aunt 

Sue McBeth died she left a dictionary and grammar, in manuscript, of fifteen thousand words. My aunt Kate McBeth copied most of it before it was sent to the Smithsonian Institute. As long as she lived, she was adding to it. Since I have had the class, when the men were puzzled over a word and we referred to that dictionary I was often surprised to find how many words were not there. Aunt Sue was expert in the Nez Perces language.

She could far more easily have translated for them into their own tongue, than to have taken grown men and given them a beginning in the English language and taught them to read and write it. Today they read the Bible in English and then translate it into their own tongue. [57]

I can see how wise she was, and far-seeing too. It was not only a mental test for them but she knew that the time was coming when they would need to know English. I often think how much easier it is for me, than for those who went before me and blazed the trail. The men that I have taught have been more interested in translation than in any other part of the work. They are getting God's word in their own language which is always clearer to them and more expressive with finer shades of meaning. 

They can read the English Bible with faces unchanged, but when they translate it it is a joy to see their faces brighten up and hear them say, "I could only see it very dimly when I read it in the English, but when I translate it into my own language it is just as though I had my eyes wide open. 

So many white people say to me, "Why don't you insist on them giving up their own language? In a few years they will all be talking English! I always answer, "Why should they give it up? It is a perfect language, not a jargon. Why throw away their own fine language to take up another which we often give them very imperfectly? They are just that much better off than white boys and girls for they have two languages instead of one. 

The white school requires their young white folks to study other languages, then why should the Indian give up his?"

Through the years I have been interested to see how the Indian mind would react to the study of Latin, for there is a similarity in construction. 

Four years ago four young Nez Perces in high school insisted on having a Latin class. The white boys said, "Why, those Indians just eat up 

Latin. It isn't half so hard for them as it is for us." Last year some of the 

Indians were in the Spanish class and the teacher told me that they were the best in it. I believe their own language is most helpful to them in the study of the languages of other races. 

You know that we white people are often conceited in our attitude toward other races and believe that they cannot possibly have anything of value unless we give it to them. When I think of our men in the Nez 

Perces ministry who thought and spoke in a perfect language, who were untiring students of the Bible and able to translate it fluently, with a fair grasp of world affairs and the work of the church, it seems to me that they should not be called unintelligent. They laid lasting foundations not only among their own people but in almost every tribe in the Northwest for they were spiritual foundations. They gave Angus Wilson the spiritual background necessary to make the success which we hope that he will achieve. I trust that he will have an unusual contribution to give to Whit- [58]

worth College because of the intelligent, constructive work that has been done for him by his own Nez Perces ministers, limited of course, in knowledge of English but wise in their own language and in spiritual wisdom. 

I believe that Angus, with all that the college and seminary can give him, will be handicapped if he does not know his own tongue and be able to translate the Bible into it. Although Angus may never have to preach in the Nez Perces and may think in the years to come that translation will be unnecessary, for all the young people speak English, yet we have some very good translators in every church and I will miss my guess if he is not asked some posers from the pews. It is not every one who can speak good English who will make a good translator. It takes someone who has studied to do it. 

These things are not known among white people and they misunderstand the Indian while mistakenly thinking they know all about him. The 

Indian men it has been my privilege to train came from three tribes, Makah, Spokane, and Nez Perces. Sometimes on Friday we would read the Sunday School lesson first in the English and then they would translate a verse successively, each one in his own tongue. There were three different Indian languages, the English making the fourth in use. So far as I can find out these other languages are just as perfect as the Nez Perce and yet they are wholly unlike. Where did they come from? How are they able to transmit them so perfectly from one generation to another?" [59]

Chapter IX ONE PREACHER'S STORY 

NEZ PERCES PASTORS are faithful and fearless in denouncing evil and in maintaining discipline. One of them was a boy of about eight or ten years at the time of the Joseph war. With his mother he fled from Kamiah following Joseph and his band over mountain and plain. He was in the battle of the Big Hole, and escaped from General Gibbons. He pushed on into Montana with the fleeing Joseph band until Bear Paw mountain was reached. 

With some others, one morning he was out searching for horses. While they were away from camp they heard firing. When they returned and looked down on the camp they saw the United States troops under General 

Miles surrounding it.

The boy's father was killed in a battle before they surrendered. The boy was with the others, who mounted their horses and fled. Being near the 

British Columbia line, it was not far beyond the border to Sitting Bull's people, who were kind to them in their extreme poverty. They lived mostly on rabbits and the boy was often very hungry. 

In the spring they heard that the war was over and that Joseph's band, in which was the boy's mother, had been taken captive to the Indian Territory. The little band of Nez Perces decided to go back home. In a party of 

Flathead Indians whom they met in Montana was a woman who was half Nez Perces. Inquiring about the boy, she found that he was related to her. 

Taken home with her, he lived among the Flathead Indians for a number of years. One day when they were out hunting and met a party of Nez 

Perces, he expressed his desire to go back to his own people. So he was given a pony and into Kamiah he came, an overgrown boy. this These Nez Perces people are thoroughly converted. Let me tell you of young fellow's conversion in his own words. "I do not know what year it was when I came back from Montana. I think it was the third or fourth year afterward, and in the winter time. [60]

They were holding meetings in the first church of Kamiah and all the Christians had left their homes and pitched their tents around the church. I did not like to go to the meetings, although the first year after I came back Miss Kate put a Bible on the Christmas tree for me. I looked through it for pictures, for I could not read. When I found none, I put it away in my pocket and hung my coat upstairs. The Book was there day after night. 

In those days I had to make my own living and earn my own clothes. 

I lived with an old man and took care of his horses. I would be away all day driving them to good pasture. I was very happy thinking about what a good time I would have next summer, when possibly I would go back to Montana. I went from the place and crossed over the river, always taking good care of the horses. I took them to what is now called Seven Mile Canyon, and wherever I could find good bunch grass for them, for I wanted to keep them in good shape all winter. I would go out in the morning and return late in the evening to the camp, but never went to church. 

One day I took those horses up on the hill to Two Buttes. They were about three-fourths of a mile apart. I do not know what time it was when 

I came to that place but I judged, by the position of the sun, that it was about ten o'clock. Then I heard the bell at the First Church ring. 

I don't know how many times the bell rang. I felt as if I was standing on the edge of a precipice or some high place liable to drop down. Tears ran down my cheeks. I never before had prayed, but I prayed there on the top of that butte and said, "My Maker, my Father, if you give me life until evening, I will repent and show myself before the session of the church. Whatever they will say to me I will do." Not long from that hour my sorrow went away but I was thinking, "I am wrong in sin.”

I went around to see the horses and then went down to the valley. It was warm in the daytime but cold mornings and evenings. I forded the river to return, tied up my horse and went to the church. I did not even wait to take off my wet moccasins, or unsaddle my horse. They were preaching and worshiping. When I came to the door, inside there was a man named 

Peter Noah. I opened the door wide enough for my body to pass through sidewise, for there was such a large crowd of people. As they were just rising to sing the doxology, there was no time for me to repent. Then the benediction came before I could repent as I told my God I would. The doorkeeper opened the door and I went out. Something like a magnet drew me back. [61]

A lot of people were passing by. They did not know me, of course. I stood there waiting and wondering what to do. The last man that came out was Abram Johnson, student of Miss Sue McBeth. He came to me and said, "What are you doing here?" I said, "I want to repent, but I couldn't do it.”

The doorkeeper said the meeting was over and so he sent me out. But I said, “I will not go away from this place. Now I will be joined to the church." Then he said, “Come,” and we went in. The preacher was Rev. 

Robert Williams and the elders James Wilbur, Felix Corbett, Solomon Whitman and Billy Williams. Abram Johnson said, "This boy wants to be a

Christian," and went out. They did not speak to me right away but were silent. As they had not seen me in the church during those meetings, they wondered why I should want to repent. When Rev. Robert Williams asked me "Who told you to repent?" I said, “Nobody." He said, “And yet you are willing to be a Christian? Who talked to you about it?" I said, “Nobody. But this morning I went out early with the horses and came to the top of the mountain. While I was there I heard the bell ring and my heart was filled with sorrow and I felt as though I was in danger. Although I had never prayed before I said, 'Lord, if you will allow me to get back I will testify before the session.' I came a few minutes ago and just tied up my horse wtihout food, and came into this house, and when the man opened the door for the people to go out, I could not walk away." The session then began to warm up. They understand me. The preacher said, "That was the 

Holy Spirit speaking to you." They examined me and I said, "I now repent of my sins, and whatever I am doing that is wrong, I repent of it; I will quit." One old man named Job was to be baptized the next Sabbath so we two stood up together and were baptized." 

This is the story of the Rev. Mark Arthur who studied with Aunt Kate, was ordained to the ministry in 1899, and was pastor of the Spalding church for twenty-two years. While there, it was necessary for the session to suspend a man for wrong doing. He announced the decision on the Sabbath and at once the man, who claimed to have some legal ability, declared he was going to have Mark arrested for libel. One day one of the students came in to 

Aunt Kate's schoolroom and said, "The sheriff is here." With a sinking heart she sent the messenger to Lewiston to give bonds for him if necessary. 

The next day Mark came to tell us that the sheriff had read the warrant to him and then said kindly, "Don't you be afraid; we all know what kind of a man he is."

His case was come up in two weeks, and the day before the trial Mark [ 62 ]

came to talk it over with my aunt. He knew nothing about law and of course was troubled. Before he left he and Aunt Kate knelt in prayer. When she came out she said to me, "I wish you might have heard Mark's prayer. 

He began by reminding the Lord of the time when he was a boy at that awful battle of the Big Hole, and said, 'Lord, you know you kept me there.'

Then he reminded the Lord how at the battle of Bear Paw mountain his father was killed and his mother was taken captive, and said, 'Lord, you know you kept me there.' He went on over into Canada and reminded the 

Lord of the time when he stayed among Sitting Bull's people and said, ‘Lord you know you kept me there when I was near to death of starvation. 

Then when I was a wild boy in Montana and knew nothing about you, you know you kept me there, and now I know you will keep me in this hour of great trial.'"

The trial came and after two weeks the judge rendered the following decision: "The Court not only finds Rev. Mark Arthur not guilty of any misdemeanor, but commends the Rev. Mark Arthur and his session for their good, religious and business sense in dealing with this troublesome man." 

Often I have seen him put his hands on the shoulder of the man who accused him and plead with him to repent and turn to the Lord, who was so powerful in his own life. His picture is included in this book among those trained here. He is still at work. 

I close with this testimony of Dr. L. C. McEwan, pastor of the First 

Presbyterian Church of Lewiston: 

"The centennial of the coming of The Rev. Henry Harmon Spalding and his wife Eliza Hart Spalding to work among the Nez Perces is to be observed this year. The fruit of their labors as well as that of their successors, Miss Sue and Miss Kate McBeth, was well illustrated at the April 

1936 meeting of the Presbytery of Northern Idaho. Three of the four commissioners who were elected to the 148th meeting of the General Assembly at Syracuse, N. Y., are Nez Perces ministers and elders. The new moderator elected in the Presbytery, the Rev. James Dickson, is also a 

Nez Perces minister.

If the spirits of just men made perfect are cognizant of the doings of earth-folk it must be for them an occasion of great joy. To know that through their efforts great companies have not only been redeemed but trained to positions of leadership and usefulness in the church must afford great satisfaction to those who have also participated in the training of [63]

these Indian friends and are still with us. Able to fill their pulpits acceptably to their people, capable of presiding over assemblies, deliberating with their white brethren and representing the great Presbyterian Church in her national assemblies, these Nez Perces Christians stand as evidence of the value of such missionary effort."
