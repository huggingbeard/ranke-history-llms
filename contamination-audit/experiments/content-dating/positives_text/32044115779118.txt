Extract From Report of the 1950 Emergency Board, No. 81 (The McDonough Board) 

"There is an unmistakable relationship between costs of operation and the capacity of an industrial and business enterprise to provide employment opportunities, produce a reasonable return on investment capital, pay taxes, and provide for depreciation and obsolescence. Lack of ability to pay is, of course, not an adequate reason for the continuance of substandard levels of wages, excessively long hours of work, and unsatisfactory conditions in employment. No such unfavorable conditions are present in the railroad industry, although some adjustments may be necessary to bring this industry abreast of standards set in other advanced industries. "Costs are of primary importance in a corporation's capacity to survive and prosper, on which the availability of jobs and a reasonable return on investment depend. It is important to remember that costs of operation and sustained employment are not separable items in the balance sheet of an enterprise. An unbalanced relationship between costs of operation and income soon discourage investment of new capital upon which railroads, like other forms of enterprise, depend to provide the improved equipment and facilities that are so necessary if the railroads are to meet successfully the increasingly severe competition of other agencies of transportation and provide the shipping and travelling public with acceptable services at acceptable prices, upon which income and jobs depend." (pp. 165-166)

Nove Ops Extract From Report of the 1955 Emergency Board, No. 114 (The Whiting Board) 

"Ability to Pay and Retroactivity. The Board is in agreement with the Carriers' contention that 'ability to pay' must be a consideration in the determination of the amount of any wage increase. This principle is especially important when any attempt is made to compare the earnings levels of railroad employees with those in outside industries. Importance then attaches to the extreme problems the railroads face from such directions as growing competition aided by public subsidy, inability to increase return substantially through rising rates and fares, reducing volume of freight and passenger revenues as contrasted to the growing volume of transportation services used in this country, inadequacy of earnings for many (though not all) railroads, etc. But, the factor of ability-to-pay does not have the same significance in a recommendation of a wage increased based on an intra-industrial uniform wage increase principle as it would in a recommendation based upon an inter-industry wage comparison. "The Board has been apprised of no precedent wherein any craft in the railroad industry has been deprived of an across-the-board wage increase, granted other crafts, on the grounds that the railroads did not possess abilityto-pay to a sufficient degree to make uniform such a wage increase. However, the record does indicate certain situations in which wage increases have been delayed for particular crafts, and it is highly probable that the Carriers' ability-to-pay the prevailing wage increase to such craft or crafts (particularly on a retroactive basis) was sufficiently in question to dictate such delays. "In the present case the Board is persuaded that there is no real question of the Carriers' ability-to-pay a 16½ cent per hour 'package' increase to the nonoperating employees in the amount of 142 cents per hour as of December 1, 1955 and 2 cents per hour as of March 1, 1956. But, the Board does feel that on the basis of the record there is a question of the Carriers' ability-to-pay the complete wage increase effective retroactively to the 

September 1, 1955 date requested by the Organizations or the October 1, 1955 date on which the 1955 wage increases of a large number of operating employees have been made effective. The Board's determination to recommend no retroactivity of the 1955 wage increase for nonoperating employees prior to December 1, 1955 is based on its conclusion that the Carriers do not possess the abilityto-pay such a wage increase retroactively to any earlier date. Moreover, the 

Board's recommendation in this regard is not inconsistent with the prior practices of the parties in selecting effective dates for wage increases." (pp. 26-27)

Extracts From "Railroad Transportation and Public Policy, by Dr. James C. Nelson. A study sponsored and published by The Brookings Institution, Washington, D. C., with funds provided by the Ford Foundation. April 1959. 512 pages. 

"The striking fact about current railroad difficulties is that they developed in the midst of nation-wide prosperity. Unlike the railroad problem of the 1930's, the problem today is not merely the reflection of widespread economic distress. On the contrary, apart from the brief postwar recessions, employment and production have been high and family living levels have risen. 

The inability of the railroads to prosper in such times suggests that their ills are traceable to basic forces rather than to temporary conditions, such as the moderate postwar recessions. The 1957-58 recession merely brought to light fundamental weaknesses which had existed for some time. "Today, the outlook for the railroads is anything but bright even though the industry is essential and, in numerous traffic situations, possesses significant advantages over other forms of transport. Inflation of prices and wages continues to raise rail operating expenses or to prevent them from falling as improvements in operating efficiency and in the art of railroading take place. The revenues from a series of general rate increases have offset rising expenses but have not materially improved the long-term earnings and capital position of the railroads. Rising rates have stimulated the most profitable types of freight to move by other modes and private carriers. (p.4) "The railroads have been faced with a serious dilemma. Rising costs from inflation and traffic volumes below basic plant capacity have prompted successive rate increases since 1946. In time this action has boomeranged by encouraging further traffic erosion. Thus, the roads have had a hard choice between maintaining solvency with higher rates and less traffic or protecting their long-term traffic position by holding rates down or lowering them at the risk of critically low profitability or even bankruptcy. (p. 233) ... 

"The ICC and prominent shipper groups have urged the railroads to depend on greater efficiency and better service from capital investment for dependable solutions to their problems. (continued on next page)

Extracts From "Railroad Transportation and Public Policy, by Dr. James C. Nelson. A study sponsored and published by The Brookings Institution, Washington, D.C., with funds provided by the Ford Foundation. April 1959. 512 pages. 

"According to estimates of 1956-65 capital needs submitted to the Brookings Institution by a representative sample of Class I railroads, the railroads as a whole would lift substantially the level of their gross capital expenditures over the average annual postwar expenditure of $1.1 billion if funds were available. With adequate capital funds, the carriers would. regularize their investments over the years and thus contribute to economic stability. They would greatly increase investments in freight cars and to a lesser extent in yard and terminal improvements. As the railroads reported that they would continue to invest the greater part of future capital funds in equipment, it is doubtful that they actually assumed, as requested, that the necessary capital funds would become available in adequate amounts. "The question is whether upwards of $2 billion or more a year should be invested in railway modernization. By investing far larger sums annually in roadway improvements, railroads could attack the problem of lowering rail operating costs and increasing train speeds by tunnelling through mountains, lessening gradients greatly, reducing curves, shortening route mileage, relocating and concentrating yards, and by adopting more electronic traffic controls. Investments of this size may well be needed to modernize the most significant rail roadways just as the interstate highways are now being rebuilt to higher standards at great expense. The possibility of important reductions in costs and improved service through higher standards of railway construction is worth intensive study." (p. 426)
