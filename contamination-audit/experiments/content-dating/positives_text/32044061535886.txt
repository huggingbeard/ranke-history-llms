INTERNATIONAL FINANCE CORPORATION STATEMENT BY HON. DOUGLAS DILLON, SECRETARY OF THE TREASURY Secretary DILLON. Thank you, Mr. Chairman. I have a brief statement.

Mr. Chairman and members of the subcommittee, I appear before you today in support of H.R. 6765, authorizing the approval by the 

United States of a proposed amendment to the Articles of Agreement of the International Finance Corporation which would permit the Corporation to make equity investments under limited conditions. 

This amendment would have a significant effect in stepping up the rate at which the Corporation is about to invest in its less-developed member countries and would thereby further the purposes for which the Corporation was established. 

The proposed agreement has been carefully considered by the Corporation and is unanimously recommended by its Board of Directors. 

This is the first time since the IFC's creation in 1956 that a matter concerning it has been before this committee. It may therefore be helpful to review the origins of the Corporation and its work: 

The IFC was established as an affiliate of the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, or World Bank, whose outstanding record the committee knows well. Any country which is a member of the Bank may become a member of the IFC, and 59 of the Bank's 68 members have now joined the Corporation. The total authorized capital of the Corporation is $100 million. Present members have actually paid in $96.6 million, in dollars. The U.S. subscription, which we paid when we joined in 1956, is $35.2 million. 

While the Corporation has cooperated closely with the World Bank since its inception, its relationship to the Bank will be even closer in the future. The President of the Corporation, Mr. Garner, has announced his intention to retire this fall after the annual meeting. 

Mr. Eugene Black, president of the World Bank, has agreed to take on the added duty of the presidency of the International Finance Corporation at that time. This will insure the closest possible coordination between the operations of these two important institutions. 

The idea behind the Corporation is a simple one. It is that a multilateral source of capital should be available to give direct encouragement to the stimulation and growth of private enterprise in the lessdeveloped countries of the free world. The Corporation seeks to accomplish this by providing seed capital-that extra margin which may very well determine whether private funds are willing to go in. 

In practice, the Corporation has invested in small or medium private enterprise projects. What is often regarded as a small private firm in a large industrialized nation may be a good-sized undertaking in many of the less-developed countries with which the Corporation deals. 

Most of the enterprises assisted by the Corporation are engaged in light and medium manufacturing in such fields as furniture, rubber products, automotive components and replacement parts, electrical equipment, steel products, and food packing. A number of firms in which IFC has invested produce basic materials such as cement, bricks, lumber products, fertilizers, and paper pulp. All of the firms have aided local economies by providing additional employment, and all contribute importantly to the growth of the private sector of the developing economies.

INTERNATIONAL FINANCE CORPORATION 

During the approximately 4 years of its operations, through last December, the Corporation has made investment commitments totaling $44.8 million, of which $29.3 million has actually been disbursed. 

The average size of its investments is about $14 million. Thirty-six investment commitments have been made in seventeen countries. In each case additional private investment funds have been committed alongside the IFC. These private investments have amounted to over $125 million or nearly $3 of new private investment for each $1 of IFC investment.

In carrying forward its operations, the Corporation has been severely limited by the provision in article III, section 2(a) of its articles that― *** financing [by the Corporation] is not to take the form of investments in capital stock.

As I indicated in my letter of April 4, 1961, to the Speaker of the House, this limitation has tended to constrict the desirable flexibility of the Corporation in making risk capital available to less-developed economies. Because of this limitation, the Corporation has had to resort to the use of convertible debentures or long-term stock options; that is, instruments which are not themselves common stock and may be converted to common stock only under prescribed conditions and only after they have been transferred out of the hands of the Corporation.

However, convertible debentures are not well known in foreign capital markets, especially in the developing countries. In many of these countries legal provisions for the issuance of such debentures do not exist. Arrangements for long-term stock options have involved techniques which are legally complex and present substantial negotiating difficulties. A detailed explanation of these problems and of the need for authority to make equity investments is contained in a memorandum dated February 10, 1961, from the President of the Corporation which I would like to submit for the record at this point.

(This memorandum explains a proposal for amending article III of the articles of agreement of the International Finance Corporation so as to enable it to invest in capital stock.)

HISTORY 

1. The original proposal to create the International Finance Corporation was developed by the management of the World Bank and endorsed in a report to the President of the United States in March 1951 by the International Development Advisory Board, entitled "Partners in Progress." This proposal, in its original form, was to create an institution which could not only make loans without government guarantees but which could also make nonvoting equity investments in participation with private investors. Objections were raised, principally by the United States, to the right to make equity investments. The management of the World Bank agreed to proceed with the organization of IFC, accepting reluctantly the limitation that the Corporation could not invest in capital stock. 

2. IFC came into existence in July 1956, with the hope that this prohibition against investing in capital stock would not unduly restrict its operations. The experience of 4 years has now convinced the IFC management that this restriction is in practice a serious handicap both to the growth of IFC's operations and the mobilization of private capital for foreign investment.

4 

INTERNATIONAL FINANCE CORPORATION 

3. As at December 31, 1960, IFC had 58 member countries with a total capital subscription of $96,207,000, all paid in U.S. dollars. Investment commitments had been made in 37 enterprises in 17 countries amounting to nearly $45 million, in which private participations and sales totaled $6.4 million. This left a net IFC commitment of $39 million, disbursements were over $29 million. 

4. IFC's own capital is small in comparison with other international and national financing institutions, and it has always been obvious that it could make an important impact only if it could encourage private investment capital to participate in its investments. Such private capital is particularly needed for, and is particularly interested in, equity investment. But, so long as IFC must take a creditor position, it cannot encourage others to join with it in making equity investments, nor can it demonstrate the advantages of equity investment. 

5. The basic concept of IFC is that it is an investment institution as distinct from one making conventional loans. IFC's articles of agreement provide that it is to take into account "the requirements of the enterprise, the risks being undertaken by the Corporation and the terms and conditions normally obtained by private investors in similar financing."

Thus, it was intended that IFC make investments on terms providing for returns appropriate to the risks undertaken. Likewise the letter dated May 2, 1955, from the President of the United States of America to the Congress recommending legislation for membership said that IFC would "provide, in association with local and foreign private investors, risk capital for financing the establishment, improvement and expansion of productive private enterprises in member countries when other sources of funds are not available on reasonable terms. 

This type of risk or venture capital is most urgently needed."

If IFC made loans only on fixed-interest terms, it would seldom be able to attract private investors and thus revolve its funds. In short, to fulfill the purposes for which it was organized, IFC is compelled to make investments which include some characteristics of equity. 

6. But, being prohibited from making normal equity investments, it has been forced into patterns of financing which sometimes appear complicated and unusual. Its terms normally provide for unsecured loans at a moderate fixed interest, but carrying also some additional income related to profits, or options on stock.

Such terms are unknown or unfamiliar in most of the underdeveloped countries in which IFC operates. Sponsors tend to be overoptimistic in their profit forecasts. On such assumptions they exaggerate the potential returns to 

IFC. IFC investment terms also persent difficult accounting and legal problems. 

They result in complicated documents which are obstacles in arranging participations with private investors. 

7. Bearing in mind the injunction in its articles to revolve its funds by selling its investments to private investors, IFC has included options on shares in most of its investments. However, because it cannot itself exercise such options, IFC may frequently be in a weak position to negotiate their sale at fair value. 

ADVANTAGES IN IFC EQUITY FINANCING 

8. IFC should be able to provide wide flexibility of financing so as to meet the varying needs of private enterprise and to attract private investors to participate with it. The ability to do equity financing, where appropriate, would substantially assist in increasing the scale of its operations, and thus increase its contribution to economic development. 

9. It would not, of course, be intended that all IFC investments should be in equity. A substantial part would continue to be made in the form of loans; and many investments would be partly in equity and partly in loans. However, given the power to invest at its discretion in capital stock, IFC would be in a position to- (1) Strengthen by its own investment the capital structure of enterprises; (2) Encourage private investors to join in with it in taking either preferred or common stock; (3) Share in the risks and profits of an enterprise in traditional form; (4) Negotiate investment terms on simpler and more acceptable lines; (5) Make more effective use of options and convertible securities; and (6) Cooperate with local institutions in the development of capital markets by underwriting issues of convertible securities and share capital for public subscription.

IFC would not go into the business of selling shares to the public; that would be done by private bankers and brokers. IFC would merely stand by with a

guarantee that the required funds would be forthcoming as needed, pending the time when public investors could subscribe to and pay for such shares. 

10. IFC management feels strongly that it should not exercise control over, nor participate in management of, private business. It has not participated in management or even named a director on the board of any enterprise in which it has invested. It would not ordinarily wish to vote stock held by it. IFC's purposes would be served it it could invest in stock which it would be prohibited from voting except in cases of jeopardy or other special circumstances.¹ It is emphasized that the sole purpose in requesting this right is to enable it more adequately to meet the needs of good private business in forms more acceptable to businessmen and investors.

THE NEED FOR MORE EQUITY -THE DEBT-EQUITY RATIO 

11. The need for more equity capital in developing countries is continually evident. Sponsors tend to set up proposals with excessive debt because such financing is usually the easiest to obtain. Furthermore, such debt is frequently of short term as such loans are more available than those of longer maturities. 

New sources of equity capital have not yet been cultivated and developed to keep pace with the growing supply of loans. It is worthy of note that many enterprises are financed in the developing countries with a much higher ratio of debt to equity than would be acceptable in the more stable economies of the highly developed countries. 

12. Many preliminary applications to IFC have been unacceptable because of shortage of equity capital and reliance on loans, particularly short term, to a dangerous extent. Apart from these preliminary applications, there have been several important projects which were seriously studied and investigated but were declined because of an excessive ratio of debt to equity. 

13. It was mainly to meet this frequent problem of excessive debt that IFC started in 1959 to develop investments partly or wholly in long-term income notes, which carry no fixed interest but participate in profits and carry substantial stock option or conversion rights.2 These are the nearest approximation to equity investment available to IFC under the present articles. They need not be considered as conventional fixed-interest debt for the purpose of deciding on a sound capital structure. Because they impose no fixed charge, income notes help to strengthen the capital structure and remove the dangers of default during the early years when enterprises are getting underway. 

14. Although the income note technique is useful in certain situations, it is not widely known. Moreover it is only an attractive investment if it is combined with substantial equity features, and this fact involves the complications and difficulties referred to below.

DEVELOPMENT OF CAPITAL MARKETS AND UNDERWRITING OF EQUITY ISSUES 

15. Capital markets of considerable scope, with established securities, exchanges and investment banking organizations, have developed in a few of the countries in which IFC operates. But in most of the developing areas, facilities for underwriting, sale and trading of securities, or for giving standby commitments on issues are limited or nonexistent. 

16. IFC has made several investments 3 on condition that equity capital be raised by local public issue within some stipulated period. Even when a project seems likely to attract public subscriptions to its equity capital, the project cannot go ahead without assurance that the capital will in fact be subscribed. 

17. There may be substantial numbers of local people in a country willing to invest in shares of an enterprise; however, it is often the case that selling capital shares to the public requires a considerable period of time. The lack of facilities for underwriting often delays arrangements for prompt financing and progress on projects. 

18. Up until now IFC has been unable to give worthwhile help in developing such capital markets. If IFC had the right to invest in capital stock, this func-

19. The growth of capital markets is an important feature in economic development. The role of an underwriting institution is well shown by the experience of the Industrial Credit & Investment Corp. of India, Ltd. (ICICI). It was established in 1955 by the cooperation of the Indian Government, the World Bank and groups of private Indian, British, and United States investors. The chairman of ICICI in his annual statement for 1959 said: "In a mere 5 years of life, ICICI has emerged as the most important underwriting institution in India, and, in fact, almost the only underwriter of ordinary shares. Its underwriting operations amount to about Rs830 lacs [$16 million] of which nearly half have been in ordinary shares." [Italic added.] 

The ICICI report shows the total operations sanctioned by the end of 1959 were Rs2,050 lacs (over $40 million equivalent), of which half were represented by direct equity subscriptions and by underwriting of equity and debenture issues. 

20. Privately owned investment institutions have been established in several underdeveloped countries, some of them since IFC was formed. The extent to which they can underwrite or invest in equity is, however, limited in practice by the amount of their own equity capital, which is usually not large. On the other hand, their lending resources, either in local currency or in foreign currency lines of credit from sources such as the World Bank and the Development Loan Fund, are sometimes substantial. IFC has been handicapped in its attempts to cooperate with these local institutions because it cannot supply or underwrite equity capital.

DISADVANTAGES OF STOCK OPTIONS 

21. IFC has hitherto obtained some form of stock options in nearly all of its investments. Such rights, generally essential if IFC's investments are to be attractive for resale to private investors, have nevertheless been one of the main sources of resistance to IFC investment terms. 

22. There are many underdeveloped countries, including most of Latin America, the Middle East, and southeast Asia, where stock options and convertible securities are unfamiliar. Their established forms of investment are straight loans, often with mortgage security, or straight equity investment in preferred or common shares. There are other countries, such as the British Commonwealth countries and Argentina, where stock options and convertible securities are legally recognized and reasonably familiar. 

23. Options on shares, especially in new enterprises, may not establish any appreciable value for a number of years. In order, therefore, for IFC to have reasonable assurance that the options will be valuable, it is frequently necessary to insist that these be of long term, usually running until the final maturity date of the loan. However, many borrowers will not grant long-term stock options in return for long-term loans, which also carry fixed interest and foreign exchange obligations. If ÏFC were in a position itself to exercise stock options or conversion rights, it could prudently accept shorter terms for such rights. 

24. If IFC could invest in equity, it would not necessarily discard stock options. 

It would be free to use these options or to make investments partly or wholly in equity, as appropriate. It would also be in a position to meet the frequent desire of sponsors to limit the duration of options and conversion rights. 

25. Because IFC is investing dollars, foreign exchange problems arise in fixing the price for long-term stock options. If the option price is fixed in dollars and the local currency depreciates seriously, the value of the option may be correspondingly impaired. Conversely, borrowers frequently object to the price being fixed in local currency. This has led to complicated formulas for sharing the exchange risk between borrower and lender. Further complications arise in protecting option rights against dilution by future stock dividends or by stock issues at less than fair value. 

26. The difficulty of negotiating adequate stock options has forced IFC to seek additional income contingent on profits in order to make investments on terms likely to attract private investors. This also is complicated. 

CURRENCY PROBLEMS 

27. Having its capital in U.S. dollars, IFC is naturally reluctant to accept, in addition to the normal business risks, the long-term currency risk involved in making long-term nondollar loans. Hitherto, it has made two investments in 

Mexico, half repayable in Mexican pesos and half in dollars; one investment in 

Tanganyika, half in dollars and half in sterling; and one in southern Italy, half

in dollars and half in lire.¹ IFC was not intended to be solely a dollar-lending institution, but having its limited capital in dollars and being restricted by its articles to investments in the form of loans, it has inevitably made most of its loans repayable in dollars. 

28. Many borrowers in the underdeveloped countries, particularly those with weak currencies, have a natural preference for straight export financing in cases where foreign exchange is required. They are reluctant to incur long-term foreign exchange obligations which also carry long-term equity features. Several negotiations with both local and foreign sponsors of projects have broken down on this issue. 

29. In several cases in Asia and Latin America the foreign currency objection would not have arisen if the whole or part of the IFC investment could have been made in equity, possibly in participating preferred stock. Equity investments are expressed in, and receive their dividends in, local currencies, and thus involve no foreign exchange obligation to the enterprise concerned. Apart from specific known projects, it is safe to assume that many other good projects would have come to IFC if it could have mitigated the foreign currency difficulty through investment in equity.

PROBLEMS OF LOANS WITH EQUITY FEATURES 

30. The most common criticism of IFC's terms is that they provide both the protection of a creditor and the rewards of a stockholder. However, a similar allegation could be made with respect to convertible bonds, a form of financing widely used by leading companies in the United States and Europe. Nevertheless, it is difficult to have these terms understood and reasonably appraised by sponsors, particularly those with limited financial experience. They sometimes overlook certain pertinent facts: that IFC furnishes long-term money at rates usually well below those prevailing within the country; frequently it takes a junior position; and that it will receive benefits in addition to its fixed interest only if the stockholders get considerably higher rewards. Furthermore, since the typical investment is a loan bearing fixed interest, some sponsors attempt to calculate in equally definite terms the cost of profit participations and options. 

Usually their evaluations are overoptimistic, and thus they calculate the possible return to IFC as unduly high, not realizing the uncertainty of future earnings.

On the other hand, it is widely recognized that prospective earnings on equity, the amount of dividends and the market value of shares are unpredictable, and sponsors do not calculate the cost of straight equity money, nor do they object to the prospects that the investing shareholder may reap large rewards. 

31. The combination of features mentioned above has proved to be a major obstacle to the acceptance of IFC terms. Where negotiations have been successfully concluded, the subsequent legal arrangements are often complicated. Apart from option problems, the formulation of additional income contingent on profits involves difficult questions of accounting, currencies, usury laws, and taxes. 

SALES OF INVESTMENTS 

32. IFC has a special mandate to revolve its portfolio and stimulate sales to private investors. But in performing this function, which is vital to the concept of IFC, it is impeded by its inability to exercise stock options or conversion rights. 

If, like private investment institutions and most public financing institutions, IFC were able to do this with a view to subsequent sales of the resulting shares, its task would be greatly simplified. When these rights are approaching their expiration, other investors can exercise them and hold the resulting shares long enough to find a market. IFC cannot do this.

GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS 

33. Substantial loan funds of national and international public lending agencies, supplemented by suppliers' credits, are available to private enterprise in developing countries. This emphasizes the need for a source of public equity capital which can work with and stimulate the flow of private equity funds, thus complementing the availability of loans. Furthermore, it is obvious that public institutions can finance only a small fraction of the needs of good business. 34. As a result of its operations on a modest scale over an effective operating life of over 4 years, IFC can claim to have established a reputation for careful and thorough investigation of the merits of projects, and it is beginning to attract private financial capital to participate in some of its investments. These transactions and the widening number of contacts with investors desirous of getting into the foreign field give confidence that IFC can secure private participation at an increasing rate. 

35. There is a great need for venture capital for private enterprise in the developing countries and to a large extent it should take the form of equity investment. 

IFC alone among the public lending agencies fixes its terms primarily with a view to attracting participation of private long-term investment funds. It is meeting with success in stimulating such private investment, but if it is to make a more substantial contribution it needs to have the use of the recognized tools of investment in order to do the most effective job. the charter limitation on the purchase of capital stock has severely restricted the ability of the Corporation to carry out its primary function of stimulating private enterprise in the less developed areas.

The original reason for including a prohibition against equity investment in the articles of agreement was to insure that the Corporation would not, as a result of stock ownership, have management responsibilities in the private enterprises in which it invested. Such responsibilities properly lie with the private owners of the enterprise. 

This concept is a sound one and remains applicable today. However, safeguards have been incorporated in the proposed amendment to insure that the Corporation will not become involved in the operational or management decisions of the enterprises in which it invests. 

The form of the proposed amendment to the articles of agreement is embodied in the proposed resolution of the Board of Governors of the International Finance Corporation. It proposes that article III, section 2, of the Corporation's articles-the sense of which I described to you a moment ago—would be deleted, and a new section 2 would be substituted, reading simply:

The Corporation may make investments of its funds in such form or forms as it may deem appropriate in the circumstances. 

In order to safeguard the Corporation's role in exercising voting rights attached to capital stock which it acquires, subsection (iv) of article III, section 3, which now reads: 

The Corporation shall not assume responsibility for managing any enterprise in which it has investedwould be amended by adding: *** and shall not exercise voting rights for such purpose or for any other purpose which, in its opinion, properly is within the scope of managerial control. 

This formulation, in my judgment, would achieve the purpose of the original prohibition on the purchase of capital stock. Yet it would also permit the Corporation to take the necessary steps to protect its interests in the event it is legally required, as a stockholder, to vote on such matters as corporate reorganization, increase of capitalization, etc.

The proposed amendment represents a desirable and logical evolution in the development of the Corporation. The National Advisory 

Council on International Monetary and Financial Problems has recommended its adoption. It is in the interest of the United States,

as well as in the interest of the free world as a whole, to improve the ability of the Corporation to carry out its task of promoting productive private enterprise in the developing countries. The proposed amendment is essential for this purpose. 

H.R. 6765 would give me authority, as U.S. Governor of the IFC, to vote in favor of the proposed amendment, and I earnestly recommend its approval by the Congress.

Mr. VANIK. Now I have another question. Equity investment has some patent objectives. For example, when the Corporation agrees to buy a certain quantity of shares of common stock in certain enterprises, is it not, in effect, becoming an underwriter for the investment? 

Is there not some danger that this might serve to trump up the sale of such stock which otherwise might not be salable?

In other words, would not the quality of the stock in the market be raised, or certainly favorably affected by the fact that the Corporation has made this kind of equity investment? 

Secretary DILLON. I would think, yes, and I would think that would be a favorable reason for doing this. The major problem with private investment in the underdeveloped areas is that there are no capital markets and there is a great need to develop them, and this would be of help, I think, in that area. Certainly because of this amendment there would be no pressure on the Corporation to make bad investments. I think they would only favor companies that were well managed, and it would draw more local money into private enterprise and equity investment in private enterprise, and I think that is a good thing. 

Mr. VANIK. Mr. Secretary, wouldn't that impose a grave responsibility on the Corporation, because relying upon that, a great many other investors are going to be induced into a market in which they otherwise might not participate? There are many people today who buy stock, particularly in these over-the-counter issues, and they rely on the judgment of the underwriter. They assume that a high quality underwriter wouldn't underwrite anything that isn't good.

So insofar as we authorize this practice for the Corporation, aren't we giving it greater responsibility? 

Secretary DILLON. I think the Corporation does have a responsibility. I think it is a responsibility that it should assume. In most of these countries, of course, there are no stock exchanges or over-thecounter markets, or anything of that nature, and most of these private investments have to be achieved by essentially individual negotiations with the people who will make the purchases, and I think this would stimulate that. But it is not a question of just advertising in the newspaper and having people come, because in these countries that isn't done. 

Mr. VANIK. Another point: Can the owner of common stock everywhere divest himself of managerial responsibility? Aren't there some several countries at least that impose upon the holder of the common stock a high degree of responsibility as a result of stockownership? 

Secretary DILLON. If there is any such country that requires that, that provision would have to be waived before the IFC could make an investment in that country.

Mr. VANIK. But to your knowledge, there are no countries that would impose penalties for either acts of misfeasance or nonfeasance relating to stockownership and the failure to exercise managerial responsibility?

Secretary DILLON. No, there are none, and there are specific provisions in the charter of the International Finance Corporation that none of its members are responsible for that sort of thing. 

Mr. VANIK. Now, my next question is, if the Corporation acquires a substantial stock interest in a given corporation and desists from

managerial control, therefore neutralizing a good part of stockownership and equity, could this not be used by a minority group in control of the corporation to perpetuate its control, contrary to the wishes of the majority of the stockholders?

Secretary DILLON. Well, again, this presupposes an area where there are general stock exchanges, where you have stockholders that are totally separate from management, which is not the case in underdeveloped countries. I couldn't imagine IFC having a very large interest. But say they had 25 percent interest, there would be 75 percent left, and whoever had the majority of that 75 percent each year would be able to control the company and elect the controlling officers, because IFC would not vote, so the majority would always control.

Mr. VANIK. Of course in this type of investment there is likely to be this kind of struggle between controlling interests because the corporations would be new and fighting for existence, and control could be a very serious and important issue. 

On another point, what assurance have we that these stock investments would be limited to the point 4 countries which are those of great need?

Secretary DILLON. Well, there is in the charter the provision that the Corporation should put major emphasis on those areas. They have in the past and they will in the future. If they did not they would be violating their charter, and it would be up to the Governors of the International Finance Corporation to take care of that, which they would do.

Mr. VANIK. Would you have an objection to an amendment which would limit this authority to the point 4 countries? 

Secretary DILLON. I think the principal difficulty with an amendment, Mr. Vanik, is that 27 countries have already approved the amendment in this form, and if any one country asks for a change everybody has to approve it all over again. I think it would cause a great deal of difficulty. An expression of intent that that is the way it should be used would provide no difficulty, but I think an amendment would.

Mr. VANIK. I have one final question. If you accumulate in the course of this undertaking a portfolio rather extensively loaded with these common stocks, what will be the position of the Corporation, what will be the capacity of the Corporation to borrow further capital on these common stocks, as distinguished from having available a portfolio of mortgages and debentures?

Secretary DILLON. Well, the Corporation has not borrowed on its portfolio, and I doubt- 

Mr. VANIK. You have that authority? 

Secretary DILLON. They have that authority, but I doubt, with the type of investment that they have, that they would do it. I think the advantage of having the common stocks will be that the portfolio will be more readily salable, and the objective of the Corporation is to turn over their investments. Once an investment gets going and does well they hope to find a purchaser for their interest and turn it over entirely to the local private investment, and then move on to something else. And with stock interest it is my opinionand certainly it is the opinion of the Corporation and all of the people who have operated with it at the World Bank—that they will be far

better able to sell their investments and to turn them over in that way as soon as they get going.

Mr. VANIK. You plan to resell the common stock so that you will, in effect, be carrying on the work of an underwriter and distributing these common stocks rather widely for sale, principally among American citizens who undertake stock investment. 

You said foreign countries don't have markets in many of these places, so wouldn't the emphasis be on making these sales among 

American citizens?

Secretary DILLON. No; it would not. The whole objective of this is to stimulate investment in local underdeveloped countries, and once a particular investment has begun to be profitable, the International Finance Corporation would attempt to dispose of its investment, generally locally. They would, of course, at the same time try to dispose of their whole investment. 

I don't think that they would want to make a practice of selling just a portion of an investment in an individual company that was in common stock and not selling the rest of it. They would probably try to sell the debenture investment at the same time. 

Mr. VANIK. I am concerned with adding to the plight of American capital by having a widescale market trumped up and underwritten by the International Finance Corporation. 

Secretary DILLON. No; I don't think there would be any such possibility, Mr. Vanik. 

Mr. VANIK. Thank you very much, Mr. Secretary.

Mr. PATMAN. Mr. Chairman, may I read a brief excerpt from the hearings when we had hearings on these bills? It is just a brief one, Mr. Chairman. 

When Mr. Humphrey, Secretary of the Treasury, was testifying on page 10 of the hearings of July 11 and 14, 1959, on H.R. 6228, I asked him this question:

Mr. PATMAN. Will the people in all countries be allowed to participate in this IFC? 

Secretary HUMPHREY. I think anybody that wants to participate, where capital is not available, where it is desirable to build up backward areas to carry out the security of the program, they can present an application. 

Mr. PATMAN. That would include in the United States?

Secretary HUMPHREY. I think so. 

Thank you, Mr. Chairman.

The CHAIRMAN. Mr. Reuss. 

Mr. REUSS. Mr. Chairman. 

Mr. Secretary, the Banking and Currency Committee is, in a sense, the father of the International Finance Corporation, since legislation affecting it comes before us. And about all we see is the annual report of the International Finance Corporation, uness there is specific legislation.

While the annual reports, including the last one, issued September 29, 1960, are, by and large, well done, I notice that neither in the text nor in the balance sheet and profit and loss statement is there material which tells the reader the status of these loans. Have any of them been found worthless and charged off? One can't tell. Are the payments in arrears? Again, while Price-Waterhouse reports that the accounting system follows the general accounting procedures, we don't get this information. I am wondering if it would be possible to

pass on the request I am making to our U.S. Directors on the International Finance Corporation, that future reports contain some current information on the status of these investments. 

Secretary DILLON. I would be glad to do that, and I think it is entirely proper. 

Mr. VANIK. Mr. Chairman, will Mr. Reuss yield on that point? 

Mr. REUSS. I yield.

Mr. VANIK. That was the very type of information I was hoping we could have before we vote on this bill, a report comparable to that which we get from the Export-Import Bank, the loans which have been granted and the status of the loan with respect to payment and interest rate.
