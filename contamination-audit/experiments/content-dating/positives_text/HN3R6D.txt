PREFACE 

The purpose of this book is to provide a general workable vocabulary for that vast number of persons who have finished grammar school and may never enter college. That group may include high or preparatory school students, secretaries, business men or women, home keepers; and particularly business school students whose fate will so largely depend on the knowledge of words. This book will appeal especially to those whose vocabulary may have shrunk from disuse or the careless usage and repetition of overworked words. With the exception of certain "catch" words, the simple vocabulary taught in the elementary school, and so frequently found in spellers for mature minds, has purposely been omitted. By that token also, the extremely difficult or unusual words in our language have been omitted. 

The special technical lists in Section III are designed to complement this non-technical background. Immediately on beginning a definite line of work the student should concentrate on whichever one of these lists corresponds with his business. It is obvious that, thus fortified, he will avoid many of the embarrassing pitfalls into which the tyro is otherwise almost sure to stumble. 

It would be a useless waste of time to attempt to learn all of this special terminology while mastering the general lists in Sections I and II. Many of these technicalities would never be used, excepting the special list which would apply to one's particular line of business. 

This book does not take the place of the dictionary; therefore, the frequently used foreign phrases and standard abbreviations, which should be found in any standard dictionary, are not included here. 

Apropos of rules, etc., the author conducted a test with twenty-five students- fifteen high school graduates and ten college graduates and asked them to recite 

---

some of the spelling rules previously learned. The response was a hazy, imperfect impression of any spelling rules and practically no remembrance of the exceptions thereto. The decision was then made in compiling this book to omit as far as possible all arbitrary spelling rules. The following rule, for instance, illustrates the point: "The final consonant is not doubled in derivatives formed by the addition of a termination beginning with a vowel, when (a) a diphthong or digraph precedes the final consonant of the word, or (b) when the accent of the primitive word falls on any other syllable than the last, or (c) when the word ends in two different consonants. 

Exceptions. In a few words ending in g the final consonant is doubled, that it may not be pronounced like j."

The folly of attempting to recall such a jumble instead of immediately consulting the dictionary, is too apparent for comment.

It is, therefore, the author's belief that the only practical way to master the spelling of words is not by rule, but by rote, by constant repetition and association, as a child learns to talk. Tediousness in learning may, however, to a large extent be overcome by combining sections of the alphabetical list with the homonyms, catch spellings, etc. In compiling this book, the effort has consistently been made to have the presentation as simple and logical as possible. 

Grateful acknowledgment is given for the help_received from the following books: "Words," Gregg Co.; 

Eldridge's Business Speller; Gilmartin's Business Speller; 

Reigner's "Secretarial Training," etc.

TO THE TEACHER 

While the presentation of this subject must be left to the discretion of the instructor, the author has found the following method helpful:

Twenty-five words are assigned to the class. At the next spelling lesson any student may be called upon to dictate this list of words, with special emphasis on the pronunciation, while the others write them. Papers are exchanged and another student is assigned to spell the list and stress the proper division of the word.* Papers are then returned to the owners who check their misspelled words. Definitions and sentence application are then discussed orally. The latter may be further developed by requiring the student to submit a written sentence for each of the twenty-five words. 

The new group of words is written on the board, and attention is directed to any peculiarity of pronunciation or spelling. When seventy-five words (three lessons) have been assigned, a ten-word written test is given, including spelling and definition. These are corrected by the instructor personally.

By this method the amount of correcting by the instructor is minimized and the student benefits by the practice of self supervision, so valuable in his business training. *The separation of the word by syllables, etc., at the end of the written line.

THE POSSESSIVE 

Since the dictionary does not give the possessive, it is deemed wise in this instance to insert a simple, definite rule for the formation of the possessive, as follows:

The possessive of all singular nouns (and all plural nouns not ending in s) is formed by adding an apostrophe, followed by s. For the *All compound nouns ending in ful add s on last syllable, as - handfuls, mouthfuls, spoonfuls, etc. Note: Letters, numbers and mathematical signs form the plural by adding apostrophe and s; as, x's, 4's, +'s, etc.

DIVISION OF WORDS AT END OF LINE 

1. Words should be divided only between syllables; as, consignment, re-store, flot-sam, reflec-tion, pro-ceed. 

2. Monosyllables should not be divided; as, through, much, reach, child, passed, crushed, screamed, slipped. 

3. When a single vowel forms a syllable it should be on the first line; as, medi-tate, hesi-tate, etc. 

4. A single letter should not be separated from the rest of the word. Such words as enough, abroad, ahead, should not be divided. * Differentiate between the plural only and the possessive.

THE HYPHEN 

5 5. Do not divide short words and never carry over to the next line two letters only. Such words as sorry, until, rated, ejected, every, heaven, should not be divided. 

6. When a consonant is doubled before a suffix, divide between the doubled.letters; as, grin-ning, slip-ping, stop-ping. 

Note: Divide after the doubled letter, however, if the root word ends with a doubled letter; as, pass-ing, jazz-ing, roll-ing. 

7. Do not divide numbers, or a word already hyphenated. 

USE THE HYPHEN 

1. When two or more words are used as a single adjective before a noun; as, a red-and-green fence; an up-to-date style. But when such words follow the noun, the hyphen is not used; as, the fence was red and green; the style was up to date. 

Note: Adverb modifiers (except when followed by participles) are not hyphenated; as, an unusually long program; a very beautiful coat; but, a well-selected program; a beautifully-made coat; a far-reaching policy. 

2. In compound numbers, below one hundred; as, twenty-one, forty-two; and in fractions; as, two-fifths; but, five hundred, two thousand, etc. 

3. With ex; as, ex-officio, ex-president. 

4. When a numeral adjective precedes rate, or hand; as, first-rate, second-hand, etc. 

Examples: 

A ten-foot pole; up-to-the-minute methods; a three-weeks' vacation; twenty-five; thirty-two; one-tenth; three-fourths; twosixteenths; five hundred; he was up to the minute; his vacation was three weeks; the pole was ten feet long; it was a beautifully wrought piece of work; Mr. Jones, the ex-president, will be there; the absent-minded man did a first-rate job on the second-hand desk.

SECTION III Technical Lists 

This section contains the terminology of many important professions and lines of business. 

The purpose of these lists is to fortify the beginner against the "boners" which are the invariable concomitant of his entrance into business. 

These words are not to be learned in conjunction with the general lists in sections I and II.
