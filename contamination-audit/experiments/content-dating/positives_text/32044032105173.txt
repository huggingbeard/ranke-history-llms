FOREWORD 

The Smith-Hughes Act of 1917 provided for the use of Federal funds to promote vocational education in the States. During the past 20 years extensive vocational education programs for agriculture, home economics, and trades and industries have been developed in the, States. Labor, employers, lay groups, and public school authorities on local, State, and national levels have cooperated in the promotion of those training activities.

The effectiveness and efficiency of the vocational training methods and techniques which have been developed since 1917 have resulted in demands for aid and assistance in meeting some of the training needs of occupations not originally included under the provisions of the Smith-Hughes Act. The passage of the George-Deen Act by 

Congress in 1936 supplemented the provisions of the Smith-Hughes Act and increased the scope of vocational training activities for which 

Federal aid might be given.

The need for training for public-service occupations has been apparent for some time and training activities have been sponsored and developed by organizations and individuals interested in that problem. From time to time the Office of Education has been requested by public-service organizations to advise and aid in the development of training programs for public-service employees. A bulletin entitled "Training for Public-Service Occupations," which deals in a general way with public-service training problems is now available. 

Bulletin No. 155, entitled "Fire Fighting," deals with training problems of fire fighters and has been available since 1931. More recently, studies have been made of problems of training personnel of the steward's department in Federal penal and correctional institutions, and also of training custodial officers in such institutions. 

The request for the preparation of a bulletin on training for the police service came from L. V. Jenkins, Portland, Oreg., then president of the International Association of Chiefs of Police. He was authorized to take such action by resolution of that association at its annual meeting in 1936.

The purpose of this publication is threefold: (1) To show the great variety of work which a police officer is required to do, (2) to point out the ways by which police training programs may be organized, 1 Vocational Education Bulletin No. 192, Office of Education.

He will be provided with opportunities for training which will help him to exercise good judgment in dealing with practical problems and situations. As a result of this training, supplemented by efficient and intelligent supervision, he will learn to work cooperatively with others toward the common objectives of law enforcement, the preservation of peace, and the maintenance of law and order.

THE NEED FOR TRAINING IN THE POLICE SERVICE 

In the past police protection was confined primarily to the enforcement of law and order and the apprehension of criminals. These were accomplished, in the main, by a show of armed force combined with the resourcefulness and skill of the police officers. Ability to do the job was acquired through years of practical experience. A man was given or required to purchase a uniform and other essential equipment; assigned to duty, and put to work with little or no training. Organized training and instruction were the exceptions rather than the rule. 

As cities have grown and municipal and private activities have become more complex, crime has increased and the need for police protection has been amplified. The development of the automobile, telephone, radio, teletype, and other means of communication has made it possible for police departments to make rapid apprehension of criminals. Also, criminals find it quite within the realm of possibility to perpetrate a crime and be many miles away from the scene within a

CHAPTER II ORGANIZATION AND OPERATION OF POLICE TRAINING PROGRAMS INTRODUCTORY STATEMENT 

In this chapter an attempt is made to point out some of the more important fundamental characteristics of an efficient training program, and also to give information concerning how a number of police training programs have been organized in different parts of the country and how those programs are operated. 

ESSENTIAL CHARACTERISTICS OF AN EFFICIENT TRAINING PROGRAM 

Experience in the organization and operation of training programs in a great variety of occupations furnishes justification for the statement that successful programs--those which are accomplishing the purposes for which they were set up possess certain characteristics regardless of the specific occupations for which the training is given. 

In making efficiency surveys of existing programs, it is customary to consider anywhere from 10 to 25 so-called efficiency factors, but for the purposes of this discussion it seems desirable to consider only what are generally recognized as the four principal characteristics of an efficient training program. These are as follows: 

1. The group enrolled in the training program should be properly selected. 

2. The instructor should be competent, not only from the standpoint of knowledge and skill regarding the occupation for which training is given, but also from the standpoint of his ability to teach what he knows and can do. 

3. The course of study should be based upon what is commonly referred to in vocational education as "functioning subject matter." 

4. The working conditions should be sufficiently favorable to make it possible to do a good job of training, provided the three requirements previously mentioned are satisfied.

These four fundamental requirements or conditions which are realized to a considerable degree in every satisfactory program of vocational training are believed to be of sufficient importance to justify some discussion of each of them.

A Selected Group. 

It is obvious that a program of vocational training for the Police Service should deal with groups of men who are: (1) already on the job as policemen or (2) who are definitely headed for active duty in the 15

For the greatest efficiency, common. sense would dictate that police officers who have had a certain amount of experience in the service should be handled together in a segregated group rather than in a mixed group made up of experienced officers and prospective officers. The difficulties which would be experienced by an instructor in attempting to handle any unit of training for the Police Service with a heterogeneous group would appear to be obvious. It seems unnecessary in this connection, therefore, to do more than call attention to the fact that the effectiveness of the training would be heavily discounted by attempting to handle in a single group men with widely varying backgrounds of experience for whom

Too much emphasis cannot be attached, therefore, to the importance of dealing with selected instead of heterogeneous groups in training police officers. If the value of this principle is not clearly recognized and acted upon, the efficiency of the entire program will be reduced. 

Qualified Instructors. 

It is a fallacy to assume that a person who has had little or no experience in an occupation can be worth very much as an instructor of a vocational training class for that occupation, regardless of his other qualifications. Aside from the practical difficulties which such an instructor would experience in attempting to teach his subject,

it would be extremely difficult for him to command the respect of his students, who would recognize that he was trying to prepare them for work in a field in which he himself was not proficient. Adequate experience in the police service is, therefore, an indispensable requirement for an instructor of police groups. (See fig. 17.)

In addition to adequate experience in police work (see fig. 18), it is essential that the instructor know enough about training methods and instructional techniques to be able to do a reasonably good job of teaching what he knows to men who know less than he does. This means that the instructor must fulfill two specific requirements: (a) he must be sufficiently experienced and sufficiently well informed regarding the practical work of the police service to have a foundation 1

Functioning Course of Study. 

There are two principal methods of developing courses of study in the field of vocational training-the "swivel chair" method, and the "job analysis" method. Where an instructor sits down and tries to imagine what ought to be included in a course of study, the instructional procedure he follows is commonly referred to in vocational education as the "swivel chair" method. Very often training courses are formulated on the basis of hunches, imagination, and by reference to what someone else may have done, quite independent of any

VOCATIONAL EDUCATION 

consideration of whether or not the other person's program or course of study may have been based upon swivel chair methods. From the standpoint of vocational training, the swivel chair method is so unscientific in its approach to the problem and so ineffective with respect to results that it merits little serious consideration. 

When a definite attempt is made to identify the essential facts which should be considered, in setting up a training program (see fig. 19), the procedure followed is often referred to as the "job analysis" approach. 

One reason why the job analysis method gives superior results is that it follows more closely the scientific method which, after all, consists

There is, therefore, considerable justification for stating that the job analysis approach is, theoretically, the best one to follow in connection with the development of a training program for any occupation, and it is believed to be superior to any other method that has thus far been devised for setting up a training program for the police service. Chapter V of this bulletin contains a fairly complete job analysis of police work, and that analysis, properly used, can be utilized as a basis for innumerable specific training courses or units of instruction.

In general, it may be stated that any course of study or any unit of training which is derived from a fairly good job analysis of an occupation is likely to result in a training program based upon functioning information rather than upon someone's imagination or upon mistakes that have been made by someone else.

During the past 10 years considerable progress has been made in developing vocational training programs for a number of fields of public service employment, and programs have been operated for fire and police department personnel, waterworks employees, custodians and janitors of public schools, inspectors of weights and measures, and stewards and workers employed in the culinary departments of penal and correctional institutions. 

On the basis of the experience gained in conducting training programs for public service occupations it may be stated that: 

1. The principal need for training appears to be in the field of "in-service" training and "refresher" courses for employed personnel. 

2. Much of the training is preferably carried on within the employing organization. This is true, especially where the organization is a large one, as, for example, a police department in a large city. 

3. Effective service can be made available to smaller communities through

VOCATIONAL EDUCATION PRINCIPAL TYPES OF ORGANIZATIONS AT THE PRESENT TIME 

At the present time there appear to be six principal types of organization for providing training for the Police Service. They are as follows: 

1. National Police Academy of the Federal Bureau of Investigation. 

2. State police training programs. 

3. Zone schools within a State. 

4. Short intensive courses or institutes. 

5. Itinerant instructors. 

6. Local police-training programs.

Each of these types is briefly described in the following paragraphs. 

National Police Academy of the Federal Bureau of Investigation. 

This school offers 3 months of intensive training to carefully selected groups of 35 to 40 experienced law-enforcement officers. 

Admission to the school is based upon written application made by the head of the police agency, and the qualifications for admission of such candidates are carefully weighed. As a result of the procedure followed in the selection of candidates for training, it is probable that no police school in the country enrolls as highly selected a group of men as found in the Federal Bureau of Investigation Police School. Instruction given covers practical and technical phases of law-enforcement work, and other subjects, taught by members of the Federal Bureau of Investigation staff and special instructors from outside of the organization. No charge is made for tuition or supplies, and every possible opportunity is provided for the men to acquire a thoroughgoing knowledge of the latest scientific and technical procedures that have been found to be effective in crime detection and law enforcement.

The objectives of the school, however, are: (1) To equip the men enrolled with the latest scientific and technical knowledge appertaining to the Police Service; (2) to equip them through a course in "training methods" so that they will be able to function not only as technical experts, but as instructors of others when they return to their own departments; (3) to professionalize the police service; and (4) to cooperate with the graduates in operating their own schools.

CHAPTER III TRAINING INSTRUCTORS FOR THE POLICE SERVICE INTRODUCTORY STATEMENT 

Training in some form is always being carried on in practically every type of organization. In all cases where new recruits are added to a working group, the procedure under which they acquire the knowledge and skill necessary for them to function on their jobs is training. 

This is true whether or not the problem of training is recognized as such by those in authority. This training may be provided in two principal ways: 

1. By the "pick-up" method which may be understood to include having the learner work with an experienced man. 

2. Through some form of organized training. 

For the purposes of this discussion, the term "organized training" refers to a training program which is: (1) Planned in advance to accomplish pre-determined objectives indicated by an analysis of the policeman's duties; (2) operated on such a schedule that continuity of training is realized with a group or groups under instruction; and (3) made up of definite, coherent units each of which is an integral part of the whole police-training program. 

Wherever organized training is set up, instructors are needed, and it may be fairly stated that the efficiency and all-round success of a training program will depend, to a great extent, upon the skill, technical knowledge, and understanding of the instructors, and the degree to which they can do a good job in meeting their instructional responsibilities.

STATEMENT OF THE PROBLEM 

Two principal problems are involved in securing police training in structors as follows: 

1. The training of men thoroughly experienced in police work to serve as instructors of practical and technical subjects. 

2. The training of qualified men to serve as conference leaders with experienced groups.

While instructors of practical and technical subjects and conference leaders would in all probability be classified as instructors or teachers in connection with a police training program, it should be recognized that there are two principal subdivisions of a complete training program: (1) The training or "breaking in" of new recruits, and (2) the 31

VOCATIONAL EDUCATION 

upgrading and further development of employed personnel. These two phases are indicated by the types of instructors or leaders needed. 

The analysis of police work as set forth in Chapter V of this bulletin indicates that there is a large amount of practical knowledge and technical information which should be acquired by a new recruit in the police service before he is qualified to serve as a functioning member of the organization. The development of the necessary "doing ability," the acquiring of the necessary technical information, and the ability to apply that information on the job is best accomplished by having the new recruits instructed in an organized way concerning the elements of their jobs. For each phase of the training, a highly qualified instructor is needed. Obviously, the instructor of green men or raw recruits should be thoroughly experienced in police work and should have demonstrated that he can measure up, in all important respects, to the demands of the job. In addition he should have had definite training in how to teach others so that they may develop the skill and "doing ability" needed and so that they will be able to apply intelligently the necessary technical knowledge in the performance of their work.

The second type of instructor-the conference leader-should, if possible, be a man experienced in police work. However, in addition to having been trained to use the best teaching methods suited to the instructional work in which he is engaged, he should have been trained in the use of special methods particularly adapted to educational work with men experienced in the police service. In other words, he needs the same type of training which is given to men who function as conference leaders with groups of foremen and supervisors in industrial and business organizations. 

It should be recognized that the conference leader, as such, is not an instructor in the ordinary sense. His work is done with men who are already more or less competent so far as the ordinary phases of their work are concerned and his job as a conference leader is to assist groups of experienced and competent men to further develop their ability to use judgment on their jobs. This and other points regarding the problems of training intructors and leaders will be further explained in succeeding paragraphs 

THE SELECTION OF INSTRUCTORS 

In chapter II, a qualified instructor was mentioned as one of the four principal success factors in a training program for police groups. 

It is, therefore, of the greatest importance that prospective instructors be carefully selected with regard to the degree to which they possess the basic qualifications considered necessary for good instructors. 

Perhaps the principal and most important qualification of a prospective

instructor is that he know his job thoroughly. In other words, he should be an expert in his field of work and should have demonstrated his ability to meet all reasonable demands which might be made upon a police officer.

The other characteristic which should be considered in the selection of a prospective instructor may in general be indicated by the statement that he should be a "well-balanced" individual. This qualification is difficult to explain, but what is meant by the term "wellbalanced" individual is indicated by W. B. Pitkin's writings concerning "personality." According to Dr. Pitkin, a man's "personality," so-called, may be regarded as the resultant or combined effect of four principal groups of characteristics which any individual may be expected to possess in varying degrees. These four groups of characteristics are as follows: 1 

1. The way a man deals with things. 

2. The way a man deals with ideas. 

3. The way a man deals with other people. 

4. The way a man deals with himself.

These four factors or group of characteristics are briefly discussed in the following paragraphs.

The way a man deals with things is indicated by the way in which he does everyday things connected with his job. If he is a mechanic it is reflected in his skill in the performance of his work and the way he handles tools and materials. Some people who have had a great deal of schooling are more or less helpless in doing even the simplest practical things in an expert manner, such as making minor adjustments on an automobile or keeping things in repair around a house. 

A man who has a certain amount of ability to deal with things intelligently or, in other words, to use his hands with some degree of skill, would measure up fairly well under the first factor mentioned. 

The way a man deals with ideas is meant to suggest the degree to which he possesses real intelligence or "brains" as distinguished from the number of years he spent in school. From another angle, ability to deal with ideas means the ability to think straight or to reason things out clearly and logically from cause to effect and to arrive at sound conclusions when all of the elements of a problem are known. 

It would seem quite clear that an instructor needs to possess this ability to a considerable degree, and the possession by a police officer of the highest skill imaginable, as, for example in marksmanship or in athletics, would not serve as a satisfactory substitute for the possession of this characteristic.

What is meant by the ability to deal with people appears to be quite obvious. It is sometimes referred to as "social intelligence." In

THE PRINCIPAL OBJECTIVES IN TRAINING AN INSTRUCTOR 

The purpose in the paragraphs immediately following is to set forth in simple form just what the aims or objectives of an instructor training course for police officers should be.

The first aim to be achieved, or the first objective, is to develop in the prospective instructor an appreciation of the difficulties of a learner. One of the principal methods used by experienced teacher trainers to accomplish this objective is to place the prospective teacher in the position of a learner on a particular piece of work which is entirely new to him and to bring out by means of demonstrations some of the difficulties experienced by a learner in acquiring a definite bit of new knowledge or a new skill. 

If the prospective instructor has not consciously done any teaching, this appreciation of the difficulties of a learner and the confusion of the learner's thinking which may prevail under an instructor who does not know how to do a good teaching job, is one of the first necessary steps in training an occupationally competent man to teach to others those things concerning which he is fully competent. 

The second objective of a teacher-training course for police officers is to give the prospective instructor an appreciation of what a teach-

ing job consists and to provide him with functioning information relative to the principal teaching methods that may be applied to his work after he has learned how to use them. This objective can be attained through demonstrations by the teacher trainer, followed by attempts of the prospective instructor to do a job of teaching with a green or inexperienced man, using small units of instruction. In this way, the prospective instructor will be brought to an appreciation of what is meant by a good job of teaching and will be in possession of the most important information he will need to do a good job of teaching. 

The third objective in teacher training is to have the prospective instructors develop their ability to use the functioning information given to them in connection with work on the preceding objective and make a beginning in developing their skill as instructors. This objective is best accomplished by having the prospective instructor do some practice teaching under the immediate supervision of the teacher trainer who should supply constructive criticism regarding each demonstration.

The fourth objective in teacher training should be to develop in the prospective instructor the ability to plan specific lessons. When an instructor has developed a fairly high degree of skill in teaching, it becomes somewhat less necessary for him to work out detailed lesson plans.

The fifth teacher training objective is to develop ability on the part of the prospective instructor to plan a progressive series of lessons. 

In connection with this objective, he should be brought to a point where he understands that a unit of instruction may consist of a series of separate lessons. These separate lessons should be set up in a progressive order so that after the completion of the series of lessons the individual will have been trained in an orderly and progressive manner to a point where he can be said to have mastered a definite unit of training. 

The sixth objective in the instructor training course is to develop in the prospective teacher the ability to plan training courses with respect to known objectives, using an analysis as the basis for a progressive series of lessons in practical and technical subjects, the units of which are so arranged that the whole experience for the learner will have continuity and will increase his mastery of the subject. 

If this plan is followed, his training will be progressive and coherent rather than heterogeneous and confused. The analysis found in chapter V of this bulletin is especially intended for use in connection. with this teacher training objective.

The seventh and last of the principal teacher training objectives is the development of ability on the part of the prospective teacher to work out organized instructional material for use in training courses, which the instructor will conduct. Here, as under the preceding

objective, an analysis is the basic material which is likely to be of value. It should be the aim of the teacher trainer to help the prospective instructor develop judgment in expanding items found in the analysis for use in connection with his teaching. The teacher trainer, if he is a thoroughly competent and experienced man in his field, will be fully aware of the fact that one of the dangers to be guarded against is the tendency on the part of instructors to write up too much detailed information to be mimeographed or printed and used in lieu of other methods and educational procedures which may be more suitable in connection with police training. Closely associated with this danger, is the danger that the prospective instructors will tend to overdevelop lecture material and later to overwork the lecture method in the police schools in which they teach. This is not intended to convey the thought that the lecture method is not suitable for certain training objectives, but rather to call attention to the fact that the lecture method is the easiest method to use and, that unless it is supplemented by some definite checks, the instructor who uses it may not know whether or not he is really accomplishing anything worth while.

Notwithstanding what has been said in the preceding paragraph about lecture material, the fact remains that a well-trained skillful instructor will always be interested in improving the efficiency of his teaching by making purely informational material available to his students or learners in the most efficient ways known to him. The development of well organized informational material to be distributed in typewritten or mimeographed form will often save a great deal of time, supply the information in such a way that it can be referred to outside of the class, and, consequently, improve the all-around efficiency of the training. A skilled and properly trained instructor in any field of vocational education recognizes the limitations of informational objectives and always has in mind those training objectives which call for more than the possession of information about things and especially those objectives which call for the development of "doing ability." 

To sum up this discussion of teacher training objectives it may be stated that the progress of the prospective teacher consists of four principal stages as follows: 

1. Securing an appreciation of the teaching job. 

2. Developing the ability to do a good teaching job. 

3. Developing the ability to plan instructional work. 

4. Developing the ability to prepare teaching material and to use it intelligently.

Logically, the development in the prospective instructor of the ability to plan should precede the development of ability to do the job. As it works out in practice, however, the "ability to plan” is often developed later than the "ability to do" in a training program for instructors.

TEACHING OBJECTIVES AND CONFERENCE OBJECTIVES 

Where the training objective is to add to the knowledge or skill already possessed by the learner, and the instructor, who is an expert in the particular line of work being carried on, is employed to teach a group of learners in such a way as to increase the knowledge and skill they will need on their jobs, all of the objectives of the training program classify as teaching or instructional objectives. There is, however, an entirely different set of objectives which are worth working for with groups of men who are competent on their jobs with respect to those things which can be taught to them by instructors using the ordinary teaching methods. 

Where men are either required or expected to exercise judgment in the performance of their work, the analysis, discussion, and detailed consideration of practical problems and questions appertaining to their work, for which there may be no single specific and correct answer, has been found to be of great educational value. For work in this field, the conference procedure is the best known method for accomplishing or for making headway toward certain worth while objectives which are quite separate and distinct from those associated with instruction as such. To illustrate what is meant by conference objectives, five of the principal ones are briefly described in the following paragraphs: 

1. To secure, as a result of group thinking, a composite opinion based upon the collective experience of a group. The purpose in working toward such an objective might be to draw upon all of the years of experience in police work possessed by a group of 20 men. If the average experience of the individual members is only 10 years each, there would be available 200 years of experience, in law enforcement and police work which could be brought to bear upon finding an answer to a problem or question. It may be stated that, under good conference conditions, a group of men experienced in police work would "think out" a better solution to a problem to which there was no single specific answer than would be arrived at by any individual member of the group.

One of the results of such group thinking would be that each member of the group would have pooled his own experience and ideas with those of the other group members and each could be expected to gain something from the contributions made by the others. As a result of this, each member of the group would be encouraged to actually "think" and by an evaluation of all of the data placed before the group, arrive at his own conclusion as the result of his own thinking. This sort of experience has been demonstrated as an effective way of helping a man increase his ability to exercise judgment on his job, to be more reluctant to take snap judgment on the basis of a few facts or partial information, and to deal with practical problems which come before him in a more scientific manner. In other words, it is one means of helping experienced men to use their heads more effectively on their jobs.

2. The second conference objective is directed toward promoting cooperation in an organization. In a police department, which is a semimilitary form of organization, cooperation along vertical lines of authority must be realized. However, to the extent to which men of equal rank in the organization have opportunities to cooperate with one another, the conference discussion of problems involving cooperative relations may be very much worth while. In other words, the second conference objective is to reduce to the lowest possible minimum the degree to which men of equal rank in the organization work at cross purposes. The conference objective then would really be to get the members of the conference group in such a frame of mind that they would want to cooperate with their associates and direct their efforts along lines leading in the same general direction as those of their associates in the organization. 

3. The third conference objective is to develop the ability of the members of the conference group to look at their jobs objectively; in other words, to analyze their responsibilities and take a good hard look at them and see what the job as a whole actually involves. This objective will be of more or less importance with a group of police officers in proportion to the degree to which they have been trained for their jobs by reasonably efficient methods. For men who have learned their jobs by the "pick up" method or by working with or associating with experienced men and absorbing ideas regarding how things should be done, this third type of conference objective has a maximum value. 

4. The fourth conference objective is to help each individual member of the conference group to organize his own experiences to the end that he may develop a clearer and better understanding of just what his job consists. In accomplishing this objective, cases can be analyzed and discussed. Men with a considerable background of experience will be reminded of all sorts of situations in which they have figured where something has gone wrong, or where some mistake has been made which need not have been made if those dealing with a particular problem had been more proficient in their work. As the result of all of these discussions and the interchange of ideas between experienced men, each member of the group should get a more clearly organized conception of his own job as the result of “organizing his own experience." 

5. The fifth conference objective is to promote a more efficient meeting of joint responsibilities. Many situations may arise in all organizations where one man is partially responsible for the final result to be achieved and a number of others are responsible for some portion of the whole job. Through a well directed conference discussion, an attempt is made to have all of these separate efforts focused clearly on the principal job or the big job or organization as a whole. This plan is really an extension of the idea of promoting or encouraging cooperation. There is, however, some difference, in that cooperation carried one step farther, can be directed toward the accomplishment of the big job of the department. While it is a good thing to eliminate as far as possible any tendency to work at cross purposes in an organization, it is even more worth while to get the parallel lines indicating cooperation focused at one point which represents the big job of the department. Incidentally, work on this fifth objective tends to build up pride in the organization and to improve the morale of all of the individual members.

In the attainment of any or all of these five principal conference objectives, results of value may be expected to the degree to which the conference leader in charge of the work is skillful and resourceful, is a good teacher, and possesses other important qualifications necessary for a conference leader.

TRAINING CONFERENCE LEADERS FOR POLICE WORK 

The specifications of the job to be done by a conference leader are fairly well set forth under the heading, "The Principal Objectives in Training an Instructor." In order to measure up to the demands of such a high grade teaching job, the conference leader should be adequately trained. The principal training objectives to be kept in mind in training conference leaders for police work are as follows: 

1. A conference leader should clearly understand the difference between ordinary teaching methods and conference methods. This difference can be brought out through demonstrations of situations where the relationship of the instructor and learner is perfectly obvious to all members of the group as contrasted with a conference group of experienced men where no such relationship exists. 

2. The second training objective for conference leaders should be to develop their ability to handle a conference discussion with regard to a pre-determined objective. The man who is an experienced teacher is apt to feel lost without any subject matter to teach and is more or less afraid to place a group of 15 or 20 experienced men on a strictly conference basis, putting up a problem to the group and then handling the discussion in such a way as to secure profitable outcome for the time spent. In connection with the accomplishment of this objective there is a considerable amount of functioning information that should be made available to the prospective conference leader; but this information becomes more real to him when he has had the experience of trying to run a conference himself. Consequently, an attempt, even though it may be a poor one, to handle a problem or question on a conference basis is one of the ways which has been found most effective for the accomplishment of this training objective. 

3. The third objective in training conference leaders should be to develop their ability to use the functioning information supplied in connection with the work outlined in the discussion of the second objective. This would include the development of ability to use conference devices and especially the ability to do good chart or blackboard work to advance the thinking of the entire group. Considerable organized information regarding these matters is available in several books which are listed in the references in the back of this bulletin, and also in mimeographed reports published by a number of State boards for vocational education. The prospective conference leader should have this information made available to him and should be given practical assistance in applying it to his work as he makes progress in handling conferences.

Leading conferences is a class of work which might be described as an art to be acquired rather than a science to be mastered. While the functioning information is important, mere possession of all the information that has ever been written on how a conference can be con-

VOCATIONAL EDUCATION 

ducted, will not result in ability to handle a conference under practical conditions, unless the leader has really acquired skill in doing the job. 

In training conference leaders; nothing can serve as a satisfactory substitute for actual experience in handling conferences with a definite attempt at all times to improve one's technique and acquire reasonable skill in handling situations as they develop. 

4. The fourth objective should be the ability to plan specific conferences in regard to objectives, devices which are likely to be of most value, including cases, illustrations, analyses, drawings, and other material which may be utilized at the proper point. A conference leader should plan his conferences in considerable detail until he has developed skill and ability up to the point where he is sufficiently experienced to handle a conference discussion and think on his feet right on the job. 

No specified time for the development of this ability can be set because it will vary for different individuals. However, the teacher trainer in charge of training a group of conference leaders should be able to recognize when each individual has developed sufficient ability that he may safely be entrusted with the responsibility of handling a conference without a detailed written plan. 

5. The fifth objective in training conference leaders should be to develop the ability of each individual to plan a progressive series of conferences. 

Conferences are set up in terms of objectives rather than in terms of teaching content; consequently, it becomes necessary for the prospective conference leader to analyze situations and organize his conference objectives for a series of conferences, the combined effect of which at least will be in the direction of a definite practical objective. 

It often happens that the ultimate goal cannot be reached in a single step or after one or two conference discussions. 

6. The sixth objective, which may or may not be practicable in a given situation, should be to develop ability to prepare conference material which will enable the leader to do a better job with a reduced expenditure of time, wherever informational material which can be set down on paper is to be utilized in the conference. No fixed method of attaining this objective is suggested here because the conditions under which a conference leader will work are likely to be so different from conditions in other situations or in other departments, that the problem practically reduces to that of equipping the conference leader, as far as is possible, to use his head on the job in developing practical and usable auxiliary material for his conference program. It is doubtful if much headway can be made in the attainment of this objective before the conference leaders being trained have had considerable experience in conference work. Such a background would be necessary to give them a substantial basis for making headway in terms of this objective.

WHO WILL RENDER TEACHER-TRAINING SERVICE? 

All of the work described in this chapter lies within the field of teacher training as it has been developed in the field of trade and industrial education. It would be expected, therefore, that the teacher trainer in trade and industrial education or a State or local supervisor of trade and industrial education would conduct an instructor training program for police service at least in its initial stages. It might reason-

ably be expected that instructors of police work, who will, after being trained, function as teachers of practical and technical subjects for inexperienced recruits, would be selected from the most experienced and most highly competent policemen in a given department. These men could be trained as teachers by a trade and industrial teacher trainer or a State or local supervisor competent to do the work, each case being decided on the basis of available personnel and the working conditions. Prospective conference leaders might also be selected from the police force. However, in the initial stages of development, outside conference leaders could probably function to advantage. 

For example, a man who has successfully conducted conferences in industry with supervisors and foremen could be expected to have a sufficient mastery of the technique of conference work to do a fairly good job with experienced police officers and he should be able to train a selected group of police department representatives to function as conference leaders within the department in which they are employed.

Looking ahead to the training situation as it is likely to develop within the next 5 or 10 years, it is probably reasonable to anticipate that a majority of the teacher trainers, conference leaders, and trainers of conference leaders will ultimately be men who have come up through police work. They should be a highly selected group of men who possess the essential characteristics of leadership and who have had experience and training as police department employees, as instructors of police groups, and as conference leaders, who will eventually become fully qualified to train their own police instructors and their own conference leaders for police groups. While this is the ultimate situation to be hoped for in connection with police training, it will probably be necessary for a considerable period of time for representatives of State boards for vocational education to render a great deal of service to police departments in connection with the training of instructors, the handling of conferences, and the training of conference leaders. 81284-38-

CHAPTER IV SUGGESTED BASIC POLICE TRAINING PROGRAM 

The purpose of this chapter is to present an outline of a suggested minimum course of instruction based upon an analysis of the patrolman's duties. The various duties which a patrolman is required to perform are used as the basis for indicating the knowledge necessary to do the work.

The time required to complete the basic course will depend upon the amount of school time available each day or week. In a training center such as is found in the New York City Police School, the required work might be accomplished in three months, whereas in a smaller city it may take much longer to cover even the fundamentals. 

Much will depend upon the amount of time available to give preentry or preparatory training. In some situations, it may be possible to give preparatory training before assigning a police officer to regular duty. In other instances, the officer may be given training after assignment. Consequently, the time element involved must necessarily depend upon local conditions. 

This bulletin is not intended to be used as a textbook on police training but rather as a guide or index to aid in the establishment of police training programs. The material in the suggested basic course is intended to serve as a guide for police instructors in planning courses. It may also be found useful in connection with instructor training programs for police officers.

For some subjects such as first aid and local geography, it may be sufficient to give an intensive course at the beginning of the training period. A course such as first aid may be given in a comparatively short time and proficiency attained in the elementary and fundamental phases of that subject. Somewhat the same comparable situation may be found with respect to local geography, and while no standard course of instruction may be available the police officer can, with but little personal instruction and some study of a local guide book, become quite proficient in this subject. While on duty he should learn about local conditions and the physical characteristics of his beat. 

The order in which the subjects are arranged in the suggested basic course has no connection with their degree of importance. Every subject mentioned may be important in the police service. Individual police officers may rate one subject as being more important than 43
