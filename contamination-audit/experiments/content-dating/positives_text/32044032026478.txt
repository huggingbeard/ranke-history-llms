I. THE NEEDS OF DEFENSE 

1. 

The International Situation, In an arresting sentence a writer in the 100th anniversary number of Harper's Magazine, surveying the historical changes which have taken place, asserts that "the United States has learned that there is no way of not using power. He means, of course, that the withholding of action by the powerful will have just as potent effects (for good or ill) as the most assertive action. In fact, it is action of a very palpable sort. The wielder of power can never withdraw from the scene and think that matters will remain the same; to think so is sheer delusion, er power will rush in to fill the vacuum, Oth- The postwar situation has raised the United States to an unprecedented pinnacle of power. Measured in military might, we are not so strong now as we were in 1945, when we started to dismantle the mightiest military engine in the world's history, but the potency is still here. Our use of it will determine our own future security and that of the free world; our failure to use it will also determine our future security and that of the free world. There is no escape from this fateful responsibility.

The world's power today is focused on two poles--the United States and Soviet Russia. The only hope for peace in the foreseeable future lies in the construction and maintenance of a balance of power between these two poles. If the equilibrium is seriously disturbed for the there will be a third world war, with an outcome which no one worse,

- 2 can foresee. If the balance of power is retained, there may be peace long enough for changes to take place in the attitudes of the Soviet world.

The balance-of-power principle, which at least has the historical merit of having kept Europe from a major war for one hundred years following Napoleon, has been thus described in a classic study: 

The balance of power is a system designed to maintain a continuous conviction in every state that if it attempted aggression it would encounter an invincible combination of the others. 

The manifest willingness of menaced states to go to war has assisted in maintaining that conviction. When any state has developed its armaments too little or too much, and when alliances have not been made promptly to rectify the balance, war has usually ensued. In a world expectant of violence, the maintenance of a constant relationship in the military potential of all has been the price both of state independence and of peace.1/ 

Whether acknowledged in these terms or not, such is the policy which has been guiding the United States ever since evidence of any sincere cooperation by Russia failed to show itself. Various descriptions are used, such as "containment," solidarity of the free world, resistance to Commmmist aggression in Korea, cooperation through the United Nations via the veto-less General Assembly. All these add up in any realistic view of the situation, to the establishment of an equilibrium between Communist Russia, her satellites and dominated areas, on the one hand, and the United States, the Atlantic Union, and the smaller freedom-loving democratic states throughout the world, on the other hand.

--  It is almost safe to say, that while the latter group is in a state of mind and military posture to offer effective military resistance to the former group, there will never be a general war, If these conditions do not prevail war will come. 

Events since 1945, and particularly our experience in what is now officially called "The Korean Conflict" (a "conflict" apparently being something rather more than a police action and rather less than a war), have made certain facts of the contemporary world abundantly clear in the minds of most Americans. Among these are the following: (a) The United States is not governed by aggressive intentions with respect to the territory or independence of any other nation. The leaders of Russia know this perfectly well, unless they have become deceived by their own propaganda, a dangerous development which sometimes takes place. (b) The Soviet Union is pursuing an expansionist policy of territorial aggression. It is only necessary to list some of the countries either dominated or openly annexed, since 1939. The former include 

Albania, Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Poland, Romania, Northern

Korea. The latter include Eastern Poland, Finnish Karelia, Lithuania, 

Latvia, Esthonia, Bessarabia and Bukovina, East Prussia and other areas. 

For many purposes Soviet-held Germany, Mongolia and China may also be included in the Soviet orbit. Without China, the population involved amounts to some 100,000,000 persons.

-- 
- (c) So far--though the reasons are not quite clear--the Soviet Union has not dared to provoke a general war, perhaps through fear of the atomic bomb, which up to now has been a wholesome deterrent, but may not remain so much longer. In any case the Soviet Union has made great gains, without war, by bluff, bluster, deceit and infiltration coupled with the willingness on the part of the free nations to appease her. 

It is probably not true to say that Russia wants war. 

Certainly the people do not, and the leaders (like Hitler in his earlier stages) simply want to get what they want without war, just as any burglar would dislike getting into a shooting affair with the owner of the house he is looting. Moreover, in spite of her prodigious Army and Air Force, which might make enormous advances in a quick war, Russia would be at a great disadvantage as against the United States in a long-drawn-out, full-scale struggle. In certain vital elements Russia is weak--food and transportation by land and sea, and, in the opinion of some observers, oil. (d) However reluctant she may be at the present time to become involved in a full-scale global war, Korea has shown that Soviet Russia has no scruples about thrusting her satellites forward into limited aggression. What happened in Korea can happen in many other areas, such as Southeast Asia, the Balkans, Austria or Germany. Or even Russia itself might begin a move against a soft spot on her vast periphery, such as Turkey, Iraq, or Iran. Moreover there is an inner dynamism in any aggressive dictatorship, which compels it to move forward in order to

keep disruptive forces from operating within, 2/ (e) It is apparent to most Americans that the only thing that will stay the advance of this new imperialism is the fear of force. If, interposing a bar to aggressive designs, there stands the armed and willing opposition of the Free Nations, the aggression will not take place. 

The fact that Russia did not intervene directly on behalf of its North Korean friends when things began to go against the latter is extremely significant. The same kind of holding back was evinced in Greece and in connection with the Berlin airlift. Dictators understand and respect force. The United States and the West generally will have to turn to force, along of course with the other measures of resistance to the Communist tide. As of the present time, military force is probably the most urgent, for it is being perceived with increasing clarity that neither economic nor political stability can be achieved, save behind a defensive shield of military security. Essentially this implies an application of the balance-of-power principle described above. 

Our problem is: How much of a military posture does this equilibrium require, and does the requirement necessarily include a system of universal military training?

A second point to be borne in mind is that there must be a high degree of readiness. It is not a question of "potential." The potential of the Free World (if assembled and unified) is undoubtedly superior to that of the Communist World as matters now stand, Russia, for example, produces only about one-fourth the amount of steel that the United States produces. The Free World is far more efficient in many other ways.

In the event of a long war, these things would tell. But how about a short war? At the present time, for example, indications are that in Europe there are, at most, some 15 divisions which might put up resistance to advancing Communist hordes from the East. Few doubt that they could be swept out of the way pretty easily. But if 

Western Germany, including the Ruhr, the Saar, and France, Belgium and Denmark, were overwhelmed, and England laid under rocket bombing, where would the Free World be? All the bases, harbors, armament plants, airdomes, in fact the whole military and industrial potential of Western Europe could then be added to what the Communist World now has, and sooner or later turned against us. It is obvious then that it is not merely a question of ultimate potential that has to be faced but a question of immediate readiness. 

Ultimate potential has so far played its part in holding off a war, or so many people believe. The present flimsy defenses of Western

- Europe have been not inaptly likened to the plate-glass window in front of a jewelry store. Looked at in one way, fragile glass is a ridiculous protection against a robust-minded robber. All he has to do is to hurl a brick through it. The difficulty for him is, however, that if he does this there will be noise; attention will be attracted; alarms will be set off; the police will come running; stronger force than the robber can command will ultimately make his act foolhardy and unremuner ative. So, if we retain the atomic superiority, and if we have respectable forces in readiness, and if the Free Nations can work together on this, the prospect is far from hopeless. It has worked so far. But time is running out. Russia's own readiness is increasing too, probably including atomic weapons. Many persons think the crucial time will come in 1952.

In the other world wars we have had a comfortable cushion of time to get ready for the fight. In the First World War, there had been three preceding years during which we learned to make munitions. By a marvel of organization we were able to put substantial, though not welltrained, troops in Europe about a year after war was declared. Even so we were short. The writer's own regiment was the only American heavy artillery regiment at the front on Armistice Day which was completely equipped with American guns, made in America, for the use of American troops. The rest of our heavy artillery was borrowed. But while the 

French and British were fighting, we did have time to get ready. We began the Second World War with Selective Service, and because it had

- been in operation for a year or more prior to Pearl Harbor, we had a million men with basic training in the Army--enough to institute 30 divisions--and we had machinery to induct any required number, machinery already accepted by the American people as fair and equitable. Had it not been so, we would have been very unfortunate indeed. Yet even under these favorable conditions, it was not until 1943 that we were ready to commit the issue to large-scale invasions and 1944 before the assault on Western Europe could be mounted. Meanwhile other people had been fighting the battle, among them the Russians themselves. It seems very improbable that we will have any such borrowed time in the next war. We should have in readiness enough basically trained men so that some large units can be deployed immediately and others within two or three months of M-day--the day the enemy strikes.

To the idea of massive forces, then, we must add the idea that these massive forces must be in being, or in readiness. 

These forces must also be of a considerable variety. For this the term "balanced" is sometimes used, but the term is unfortunate, because it seems to imply that all the several varieties must be of the same size or that an approximately equal amount of money must be expended on each. What is really meant is that no one strategic concept is so to dominate preparations that other aspects are starved or neglected. fact is that we must be prepared to fight anywhere from the tropics to the arctic, by land, sea and in the air, in Eurasia or North America, The

ban areas. in jungle, desert or snow, over mountains or steppes or industrial ur- Korea has demonstrated that while our fundamental enemy is certainly the Soviet Union, the actual thrusts received or given are likely to take place elsewhere than in Soviet territory, at least at first. This poses a colossal task. Preparation for it cannot be complete and the principle of calculated risks or balance of probability must be constantly called into play.

A keen military critic, writing from Korea, has described this as the salient lesson from our experience there: 

The most important lesson for the Pentagon as well as for the public is that no one service, no one arm, no one strategy is sufficient in modern war. Air superiority and naval superiority cannot compensate in a continental war for gross inferiority on the ground. The doughboy in a foxhole sitting across the enemy's line of supply, the G. I. in a tank opposing armored thrusts, are the most important elements in continental war. A corollary is that our high-level war plans in Washington should never be based on any single assumption. 

Man cannot forecast the future; in retrospect it is clear that our war plans were not geared to the kind of war we had to fight in Korea and therefore we nearly lost it. Our top planners should have open minds to frame plans and build a military establishment capable of meeting a number of different situations rather than just one. In other words, we should, in the phrase of one high-ranking officer, be able to 'end-run, kick, lineplunge, or pass 2/

Accordingly, we add to our concept of preparedness, and say, massive forces, in being or in readiness, of a wide variety. 

To this we must join the idea that these defense forces must be maintained at a steady level. We cannot afford, in old-fashioned language, to "chop and change" with each breeze that blows from the Kremlin.

The military history of the United States is very sad reading from this point of view. Our custom has been in times when war was not actually upon us to let our defenses sink lower and lower. Then, when the crisis comes, to extemporize relatively ineffectual forces at colossal unnecessary expense of human life and of dollars.10/ Not by good management, but by good luck or by the mercy of Providence, we have eventually come out on top in the past, but we have no assurance that such ineptitude will be so charitably rewarded in the future. 

In the present state of difficult international relations, we must expect periodic times of tension, but we must not rely on periodic orgies of defensive preparation. This is another lesson of Korea in 1950. 

Defense leaders, judging by the past, have some justification for the fear expressed that if we emerge successfully from the Korean crisis, excitement will give place once more to apathy, until the next crisis is upon us, an apathy which the Soviets will be only too willing to exploit.

There is an additional reason, beyond military effectiveness, for maintaining a steady level of defense spending and other effort, a reason based on economics. It is pointed out that a steady level of defense effort has less adverse effects on the national economy than a fluctuating one. Policy and spending improvised from year to year make for speculation and inflation, while a steady expenditure even at a 10/ Summarized in [Palmer, Williston B.] The evolution of military policy in the United States. Young men, their prospective employers, their families, and their schools and colleges, would welcome a program permitting them to make plans based upon a relatively stable set of circumstances. 

It is primarily in the interests of effective defense, however, that we must add another concept to our statement of principles and say: defense needs call for massive forces, in being or in readiness, of a wide variety, maintained at steady level from year to year. 

There is yet one more note to be added to this statement of principle. We must anticipate an indefinitely long period during which the present requirements will remain in force. It may be a matter of ten years, or a generation or a century. Certainly no one can tell. But it is not a matter of this year or next, which we must consider. The international situation may clear up, but tensions have been deepening rather than dwindling since 1945. Even if there should be a favorable change in the personnel or the psychology of the Kremlin, of which there is now no prospect, it cannot be expected that the world will settle down. It is no secret that it is the Army, and not the Navy or the Air Force, which is primarily interested in questions of drafting and compulsory military training. The latter two services can usually procure the men they need, as volunteers.

The Army cannot do so in sufficient numbers. The reasons for this are fairly obvious but are seldom stated.

Army duty is mainly a matter of ground forces, and of these the main one is the infantry, which calls for large numbers of personnel. 

Now though the infantry has been historically called the "Queen of Battles," it is in some respects the Cinderella of the armed services, It is not nearly so attractive to the volunteer as the other services. 

The infantryman does not have a glamorous uniform; his average pay is lower (or was during the war); he is, in actual war, most exposed to hardship; he receives very few awards (in the Second World War, the infantryman got 70 percent of the total casualties but only 11.6 percent of the decorations, outside the Purple Heart, which went to all wounded men--here the infantrymen ranked high; the number of men killed per each 100 medals awarded was 2 in the Air Force and 67 in the infantry); there are other disparities, all to the disadvantage of the infantryman.

II. COMPULSORY MILITARY TRAINING AND THE AMERICAN TRADITION 

1. 

In Colonial Times 

The obligation of the free, male citizen to serve in the defense of his country in time of war, and to train for such service in time of peace, constitutes the basis for the "militia," an institution first mentioned in our historic tradition in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle in the year 605 A. D.

The fyrd, as it was called, was a local defense force, locally controlled, made up of the entire free male population between the ages of sixteen and sixty, each member of which was charged with providing his own armament. It was with this organization that King Alfred the 

Great won the wars of liberation which unified the first free England. 

The fyrd took various forms during the succeeding thousand, or twelve hundred, years, but the institution itself was never lost. At times it was overshadowed by dependence on feudal levies and mercenary forces. 

The militia idea and practice dwindled in England in the nineteenth century after the Napoleonic Wars. England at that time had entered upon a long period of peace, a century in fact. Main reliance for defense was on the mighty British Navy, and England's "little wars" in remote parts of the world were fought with small numbers of professional troops (as was also the larger Crimean War). There was never any possibility that England itself might be invaded. In this long period of security the obligations of militia service were gradually taken over by volunteers and in 1852 the militia itself was put on a voluntary

- Soon basis. The total enrollment on April 1, 1914, was only 6,246. thereafter the First World War compelled a return to compulsory enrollment, but this was on an emergency conscription basis rather than a militia basis. In 

In 1947 Great Britain, for the first time in her history, set up a complete system of compulsory military training and service, i.e., conscription, as a permanent future policy. 

On our side of the Atlantic the earliest colonists brought the militia-idea with them, when they came from England, and it formed the basis for defense throughout the entire colonial period. With it the colonies participated in the French and Indian Wars which secured the eastern seaboard to the English-speaking colonies. Then it played a large part in the Revolutionary War which won freedom from England. 

The men of the Boston Tea Party and the Boston Massacre, of Lexington and Concord, were militiamen. Of the men under Washington's command in the Revolution, 164,087 were militiamen, approximately 40 percent of his total force.

The history of colonial enactments requiring both military training and military service has been the subject of a good deal of research in recent years; the details now known are too numerous for rehearsal here. The Militia Act of 1792 and some parallel legislation remained on the statute books to testify to the right of the Federal Government to require militia service of all citizens, but the idea dwindled away. 

During the 19th century we enjoyed the "geographic luxury" of being protected from all powerful potential enemies by having weak countries north and south of us, and vast oceans east and west of us--the latter patrolled by the powerful and friendly British Navy. In the Civil War, after the State militia failed, and voluntary recruiting failed, President Lincoln was driven to the drafting of individuals as the Confeder ate leaders had been even earlier, for the Confederate Government found its own States' rights theory hampering it in raising troops. After 1903 many of the functions of the militia came to be taken over by the National Guard.

This usage is not entirely correct, however, as the National Guard is organized entirely on a volunteer basis, and the militia concept implies peacetime military training and emergency military service as a compulsory requirement for all ablebodied male citizens. The Act of 1903, which is still in force, made all male citizens 18 to 45 years of age subject to military service for a brief period, and set up the Guard as "organized militia.

III. HISTORY OF THE DISCUSSION 

Probably few major questions of public policy have ever been so amply discussed in or out of Congress, as Universal Military Training. 

As we have seen, the story may be taken back to colonial days, when all thirteen original Colonies had their training-days and muster-days, on which able-bodied male citizens up to fifty or sixty years of age were required to turn out for military drill on penalty of fines, imprisonment, the stocks, or other punishment. President George Washington pleaded for "a citizenry trained to arms"; what he actually got was the famous Militia Act of 1792. This Act put the military training of citizens entirely under the jurisdiction of the several States, a system which turned out to be so completely inefficient that in time it was generally discarded, although even now all the States of the Union except three have written either in the Constitution or in the Statute Law, or both, the requirement of military service for all male citizens between (usually) the ages of 16 and 60. President Jefferson urged the same kind of federal militia as Washington had done, with the additional requirement that young men between 18 and 26 should give one year's service.

Skipping to recent times, the Gurney-Wadsworth Bill for one year of universal military training, was introduced in 1943. The first May 

Bill, somewhat similar, was introduced in 1944.

Hearings on this and other proposals of similar tenor were held in November and December of 1945; at the same time there was a great deal of public discussion on the subject throughout the country. The printed reports of the hearings contain over 800 pages. 

No action was taken by Congress on the May Bill. In part this was due to the fact that most of those who opposed UMT, and some who were not opposed in principle, urged delay. It was insisted that no action should be taken until the postwar situation should become clarified, the United Nations organization should be set up and our obligations under it known, and--most important of all--until the mass of as yet undemobilized veterans could return home and let their voices be heard. 

Any action taken in 1945, it was widely insisted, might be dictated by the hysteria of war psychology, and we should "make haste slowly" and let excited wartime feelings settle down before arriving at a decision. 

It was also urged that a civilian commission be appointed to study the matter. Naturally many of those who asked delay hoped that popular interest would subside and that delay would defeat the proposal, and proponents feared exactly the same time, remembering the state of reckless aversion to all things military into which the Nation fell after the

THE PRESENT PROPOSALS 

S. 4062 differs from the proposals of 1948 in only two important details. The first is that in the present form of the bill UMT is left discretionary with the President, acting on the advice of the Secretary of Defense as to the time and rate of implementation of the training program. The purpose is to defer action until the Korean situation shall be liquidated and a larger portion of the Regular Establishment will be available to give attention to the task of organizing and administering the training. The wisdom of leaving this discretion to the 

President was questioned in the Senate hearings and a desire expressed to make the installation of UMT mandatory and with a set date. The second change from 1948 is that, while disciplinary action in the case of trainees is to be governed by the Code of Conduct expressly drawn up for the purpose, and not by the Articles of War (the old "court-martial code"), the new proposal envisages a change to the new uniform code of military justice for all the services when that goes into effect on 

June 1, 1951.

Briefly stated the plan set forth in S. 4062 is as follows: 

Every male citizen of the United States will be obliged to register on reaching the age of 17. At the age of 18, if physically and mentally qualified, he will be inducted into the National Security Training Corps (except that he will be given until the age of 20 to finish a high school curriculum). Registration and induction machinery will be run by the Selective Service System. The period of training

Subsequent study by the Army Comptroller has reduced these estimates to a little over one and one-half billion dollars the first year and about $1,070,000,000 annually thereafter. 

The program is to be administered by a National Security Training Commission of three members, two of whom shall be civilians. The Chair man must be a civilian. There is to be in addition a National Security 

Training Advisory Board of twenty-five members (including three members of the Armed Forces) to advise the Commission with particular attention to the moral, religious, recreational, informational and educational phases of the operation, and to make investigations and studies in connection with these aspects of the training. 

In general the training is to be carried on along the lines of the Fort Knox pilot project, which was investigated by civic, religious and educational leaders in the Louisville area and favorably commented upan by them. There are severe restrictions intended to enable the Commission to eliminate prostitution, the sale of liquor and other moral hazards in areas of training camps. 

Violations become matters of Federal law, and do not have to be left to possibly lax local enforcement. 

Nonmilitary training is provided for those who claim exemption from combatant training on conscientious grounds, along lines defined in the Selective Service Act of 1948. Claims must be sustained by the local draft boards. Provision is made for appeals from decisions of the draft boards.

As said before, trainees are not available for combat duty, and could not be sent to Europe or Korea, for example, to fight. 

UMT is not regarded as a substitute for the draft, which is needed now and may continue to be needed even after UMT is in established and regular operation. UMT does not call men to serve; it merely prepares them to serve quickly and effectively if needed. In an emergency they may be called by the draft. The latter is conscription; UMT is not conscription but training. It is a long-range program, not a means of raising soldiers for the present or any definitely known future emergency.

V. CONTEMPORARY ARGUMENTS ON BEHALF OF UMT 

As has been indicated above, the long discussion over Universal Military Training which has taken place since the close of World War Two has reached a point where nothing significant seems to remain unexpressed on either side of the question. Events themselves have made some arguments look rather thin, on both sides, and these, if offered at all, are now produced with less confidence. On the other hand some points of view have been given added significance by the course of history. In this and the following section, effort is made to summarize the contentions that still have validity or at least are advanced by responsible persons. In favor of a program of Universal Military Training, the following points are made: 

1. Universal military training is needed to provide future trained reserves for the armed services.

This is not only the main reason advanced for adopting UMT, it is essentially the only reason. If it is not compelling, there would be no compelling grounds for advocating the program, although there are ancillary considerations in its favor. As General Bradley states it, "physical conditioning, technical training, education and citizenship benefit, are of great value to the individual and eventually to the country, but are only by-products. National defense is the only justification for UMI."

National defense rests in the last analysis on manpower. Military men have always known this, and our chief military leaders have testified to it again and again, but there has been a cherished tendency in

-the mind of a large section of the public to feel that now we have airplanes and atomic bombs and other forms of mechanized warfare, we do not need men, or at any rate not in very large numbers. Secretary 

Forrestal replied to this mode of thinking by saying (in 1948): "The comfortable assurance of a push-button war is an illusion. The enor- mous complication of the machinery of total warfare of today rests not in mass, but diffused through thousands of speciupon manpower alities. t? The events of Korea have brought this fact home to us very clearly. 

We have had complete domination of the skies, and eventually we have succeeded in bringing superior weapons on the scene, but up to the time of writing we have not succeeded in overcoming the onrush of numerically superior enemy ground forces, described by General MacArthur as "a far natical foe, well trained, expertly directed and heavily armed. 11 If future developments should require us to conduct operations, not only in Korea, but in Europe and in the Near East, and in Southeastern Asia, and elsewhere and at the same time to defend ourselves here at home--any or all of which contingencies may happen--we shall need very large forces,

No one believes that we shall have time to train these forces after the war begins. We had years for this in both the world wars, but now we shall need to be able to effect a very swift mobilization. 

Unless we are to maintain standing forces of monstrous size, which would cripple our economy and do us harm in other ways as well, there

is only one answer to the problem: it is to have ready a reservoir of trained citizens who may be called upon. 

We have been building up our reserves in recent years, an increase from 435,000 to 834,000 from 1948 to 1950. This increase is due largely to reserve enlistments of patriotic veterans of the Second World War, The number of these veterans who are capable of service is decreasing year by year, due to age, physical condition, family responsibilities and the like; in any case they should not have to serve in another war while there are millions of younger men who have never done their part. 

UMT will provide an annual flow into the National Guard and the various reserve components of the Forces of perhaps 800,000 men a year. 

All of them will have had basic military training, all of them will have had unit training, many of them will have had specialized training of one kind or another, many will have had officer training in some branch of the service. With very little more training--perhaps only two months in many cases--they will, in the opinion of General Bradley, be prepared to be committed to combat. During the recent war when we got down to "scraping the bottom of the barrel" in our manpower, we had to commit men to battle with only a few weeks of training. They made inferior soldiers and many more were killed and wounded than would have been the case had they been properly prepared. 

It may be pointed out that if there is a global war--a possibility which we have to envisage--our chief potential enemy has in being at least 175 divisions, with a reserve of (it is said) 300 more divisions.

6. Universal Military Training will result in personality gains for the trainees. Reasonable discipline, physical examinations discovering early remediable ailments, educational opportunities, the experience of meeting with other Americans from all over the country, increased sense of responsibility, in many cases training in technical skills which can be used in civil life, a sense of citizenship and belonging, a realization that the country has a right to demand something in return for what it has given him, and many other elements of maturity will be brought to thousands of young men who need just these things. At the same time every effort will be made to protect the trainee from the commercialized evil which young men are liable to come into contact with everywhere in civil life. 

7. Universal Military Training is essentially the democratic way to meet the problem. It is a sharing of the burden of defense that falls upon all alike--the privileged and the poor. No one can escape it because of means or influence; nor is any one forced into the military because of his inferior economic position, as has too often been the case in the past. Even now, Even now, with the operation of Selective Service, there are complaints arising over the country because, while few are being drafted, one boy will be taken from a neighborhood, while his companions of the same age and position are entirely free to go their ways. Under UMT all will be trained, and trained alike. If an emergency arises, all may be called and called alike.

In the past, when disasters have occurred, the militarily trained young men, veterans, have often been the first on the scene. They are used to emergency measures, to quick common action, to leadership and response. In our coming civil defense set-up, it will be of inestimable value to have in every nook and corner of the land citizens who know how to act in an emergency.

VII. ALTERNATIVE TO UNIVERSAL MILITARY TRAINING 

The decision this summer to recommend the expansion of the armed forces to three or three and one-half million men has brought a new angle of the UMI question to the fore. That decision is of course due to a new apprehension of the necessary requirements for continuous military strength for years to come. While stimulated by the Korean situation, it is not based on that but upon the realization that Communist aggression will be a world possibility for an indefinite period in the future, and from a concomitant realization that the only way to deal with this threat even on a diplomatic level is to "deal from strength.' 11 A figure of 3,000,000 men means doubling the pre-Korea strength of the services. This process is under way at the present time. It has involved calling in of some reservists for 21-months duty and some National Guard units, together with a substantial increase of drafting under the Selective Service System. That this process is not going smoothly is obvious, and involves phases not particularly relevant to this study. The question which concerns us is, how can a system of 

Universal. Military Training can be geared into such a situation? 

We simply do not seem to have enough young men to meet the dual demand. According to the Bureau of the Census, while the total population has grown rapidly during the past decade, the growth by age groups has been very uneven, The most significant increases have occurred in the youngest and oldest groups. The number of persons 55 years of age and up has increased 29.9 percent since 1940 as compared

- with 15 percent for the Nation as a whole. At the other age extreme, the high birth rates of the past decade added almost 9 million, or 27 percent to the group under 15 years of age. But the age group 15-19 declined 14.4 percent and the group 20-24 years remained unchanged. It is these younger groups that must supply most of the manpower both for the armed forces and to a growing labor force for some years to come. 

The maintenance of the three-million force or more will require a turnover of about 800,000 men annually, on the present 21-month draft and National Guard basis of service. To keep this up this will involve a "scraping of the barrel. The annual turnover of youths for Universal Military Training, as planned, would be 850,000 or 900,000 after the scheme comes to in full running order. We do not have that many men, in addition to the 800,000 annually for actual service. 

Of course there are other points of awkwardness, too. Here might be one young man drafted for 21 months training and duty, and perhaps for actual warfare in Korea or elsewhere, while the fellow next door would be required to leave home for only six months of training, and could not be sent into actual military duty overseas or even in continental United States. The essential unfairness of this would certainly arouse resentment.

To meet this situation consideration is now being given by the Armed Services Committees of the Senate and the House, to a program which will possibly be put before the 82d Congress when it convenes in 1951. This program discards entirely the whole program for Universal

Military Training, and enters in its place a proposal for a universal training-and-service draft. While details are yet to be worked out, it is understood the plan will be somewhat as follows: 

All young men will be called at the age of 19 for 21 months or two years of actual military service. They can defer this until they reach the age of 20, in order to finish secondary school. There will be substantially no exceptions "except the bedridden and the insane," as one writer puts it. Those not qualified physically for the armed forces according to present standards would be placed in non-combatant jobs in the Army, Navy, or Air Force. On the other hand some of those who are actually fit for combat duty may be trained for wartime jobs in defense industry, a portion of the able-bodied manpower thus being set aside for essential home-front jobs. Rear-area jobs would be found for the partially disabled. There would be no student deferments, and very few hardship deferments. 

The following description has been given of the way the plan would work in individual cases:27/ "A youth of 19, just out of high schook, would be called up in July, given a thorough qualification test, and assigned to the Army as a recruit. After basic training, he might be shipped overseas--there are to be no restrictions such as were planned under UMT. He would complete his tour of active duty, say, in two years, then be released

64with a reserve status, that he must keep for another three or five years. 

Then at 21, he could begin his college training and expect to be graduated when he is 25. His would be a typical case, as the plan is being drawn now. "Another youth, also 19 and with the same general qualifications, would be called up and assigned to a specialized training unit. He would spend the same amount of time, getting the basic know-how to become a key man in a wartime munitions industry. He, too, would have a reserve status after discharge at 21 and be free to begin his college or professional career at that time. "A disabled youth, who lost a leg in an auto accident, would be called up at the same time if the plan is accepted. He might be assigned to the Army's Quartermaster Corps, a Navy desk job, or a civildefense planning unit. But he would have to put in a like amount of service time.

Discussion of the new proposals, based on the apparent incompatibility between the demands of a 3,500,000-man standard strength for the armed services and the need of building up a trained reserve by means of a system on Universal Military Training, may of course lead to an examination of the validity of the three-million-man figure. That figure presumably represents the thinking of defense leaders, and has been accepted by the Executive Department, but it has not been explicitly passed on by Congress, although Congress has lifted ceilings on armed forces personnel until 1954. It may be urged that this figure is

M 

.. excessive, on grounds that the world situation is such as to permit a greater risk, or that the 10 or 15 German divisions which some have hoped would be raised will lower the demands for our own manpower, or that the allegiance of the satellite countries to the Soviet cause is weakening, or that our capacity for strategic bombing does away with the necessity of extensive ground forces, or that the qualitative superiority of American soldiers counterbalances the need for quantity, or that the superiority of American weapons is such as to make up the difference between the sizeable Communist forces and those of the free world, On the other hand it may be dintended that, in view of the present threat to world civilization and our own existence, the largest number of men we can prepare for possible conflict is likely to be dangerously inadequate. It is not within the scope of this paper to go into this question.

In this connection it is interesting to note that while Great Britain has since the war adopted a conscription system (18 months, recently extended to 2 years), the distinguished military historian Liddell Hart has recently contended very strongly that this has weakened rather than increased the strength of British national defense.28/

ADDENDUM DEFENSE DEPARTMENT'S PROPOSALS 

(As the manuscript of this Bulletin was going to press, proposals were made at a hearing of a Subcommittee on Preparedness of the Senate Armed Services Committee, 10 January 1951, by Secretary of Defense George C. Marshall and Assistant

Secretary of Defense Anna M. Rosenberg. It is understood that these proposals, which constitute a program for Universal Military Service and Training, are approved by the President. They differ in detail rather than in principle from proposals discussed in the Bulletin, and are outlined in summary below for purposes of comparison.)

All young men on reaching 18 years of age would be subject to service if physically and mentally fit. (The present induction age is 19.) 

Although about 1,050,000 young men are expected to reach 18 years in the year starting July 1, 1951, only about 450,000 would be called, and most of these would have reached four to seven months beyond 18 years at the actual time of induction. 

In all probability married men and veterans in the 19-26 age group would continue to be deferred. 

So far as possible students would be deferred to graduate from high school or finish a college year. 

All 18-year-olds called would receive from four to six months' basic training before going into service. In general youths under 19 would not go overseas, except for emergencies.

· - Normally, after four months of training the draftee would give 23 months of actual service, though this period could be cut or dropped altogether by the President, if conditions should be deemed to warrant such action. 

Following the 27 months in Universal Military Service and Training, the draftees would be required to spend three to six years in a reserve component. (The reserve duty would be shorter if assigned to National Guard or Organized Reserves, longer if inactive.)

The Navy's present officer-training program in civilian colleges would be extended to the other services. Youths chosen for such training would get basic military training first, then go to college. 

After graduation they would owe twenty-three months service plus two years.

Arrangements would be made to send some 50,000 men in active military service to colleges and universities during the first three years for supplementary training.

Inductees would receive $30 pay monthly during the basic training period, after which they would be jumped to the $75-a-month rate of Army privates.

Draft-age students studying to be doctors, scientists and technicians would be deferred upon graduation to satisfy civilian requirements for specialists. If not called after ten years they would be exempt from military duty.

For the first three years of the plan, 75,000 a year would be deferred for study in medicine, the sciences, and other necessary specialities, but they would get their four months' basic training first and then would owe twenty-three months' service.

Present voluntary recruiting of young men, before induction, for four-year regular enlistments in the branch of their choice, would continue.

The intention of the present proposals is to build the total strength of the armed forces from the level of 2,500,000 of last 

November to 3,200,000 by next June 30, and at the same time start a never-ending flow of competent men to the reserve components of the military establishment, components which are always expected to furnish the bulk of the armed forces in time of war.

Even without lowered standards, 50 percent would appear to be extremely conservative inasmuch as rejections and deferments are even now running at less than 50 percent, as the younger age brackets are being reached.

At this point uncertainties multiply. The five major uncertainties are: (1) Policy as regards student deferments. If policies remain approximately as they are, about 200,000 students now deferred who are physically or otherwise fit will become available by September 1, most of whom will be available during June. Small additional numbers will become available during February 1952, and (depending on too many policy decisions to give anything like an accurate estimate) perhaps another 100,000 during June 1952. On the other hand, certain new policies re deferment are under consideration. (2) Policy as regards physical and other standards. A lowering of requirements might add 80,000 to 150,000 to the pool of availables, depending upon type of lowering utilized. (3) Policy as regards the number "in the works," or the so-called "cushion." In order to maintain the necessary flow of actual inductions, it is necessary that a substantial additional number be examined and in the "pipe line," in part to counterbalance limited deferrals. Estimates submitted by General Marshall place this cushion at 400,000; by General Hershey at six times the number to be called in a given month, which at present would be 480,000. The cushion would on this basis be increased as the number becomes larger.

It should be borne in mind that a draft rate of 100,000 a month (or even of 90,000 a month), if sustained, would apparently reach the present combined armed services goal of 3,456,000 fairly quickly; when the 2,450,000 (approx.) already in, the anticipated volunteers, and the reservists (est. 200,000) still to be recalled are taken into account. Under these circumstances a substantial falling off of the draft rate may be anticipated, thus reducing the average. 

Exits from the Services are the major indeterminate factor. 

Korean casualties are about 10,000 a month--not all of them resulting in permanent withdrawal from active service. Discharges for other reasons, such as completion of terms of enlistment or transfers to reserve status, will be substantial, especially by 1952. 

Draftees under the present 21-month term will not be completing their terms in appreciable numbers until July 1952. The Department of Defense estimates a total of 310,000 separations between October 31, 1950, and June 30, 1951, and a further total of 872,000 prior to July 1, 1952. The breakdown of this latter group is given as (1) 21month inductees, 103,000; (2) National Guard and reserves (21 months), 351,000; net enlistment terminations, 418,000. At the same time, they submit figures indicating that the net addition to those in active service which would result from extending the terms of service by law to 27 months and by adding one year by law to the enlistments as only 213,000 to 292,000. This would appear to be an extremely conservative estimate. It can only mean that it is anticipated that large numbers would be let out (especially from the reserves and National Guard) prior to the end of their term of service.

If we assume a current exit rate of 400,000 a month only), it is highly questionable whether, given the total goal as indicated, an average induction as high as 100,000 would be needed during the next 17 months. It might be needed for about 11 months, leaving a serious gap between August 1, 1951, and January 1, 1952, of about 350,000. A substantial contribution toward meeting this gap could be made (without reducing the draft age) by extending the term of all draftees, National Guard, and reservists to 27 months; and by adding 1 year to the terms of the shorter term enlistees.
