NELLIE FRANCES FLYNN 

Nellie Frances Waite was born in Peru, Vermont, September 22, 1861.

She attended the public schools of her native town and later became a student at Burr and Burton Seminary in Manchester. Her formal education was completed in New York, where she attended various educational institutions for several seasons. Throughout her school career she was an outstanding student and received many awards for the excellence of her work. 

Her marriage to John J. Flynn occurred November 22, 1882, and the family came to Burlington to live. She and her husband were interested in, and soon became actively associated with, various civic enterprises. Both 

Mr. and Mrs. Flynn played prominent parts in all projects promoting the general welfare of the community. 

The absorbing interest of her life was the study of the wild flora of the State. This dates from her school days when she and her sister collaborated in the making of an herbarium, required as a part of the school work. This was the beginning of an interest which grew with the passing years until she became regarded as an authority in her chosen field. Her knowledge of the local flora, in its intimacy and thoroughness, was equalled by that of few, if any, of the Vermont botanists of her time. After her marriage she began the study of the Burlington flora. She grasped the opportunity to make the acquaintance of Doctor Jones and Doctor Pringle and their colleagues at the University and spent many happy hours in the laboratories and herbaria there. She was an indefatigable collector and many of the "first finds" of the Burlington region were made by her. 

She took an active part in the organization of the Vermont Botanical Club and was always most intimately associated with it. As the first vicepresident, and later the secretary-treasurer, she, perhaps more than anyone else, was responsible for the early growth and success of the club. She held many important offices and was a member of all important committees. At the time of her death she was the chairman of a committee on the revision of the State flora and was making detailed plans for the somewhat complicated task of securing reliable field data from every part of the State. 

She did not confine her botanical activities to Vermont, however, but wherever she went she always carried her vasculum and her manual. She traveled quite extensively and her private herbarium contains plants which she collected in Canada, North and South Carolina, Virginia, Florida, Cuba, Morocco, Switzerland, England, Italy and France, as well as in other places.

vi 

Her collections from Nantucket, Massachusetts, and Bermuda are especially noteworthy.

Mrs. Flynn's interest in the flora of Burlington found its first public expression in 1899, when she presented a paper before the Vermont Botanical Club on "The Flora of Burlington and Vicinity." Later she contributed many other papers to the club programs on the same subject and wrote extensively in the club "Bulletin” and other botanical periodicals on similar and allied topics. This work culminated with the publication of her Flora in 

1911. Very appropriately this volume bears the same title as her first public utterance on the subject.

It would be difficult indeed to overestimate the value of Mrs. Flynn's contributions to our knowledge of the flora of the State and particularly of the region about Burlington. She combined the painstaking care of the scientist with the zeal of the amateur collector. Her thorough knowledge of the subject is attested by the proven accuracy of her annotations. Although time has added new species and the progress of the science has caused us to change our concept of specific groups, the "Flora of Burlington and Vicinity" still remains of great value to all who are interested in the study of the local wild plants. It has been a stimulus and inspiration of many students and has served as a model for floras of other regions. It forms a nucleus for the compilation of data for a flora of the State. 

While still actively engaged in the pursuit of her botanical activities, the preparation of an extensive herbarium, the cultivation of her gardens, and the gathering of data for the proposed State flora, Mrs. Flynn passed away at her home in Burlington on December 9, 1922.

PREFACE 

This list was begun at the suggestion of Dr. L. R. Jones, formerly of the 

University of Vermont, now of the University of Wisconsin. It was intended to be accompanied by an account of the plant geography and ecology of the region by Dr. Clifton D. Howe. Since the removal of Doctor Howe to 

Toronto has delayed the completion of his surveys* it seems best to publish this now as a preliminary list. It is hoped that when Doctor Howe's report is completed, a revision of this list may be published with it. 

The list is intended to include the names of all the fern and seed plants which are natives of or grow without cultivation in Burlington and its vicinity, including with Burlington, the towns of South Burlington, Colchester, Essex, Williston and Shelburne. Each name has an extant specimen back of it, except in three or four cases which are specifically mentioned. In these cases we are sure of the identity of the plant cited. Except in a few instances, specimens are in the herbarium of the University of Vermont or in the private collection of the author. 

To stimulate further search, a supplementary list has been added containing the names of additional plants which have been doubtfully reported for the region or which for reasons named should be sought here. In the case of rare plants, stations and names of collectors have been given. For the sake of brevity, initials of collectors' names have not been given except where there are two of like name. It is a matter of regret, that the older botanists who were the first collectors of this region, namely, Macrae, Robbins, Torrey and Benedict, who collected here about the middle of the last century, have so few authenticated specimens in herbaria, that due credit cannot be given them. It is hoped that anyone having such specimens or those of other collectors which are not fully credited, will inform the author. 

The seventh edition of Gray's Manual has been followed strictly in nomenclature, except where for the sake of brevity, initials have been eliminated or names abbreviated, and synonyms have only been given where corrections have been made since the Manual came out. Common names have been inserted freely as befitting the general use which may be made of such a list.

Following the usage of the Vermont Flora, four adjectives, namely, "common," "frequent," "occasional" and "rare,” have been adopted to denote frequency of occurrence. These are intended to be associated with the habitat -thus, "sandy soil, common," means, common on sandy soils, though it may be rare in other habitats. The term "rare" is used for those plants which occur in but few stations and there but sparingly. When they are abundant

ix in such stations, the word "local" has been preferred. The habitats given are based on personal observation where possible. 

Following the common practice, small capitals have been used for names of plants not native to the region as far as our knowledge goes. In a few cases these may be native of some other part of the state, but introduced here. 

This list is necessarily provisional and incomplete, and will have served its best purpose if it prepares the way for a fuller one later. To that end it is hoped that anyone who can give added information about the plants in this list, or who finds any additional species, will let the author know, and deposit specimens either with her or in the herbarium of the University of Vermont. 

Proper recognition will be made of such assistance in the proposed revision. 

My thanks are due not only to Dr. L. R. Jones who has been of the greatest help and encouragement, but to Dr. Ezra Brainerd, Mr. W. W. Eggleston, Rev. J. A. Bates, Dr. T. E. Hazen and many others for assistance given.

Thanks are also due to Prof. M. L. Fernald, who has examined and verified many doubtful specimens.

PREFACE TO THE SECOND EDITION 

The first edition of the "Flora of Burlington and Vicinity" by Nellie F. 

Flynn has long been exhausted. This was issued as "Contributions to the 

Botany of Vermont IX" and published in 1911. Mrs. Flynn's well-known ability as a botanist, and the care and accuracy with which she had prepared the copy, gave it a prestige which has seldom been accorded a local flora. 

It was undoubtedly the best, most complete and most accurate of the floras of the region covered, and was of great value, not only to students of 

Vermont botany, but to systematic botanists throughout New England.

Time has proven the correctness of the annotations and the thoroughness of the work. Mrs. Flynn's flora still remains standard, and the need for a reprint has long been apparent. In response to the popular demand, Mr. 

Flynn has generously provided for this new issue. 

In preparing the new copy certain changes and additions have been made. 

The data for the new text were compiled from a copy of the original in which Mrs. Flynn had made marginal notations. She had added a number of species and stations and had made a few nomenclatorial changes, chiefly by reducing certain varieties to the formal category. A total of eighty-four new species and subspecies were thus added to the original list. These marginalia have been incorporated into the new text. The table of “Additions and Corrections" of the original has been eliminated and the changes

it necessitated been made a part of the present tabulation. From the "Supplementary List" of the 1911 edition, those species have been deleted which Mrs. Flynn's notes have now placed in the text. Changes in the introduction, tables of statistical summaries, index, etc., have been made to conform to the requirements of the new list. Thus all of the changes made are those indicated by Mrs. Flynn herself, and the new list has been made as nearly as possible as she would have made it. 

No attempt has been made to bring the list up to date, either in content or in form. The omission of certain species now known to exist in this region, as well as certain apparent anachronisms in nomenclature, are thus explained. 

The statistical summaries of the original list enumerated species, varieties, and hybrids. Mrs. Flynn did not use the concept of "form" at all. In the present summaries, all named plants—including forms-are included. 

Most of the forms of the present list were varieties of the original and so appeared in her summaries. She properly reduced them to formal rank and it undoubtedly would have been her procedure to include them in the summaries.
