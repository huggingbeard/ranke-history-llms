INDUCTION 

"You men will have a choice to make as to the branch of the service in which you will serve in this war-Army, Navy, or Marines! Only one man in four in this room, at a maximum, can go to the Navy, under the present law. And the Navy does not accept all who apply. 

Only those who come up to the standards set by the Navy will be taken."

A ripple of murmurs ran through the room. "I want the Navy," said one man to the man beside him, "so I can take that machinist's course they have. How about you, Mate?" "I want to get into the aviation corps, and do weather work. The 

Army is bigger . . 66 ... But not better," interpolated the first speaker, “There's a lot of opportunity in the Navy.' 

"That Commando force in the Marines sure looks good to me," a burly youngster of seventeen was saying, 'I got Pop's consent last week, and Mom went along, so here I am. I won't be eighteen until next month, but look's there." He flexed a huge bicep. "I gotta buddy training at the Farragut, Idaho, Naval Training Station, under a Chief named Sam White, who was an All-American football player." "Whadda' you gonna' do mate?" "Aw, I don't care. I'm still mad at my draft board," griped back an answer.

The men were all seated on benches in a huge room in a reception center awaiting final induction. A sailor came out and announced that the final physical examination would begin immediately. "Line up in single file, men, and march upstairs. You will get your final physical examination. If you pass, you are in the service. Some of you will fail this examination. Come down and see me immediately after you have this news. Now everybody line up, strip from the waist up, and march upstairs. Don't get out of line. You will be checked by about fifteen doctors each. Remember that these men do nothing but check men, and they know their business. If any of you have any complaints afterwards, please see me then."

The men did as directed. A single line wormed its may through the fourteen reception rooms in which the various parts of the physical examination were given. "Any broken bones?" "Ever have a hernia?" "Ever had a venereal disease?" "Sit down and read all the letters on that chart with your left eye." 1

I want to go into aviation." "See your selection officer after you are inducted. Any insanity in your family? Ever had a nervous breakdown?" "No, sir." "All right. Move on to the next room. Next man! Now, let's see, your name is Brown?

Your papers say you went to Harvard College. Is that right?" "Yes, sir." "Hmmm." A ten-second pause, then, "What do you think of Yale? 

That's all right, don't answer. Ever had any insanity in the family?"

The line was working its way around through the battery of rooms, numbered one to fourteen in big black or red letters on cardboard cards, to the selection benches. "You want to join up with the Navy, eh? How old are you?" 'One week to nineteen, sir. Graduated High School last fall, and worked as a printer's apprentice until last week, when I was drafted.

De boys in dis' train comes fust, suh, dey is goin' to whip de pants offn' Hitlah, Mussoloney, and Herohito!" "Quiet, men! In thirty minutes you will all be in Farragut, to begin your training. You might want to know something about what you can expect." 

---

The car became quiet, and the transportation specialist, whose job it was to cart men from induction centers to the camp, continued. "You will hear a lot of rumors about what is going to happen to you. Most of it is just plain scuttlebutt, so forget it. You do get shots in the arms, but only as medical prevention. One of those shots may save your life. There is no such thing as a square needle six inches long which is shot into the fleshy portions of your hind seat. "Now, every one of you has a name of his own now, but in fifteen minutes that will stop. For the next several weeks, everybody here is going to be called 'Mac.' "You men are going to wash your own clothes, make your own beds, and clean your own bathrooms. You will be under the toughest training period in the Navy. But everybody who tries will come through it O. K. And most of you will weigh twenty pounds more, and be feeling better than you ever felt before in your lives. "Now if you have any personal belongings, get ready to mail them home. The Navy gives you everything you need. And when you are spoken to here, don't answer 'O. K.' The correct form of address to a superior is 'Yes, sir!' "There is only one thing more you men need to know about being happy in training. That is the gem of advice, a pearl of wisdom snatched from the ripe hands of old Mother Experience. In the Navy, men, if you want to get along, there are two rules to remember above all: Keep your mouth shut, men; and keep your bowels open! That's all!"

ANSWERS TO PERTINENT QUESTIONS ABOUT THE NAVY 

(Compiled especially for Yard Birds) 

Q. Why might a sailor have a pig tatooed on his foot?

A. It is considered a charm against drowning. Tradition has it that a pig will not sink easily. 24

Q. Give the sailor's way of saying the following: (a) Stairway; (b) Floor; (c) Wall; (d) Kitchen; (e) Hospital. 

A. (a) Ladder; (b) Deck; (c) Bulkhead; (d) Galley; (e) Sick Bay. 

Q. Who is second in command of the U.S. Navy?

A. Secretary of the Navy Frank L. Knox. The President is Com mander-in-Chief.

Q. What is the significance of: (a) The three white stripes adorning the collar of a sailor's dress uniform? (b) The black tie worn with the uniform?

A. (a) The three white stripes are a carry over from the British 

Navy where they were worn to commemorate the three great battles in which Lord Nelson took part; namely, the battles of 

Copenhagen, Trafalgar, and the Nile. (b) The black tie is worn in mourning for Lord Nelson who died in the battle of Trafalgar. 

Q. Why were the white sailor hats of enlisted men designed as they were?

A. So they could be used for bailing water out of small boats in an emergency. 

Q. When is Navy Day? 

A. October 27. 

Q. A grain of powder used in a Navy big gun is the size of: (a) 

A particle of dust; (b) A pea; (c) A man's thumb. 

A. A man's thumb.

Q. In time of war which is used for communication between ships; signal flags or radio?

A. Where possible flags are used, because a radio signal might give the ship's position to the enemy.

Q. What is the oldest and most distinctive piece of nautical equipment?

A. The Boatswain Pipe. There is a record of its use in the Crusade of 1248; it is also mentioned by Shakespeare in "The Tempest."

Q. After what are the following types of ships named? (a) Battleships; (b) Crusiers; (c) Submarines. 

A. (a) States; (b) Cities; (c) Fish. 

Q. When are baths mandatory in the U.S. Navy? 

A. Just before battle to reduce danger of infection.

Q. If the starboard is the right side of a ship and the port the left side, which is the larboard side?

A. The larboard side also means the port side. Due to the similarity between larboard and starboard the name was changed for the sake of clarity. 

Q. What two Admirals in the U.S. Navy history had the same first name? 

A. David Farragut and David Porter. 25

Q. In which of the following places would you search for the ship's magazines: (a) Far below the top deck and well protected; (b) 

In the Captain's portfolio; (c) in the ship's record room. 

A. (a) Far below the top deck and well protected because the ammunition is kept there. 

Q. What change has been made in the U.S. Coast Guard since war was declared?

A. The U.S. Coast Guard became a part of the U.S. Navy since war was declared. 

Q. Why are the sailor's pants bell bottom in shape?

A. In order to facilitate rolling them up for climbing in rigging and swabbing decks. 

Q. What is the origin of saluting the quarterdeck? A.

In ancient times idols and images of gods were located there, and the sailor used to pay homage to them with a salute.

Q. When was the American Flag first carried around the world? 

A. In 1790 Captain Robert Grey sailed with a cargo of guns to China and returned to Boston on August 10, having carried the thirteen stars and thirteen stripes around the world for the first time. 

Q. Which of the following is a "bos'ns chair?" (a) The chair that the boatswain sits on when on watch; (b) The piece of board on which a man working aloft is swung; (c) the chair at the head of the chow table for the boatswain to sit in.

A. (b) The piece of board on which a man working aloft is swung. 

Q. What is the difference between a "Line Officer" and a "Staff 

Officer" in the U.S. Navy? A.

A "Line Officer" is an officer trained to give commands in all fighting divisions of the U.S. Navy whereas a "Staff Officer" is trained in one special division and gives commands in only that division.

Q. What is the most famous historical ship of the U.S. Navy, and how did she get her nickname?

A. The "U.S.S. CONSTITUTION" which was nicknamed "Old 

Ironsides" because during a battle in 1812, with H.M.S. Guerriere, several shots fired at her were said to have hit the sides and bounced off into the water.

Q. The American Flag was flown over the British Parliament Building on one occasion. What was the reason?

A. In celebration of the United States' entry into the World War, April 20, 1917. 

Q. Who are or what is the "Black Gang" aboard a ship? 

A. Members of the engineers force who work below decks and in the old days got very dirty. 26

Q. What determines the height, breadth, and length of a U.S. Warship?

A. All ships must be able to pass under the Brooklyn Bridge and get through the locks of the Panama Canal. 

Q. Is the Captain of a ship always a captain in rank in the U.S. Navy?

A. NO. The commanding officer of a ship is always known or designated as the captain of that ship whether or not he is a captain by rank.

Q. What is the meaning of Navy Day? 

A. Navy Day is dedicated to better understanding between the people and their navy. October 27, 1944 is the 168th birthday of the U.S. Navy, and it is upon this date that Navy Day is celebrated. 

Q. What was the death flag of the pirates?

A. NOT the skull and crossbones which meant, when flown, "Hand over your cargo and go free." A solid red flag meant Death. 

Q. What is the meaning of the term "balling the jack?"

A. It means the ship is making good speed or going fast. The British flag is called the "Jack", and when the ship was making good speed the wind caused the "Jack" to "ball" or wind around its staff; thus the term "Balling the Jack." 

Q. Everyone has heard of a "Booby Hatch." Where did the term originate?

A. It is a wooden covering for a small opening at the back or stern of a ship. 

Q. In what year was the U.S. Marine Corps founded?

A. The U.S. Marine Corps was founded in 1775, prior to the origination of the U.S. Navy.

Q. What is the only flag ever flown above the national flag on U.S. 

Naval vessels?

A. The Church Penant is flown above it during religious services aboard ship.

Q. Which is the greater distance; a nautical mile or a statue mile? 

A. A nautical mile. It is 6,080.27 feet. A statute mile is 5,280 feet. 

Q. What type of cloth is used to contain an explosive powder for guns of the U.S. Navy?

A. A raw silk sack a little larger than a woman's stocking. 

Q. What is the difference between a heavy and a light cruiser?

A. The difference is in the armament, or size of the guns, not in the weight of the ship. 

Q. What is a "lucky bag" on board ship?

A. A locker for storage of lost clothing found lying around the ship. 

It is opened every so often and a man is "lucky" if he gets his things back. 27

Q. Why are medals and decorations worn on the left breast?

A. It was the custom of the Crusaders to wear the badge of honor of their order near their heart. Also, the left side was the shield side of the Crusader, and so, the shield protected both the heart and the badge of honor. 

Q. How many gun salutes is the President of the United States given when he comes aboard a ship? 

A. Twenty-one. 

Q. What is meant by the following terms: (a) Weather-breeder; (b) Weather-eye; (c) Weather-bound; (d) Weather-side.

A. (a) A clear day but with evident signs of approaching bad weather. (b) To keep a weather eye is to keep alert. (c) Detained in port by weather conditions. (d) Weather side is the windward side. 

Q. What does a sailor mean when he uses the following expressions: (a) “Handsomely now;" (b) “Smartly now;" (b) “Bear a hand." 

A. (a) Do your job slowly and carefully; (b) Do your job quickly and efficiently; (c) Help do this job quickly. 

Q. What types of ships do the following refer to? (a) Pig-boat; (b) Tin-cans; (c) Mosquito boats. 

A. (a) Submarines; (b) Destroyers; (c) Motor torpedo boats.

Q. What form of punishment was inflicted upon men in ancient times when they slept on watch, third offense?

A. They were placed in a basket which was hung from the bowsprit by a rope and were supplied with a can of beer, a loaf of bread, and a sharp knife. They had the choice of starving to death or cutting the rope. 

Q. What is a fathom?

A. A nautical unit of measure equal to 6 feet. 

Q. Who was the first Naval Officer to become an admiral? 

A. David Glasgow Farragut, July 25, 1866. 

Q. You have heard the expression "Casting oil upon troubled waters." 

What kind of oil is used to calm rough seas?

BOATS Like a divine gift to the men of Farragut from the Prince of Heaven, Lake Pend Oreille lay like a blue gem set high in the crown of rugged, surrounding mountains of Western Idaho. Behind the rich, clear blue of the deep glacial lake the sturdy pine which scattered over the mountain sides and crowded into valleys and ravines formed a background of velvety green. And across the immeasurable vault of sky a brilliant sun moved in an immense arc, shedding on the world that golden glow which is rightly found only in the palette of Nature. It was a scene such as only the far Northwest knows, and which travellers never forget.

Company Commander Sam White marched his men to the edge of the freshly painted docks and commanded them to halt. "At ease, men, and catch your wind," he said.

"Attention!" said CPO White as his visitor approached. "Men, is there anyone here who hasn't read his Bluejacket's Manual on boats? "Good. But just in case you might have forgotten it, Chief Johnson here who is in charge of these docks will help refresh your memory, before you go on the boats."

With these words he turned his company over to Chief Johnson. "Men," began Chief Johnson, "boats are a lot of fun. But they are most fun if you know how to handle them right. And it's also true that boats are a lot of work, but they are less work if you know how to handle them. "So pay attention to what I have to say, and you'll get the most possible out of the next few hours. 47

“A boat is handled by thirteen men, twelve of whom pull oars. The thirteenth is the coxswain, and he thinks for the rest of you. A good coxswain often means a good performance. He will give you commands for shoving off, he will count strokes for you, and will bring you into dock. You must obey every command he gives to the best of your ability. "Now the twelve oarsmen are counted off in pairs. Pair number one sits at the bow of the boat, on the port and starboard sides, pair number two one position closer to the stern, and so on to pair number six which is right in front of the coxswain. The coxswain sits at the stern of the boat and guides it by means of the rudder. When you enter the boat, let pair number six enter first, then pair number five, then four, then three, then two, then one, then finally the coxswain. "Now, every man must sit as close to the gangway as possible, because only by doing that will you get enough room to give a good account of yourself on the oars, and not get in each other's way. "Now, when the coxswain says, 'Stand by the oars,' every one of you will take his oar by the handle and see that the blade of the oar is in the clear and not touching or interfering with any other oar. "The second command is "Up oars!" When it is given, you will toss your oars quickly to a vertical position with the handles of your oars resting on the bottom of the boat. Your outboard hand should be even with your chin and your inboard arm should be steadying the oar from farther down. "The third command is 'Shove Off.' Your bowmen and coxswain will execute this command. "The fourth command is 'Let Fall.' This means simply that you drop the blade of your oar in the rowlocks. Your inboard hand stays at the handle of the oar. The oar is still out of the water. "Now on the command, 'Give way together,' you begin rowing.

Now here is where your coxswain and your starboard stroke make a difference. Your starboard stroke sets the pace, and you others simply keep your eye on his oar and stroke simultaneously with him. The coxswain will step it up or slow it down, and work out all the strategy. “Men, there are three main things to remember. First, take a full stroke! You get more power and waste less energy that way. "Second, pull a strong steady oar, quietly. A boat goes best if the flow of power which moves her, is even. A jerky flow of power disturbs her rhythm and makes it impossible for the crew to work together well. And only the coxswain talks on a boat. "Third, feather your oars. Now that means simply that when you bring the oar back, let the flat side of the paddle skim over the sufrace of the water. Feathering your oars helps steady the boat and helps retain the power which you put into the stroke. 48

"Now when you stroke, there are three things you must not forget. You must not forget that your oars belong in the water only three quarters of the distance of the blade, that is, one quarter of the blade head should remain above the surface of the water. That means you sort of paddle along on the surface of the water, or skim along to be more descriptive. Second, pull with your backs first, then your arms. You get most power that way. And third, drop your wrists after every stroke because that helps relax the muscles. "Now after that, you men are on your own until you come back into the docks. It's just a question of team work out there on the lake, that's all. "When you come into the docks, your coxswain says, 'In bows,' and that means that you put your oars inside the boat. The bowmen will take the boat hooks and secure the boat to the docks. "Now, any questions?” "Sir, when do you use 'Point Oars?"" "You never use that command on this lake. It is intended for shallow bodies of water, where a boat runs onto a sandbank, and then the coxswain uses it to get the boat free. Incidentally, this lake is a survival of the glacial age. It is several thousand feet deep in parts, and drops sheer from the shore here many feet. So when you each get your lifebelts, put them on. And no horseplay. We haven't had one boat capsize yet, and don't expect to have one today. "Now form into groups of thirteen. You men who have had experience with small boats step forward, because we need a coxswain for each crew ... 

A crew of thirteen stood in front of one of the boats under the new roof of the boatdocks. "Our platoon leader is going to be our coxswain. He knows something about boats." "Yeh, let's hope so. Say, these boats are practically new!” "So is the whole dock. The whole camp is only a year old." "Well, they certainly did a good job on these docks." "They certainly did a good job on the whole camp."
