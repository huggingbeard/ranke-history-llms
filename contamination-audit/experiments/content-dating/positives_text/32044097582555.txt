MESSAGE FROM THE CHAIRMAN Report 

Fiscal Year 1988 has been a period of constructive change and continued commitment at the Commission. After completing an internal evaluation of its operations, the Commission implemented two major administrative initiatives. First, a long-range planning process was instituted so the Commission could develop a sense of direction rather than merely reacting to the multitudinous filings and diverse demands that are constantly received. With priorities established, the Commission is able to better manage and balance ongoing workload with other necessary and special projects. This planning process is described under FY89 Plan. Second, the Commission's monthly schedule of hearings, public meetings, and decision conferences was regularized so as to better serve the public. in productivity and timeliness resulting from the new scheduling are already reflected in the FY88 Report. 

Improvements 

The operational changes which have been made have allowed the Commission to plan and begin implementation of a comprehensive approach to the telecommunications issues facing the utilities and consumers of the state largely as a result of federal decisions. It has also made it possible for the Commission, in coordination with the Alaska Power Authority, to develop a plan to review and improve administration of the Power Cost Equalization program and to fulfill legislative intent. In addition, the Commission has established as a priority project a 1-

matter of longstanding interest: the examination of ways to simplify the ratemaking process for small utilities. 

These and other initiatives are discussed in detail in the FY89 Plan. 

While these changes reflect the Commission's new sense of direction, it has not lost sight of its commitment to the fundamental duties which have been assigned by the Legislature. 

The Commission continues both to aggressively promote extension of utility services to those areas of the state where needs exist and to monitor the rates, terms, and quality of service provided by established utilities and pipeline carriers in an economy which brings unique challenges to both operators and consumers.

The Legislature will be considering a sunset review of the Commission during the upcoming legislative session. The 

Commission invites each legislator to visit its offices, to observe its proceedings, and to ask questions about its operations. The Commission also hopes that this report will provide useful information on what it has been doing and what it anticipates doing in the future to serve the public that the Legislature has charged the Commission with protecting.

AGENCY PERFORMANCE 

The Alaska Public Utilities Commission is a public protection agency with responsibility for regulating public utilities and pipeline carriers. Its duties include issuing certificates of public convenience and necessity for areas or routes to qualified service providers; establishing rates, terms, and conditions of service to the public; and overseeing the practices, services, and facilities of regulated utilities and pipeline carriers. In addition, the 

Commission is responsible for computing the power costs and resultant assistance amounts for electric utilities participating in the Power Cost Equalization (PCE) program. 

The Commission has partial or full jurisdiction over the operations of cable television (CATV), electric, gas, radio common carrier, refuse (garbage), sewer (wastewater), steam, telephone (local exchange and interexchange), and water utilities and oil and gas pipeline carriers. The regulation of some types and sizes of utilities is limited under the law. In addition, there are statutory provisions which allow the customers of cooperatives and certain small utilities to elect to be economically deregulated and which allow electric cooperatives to adjust rates using a simplified rate filing procedure.

Certificates of public convenience and necessity are currently held by 313 utilities and pipeline carriers. 3.

of these, 106 utilities and 16 pipeline carriers are regulated as to rates, service, practices, and facilities. 

The Commission's regulatory responsibilities are fulfilled through a number of procedures, including audits, investigations, public meetings, tariff action meetings, formal adjudicatory proceedings, promulgation of regulations, informal meetings, conferences, and complaint resolution by telephone, mail, meeting, or adjudication. The Commission functions as a quasi-judicial body when rendering decisions in formal proceedings and as a quasi-legislative body when promulgating regulations. Its proceedings and determinations are governed by the statutes and regulations of the state.

This section of the Annual Report will provide a statistical summary of the major activities of the Commission during FY88.

Certification Proceedings. 

A certificate of public convenience and necessity must be obtained by every utility (with certain exceptions) and pipeline carrier proposing to provide service to the public for compensation. In addition, the Commission must approve all amendments to, or transfers of, certificates as well as acquisitions of controlling interest in certificate holders. The criteria for issuing certificates are

Events prescribed by law: the service must be required for the public convenience and necessity and the applicant for a certificate must be fit, willing, and able to provide the service. Uncontested certification requests are normally decided at the Commission's monthly public meetings, which are conducted under the state's open meeting laws.

Tariff Revisions. 

Every economically regulated utility and pipeline carrier is required to maintain a tariff which sets out the terms, conditions, rules, and rates governing its provision of service to the public. All changes in a tariff must be approved by the Commission under procedures and timetables which are prescribed by statute and regulation. Tariff filings are considered at public tariff action meetings which are held twice monthly, generally on the second and fourth Fridays of each month.

Open Dockets. 

All formal proceedings before the Commission are administered through dockets which are numbered to denote the type of proceeding, the year of its initiation, and its numerical sequence in that year. The materials, legal pleadings, and decisions relevant to a case are identified by this number and filed in the docket. 

After a case is 16 -

Events decided and costs allocated, if necessary, the docket is formally closed.

For the statistical analysis of open dockets presented in Exhibit IX, active dockets are those in which substantive decisions were pending at the end of FY88. Inactive dockets are those in which substantive decisions have been rendered, but additional action, either administrative or procedural, was still required at June 30, 1988.

There is an overall decline in the number of active dockets from FY87. This trend is even more notable if the "other/regulations" dockets, which are inherently longer term in nature and which generally are initiated by the Commission, are excluded from consideration. In addition, there continues to be a decrease in the number of inactive dockets in the "awaiting cost allocation/closing order" category. The 50 percent overall reduction experienced in this category since FY85 is evidence of the Commission's continuing efforts to timely close its dockets. 

However, a court decision on cost allocations, issued in May 1988, may limit or erode improvements in this area in the short term. · 17 ·

Power Cost Equalization. 

As required under AS 42.05.141 and AS 44.83.162 

44.83.164, the Commission determines the eligibility for participation in and the amount of assistance that an electric utility is to receive under the Power Cost Equalization (PCE) program. The Commission makes these determinations for both regulated and unregulated electric utilities; and as of the end of FY88, there were 25 regulated and 77 unregulated utilities receiving PCE assistance. (See pp. 108111 of this Report for detail.) 

Following review of its PCE monitoring and filing procedures, the Commission in FY88 simplified the annual report forms for unregulated utilities. The Commission also entered into a Memorandum of Understanding with the Alaska Power Authority (APA) in which the Commission and the APA agreed, among other things, that, in addition to its audits for establishing the level of PCE assistance, the Commission would perform desk and field audits of participating utilities covering areas of interest to the APA; that the PCE participants should be required to maximize the generation efficiency of their systems and to minimize line losses; and that the two agencies should prepare draft PCE regulations. · 26 ·

Requests of Chugach Electric Association, Inc., To Increase and Redesign Rates. 

In late FY87, Chugach Electric Association, Inc. (Chugach), filed a request for a 4.48 percent rate increase, which the Commission approved on an interim, refundable basis for energy and demand changes. Subsequently, in early 

The 

FY88 Chugach increased its rate request to 5.2 percent.

Commission granted Chugach a 4.94 percent interim, refundable increase to energy and demand charges pending investigation and establishment of permanent rates. At the request of Matanuska Electric Association, Inc. (MEA), and Homer Electric Association, Inc. (HEA), wholesale purchasers of electricity from Chugach, the Commission subsequently modified the methodology used to allocate interim rate increases between wholesale and retail customers, and the 

4.94 percent interim increase was adjusted to 4.65 percent for wholesale customers and 5.10 percent for retail customers.

The request by Chugach was opposed by MEA and HEA, and also by the Alaska Consumer Advocacy Program. Issues which were raised by the parties included the allocation of interest coverage requirements between generation/transmission plant and distribution plant and the allocation of - 27-

reserve capacity to "all requirements" and "contract" customers, both of which issues had been litigated in a previous Chugach proceeding but which the parties wished to revisit. Other issues included the appropriate ratemaking treatment of expenses related to the strike of Chugach by its union employees and adjustments related to employee reductions associated with lower economic activity levels. 

The Commission held extensive public hearings (April 4-19, 1988) on Chugach's rate proposal. Decision on this matter will be made in early FY89.

Bettles Light and Power, Inc.'s Request for a Rate Increase. 

In November, 1987, Bettles Light and Power, Inc. (Bettles), filed a request for a 43.12 percent increase in its rates. Because of concerns raised by Commission Staff (Staff) with regard to several of the expenses underlying the filing, the Commission suspended Bettles' request for investigation and in January, 1988, granted Bettles a 

24.23 percent interim, refundable increase pending completion of the investigation. In prefiled testimony submitted after audit of the utility, Staff recommended that Bettles be allowed an increase of only 10.53 percent above permanent rates but later amended its calculations to recommend an 11.12 percent rate increase. After initially failing to file testimony in reply to that submitted by Staff, Bettles requested, and was granted, an extension of time to respond · 28 ·

to Staff's testimony/recommendations. 

Ultimately, however,

Bettles elected not to proceed to hearing to support its rate request. After review of the written record, the Commission ordered Bettles to reduce its rates to the 11.12 percent permanent rate increase level recommended by 

Staff and to refund to its ratepayers all monies collected above that level as interim rates; i.e., the difference between 24.23 percent and 11.12 percent. The refund requirement also applied to overcollection of power cost equalization monies.

Request of Manley Utility Company, Inc., To Increase Rates. 

Manley Utility Company, Inc. (Manley), a whollyowned subsidiary of United Utilities, Inc., filed a request to increase its rates by 66.72 percent. Manley's increase request was attributable, in large part, to the fact that after acquiring the utility from the previous owners it had replaced much of the utility's generation and distribution system. The proposed increase, however, drew significant opposition from Manley's ratepayers. At a public hearing held in Manley, Alaska, numerous ratepayers testified that a great deal of time and money had been wasted by the utility during construction of the distribution system due to inefficient work practices and lack of management oversight. 

Witnesses further testified that the utility's generators · 29 ·

had failed because of improper operating procedures and a lack of proper maintenance.

Based on the testimony of Manley's ratepayers and the Commission Staff, the Commission made several adjustments which reduced the rate increase granted to Manley. 

Based on a finding that the construction of the distribution system had been imprudently managed, the Commission disallowed $10,000 of the construction cost from the utility's rate base. Also, based on a finding that the utility had improperly maintained its previous generator and had then purchased an inappropriately sized generator, the Commission disallowed write-off of the previous generator and computed fuel cost based on the fuel efficiency which would have been achieved by an appropriately sized generator. The decisions resulted in an increase to Manley of 45.97 percent and a refund of interim rates collected in excess of that level. 

Request of Homer Electric Association, Inc., for a Rate In crease and Rate Redesign.

The request of Homer Electric Association, Inc. 

(HEA), for a rate increase and rate redesign was resolved by Commission approval of a stipulation between HEA and Commission Staff thus obviating the need for hearing. The revenue requirement approved for HEA was based on a Times Interest · 30 ·

Earned Ratio (TIER) 1 

of only 1.64, which was significantly below the TIER previously approved for HEA and other electric cooperatives, but HEA requested a TIER at that level to forestall a greater rate increase. However, the Commission required HEA to address the ratedesign and cost-of-service questions in its next rate case. 

Request of Chugach Electric Association, Inc., To Change Pricing of Economy Energy Transactions; Trial Period of Deregulation of Economy Energy Transactions. 

Chugach Electric Association, Inc. (Chugach), filed a proposal to revise its tariff to allow the pricing of economy energy sales based on the replacement cost of gas rather than on the actual cost of gas at its Beluga generating facility. In its proposal Chugach also requested special accounting treatment for margins earned on such economy energy sales. 

Chugach's request stemmed from the fact that it now has a contract to purchase a limited quantity of gas 1TIER is the ratio of net operating margins plus interest expense to interest expense and is the mechanism most often used in calculating the allowed return for Rural Elec trification Administration cooperatives. 

· 31 ·

at rates which are very significantly below current market prices. Because it considers that gas supply an important asset of its retail customers, Chugach did not wish to use that gas to generate electricity for economy energy sales to other utilities (primarily utilities in Fairbanks) at prices based on the actual cost of the gas. However, Chugach desired to use the gas to generate energy for economy energy sales if it could make such sales at a price based on its expected replacement cost of gas. Chugach also desired to place a portion of the profits from such sales in a special account to offset gas cost increases which would occur when the low-cost gas supply was exhausted. Chugach's proposal was supported by Matanuska Electric Association, Inc. (MEA), one of its wholesale customers, but was opposed by the 

Municipality of Anchorage d/b/a Municipal Light and Power 

Department (ML&P), Chugach's primary competitor for sales of economy energy to Fairbanks.

After a public hearing, the Commission found that Chugach's decision not to sell economy energy based on the actual price of its low-cost gas was reasonable and that its proposal to make such sales based on a gas cost nearer market price offered numerous advantages which outweighed the limited harm to ML&P. The Commission also determined that Chugach should be allowed to deposit a portion of the · 32 ·

profits from such sales in a special account to offset future gas costs.

Moreover, the Commission also found that substantial evidence had been introduced during the hearing that the market for economy energy sales was very competitive and that price regulation of such sales by the Commission was not needed, except for the establishing of minimum and maximum prices within which actual prices could be negotiated by the seller and buyer. Therefore, the Commission proposed such limited deregulation for a trial period of one year and allowed an opportunity for comment by interested parties. 

All interested parties except ML&P supported the proposal, and the Commission rejected the arguments of ML&P against the proposal. Feiger's complaint attacked the utilities' practice of charging payments in lieu of assessments (PILAS) to lot and · 62 ·

-- even home owners seeking to connect to utility facilities initially installed to benefit others, who may also have paid all or part of the cost of that initial extension and are reimbursed through the PILAS. FISCAL YEAR 1989 PLAN 

Beginning with FY89 the Commission has instituted an annual planning process. The purpose of this process is to enable the Commission to develop a program for the coming year which recognizes and manages its ongoing workload, establishes priorities, and identifies initiatives or special projects.

The statistical analysis of past workload included in the Agency Performance section of this report shows a pattern of regular, fairly predictable levels of certification proceedings, rate cases, tariff filings, and complaints. This work alone could reasonably consume the resources of the Commission. However, the Commission is also aware of the need and desire to address a number of substantive regulatory issues and to implement procedural reforms within the agency. The planning process is an attempt by the Commission to balance these often competing demands and aspirations. substantive

The Commission's FY89 plan and program includes the following key components: work in progress, issues, and procedural initiatives.

Work in Progress. 

A number of decisions will be published in cases heard during the last half of FY88, which have been · 73 ·

STATUTORY REVIEW 

The Commission is governed by AS 42.05 and AS 

42.06, which define its powers and duties; establish procedures for fulfilling its responsibilities; and impose certain requirements on utilities and pipeline carriers. The specific provisions of these statutes incorporate the regulatory policies of the Legislature, which are subject to regular review and reassessment. 

The purpose of this section of the annual report is to provide the Legislature, for its information and consideration, a first-hand assessment of the effectiveness of AS 42.05 and AS 42.06 in fulfilling existing policies and addressing pending issues. 

Cost of Regulation. 

a. Allocation of Costs

One of the most pressing policy issues that will be facing the Legislature is who should pay the cost of the public protection provided by the Commission. Presently, the Commission's budget is funded from the general fund and program receipts, with expenditures from the latter subject to repayment to the state. This funding mechanism is possible because of the Commission's statutory authority to allocate consultant and out-of-pocket costs incurred in a hearing or investigation among the parties, including the 

Commission, according to certain criteria (AS 42.05.651 and

Information - 84 -

AS 42.06.610).

As the result of a recent Supreme Court decision, the Commission can no longer allocate the costs of Assistant Attorneys General and can no longer routinely exclude itself from cost responsibility. While this decision creates some budget problems which will be addressed in appropriate submissions to the Legislature, it also raises a broader, long-term question: Who should pay for regulation: taxpayers (general fund) or ratepayers (program receipts)? 

There are advantages and disadvantages to each approach. 

However, a preference for the former is largely based on a policy premise that regulation is a responsibility of government and a right to which all citizens are entitled, while the latter may best be supported on a policy premise that the cost-causer should be the cost-payer. In the end, the policy that is adopted may depend on balancing those objectives as well as the budget itself. 

The Legislature has a number of options for addressing this policy question in the context of determining the Commission's FY90 budget. First, it can eliminate the statutory requirement that the Commission be included in any cost allocations for consultants or out-of-pocket expenses. 

Of course, the services of Commission personnel would continue to be provided without charge, with smaller utilities receiving priority for use of "free" services. Under this approach, the only remaining budget impact would be to add - 85 -

sufficient funds to the general fund budget for an Assistant 

Attorney General. 

Alternatively, the Legislature could eliminate mandatory Commission cost allocations with the caveat that the Commission would assume cost responsibility when money is available and when the public interest so requires. approach suggests that, in addition to the budget increment for legal services, it would be appropriate to increase the Commission's general fund allotment to allow for assuming additional costs in extraordinary cases.

As a third option, the Legislature could decide that regulation should be funded largely by the state and add monies to the Commission's general fund budget (with a corresponding decrease to the program receipts budget) to allow for case-by-case amounts or a fixed percentage of costs being assigned to the Commission and to pay for the 

Assistant Attorney General position.

Also affected by this court decision is the Commission's ability to hire temporary legal counsel under AS 

42.05.111. Specifically, the Commission can no longer allocate the costs of temporary legal services received from the Department of Law or elsewhere unless certain limited conditions are met. Consideration should be given to modifying this section of the statute to allow for maximum administrative and financial flexibility.

Information 86-

The Commission also believes that it would be desirable for AS 42.05.651 to be amended to conform to AS 

42.06.610 which allows for cost allocations during a proceeding with subsequent reallocations, if necessary. Under 

AS 42.05.651, the costs of a proceeding cannot be allocated until the hearing or investigation is completed. In the case of a long, multi-phase proceeding, there can be a substantial lag between incurring and allocating costs and a resultant delay in remitting to the general fund monies collected from cost allocations. This change would allow for interim cost allocations and prompter repayment of program receipts funds to the State. 

b. Certificate Fees 

This 

The statute at AS 42.05.661 requires that a fee of $50 be paid with all applications for certificates. fee was established in 1970 and is not cost compensatory for processing an application. As a practical matter, it has become more of an administrative burden and impediment to timely processing of applications than a source of revenue to the state. (Less than $2,500 per year is currently collected from this fee.) The Commission suggests that the 

Legislature consider eliminating this requirement or, in the alternative, developing (directly or by delegation of authority to the Commission such as in AS 42.06.607) a fee 87 -

Such a system is used by some federal regulatory agencies. 

Again, the choice is a function of legislative policy preference regarding assignment of responsibility for paying the costs of regulation.

Scope of Regulation. 

There are a number of exemptions from regulation included in AS 42.05.711 which have developed on an ad hoc basis over time in response to particular regulatory issues or concerns involving particular utilities. The evolutionary process has affected the section's overall readability and consistency. In addition, the exemption scheme may not provide the Commission necessary flexibility to respond to particular circumstances or to reduce regulatory requirements prescribed elsewhere in the statute. 

For example, it is unclear whether streamlined certification/licensing procedures for competitive telecommunications services, such as radio common carrier or cable television operations, would be permissible under AS 42.05. Accordingly, the Commission believes that it would be desirable for the Legislature to generally review the exemptions in light of its current policy objectives and to consider refining this section to enhance comprehension, consistency, and flexibility. Under AS 42.05.720(4) (F) and AS 42.05.711(i), the

Commission certificates all refuse utilities but economically regulates only those with gross annual revenues in excess of $200,000. The Commission continues to find that the amount of time dedicated to this regulatory responsibility is excessive relative to both available agency resources and resultant public benefits. Accordingly, the Legislature is urged to reconsider and to adopt previous sunset audit recommendations to eliminate refuse utilities from the Commission's jurisdiction. This change is appropriate because of the characteristics of the industry; the Commission's current and emerging policies regarding competition in this industry; the limited effectiveness of refuse utility regulation without corollary regulation of monopoly landfill or incinerator services; the current limitations on economic regulation of the industry; the existing role of the Department of Environmental Conservation in regulating health standards for transportation and disposal of refuse; and the current authority of local governments for public health and welfare in this area under AS 29.48.033 which is now preempted by AS 42.05. However, if the Legislature decides that, as a matter of policy, refuse utilities should continue to be subject to the Commission's jurisdiction, then consideration should be given to expanding the scope of that - 89 -

authority to include landfills and incinerators to the extent necessary to adequately protect the public. 

One of the most convoluted sections of the Commission's statutes is AS 42.05.431, which has been modified in recent years in response to a number of special concerns. 

In particular, the Commission notes that those provisions of subsection (b) which govern the evaluation and modification of rates under wholesale power agreements are awkward, if not unworkable. For example, it is unclear how the dispute resolution procedure would be used as the process to modify contract rates which the parties previously have accepted but which the Commission subsequently found are not just and reasonable. The Commission alerts the Legislature to the possible problems with this section so that it can be rewritten to clarify its application before it is necessary to be used and found to be less than useful. 

Miscellaneous. 

Pursuant to AS 42.05.365, utilities are required to pay interest on customer deposits according to certain terms and conditions. If it was the Legislature's intent to have this provision of the statute apply to all public utilities regardless of their regulatory status, then it Information - 90 -

should be clearly stated in this section or should be excluded from the scope of the exemptions listed in AS 42.05.711.

Circumstances occasionally arise where utilities are unable to return interim rate refunds or deposits to former customers. Under AS 34.45.190, a utility is required to hold these funds for a year and then follow the procedures for abandoned property set forth in AS 34.45.280 .340. These sections require the holder of unclaimed property which is presumed abandoned to file a verified report with the State; send notice to the owner's last address; maintain certain records; and, finally, pay all the unclaimed refunds to the Department of Revenue within a specified time. The Commission believes that such unclaimed funds should be used for the benefit of all customers of a utility as may be directed by the Commission. For example, the funds could be used as cost-free, customer-supplied capital or as an additional refund to the remaining customers. In addition, there are already substantial regulatory recordkeeping requirements for deposits and interim rates, and AS 34.45.190 imposes additional recordkeeping and costs on the utility's existing customers with no commensurate benefits. POWER COST EQUALIZATION 

Power Cost Equalization (PCE) is a program under which the State of Alaska pays a portion of the electric bills for consumers served by utilities participating in the program. Participation in the PCE program is limited by statute to utilities meeting certain requirements. For example, during 1984 a utility must have used diesel-fired generators to produce more than 75 percent of the electrical consumption of the utility; and if a utility served a single community, its 1983 residential sales eligible for PCE could not have exceeded 7,500,000 kilowatt-hours (kWh). 

The Commission sets the PCE amount (cents/kWh) applicable to each utility's customers, regardless of whether the utility is otherwise subject to the Commission's economic regulation. The PCE amount varies according to the utility's rates and its costs of producing electricity. In most cases, the PCE per kWh is the same for all the customers of a utility, but it may vary by rate schedule.

Generally PCE reduces the cost for each kWh up to 

750 kWh per month per customer. Customers designated as "community facilities" are entitled to PCE in the aggregate for each community served by the utility for actual consumption up to 70 kWh per month for each resident of the community. For example, if a utility served one community with a population of 150 and two community facilities, those two community facilities together would be entitled to PCE for their total consumption up to 

10,500 kWh per month.

As most consumers are aware, the regulation of the telephone industry has changed significantly during the 1980s. In 1980, for example, the rates shown in this section would have been based on the premise that the utility was responsible not only for providing consumers access to the telephone network but also for providing the telephone instruments and maintaining the telecommunications wiring inside a consumer's premises. 

Responding to Federal Communications Commission directives issued in 1982, this Commission required all regulated local exchange utilities to unbundle (break down) their monthly telephone rates into separate components for customer premised equipment, inside wiring, and access lines. Since that time, customer premises equipment and inside wiring have been deregulated--customers now buy their own telephone instruments wherever they wish and are responsible for maintaining the telecommunications wiring inside their homes. The majority of the rates shown in the following chart are for access lines only and do not include any charge for inside wiring or telephone instruments.

STATUTES AND REGULATIONS The Statutes. 

The Alaska Public Utilities Commission was created in 1959 under the Alaska Public Utilities Commission Act (AS 

42.05) and, since 1970, has been a full-time administrative agency charged with the duty of regulating public utilities within the State. The jurisdiction of the Commission extends to electric, gas, refuse (garbage), sewer (wastewater), steam, telecommunications (cable television, local exchange, interexchange, and radio common carrier service), and water public utilities as defined by the Act. In 1981 the Legislature amended the Alaska Pipeline Commission Act (AS 42.06) to merge the Alaska Pipeline Commission into the 

Alaska Public Utilities Commission, and the Commission's jurisdiction was extended to pipeline carriers and pipelines.

The Commission is comprised of five commissioners appointed by the Governor and confirmed by the Legislature for six-year terms of office. In addition to the business, engineering, and law members, there are two consumer members of the Commission, and the Commission is empowered to employ personnel to assist it in the performance of its duties. 146 -

E The Commission is responsible for making or requiring just, fair, and reasonable rates, classifications, regulations, practices, services, and facilities for public utilities and pipeline carriers. The Commission has the authority to investigate, hold hearings, prescribe systems of accounts, determine depreciation rates, require the filing of reports, adopt regulations, and take other lawful actions necessary to accomplish the stated purposes of 

AS 42.05 and AS 42.06. The Commission also determines the eligibility of electric utilities for power cost equalization and the kilowatt-hour subsidy amount under the provisions of AS 44.83.162 44.83.164. empowered under AS 31.15.010
