FOREWORD 

In his delightful and informative treatise on the wines of France which he modestly calls "Notes on French Wines", Mr. Philip 

Dexter promulgates the fundamental principle that 'a good name must be in conjunction with a good year to produce a satisfactory wine'. Not being blessed with a memory for figures, I have on numerous occasions, when confronted with a wine list, found myself humiliatingly uncertain as to the merit or otherwise of a particular year for a particular wine. With a view therefore to improving not only my memory but my knowledge of 'good years', I have been delving into the works of such eminent Anglo- 

Saxon professors as Morton Shand, Saintsbury, F. H. Butler, and Mr. Dexter himself in an attempt to synthesize their views regarding vintage years with the rather more decided and often differing opinions of the leading Gallic_authorities on wines in particular those of the Messieurs de Cassagnac, des Ombiaux, le Docteur Paul Ramain, Raymond Baudouin, and Constantin Weyer. The following regional listing of some of the better known named growths or crus with accompanying vintage years represents in skeleton form the opinions held in common by a majority of the above mentioned savants as to the best years for each region and in some instances for individual crus as well. While in a few cases there will also be found the results of a slight amount of personal field work in French vineyards and cafés, the writer wishes to clearly establish the fact that this opus is mainly a compilation of names and years derived from the findings of the aforesaid 

M.A.s and PH.D.s of the Art-science of Gourmetics. (See bibli ography together with qualifying remarks on last page.) 

I have confined myself to French wines because I believe that France produces the finest wines in the world and in such great variety that there is an appropriate French wine for every conceivable occasion. I know of no natural wines, with the exception of the great Rhine wines,* produced outside of France that are * Tokay should also be excepted I suppose.

worth making a fuss about. Port, Sherry and Madeira, are of course all fortified wines and while they can be excellent hold no great interest, I confess, for me.

What other country but France can supply from soup to nuts the complete and perfect vinous accompaniment to each course of a good dinner? A Chablis, Pouilly, Montrachet, Meursault, Hermitage blanc, a dry white Graves, Vouvray or light Sauternes, not to mention Tavel, Seyssel, and Champagne nature, with the fish; a Medoc, a red Graves or Arbois, or a light Beaujolais with the chicken or 'viandes blanches'; with the roast, game, and cheese, any of the great red wines; with dessert a great Sauternes,

Anjou, Vouvray, or Champagne. Certain unique wines like 

Montrachet, Château Châlon, and Coulée de Serrant can be imbibed throughout a meal, but most gourmets prefer to exercise their palates in conformity with the three fundamental laws governing wines. They are: (1) each wine should be accompanied only by food appropriate to that particular wine; (2) never serve a full-bodied generous wine before a more fragile one of delicate bouquet; (3) effect a crescendo in the quality of the successive wines served.

All civilized people the world over are familiar with the great crus of Bordeaux and Burgundy. Lafite, Haut-Brion, Mouton 

Rothschild, Yquem on the one hand, and Romanée Conti, Vougeot, Chambertin, Montrachet, on the other are as much household words as the names of the great Champagne firms. Your experienced drinker of Claret and Burgundy does not order simply a 

Médoc or Beaune sans année, he treats himself to Château Margaux 1900 or Clos du Roi 1904 if he can get it. He knows that the more or less accepted axiom to the effect that the Burgundies should be drunk when they are from nine to twelve years old and the Bordeaux from ten to sixteen is, like every rule regarding wines, subject to numerous exceptions, and, that depending on the year and the cru, the ideal drinking-point for either Bordeaux or Burgundy may be reached anywhere from six to thirty years, or, in exceptional cases more, from the date of harvest. (For certain white wines the period may extend for fifty years and more). He also knows that wines of lesser years mature rapidly and must be drunk young since they do not last, whereas the wines of the great years usually mature slowly and are often disagreeably hard for as long as fifteen years. He knows too that in some cases the drinking-point once reached, a particular wine may fade rapidly (e.g. the 1911 Burgundies, also probably the 1919s which are very good now but will not reach their full-blown drinking-point for another two or three years) and that on the other hand the Burgundies of 1865 and 1904 apparently keep indefinitely. He knows further that many of the fine Bordeaux of a great year remain hard for a long time and only attain their perfect drinking-point between the ages of fifteen and thirty. Deposit this same experienced 

Claret and Burgundy drinker in Anger, Tour, or Avignon, let us say, and the chances are that unless he has made a special study of the given region he will be rather hazy as to crus and years. 

Probably he will resign himself in a half hearted way to 'doing as the Romans do,' allow the hotel sommelier to foist upon him some

nondescript and yearless Anjou, Vouvray, or Chateauneuf du Pape, as the case may be, and on finding same unpalatable and indigestible, as it is likely to be, our experienced C. and B. drinker makes a firm resolve never again to waste time trying out local wines in the provinces. Now I feel sure that a certain lack of enthusiasm on the part of a number of English writers on wines for the product of some of the French wine regions other than Bordeaux or Burgundy can be explained by their failure, like that of our hypothetical friend, to observe that cardinal principle already set forth namely, that 'a good name must be in conjunction with a good year to produce a satisfactory wine.' A 'good name', of course, implies not merely the name of a province or sub-region or department but a definite vineyard, chateau, clos, or cru of established reputation. (In the case of the champagne of commerce which is a blended and fortified wine the 'name' is that of the house that puts it up.) Many good crus, and some great ones, are to be found in the above regions, not as readily of course as at the Chapon Fin in Bordeaux or the Hotel de la Poste at Beaune, but a certain amount of persistence will usually result in unearthing something worth the effort. Last spring at Pau, Mr. Dexter in spite of having often professed to be satisfied to stick to the great Bordeaux, Burgundies, and Champagnes, being a genuine oenophile at heart took the trouble to make an expedition on foot up into the hills for the purpose of tracking down in its lair the elusive Jurancon which is after all one of the remarkable white wines of France. While not partial to the sweeter wines he assures me that the day was not wasted and that the Clos la Joliette gave up some interesting specimens. You, for example, who are possibly not very favorably disposed towards Vouvray, next time you are in Touraine hunt up a bottle of Château de Moncontour 1904 or Clos-de-Cosson 1908 and I am convinced that you will enjoy yourself, at any rate you will be prepared to admit that there is as much difference between a fine natural Vouvray of a good year and the watery mousseux that they may try to palm off on you at the hotel in Tour, as there is between a Château Climens 1924 and some sulphurous specimen of Haut-Barsac bought at the railway station 

The point, if any, that I am trying to make is that for all the wine regions of France, those with only a local reputation as well as the famous ones, it is highly desirable to rigidly apply the precept as to names and years. The list which follows is, as I have said before, only a skeleton. In many cases the earlier years cited are risky to experiment with and in as many more are mere memories. Again in most cases the very recent years are for laying down purposes only. Midway between, the earnest oenophile should by dint of repeated libations find something to his taste, though God knows that even when name and year pass muster there are endless pitfalls for the unwary, bad corks, unsuitable storage conditions, improper temperature, brutal or incompetent servitors, uncongenial food or companions, a bad liver, and downright fraud being but a few. However the surmounting of difficulties is stimulating in itself and if my list should prove even remotely instrumental in bringing together a good bottle and a pleasant person it will have more than served its purpose.

BORDEAUX 

"Il y a une raison pour laquelle les Bordelais peuvent faire avec leurs vins, et uniquement avec eux, un repas de grand style. C'est que dans le seul Bordelais, vous trouvez une gamme complète de vins en mariant, non seulement les crus, mais les années. L'extrêmé sensibilite du vignoble girondin n'est approchée par aucune autre région." Constantin-Weyer.

From the Bordeaux region come the world famous Clarets, Sauternes and Graves. Claret, i.e., Red Bordeaux, should be brought from the cellar long enough before serving time to allow it to attain naturally, and not by immersion in hot water or other artificial means, the temperature of the room. The proper serving temperature for a White Bordeaux depends on its body and flavor. 

The White Graves and lighter Sauternes should be cooled but not iced too much if at all. A heavy Sauternes should be iced but not below freezing point.

The serving temperature of the Red Burgundies need not be quite the temperature of the room as in the case of the Red Bordeaux.

While many authorities would disagree, I believe it advisable not to serve a red Bordeaux and a red Burgundy at the same repast. The wines of a given region though they may be totally dissimilar have a subtle affinity for one another and a more harmonious symphony may be achieved by leading up to a great Romanée Conti, for example, by first offering one of the fine Beaunes or a sound Beaujolais, (preceded presumably by a Pouilly, Meursault, or Chablis) than if a Bordeaux, even one of the finest, is introduced midway. Where a great claret is to be the crowning glory of a given meal, the surrounding region, as we have already seen, is well able to provide every degree and shading in the vinous scale as a fitting prelude. 12

ANJOU

Vouvray There is a great deal of spurious Vouvray. The genuine article does not travel well and it is only in Touraine that it reaches its perfection. Sometimes "petillant", sometimes still, the wines of the named crus of Vouvray are semi-dry in some years and sweet in others. A good Vouvray of 1893 is almost like a liqueur and should be iced and served as a dessert wine like a great Sauternes. 

If Vouvrays of different years are to appear at the same meal the older ones must be served first as their delicacy would otherwise be impaired by the younger and stouter ones. As far as I know this is the only exception to the orthodox procedure.

CHAMPAGNE 

Since the advent of Prohibition I have noticed with interest at

American dinner-parties and speakeasies bottles bearing the labels Heidsieck, Lanson, Mumm, etc., which, on being opened with a loud report, give forth a liquid resembling a mixture of carbonated cider and sulphuric acid combined with the violent but momentary effervescent qualities of Seidlitz powders. For the edification of those who pay good money for these strange. chemicals let me quote Dr. Ramain: "Vous ne devez pas ignorer qu'une bouteille de Champagne qui mousse beaucoup et quie 'bave' son liquide hors du goulot, est le signe certain d'un mauvais vin. Le grand 

Champagne loyal, lorsqu'on le débouche ne fait entendre qu'un petit bruit sec et discret, et la mousse légère, precedée d'un nimbe de vapeur bleue, ne fait qu'affleurer les bords du goulot. Dans la flute, la mousse retombe vite et les mille et une perles bulleuses restent minuscules, serrées, et montent longtemps, indefiniment le long des parois cristallines."

BORDEAUX 

The problem of purchasing fine Bordeaux is a relatively simple one due to the large number of first rate vineyards, most of which are officially classified, and, above all, to the custom of Chateau bottling which affords a real guarantee of authenticity. Luckily for those who are planning cellars, the last decade contains some excellent Bordeaux years. By common consent 1928 and 1929 will prove great years and should be laid down now. 1926 was a good year, but not quite up to what was expected of it. 1924 was a very fine year and many of its wines are ready to drink now. 1921 was a great year for the 

White Bordeaux, but not superlative among the Red. 1920 was a good year but the wine is rather light, so that great care must be exercised in picking those with enough body to stand the transatlantic trip. Before 1920 one would have to go back to 1906 to find a really great year.

BURGUNDY 

The purchase of really fine Burgundies presents far greater difficulties. The number of great vineyards are small and their area and annual production in some cases minute. Most of the great vineyards are owned not by a single proprietor, but by innumerable small owners who sell their grapes to the large wine firms of Beaune, Nuits, Dijon, etc., who in turn ferment and bottle the wine. Chateau or estate bottling is only practiced in rare instances, so that the authenticity of Burgundy is largely dependent upon the integrity of the wine house that puts it up, since the French law relating to "appellation d'origines" does not in the case of the Burgundies preclude a great deal of adulteration. 

With the above facts in mind, the writer has contacted only those firms in the Burgundy district that are either (1) sole owners of an important vineyard, the wine being estate bottled, or (2) actual part owners of one or more of the great vineyards and who, therefore, take a special pride in the wines of those particular vineyards bearing their house label.
