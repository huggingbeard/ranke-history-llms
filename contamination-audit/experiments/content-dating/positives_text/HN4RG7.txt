TO BOYS AND GIRLS 

Ideas which a person can't express are of little value to him or anybody else. 

With their hands most people express some of their ideas in the form of drawings, paintings, music, cakes, gardens, houses, and automobiles. Many ideas, however, must be expressed in words or not at all. One who wishes to share with his friends what he knows, thinks, and imagines needs a good command of the English language. In school and out of school the person who can't tell clearly what he knows is seriously handicapped. 

How does one learn to play a game well-football or baseball, for example?

He studies the rules of the game, watches good players, reads newspaper and magazine articles and books about the game, and listens eagerly to every word the coach utters. But he also day after day practices alertly and earnestly to translate his ideas about the game into habits and skills. 

Good English is a habit and skill which you can acquire in the same way-by reading, listening, studying, and then practicing daily and hourly the use of your best English. This Practice Book furnishes many suggestions for the improvement of your English and a variety of interesting practice in the application of these ideas. If you play the game intelligently and enthusiastically, you will grow up rapidly as a speaker and writer. 

Before working an exercise or taking a test, study the explanations, examples, and directions thoroughly. Then do exactly what you are told. 

After you have done an exercise, you will often mark your own work and enter the score at the top of the page and on the progress chart at the end of the book. 

In most of the tests the score is the number right (My Score Right). When, however, by guessing you could answer half the questions correctly, the score is the number right minus the number wrong (Right - Wrong Score). In making up marks your teacher will use the scores on your mastery tests, but not the scores on your exercises. Hence, if you cheat, you only cheat yourself out of preparation for the test which counts. 

Write neatly. Not much writing is necessary. Also draw neatly the straight lines which take the place of written explanations. year.

Correct your exercises and tests and keep them all till the end of the school In each exercise you will find first some easy sentences and later slightly harder ones. I hope that you will enjoy solving the problems and discovering that your answers are right and that you will travel rapidly and happily along the English in Action highway to the delightful land of correct, picturesque, forceful English.

TO THE TEACHER 

This Practice Book provides practice, tests, and a final examination to supplement Junior English in Action, Book III; English in Action, Book I; English in 

Action, Course I; or another textbook for the last year of the junior high school or the first year of a four-year high school.

The terse explanations and the examples preceding the exercises supplement the textbook, obviate the necessity of frequent references to it, and enable pupils without textbooks to work through English in Action Practice Book H. 

The exercises provide training in building correct and effective sentences, paragraphs, and letters. Many are based on stories or compositions. The grammar included is functional that is, it is reduced to the lowest terms compatible with learning to speak, write, and read. The primary objective is habit formation. 

Because the tests parallel those in English in Action, a teacher using one of the In Action textbooks for the grade has available on every division of the work two or three tests equal in length and approximately equal in difficulty. In his own way each teacher will use these tests to discover initial ability, stimulate progress, and measure achievement. A pupil who makes a perfect score on a pre-test should, of course, be excused from drill on the unit. The final examination or achievement test facilitates long-range testing. The first fifty questions of the examination may be used as a mid-year test and the last fifty as a final examination. 

The mastery tests and the final examination are in a package by themselves. 

When distributing the books, retain the tests. Then you will be able to hand out a printed test whenever the class is ready for it, or when one pupil is prepared for it if you use the individual method.

My aim has been to give pupils abundant practice in constructing effective sentences and correcting faulty ones and at the same time to reduce to a minimum the type of exercise which requires recopying and which pupils can't mark. In other words, I have tried to devise exercises which provide a maximum of mental exercise and habit-forming practice for the pupils with a minimum of pupil finger exercise and teacher correction. Under sharp supervision pupils score accurately most of the exercises. Ordinarily it is wise, I think, for the teacher to score the tests or to have intelligent, careful assistant teachers score them. 

The progress chart on the last pages may be made a record of achievement, a stimulus to high endeavor, and a help in establishing the habit of succeeding. Most boys and girls play and work harder if a score is kept. J. C. T.

The predicate verb of a sentence makes a statement, asks a question, or gives a command. (Statement) Uncle Sidney came back with a fox. (Question) Is that lanky boy your brother? (Command) Draw a rough diagram of the accident. Auxiliaries

Some verbs have auxiliaries which help them to make statements, ask questions, or give commands. The auxiliaries are: is, be, am, are, was, were, been, do, does, did, has, have, had, may, can, might, could, must, shall, will, should, and would. (Two-word verb) Frogs were calling in the marshes. (Two-word verb) Do all cypress trees come from Cyprus? (Two-word verb) Don't run away from the scene of an accident. [Don't = do + not] (Three-word verb) Will a prize be given to the winner of every race? (Four-word verb) The three pirates must have been watching us closely.

Complete Subject 

Modify is a longer word for change. To modify a plan, a contract, or a machine is to change it.

The roots and the trunk of a tree are necessary but alone do not make a complete tree. Large branches, tiny limbs, and green leaves add beauty and supply shade. Likewise the simple subject and the verb, the most important parts of the sentence, often need modifiers to complete, enrich, or change them. (Without modifiers) Emerald is. (With modifiers) The largest emerald in the world | is in the Turkish throne. [The vertical line separates the subject with its modifiers from the verb with its modifiers.]

The simple subject with its modifiers is called the complete subject.

Complete Predicate 

The complete predicate includes the predicate, its modifiers, and words used to complete its meaning. Usually every word in the sentence belongs to either the complete subject or the complete predicate. 

Charles A. Lindbergh | flew from New York to Paris alone in his monoplane with one motor.

The vertical line separates the complete subject from the complete predicate. The simple subject is underscored; the simple predicate has two lines under it.

When the complete predicate or part of it is before the subject, the order is inverted. 

1. Near the fire stood a great wooden bowl in the shape of a frog. 

A great wooden bowl in the shape of a frog | stood near the fire. 

2. Does the use of tobacco actually affect athletic ability?

The use of tobacco | does actually affect athletic ability. 

3. There are many wonderful national parks in the United States. 

Many wonderful national parks | are in the United States. 15

When there is used to invert a sentence, it is called an expletive or "introductory adverb," and is not a modifier.

Pronoun A pronoun is a word used in place of a noun. The word pronoun means for a noun. These short substitutes save our time and make our sentences more pleasing. (With pronouns) While Mr. Tilden was trimming his rambler roses, he hurt his hand. (Without pronouns) While Mr. Tilden was trimming Mr. Tilden's rambler roses, Mr. Tilden hurt Mr. Tilden's hand.

Some commonly used pronouns are - 

I, my, mine, me we, our, ours, us you, your, yours they, their, theirs, them he, his, him she, her, hers it, its

Several pronouns are formed by adding self or selves to other pronouns: myself, ourselves, himself, herself, themselves. 

This, that, these, those, which, and what are often pronouns but are not pronouns when they modify nouns. (Pronouns) This is the tree which Eugene planted. (Modifiers --not pronouns) Which tree in this row did Eugene plant? (Pronouns) What is that? (Modifiers) What books are on that mahogany table? 20

Other Uses of the Comma

An expression slipped into a sentence and loosely connected with the rest of the sentence is called parenthetical and is set off by commas. 

The Greeks, like us, considered athletics an important part of their education. 

In the game with Jefferson High, however, Joe White was a star. 

The comma, as a rule, is not used to set off also, perhaps, indeed, therefore, at least, in fact, nevertheless, or likewise. 

The comma is used to set off contrasting expressions introduced by not. 

I said tents, not tense. 44

Place a comma after yes or no when used as a part of an answer. 

Yes, I'll be ready at eight.

Occasionally a comma is used to prevent misreading when no other rule justifies the use of punctuation. 

Ever since, I have had more respect for goats.

In print names of books and plays are usually italicized. In penwritten or typed work they may be inclosed in quotation marks or underscored.
