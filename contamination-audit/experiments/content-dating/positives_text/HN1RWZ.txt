"Whoop-ee, Buddy, look where we are! 

Zowee, what a State!" "She's the queen of them all,” came the hearty response. Enthusiastic grins decorated the faces of James Austin and his stepbrother, Robert Caldwell, Jim and Bob for short; Buddies for shorter. Jim was piloting their right-up-to-the-minute plane, Her Highness, and they were returning to their home land after giving the U. S. and a portion of Canada the once-over. It had been a wonderful trip, and in sturdy old Vermont the boys had run into enough thrills to content any pair for the rest of their natural lives and now the young fellows looked for-

ward to hard riding, cattle punching, and such every-day routine work as the Texas ranch along Cap Rock had to offer.

In making the trip north the step-brothers had followed a hop-and-stop course along the coast line, so they set an entirely different route, a sort of zig-zag way which took them over Washington, then as far as the Pacific, and finally back across the western 'border of their own State. Both had fine observation glasses, and a pair was pressed to Bob's eyes to give him a closer view of the loved landscape. He saw the Rio Grande winding leisurely two thousand feet below, beyond were miles and miles of rolling sand, desert-like in its sameness, then he picked out spindle-legged steel towers that marked the new oil fields. Tiny towns that had grown up like mushrooms in a few week's time, and bunches of oyster-colored donkeys grazing in parched pastures.

The young fellow knew that the wiry beasts of burden were as valuable to the communities as cayuses or bronchos were on the cattle ranches further west, but he gave them only a casual glance. What was a donkey in comparison to a fury-eyed pony that

would buck with joy when the saddle was thrown across his back? Bob grinned again as he thought of the two pintos-his own King Tut, and Jim's Scuttle, who were probably at that moment tearing around the house corral, stopping occasionally to poke their velvety noses between the heavy posts of the enclosure and nickering anxiously for freedom. "An hour more and we'll be forking the horses," Jim shouted. "Aw, speed up," Bob urged. "Don't go to sleep!" There was a note of impatience in his voice, and his step-brother gave him an understanding glance. Her Highness was banked and zoomed to thirty thousand feet, the course was set on a bee line, and every ounce of power was put to instant use as they cut their way forward. The wind howled through the wires, shrieking a protest, but neither of the boys paid a partical of attention.

Keeping his wits about him Jim attended to his job, but it was more or less mechanical so he could permit his mind to wander a bit. He thought of the Fenton's in North 

Hero, Vermont, and was glad that Bob's

hospitable Aunt and Uncle need no longer worry because of the loss of their principle source of income, the turkey farm. It struck him as odd that no one had ever suspected 

Hezzy Burley of stealing the young birds himself, then he accounted for it by recalling that Hezzy had lived all his life in the vicinity, and had apparently been as honest as his neighbors. It wasn't until the borderjumpers got him into their clutches, determined to use the old farm house on Isle La Motte as a hang-out for undesirable aliens smuggled into the United States, that he turned to being a thief on his own account. 

Jim thought of those breathless minutes before his lariat caught the gang-leader by the leg, and he wondered where he got the nerve to attempt such a feat. "Dumb-headedness," he assured himself. "This hero stuff is bunk," he added. "A fellow does the only thing he thinks he can, and if he doesn't get bumped off he's lucky." Then his mind went to their new friends, the 

Mounties, Allen Ruhel the chief at Quebec and Sergeant Bradshaw. He thought of the night after the furious battle in the ravine when the medals had been pinned to their

lapels, and when Bob had promised to decorate them with horseshoes if they visited the ranch. He hoped they would come sometime, they were first rate fellows every one of them. Besides all this, in the boy's luggage was a letter from the U. S. Post Office Department thanking the youngsters for their prompt action in saving the life of Phil Mason, the air-mail man, and the mail. "It's been a grand trip," Bob spoke through the speaking tube and Jim nodded agreement. 

They certainly had had a whale of a time. 

In his mental vision Jim saw again the strange boy-the young prince of an ancient race. This was the hardest part of their experience to make real, but in his shirt pocket, pressing hard against his chest, was the huge emerald with its queer little tube, while each of the boys wore a ring with one of the green stones set deep and surrounded by queer designs that meant something to any member of that strange clan. Jim wished that they had taken a moment to question Corso or the boy as to the meaning of the writing, but great guns, they had been so excited after hours of helping stranded

families from the danger zone of Lake Champlain, then discovering the boy in the strange costume of his own race, that it was no wonder that a little matter like an individual's identity, or a phrase of a lost language, had been entirely forgotten. Suddenly, in the distance he spied the jagged edge of Cap Rock as it cut across the country like a rugged back bone. "Almost there," he shouted, but Bob was too happy to do more than nod his head. 

Directly beneath them stretched miles of grain fields and further south began the huge cattle ranches. Her Highness' nose was brought about sharply for the wind had taken advantage of the pilot's abstraction to drive them a bit out of the course. Five minutes later Pearl River came into their range of vision, like a piece of blue ribbon dropped carelessly across the landscape. Then the engine gave a warning cough and Bob glanced at the indicator board. "Hey Buddy, you're almost out of gas," he exclaimed in disgust. "Howling coyotes, so I am, and that's the last tank." finally picked out a ranch house in front of which stood a limousine. "They'll have some," Bob announced with relief.
