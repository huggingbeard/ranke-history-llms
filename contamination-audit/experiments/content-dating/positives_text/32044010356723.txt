FOREWORD 

This little volume is an initial major effort to give a full account of essential and significant facts and figures on the Negro Ph.D., and associated doctorates. It is the result of long and painstaking investigation and documentation. It is designed to secure at least a record of notable academic men and women at once authentic and reliable and useful for educational planning during and after the war.

Aside from an objective account and the basal data underlying it, one finds of primary importance the list of the names of those American Negroes who have reached the rare altitude of the Ph.D., and equivalent doctor's degrees. Here for the first time the reader can examine the nature of the work of these persons and determine, to some extent of accuracy, who they are and what they have or have not accomplished as citizens in American democracy. While the author is not sure that he has recorded here the names and academic work of all the members of the race who have been admitted to the degree during the period under review, it is his belief that the list does not fall very short of being complete; and the documentary evidence supporting it makes possible the statement that the representatives are genuine possessors of the degree which itself is not "phony".

The implication of a lack of genuineness of the kind of doctorate investigated in this book might seem to some reader (unacquainted with the literature in the field) as quite superfluous. Until recently, however,

FOREWORD 

(as the author's brief studies show) so many reports had flooded the educational market that, if one took the time to count them and then relied on his number, at least five hundred such doctorates would have resulted. Often well-nigh tragic mistakes have been made by newspapers, magazines, and even college catalogs in reporting doctorates never won. Confusion with honorary degrees accounts for some of the errors.

It is hoped, then, that the present effort might serve as an instrumentality for supplying useful material corrective of misconceptions relative to the Ph.D., as well as providing the reader with a more thorough-going insight into the total problem.

CHAPTER I INTRODUCTION 

The Ph.D., or an equivalent doctoral degree, continues to be of value to American scholarship. Despite occasional, caustic criticisms of the purpose and method of granting it,' the doctorate still emerges as a very significant and influential symbol of scholarship and education in American life. Americans repose great, even at times, reverent faith in the efficacy of education. 

The academic degree which an individual as well as a group of individuals achieves often is seriously adjudged as an important criterion of his or their education. Indeed in some educational centers, such as in small American colleges, possession of a doctorate is wellnigh of survival value. An extensive list of reference work by Good' and others, indicating the nature of the investigations incident to the Ph.D., is illustrative of

Related Studies 

No systematic or anything resembling a comprehensive study has been made on the Negro in relation to his earning doctoral degrees. The Crisis magazine has listed annually in its Education Number names of Negroes who were reported to have secured the degree along with the universities that conferred it. 3 The Negro Yearbook, a publication of Tuskegee, and directed by Monroe Work, did a similar service for a number of years. In two books, Johnson supplied some important statistics on the subject. But these and other works were quite inadequate and incomplete in their treatment of the particular problem at hand. They, in this respect, can even be spoken of as inaccurate in a number of instances-really not admittedly designed to satisfy the conditions of a minor study. For a number of years, Dr. Ellis O. Knox has analyzed for the Journal of Negro Education the doctoral dissertations which treat the Negro as a subject of research.

The present writer has for sixteen years expressed special interest in the output of doctorates among this group. As a direct result of this interest, five minor studies have been published. The first article to appear carried as its purpose "to furnish some definite information on the relation of the degree, Doctor of Philosophy, to the American Negro." The second article aimed to "present some new facts and statistics relative to Negro holders of the doctorate" in order that certain misinformation and errors on the subject current at the time might be made known and corrected." The third and fourth' contributions in the series were designed especially for supplementation of the data as presented in the publication entitled "Negro Holders of the Ph.D. Degree." The fifth and last article pur ported (1) "to extend the present knowledge of the number and quality of these doctoral degrees and the several fields in which they were won; (2) to try to discover other significant facts on the present status of the doctorate."'s

All five articles were based on the results of careful investigation, entailing painstaking official documentation in every case.

Purpose and Problem 

Avidity on the part of Negro colleges to secure and maintain collegiate accreditment accentuates the need for teachers with doctoral degrees. This need is further emphasized in the rapid development of graduate education in Negro institutions of higher learning. 

The demand for research and creative studies within the areas of Negro life must be continuously met, if anything like a constructive and functional program of education for this group is ever to be formulated. And the rigorous training of an original nature required of those who seek the Ph. D. degree would seem to make a person highly competent to undertake such research. 

In the light of these assumptions, questions and doubts naturally arise as to the extent to which these needs are now being satisfied.

The purpose of the present study is to present as accurate and complete a picture as possible of the status of "earned" doctorates among Negroes in the hope that educational planning in this area of experience might 、 secure a more factual basis for generalization and a sounder sense of direction toward fulfillment of its needs.

The problem is involved in the effort to find answers to the following questions: (1) How many Negroes in America have actually earned Ph.D., and other equivalent doctoral degrees? (2) What is the source and quality of these doctorates? (3) In what fields or areas of concentration was study for the doctorates done? (4) What is the extent of the research and creative output? (5) What are the occupations of the holders of doctorates? (6) What are significant achievements incident to the possession of the doctorates? These six questions constitute the basal outline of the problem, and on this outline the entire work is predicated. The word "actually" as used in question (1) implies that a number of reports circulated about doctorates have been false.

Procedures and Scope of Study 

In the attempt to find answers to the questions, careful steps were taken: (1) Compilation of names and data as from time to time appear in the Negro weeklies, magazines, college catalogs, and other secondary sources. In respect to this approach, certain weaknesses and limitations are apparent. While such sources are profitable as basis for initiating an inquiry of the kind, they are utterly unreliable in matters tending toward scientific investigation. It was necessary then (2) to authenticate the case of each unofficially reported doctorate by writing directly to the respective graduate school official involved. The information supplied by the school, however, was the result of continuous requests over a period of sixteen years. This method proved, of course, the most reliable; but despite this, one may discern a slight weakness even here.

The investigator, in requesting official confirmation for a degree, would himself have to be sure of the racial identification of the recipient, since most of the universities do not segregate their records on the basis of the race of their students. (3) Other data were also secured by letter or questionnaire sent to the institutions; and (4) examination of national directories, books, and periodicals yielded relevant facts and information that could not be obtained from the graduate schools. (5) Information was secured directly from persons affected.

Notwithstanding the limitations of the methods and procedures as thus employed, the investigator believes that the data are reliable and represent a high degree of accuracy.

The general inquiry is divided into three main parts. 

Part I is specifically concerned with the total output of the doctorates along with the nature and sources of the degrees the institutions that conferred them and their general quality-and other aspects of the academic background of their recipients.

Part II attempts to disclose the status in connection with some significant achievements incident to the doctoral and pre-doctoral study. It classifies these degrees on the basis of broad subject-matter specializations of their holders and seeks to present a brief, objective account of each specialist within the respective classified fields.

These descriptive (or catalog) accounts are designed to supply a general framework against which a group study of each specialized field is to be projected. Part 

II also is intended to serve the purpose of a general directory of holders of doctorates among Negroesa service believed long since overdue.

Part III includes a study of occupational status, together with some economic, social, and political considerations involved in membership and participation in social organizations. It contains a general summary and a relatively large effort at analysis, interpretations, and synthesis of the total work.

PART I. 

CHAPTER II OUTPUT AND SIGNIFICANCE 

The Ph.D.—an original German product-has been conferred in America since 1866. Yale University was the first American institution to award a Ph.D. degree and likewise the first so to honor a Negro.

The present chapter is specifically concerned with the output of Ph.D.'s and those other doctor's degrees which are usually regarded as having equivalent academic value. It should be noted, however, that not all universities are willing to agree that the associated doctorates, as will be shown here, are "actually" on parity with the Ph.D. degree. The latter degree has the advantage of a long and noble tradition, and as such, is carefully guarded from the "infringement" of newcomers as a mark of scholarly distinction. Moreover, the degree of Doctor of Philosophy is universally accepted as the reward of proven research competence, resulting in an original contribution to knowledge. The so-called equivalent degrees, on the other hand, do not seem to be as clear in meaning and purpose as the first doctorate (Ph.D.). One point appears clear to this writer: the "equivalent doctoral degrees" were designed to achieve a professional objective. And since there is no evidence of any discrimination against these degrees by institutions who employ their holders, and since the great majority of the universities that grant both degrees do not deny such equality, such degrees, then, are accepted in this work as equivalent to the Ph.D.

The Output 

Since 1876 at least 381 persons of Negro descent have been admitted to the degree of Doctor of Philosophy or its corresponding academic equivalent. This report analyzes the work of 368 of them. At least 17 of this number have deceased. Table I presents the data in periods of five years, except in case of the period beginning in 1940. 51 Ph.D.'s conferred over a span of 53 years upon

American Negroes. This comparatively small number of doctorates during a period extending over a halfcentury would seem alarming, if the comparative infancy of the educational movement among colored folk was not taken into consideration, especially for the period under review. During this time the Negro college at its best was nothing more than a glorified Junior College when compared with the standards of the American college in general. Regional accreditment of the Negro college began as late as 1920. Public high schools for the race at the time were far from being fully developed, and its collegiate institutions were often hampered by the onus of high school work-even elementary studies in a number of cases. In the North, Negroes who had access to the schools of higher learning by reason of location and preparation, frequently found themselves greatly handicapped by lack of sufficient funds. These causes help to account for what otherwise would seem to be a surprisingly low output of doctorates among Negroes during the fifty odd

years prior to 1930. Without doubt, the effects of the first World War were an added causal factor in the conditions affecting the output.

For the next fourteen years (1930-1943) a far different and interesting story could be told. Addition of figures covering this period shows that 316, which is well over four-fifths of all doctorates recorded in this volume, have been produced since 1930. This almost phenomenal development seems the more remarkable when it is realized that those years in the main represented the period of the worst national economic depression in American history; and the last three years of this same period found the nation in total war. 

Nevertheless, inspection of the table of distribution reveals that during the five-year period (1935–39)regarded by many as representing the "deep" period of the depression-119 or nearly one-third of the total number of doctorates had been turned out. During the last three years of study, 128 or over one-third of the total were effected. The social dislocation inherent in the present World War had not yet arrested the development toward doctoral degrees for Negroes.

It is worth noting that during the period extending from 1926 to 1942-constituting 17 years—a total of 

38,765 Ph.D.'s were awarded by American universities.

And this figure represents a large increase over previous periods.¹

To account for this accelerated growth in doctoral degrees among Negroes is a problem of large proportion. The data affecting the fourteen-year period from 

1930 would seem to imply that the Negro succeeds better in winning doctorates and other advanced de-

This implication tends to lose its apparent basis in fact, however, when one notes that during the period under review higher education among Negroes was greatly accentuated. The figures as tabulated show conclusively that less than half the students were graduated with the doctorate at the end of the minimum period of three years (27 months) of residence study and more than a third were graduated after four years (36 months) of such study. Thirty-nine received the degree at the end of five years (45-53 months) and 37 at the end of six years (54 months and over). It should be made clear, however, that in the 'atter cases the years beyond the minimum period were employed exclusively, in most instances, for the preparation and final writing of the thesis or dissertation.

There are a few facts in regard to this phase that ought to be pointed out: (1) the matter of residence study was not always continuous, that is, done in consecutive years.

A large percentage of the persons were

teachers-in-service while pursuing the degree, and in consequence took longer to complete requirements; (2) some candidates satisfied course requirements within the three-year period, but employed several years to do the research essential to the rounding out, and in some cases, the required publication of the thesis; (3) some candidates maintained "matriculation" status for the degree while staying on their jobs, and therefore were credited with residence standing; (4) students of law, medicine, theology must meet professional requirements in addition to the first degree before qualifying for candidacy for the doctorate.

The few who were graduated with the degree in less than three years of study are a part of the period prior to 1925.

In consideration of the total output of doctorates, it is interesting to note that when the period of study was prolonged (as shown above) the rate of growth in production was more rapid. But this situation was due to factors associated with other causes, as referred to previously, and not with prolongation of the period of study. The important point of emphasis in this regard, however, is that more than half the doctors (57 per cent) exceeded by far the regulated, minimum residence requirements for the degree.

Fields of Specialization 

The doctorates were awarded in 37 fields of specialization. Table VII arranges these fields in seven divisions and presents the data in each case.

A similar statement can be safely made with respect to the division of the physical sciences which also accounts for 58. 

At least three-fourths of the 37 fields of concentration represent graduate schools that are "distinguished" for the departments affected on the basis of the investigation of the Commission on Graduate Studies of the American Council on Education. This fact increases the evidence which indicates superior quality of the doctorates as a whole.

Summary 

Important findings on the output and significance of earned doctorates among Negroes are summarized as follows: 

1. Since 1876, when Yale granted the first Ph.D.

to an American Negro, at least 381 doctorates have been awarded to this racial group. Seventeen holders of the degree or its equivalent at the time of this writing are known to have deceased. 

2. Three hundred sixteen or over four-fifths of all doctorates here recorded have been produced during the past 14 years (1930-1943), affecting periods both. of economic depression and total war. In the 53 years prior to 1930, only 51 of the doctorates were conferred. 

3. Fifty-seven per cent of all doctoral candidates used from four to six years and over to complete requirements for the degree. 

4. Of the total output, 338 or over nine-tenths are

Ph.D.'s and 30 or less than one-tenth represent the degrees of Doctor of Education, Doctor of Juridical 

Science, Doctor of Letters, Doctor of Science, Doctor of Modern Languages, Doctor of Public Health, and Doctor of Social Science. 

5. The degrees were conferred by 57 universities. 

Ten ranking institutions-led by Chicago, Columbia, Pennsylvania, Cornell and Harvard-account for 234 or over three-fifths of the total output. When a geographical consideration is arbitrarily taken into account, the schools in the East have 187 and those in the West 161. These data seem to indicate a trend toward western schools as preferential for doctoral study by 

Negro students. 

6. Study prior to doctoral candidacy, in case of the master's degree, was done at 50 colleges and universities; in case of the bachelor's, at 101 institutions. Most of the schools involved represent America's best. 

7. Thirty-seven fields of specialization have been identified in connection with the granting of the doctorate. When these fields are arranged in large cate-

gories, the divisions of social sciences and education become the leading contributors-accounting for nearly two-fifths of the total output. 

8. In view of the "eminence" of the graduate schools as evaluated by a reputable group of scholars, both as individuals and members of educational agencies, a large majority of doctoral degrees granted to the representatives in this study seem to be of a decidedly high order of merit.

Doctor's Dissertation 

A piece of "original" research is required in this field of all "matriculated candidates" for the degree, Doctor of Philosophy, or an equivalent degree. This research is commonly reported under the title "Doctor's 

Dissertation." Some schools employ the term "Thesis" in the same connection.

In the field of the social sciences here recorded, 67 dissertations are accounted for.

It would seem of interest and value to note the nature of these dissertations. An examination of the titles, as listed in this chapter, clearly indicates that 49 dissertations deal strictly with the Negro and 18 treat subjects outside this special sphere of investigation.

Since most educators emphasize the need of more and more reliable, scientific investigations on the race for purposes of intelligent educational and social reforms, this comparatively large proportion of concentrated study on the life of the Negro by his most highly trained members seems fraught with extraordinary significance.

The 18 dissertations, which apparently treat social, economic, and political subjects outside the segregated field of Negro life, at least explode an old myth now current to the effect that all Negroes, working for the doctorate in this field, lack interest in, or competence for, thorough research in areas of life beyond the boundaries of the so-called Negro problem. 

Honors or Awards 

Although no formal honors are usually awarded to a student upon his reception of a doctorate, in the case of this particular group certain descriptive statements of evaluation have been added by officers of the institutions in question. Ten of the specialists in this division were cited with one of the following: "extraordinarily good record," "a most brilliant student," "a thoroughly competent student", "one of the best students we have ever had", "one of the best Negro students". The most frequent complimentary statement, however, was "general excellence in graduate studies". 

Less than a half dozen of these specialists are recipients of doctorates causa honoris. This is surprising in view of the fact that some Negro colleges, until recently, had been extravagant in bestowing such gifts on "lesser minds". There is no record known to this

Research or Creative Output 

Research and other creative efforts are represented here in the form of published books and monographs, including bulletins. While general reference is made to articles or papers published, only books, monographs, and bulletins are listed among the 77 personal sketches. 

Negro holders of doctorates in the social sciences had produced 82 books, monographs, and bulletins at the time of this writing. It was not possible accurately to account for all articles, editorials, book reviews, and other pieces of published work written by these specialists; but together they perhaps number in the hundreds.

It should be especially noted that failure to list titles of articles behind a person's name (either in this chapter and others) does not necessarily mean that no work in this respect has been done. It was not possible to secure complete data on this part of the study. It is true, however, that six persons report no published articles whatever. The present record shows that at

the least 42 or over one-half the representatives have done some writing before or after taking the doctorate. 

Among heaviest contributors in this field of inquiry are W. E. B. Du Bois, C. G. Woodson, C. H. Wesley, 

E. Franklin Frazier, Ira De A. Reid, Abram Harris, Rayford Logan, Allison Davis, Ralph Bunche, Laurence 

Foster, A. A. Taylor, Lorenzo Greene, Luther P. Jackson-men long known within the circles of American higher education. A few are acknowledged first rate scholars. As much for their creative scholarship as anything else, Du Bois and Woodson have been awarded Spingarn Medals. These two well-known scholars are pioneers in the field of social research, embracing Negro life in America as well as in Africa. Du Bois' recent election to the National Institute of Arts and Letters is a rare distinction within the gift of American scholarship.

CHAPTER IV HOLDERS OF DOCTORATES IN EDUCATION 

Although the specialists in this group are comparatively newcomers on the educational and social scene, both the number of them and their research products are somewhat impressive. The scientific, historical, and philosophical study of education at the undergraduate-school level is the outgrowth of normal schools which at their best were nothing more than teacherpreparing high schools. As late as 1916, most Negro higher schools had not provided college departments of education, even though one of their major objectives and functions was the education of teachers.¹ Both the pattern and spirit of the curriculum were dominated by the traditional, classical studies. In the light of the fact that most of the better Negro colleges had made by 1920 a beginning toward organization of departments of education in their programs of studies, survey of the output of doctorates in this field would seem to have more than ordinary interest and value. 

Conferring of doctoral degrees in this field covers only an 18-year period. Over three-fourths of them were granted during the past decade.

Thirty-three, or nearly one-half the subjects, center around administration of schools affecting arts-colleges, normal schools, and secondary schools. This manifestation of major interest in school administration can be accounted for partly in terms of the prestige which acquisition of a doctor's degree carries along with its relatively high pecuniary value in most Negro colleges, especially when this degree is coupled with an administrative position. As a consequence school administration is attractive as a subject for study at the doctor's degree level. A considerable number of college presidents, deans, division directors, public school principals and supervisors are represented in this group. A majority of such persons were administrators before taking the doctorate. Choice of this area for advanced study, therefore, would seem especially appropriate.

Dissertations and Projects 

As in the division of the social sciences, likewise in this division, dissertations predominantly are the results of major efforts at solving problems in connection with Negro education and life. An examination of the titles of dissertations and projects, as listed at the end of this chapter, reveals 56 to have special reference to the Negro, or to treat some aspect of his life entirely; while only 17 seem to deal with subjects of a purely non-racial character. Assuming the need for scientific investigation of Negro life to be well-grounded in fact, this major research interest manifested by the scholars affected would seem to be of value to any future attempt at formulating a well-organized, coördinated, and concerted attack upon the problems of Negro education. 

Honors and Awards

At least 21 representatives in this group were recipients of undergraduate or some type of graduate honors and awards. Five received their bachelor's

degrees with citation magna cum laude and eight with cum laude. Three were elected to the honorary society, Pi Lambda Theta-an organization for "outstanding women graduate students"—and six were mentioned in the official records as having maintained a record of "general excellence in graduate studies.' 

Among the group are five General Education Board 

Fellows and four Rosenwald Fund Fellows. Three have had honorary doctoral degrees conferred upon them by leading Negro colleges and universities. One received a prize of $1,000 for research excellence as evidenced by his doctoral dissertation.

Research or Creative Output 

It was impossible at the time of this writing to ascertain the full extent to which these specialists have contributed to the literature of their fields. Questionnaires, which the investigator sent to three-fourths of the persons, and the results of examination of other sources, reveal 38 books, monographs, and bulletins to have been published under their authorship or editorship. Over 200 articles are credited to them. It seems worthwhile to mention that 13 persons of the present group, on their own admission, had not a single printed piece of work to their credit at the time the inquiry was made. It is important to note that 14 of the 38 books are doctoral dissertations.

CHAPTER V HOLDERS OF DOCTORATES IN PROFES- SIONAL AND VOCATIONAL FIELDS 

This chapter seeks to determine the status of the earned doctor's degrees in fields outside the pure domain of the liberal arts and sciences. Like the last two chapters, it also records some noteworthy achievements of the recipients of these degrees. 

Definition of Terms 

The very comprehensiveness of the terms "professional" and "vocational" necessitates at the outset an explanation or definition of their meaning as employed in this chapter. By professional here is meant the organized studies of medicine (and closely related studies), law, religious education, and theology. The term vocational refers to the several branches of agriculture, engineering, industrial education (arts), business education, technical education, library science, home economics, music, and music education. From the viewpoint of traditional academic classification, some other arrangement of the subjects could have been perhaps more appropriately used.

It must be noted also that specialists in certain branches of chemistry and biology, who give regular instruction to students in colleges or schools of medicine, are represented in this chapter.

The doctorates recorded in this division have been awarded by 23 universities. The names of the institutions are listed in Table XIII with the number of degrees each has contributed. The total number is 58.

Professional Fields of Specialization 

The field of medicine and its cognate subjects are represented by 15 doctorates. They are distributed as follows: anatomy (3), pathology (3), pharmacology 

(2); and one each in hygiene and public health and preventive medicine, bacteriology, chemistry, and biochemistry.

Law has only seven representatives. Doctoral study in law involved the areas of common law, labor law, taxation, and other economic and social aspects of the subject of law.

The field of religion is represented by religious education (3), old testament literature (2), psychology of religion (2), and church history (1).

Vocational Fields 

In the field of agriculture the following concentrations are identified: agricultural education (3); mar-

keting, plant physiology, agronomy, dairy husbandry, soil microbiology, agricultural economics, physiological bacteriology, veterinary bacteriology-all registering one each.

Industrial education and vocational education are represented by one each. There are two in home economics (nutrition and home economics education) and four in engineering (chemical, civil, and structural).

Music and music education are represented by two, and library science by one.

First Doctorates 

The persons who were first to be admitted to the doctorate in their respective fields within this division are as follows: 

In medicine, Julian H. Lewis, Chicago, '15, who is an associate professor of pathology in the University of Chicago; in law, Charles H. Houston, Harvard, '23; in religion, the late J. W. E. Bowen, Boston, 1887, a former president of Gammon Theological Seminary; in agricultural science, Fred Douglas Patterson, Cornell, '32; in home economics, Flemmie P. Kittrell, Cornell, '36; in music, Oscar Fuller, Iowa, '42. First and only in their respective fields are George M. Jones, Michigan, '34 (Engineering); Eliza Atkins Gleason, Chicago, '40 (Library Science); Maurice W. Lee, Chicago, '39 (Business); Alfred B. Turner, Pennsyl vania State, '41 (Industrial Education). 

Honors and Awards 

Some marks of distinction are also in evidence among this group of specialists. An examination of the relevant data shows that the undergraduate degree of at

least seven representatives was conferred with summa cum laude (1), magna cum laude (3), cum laude (3). 

Three are members of Phi Beta Kappa. Graduate honors and awards take the form of eight fellowships for the same number of men; seven memberships in 

Sigma Xi, three in Phi Kappa Phi, and one each in

Alpha Omega Alpha and Pi Lambda Theta. 

Research or Creative Output 

Contributions to scientific knowledge as here recorded can be said to be meager. This report, however, must not be accepted as conclusive or complete. 

A count of prepared and published papers (not all shown in the individual records) reveals over 100 such contributions. And these in the main go to the credit of seven specialists, most of whom are men of medicine. 

In addition to articles published in several standard journals of science, the holders of doctorates in this division have written at least 12 books and pamphlets. 

Men whom the present record identifies as having done the most to advance science in this particular division-in so far as quantity of production alone is concerned-are Arnold H. Maloney, Hildrus A. Poindexter, Julian H. Lewis, W. M. Cobb, Paul B. Cornely,

H. D. West, Moses Young-all in the field of medicine or associated fields. In law, are the names of 

Charles H. Houston, William H. Hastie, J. R. Houchins, and Leon Ransom. Religion is represented by 

Benjamin Mays, Willis J. King, Joseph W. Nicholson, and J. Leonard Farmer. In the vocational field are the

names of Fred D. Patterson and Flemmie P. Kittrell.

Some of these workers are well known in American life.

Fields of Specialization 

This division includes chemistry, physics, mathematics, and geology. Chemistry leads by far-ac-

counting for 36, or over three-fifths of the total output in physical sciences. Physics have 12, mathematics eight, and geology one. Doctorates in chemistry have been identified here as organic chemistry (4); inorganic chemistry (3); physical chemistry (2); physiological chemistry (1); biochemistry (1). At least two each are found in the fields of physics and algebra. 

First Doctorates 

Edward Bouchet-the first Negro to hold a Ph.D., in any field and the first to be elected to membership in Phi Beta Kappa-was also the first in the division of physical sciences, having received the degree from 

Yale in Physics in 1876. Saint Elmo Brady became first in chemistry when he secured the degree from the 

University of Illinois in 1916. When Cornell Uni versity in 1925 conferred the Ph.D., on Elbert Cox, that student became the first Negro to hold such degree in mathematics. In 1942 Marguerite Thomas Wil liams received the degree in geology from the Catholic University of America, and thus became the first and only in this field.

Honors and Awards 

At least seven representatives in this group were elected to the honorary scientific Society of Sigma Xi during the period of their graduate study. Also seven were awarded university fellowships and scholarships. 

Rosenwald Fund and General Education Board fellow ships have been granted to at least a dozen of them. 

With respect to undergraduate honors, at least three received the bachelor's degree with summa cum laude; one each with magna cum laude and cum laude. Three

1 were elected to Phi Beta Kappa. Two of the mathematicians were admitted to the Institute for Advanced Study at Princeton, such admission being regarded as a distinguished achievement.

Research Output 

In the division of physical sciences, no large amount™ of data on the productivity of the representatives could be secured. The present investigator solicited, however, coöperation from a number of the men. About three-fifths of them returned the questionnaires for the purpose in question. The data show that two books and four monographs or bulletins have been written by three specialists in the field, including chemistry and mathematics. Seventy papers have been prepared and published in various journals of science. These papers were produced by eight persons. Over half the coöperating scientists reported themselves as having done or published no research in their field, except of course the required doctor's dissertation. 

The record shows that the heaviest producer in this group by far is Percy Julian, at the present a research director of the Glidden Company of Chicago, who alone has contributed over one-third the papers that are represented in this chapter. Other producers worth mentioning are Nathaniel Calloway, Irvin Derbigny, Robert P. Barnes, Steward Cooper-all chemists-and 

Joseph Pierce, a mathematician.

Status of Research Output in Chemistry 

In his study of the status of chemistry', Wallace points to the serious need of investigation in this specialized field. His survey of 35 Negro colleges indicates clearly that only 27 pieces of research had been turned out by the departments of chemistry at these institutions, involving in the aggregate 61 teachers. 

Nine of these teachers hold the Doctor of Philosophy degree. Even though Wallace found "a close correlation between the limited funds for research and the number of publications by the teachers in these colleges," he is yet of the opinion that this output for 61 teachers (including nine Ph.D.'s) is "meager."

First Doctorates 

Two men appear as of especial interest in this respect. The late Alfred O. Coffin, who for several years gave instruction in romance languages at Langston University, was admitted to the Ph.D. degree by Illinois Wesleyan University in 1889, and thus became the first to hold a doctorate in biological science. The best known scientist of this group-the late Ernest E. 

Just-received the Ph.D. from the University of Chicago in 1916, having specialized in physiology and zoology, and as a consequence, was the first holder of the degree in the two fields. The data arranged in the sketch written after his name in this chapter indicate clearly that he was one of the eminent zoologists of his day.

Roger Arliner Young is the first woman to receive

a doctorate in zoology. Her degree was conferred by the  in 1940.

Research Output 

In this chapter not only books but contributions in various science journals are listed in case of a known contributor. It seems well to explain that it was not always possible through the methods employed to ascertain the research output to the fullest extent. And even when a full account of the work of an individual researcher was at hand the full list of his publications are not always carried here under his sketch. All known contributions, however, are counted irrespective of whether they were listed or not.

Examination of the data indicates that Negro biologists with doctoral degrees have not shown themselves as fertile or industrious as their Ph.D. colleagues in other fields. Granted the limitations of this study, the foregoing general statement of evaluation has some basis in fact. Seven men who returned the writer's questionnaire reported that they have published no research except all or parts of their doctoral dissertations.

Seven of these doctors account for five books and 71 papers published in the journals on biology and related fields, and in a few instances, as chapters in books or bulletins.

Finley,' who has given some serious thought to the creative output of Negroes in the field of biology, thinks that this area of scientific investigation has "lagged behind that of the social sciences." He makes this statement even though taking into account the severe limitations of time and scientific equipment in Negro colleges. He proposes a program which, however "idealistic" from some viewpoints, is designed to stimulate at least doctors of philosophy for further and more qualitative research output. 

Among the leading contributors in the field have been the late Ernest E. Just, the late Charles H. 

Turner, Thomas W. Turner, Harold E. Finley, S. M. 

Nabrit, Russell L. Anderson, and Lloyd E. Alexander.

CHAPTER IX HOLDERS OF DOCTORATES IN PSYCHOL OGY AND PHILOSOPHY 

Apparently, the need for highly, technically trained psychologists and philosophers of the kind here approached is so grave today that the present emphasis given to such specialists in setting their records apart in a separate chapter would seem not only fitting, but essentially important. It is recognized, however, that the status of these students, as here disclosed, could have been (more appropriately perhaps) treated in the discussion on the social scientists. Traditionally, the two fields have been closely associated. An example of this association can be seen in the common practice in small liberal arts colleges to combine the two disciplines under one department.

PART III ECONOMIC AND SOCIAL ASPECTS IN CON- NECTION WITH ACHIEVEMENTS OF THE  

The last six chapters, composing Part II, attempted to present individual academic records of the recipients of the doctorates from the viewpoint of the major field of specialization. With respect to each such field, data were set forth and analyzed in somewhat detail. 

Part III is concerned with data on and subsequent discussion of some economic and social factors incident to the inquiry as a whole. Part III also gives an account of (1) the occupational status and employment aspects, (2) membership and participation in learned societies, and (3) a general summary and interpretation, together with indications of the need for further investigation of the problem and the problems growing out of it.

CHAPTER X OCCUPATIONAL STATUS AND EMPLOY- MENT ASPECTS 

This chapter is concerned chiefly with the nature of the occupations and employment of persons represented in the study. Other economic and social aspects associated with occupational status are also considered. 

Conditions incident to war have effected profound changes in types of services which Americans are called on to perform. And the impact of these changes are no less tremendous upon the social situation of the intellectual Negro than upon that of other people of whatever race or ethnic group. As a consequence, complete accuracy with respect to locating a given representative in this study was not possible, even when the evidence was supplied (as in a number of cases) by the employing agencies themselves. A considerable number of the men are in the armed forces or in other services of the government. As these words are written, several of the workers identified here with certain employers will have changed their positions. In interpreting the data, especially in respect to place and position, it is important that the reader consider these points. Basically, however, the essential vocation of a representative is the significant fact. 

Teachers and Administrators

The popular belief that Negro holders of doctorates select teaching as their life's work is well borne out in

this study. The data reveal that 345 or 96 per cent of all holders of the degree, were employed at this writing in organized education, including college, university, and the lower schools. This finding is not at all surprising when one considers the very heavy concentration of doctoral study in teaching fields, together with the persistent need of teachers of the kind. 

Twenty-two educators of the group are college or university presidents who specialized for the doctorate in the following fields: professional education, thirteen; social science, five; religion, two; philosophy, one; agricultural science, one. It is significant that of 24 vacancies in the college presidency that have occurred since 1930, 22 have been filled by colored persons who hold the Ph.D. degree. This fact would seem to indicate a definite trend toward holders of doctor's degrees for possible presidential material in distinct contrast with the old and venerable practice of choosing theologians for such educational positions.

The data account for 19 deans and 267 college teachers of all professorial ranks. There are also five directors of major divisions in several colleges who serve in the capacity of deans and two who serve as registrars. 

Eleven public school principals and teachers are represented. Not a usual practice is that of the employment of an assistant superintendent (Washington, D. C.) and a psychologist (Chicago) in the public schools. 

At least a dozen of these representatives in the medical and law professions are also professors in the respective professional schools. 

Other Workers

There are outside the field of organized education

three journalists, three lawyers, four social welfare workers, two professional social workers, three bishops of church denominations, two in the services of the federal government, one librarian, and one senior spe cialist in the United States Office of Education. Three chemical plants employ five as research chemists and chemical engineers. Eighty-two, or one-fifth of the total, are employed by eleven Negro land-grant colleges.

CHAPTER XII GENERAL SUMMARY AND INTERPRETATIONS 

This inquiry was the result of a rather comprehensive effort to discover solutions to basic problems incident to the output, source, and significance of doctor's degrees which American Negroes had earned through advanced study. It was also an attempt to highlight achievements of the recipients of the degree in their respective fields of concentration. In consequence there was presented a brief group study in each case with individual records as largely the base, and, subsequently, a survey of occupational status and affiliation with professional and learned societies. 

The specific purpose of the study was to present as accurate and complete a picture as possible of the status of earned doctorates among Negroes, to the end that educational planning affecting this area of experience might secure a more factual basis for operation and a more realistic sense of direction toward fulfillment of basic educational needs. 

The inquiry covered a period of 67 years-beginning in 1876 and ending in December, 1943. This chapter attempts to summarize the major findings along with interpretations in respect to the following: (1) output of doctorates, including the same in specialized fields; (2) their source and significance; (3) honors and awards; (4) research or creative output; (5) factors prohibiting creative scholarship; (6) occupational status and employment; (7) contribution

to the work of professional and learned societies, together with connections with other social organizations; (8) need for further investigation. The present treatment is designed to effect a somewhat synthetic whole rather than a mere summary of the summaries. 

1. The Output 

Three hundred eighty-one students of Negro descent had received (1876-1943) doctoral degrees in course from American and foreign universities. A detailed analysis of the record and work of 368 were made. Of the latter number, over nine-tenths had been admitted to the Ph.D., and less than one-tenth had been awarded doctorates designated, respectively, as Ed.D., S.J.D., D.ésL., Sc.D., D.M.L., Dr.P,H,, and D.S.S. The output included 48 women. Seventeen of the representatives, including both sexes, are dead. The data supporting the study were derived from an investigation of original documents. 

The output for over a half century was only 51. 

But even this figure was thought to be of no little significance in view of the infancy of higher education among Negroes. The number began to increase appreciably from 1920 (shortly after the close of the first World War) and continued to increase to 1930 when it reached the 60 mark. From 1930 to 1943a period of 14 years-313 or four-fifths of the total production were effected. And this finding was considered especially significant in light of the acute economic dislocation in the nation during that time and the all-pervasive social disturbance due to the impact of global war. If it cannot be asserted with reasonable safety that the economic depression stimulated. the output, certainly it is well-nigh impossible to say

that the depression greatly hindered the output. 

The correspondingly large increases in number of undergraduate students in the institutions, Negro and mixed, were doubtless an important factor in this accelerated increase of advanced degrees in economically unfavorable times. The five Negro colleges that led in turning out the largest of the products were Howard, Lincoln (Pennsylvania), Fisk, Virginia Union, and Morehouse. Despite the rapid production, even in abnormal times, acquirement of the degree on the whole took a long time. Well over a half of the persons used from four to over ten years to complete the requirements.

Specialization 

When the output was distributed on the basis of division of subject-matter specialization, the result was as follows: social sciences, 77; education, 71; professional and vocational fields, 58; physical sciences, 58; languages and literature, 43; biological sciences, 35; psychology and philosophy, 26. Chapters directly concerned with these divisions carried in each case subdivisions or specialized departments of study along with their respective outputs. Thirty-seven such departments or fields were accounted for. 

On the surface, at least, it is clear that the output of the degree among Negroes has (1943) enjoyed a remarkable rate of growth for the past two decades. 

In view, however, of ever-increasing need inherent in the imperative demand for graduate instruction in colleges and universities for Negroes, the present number of doctoral products is far from being adequate. 

And this observation seems the more important when

considered in relation to the paucity of the products in several specialized fields.

There were in 1940 12 higher schools for Negroes which had felt impelled to superimpose graduate departments upon their all too inadequate undergraduate offerings. These schools, and perhaps others, will certainly need more and better teachers of the kind, even though they continue the practice of employing a few white persons for their staffs. These colleges, moreover, provide departments of instruction in which there is no personnel of the quality considered. Cases in point are home economics, agriculture, engineering, library science, business administration, and music education. Mathematics, physics, foreign languages, and general linguistic studies are perilously lacking in output. And this deficiency is all the more dangerous in view of total war and post-war demands for advanced training in and technical experience with these subjects. From the foregoing statements it must not be implied that only holders of doctor's degrees are competent for the purpose in question. But in any event graduate schools for Negroes will have to restrict themselves for a long time to the bare minimum of academic offerings, if a reasonable quality of education at the master's degree level is to be provided and maintained.

Finally, it might be said with much truth that, despite the commendable output of doctorates, in consideration of complex factors related to graduate and professional offerings, there are yet not enough mature scholars of the kind to staff completely one real outstanding university. By "real outstanding" in this case is meant a university that is comparable to the median institution of the group from which the representatives were graduated.

2. Source and Significance 

The degrees were conferred by 57 American and foreign universities. Chicago, Columbia, Pennsylvania, Cornell, and Harvard contributed the largest single numbers. Over three-fifths of the total output were the contribution of ten institutions.

On the basis of broad geographical lines it was shown that universities in the East had granted slightly over 50 per cent and those in the West nearly 44 per cent. A comparatively small number (19) was conferred by universities outside the United States.

In the light of investigations accepted in the study as valid and reliable for indicating relative academic eminence or worth of an American university, a considerable number of these degrees can be safely said to signify the quality of excellence. This is also true when certain academic background factors, such as the master's degree and the institutions that granted it, are taken into account. And when the supporting degree (the bachelor's) was studied in relation to the colleges (white and Negro) responsible for it, the conclusion is substantially the same, namely, Negro winners of the doctorate as a whole have been at least exposed to the best advanced training within the gift of American higher education. 

3. Honors and Awards 

Another rather general method for further testing the quality of the continuous academic work of the students in question was that of determining the nature and extent of honors and awards received during the period of undergraduate and graduate study. Data on

this phase of the investigation were not complete, but the amount assembled did shed some light on the problem.

There is no designated honor accompanying the conferring of the Ph.D., or the other degrees with which it was here associated. The degree is an honor per se. 

It was thought worth mentioning, however, that several among the students were cited by graduate school deans as really "brilliant" and as having done an extraordinarily good piece of work. 

Accumulation of the data anent honors and awards shows that 56 such students received their first degree with one of the following traditional types of distinction: cum laude, magna cum laude, summa cum laude. 

At least 24 were elected to Phi Beta Kappa. There were no "chapters" of this society where many of the persons took their first degrees. At the graduate school level, 36 at least were admitted to the Society of Sigma Xi. As much perhaps for their exceptional ability as scholarly promise, 70 were awarded fellowships, the largest number of which was the gift of the General 

Education Board and the Julius Rosenwald Fund. 

Work of these philanthropic foundations and others emerged in the study as a distinctive factor in the advancement of Negro higher education. 

That some persons represented had won honors of various kinds was no surprising finding; since, presumably, only students of decidedly outstanding achievement are accepted as doctor's degree material.

But it was surprising to this writer to find that a considerable number had received no honors or awards whatever either in graduate or undergraduate study. 

In recording this observation, it is recognized that mere honor-point accumulation on the part of students does not necessarily argue for the kind of creative

scholarship or research mind that is claimed to be required of candidates for the Ph.D. degree and certain other doctoral degrees.

Although the honors recorded in the study were chiefly of academic nature, it seems here noteworthy that five of the men won Spingarn Medals. One of the number enjoys the rare distinction of election to the National Institute of Arts and Letters. 

4. Research and Creative Output 

As

An attempt was made in the study to ascertain the amount of creative work the persons had brought to light. There were few reliable or objective measures for determining even general quality in this case. a consequence the qualitative aspects of the output were almost completely left out. One might consider, however, the publishers with their reputations for knowing good books, as well as the editors of standard journals in the several specialized fields who accepted a great many articles from the pens of these scholars. 

But even with respect to the quantitative phase, only books could be counted with reasonable certainty. 

When all the figures anent the production (as entailed in Chapters III through IX) are combined, the results obtained are as follows:

Books, monographs, and pamphlets to the amount of 158; articles, book reviews, editorials, addresses, to the extent of at least 1,472.

As previously implied, it was impossible to account for anything like an accurate number of the articles or other pieces of published work except those in book form. The coöperating persons in this respect were not themselves sure of their total output, since they had not kept a complete individual record. But the

data that were collected did seem valuable for the purpose at hand.

It seems worthwhile to point out that when combinations of figures on this phase are made (figures set down in the several chapters), it can be shown that 20 among these specialists had contributed, up to the year 1940, 711 pieces of work, including 58 books, 29 monographs, and 637 articles. On the basis of bare figures alone, this output by less than six per cent of the total number of workers would seem formidable. 

Seven of the 20 are editors of journals. Several more are associate editors.

Notwithstanding this commendable aspect, there is, as usual in matters of the kind, the adverse side of the picture. Forty-one persons in their own report laid claim (1940) to no published work whatever either before or after the reception of the doctorate. Sixtythree reported a very meager output. In view of this statement of evaluation, the caution should be repeated that absence of an account of scholarship in a person's sketch as carried in this study did not always mean that no such work was done, although of course it did mean just that in some cases. It was impossible to contact all recipients of the degree or to secure coöperation from all those with whom contact was made. 

5. Factors Prohibiting Creative Scholarship 

Citation of evidence derived from an examination of studies by specialists in the fields of chemistry, biology, and psychology indicates that, among other things, the lack of a research fund and a research policy in colleges for Negroes are leading contributing factors in the relatively little scholarly activity and

creative output in these institutions on the whole. In a recent bulletin,' Caliver writes: "Research and scholarly activity are generally regarded as being more the function of the university than of the college. Attention is called to the fact, however, that only one of the institutions studied is organized as a university. Some of the colleges for 

Negroes consequently, must, in the light of the biracial character of higher education facilities in several of the states, assume some of the functions of the university. If capable Negro scholars are to have the opportunity to make their best contribution to society, more of the colleges, and especially the land-grant colleges, must assume the responsibility of making definite provisions for the stimulation of scholarly activity on the part of faculty members." 

If this point of view, representing the seasoned observation of a reputable group of specialists, is to be effectively implemented, the several research barriers long known to the administrators of higher education for Negroes must be greatly minimized if not eliminated altogether. Caliver's statement of course is not specifically addressed to the need for more scholarly activity among Ph.D.'s alone. But the implication is clear that if men and women of the highest research. competence are too greatly frustrated in their efforts to make scholarly contributions to society, Negro education will continue to lag dangerously behind the academic procession in American life.

The factors most frequently reported as prohibitive of research or creative output are lack of clear under- 

1Ambrose Caliver, A Summary, Vol. IV, National Survey of the Higher Education of Negroes, Misc. No. 6 (Washington, D. C., Gov ernment Printing Office, 1942), p. 26.

standing of the relation of research to teaching, lack of a well-planned research fund, lack of scientific equipment, lack of an adequate and consistent method of staff stimulation on the part of college administrations, the most debated aspect of which is an equitable salary scale.

The factor mentioned the most frequently by teachers is the teaching load. Such load includes both the number of class meetings per week and the number of students per class. In fact a total service load is referred to, involving committee activities, student counseling, daily preparation for class work, activities embracing community and public relations. But here it must be pointed out that while heavy service loads of teachers unquestionably do hamper production of good research (as well as good teaching), they do not paralyze a teacher's efforts altogether. Probably it can be shown that teachers with onerous instructional tasks have produced much if not most of the research and interpretative writing recorded in this study. It can also be shown, perhaps, that the mere reducing of a 

Ph.D.'s teaching load will not eventuate necessarily in research activity on the part of such teacher. Again it might not be too much to speculate that quite a bit of the lack of scholarly activity on the part of Negro "scholars" is due in part to a kind of intellectual inertia stemming from a false sense of academic completeness and security. To carry the speculation still further, one could say with some basis in truth that this supposed inertia with its resultant undesirable effect is not so very much different from that which exists in the typical American college of similar size.

6. Occupational Status and Employment 

The popular conception that Negro holders of Ph.D.'s and equivalent degrees seek teaching and school administration as their major occupation was amply borne out in the study. Ninety-six per cent of the representatives were found to be engaged in organized education, embracing college, university, secondary school, and elementary school. A considerable and ever-increasing number of presidents, deans, and high school principals are among them. The great majority are college teachers. As the major administrative offices are being increasingly filled by scholars of the kind here discussed, one might surmise at least that the cause of creative scholarship is soon to receive an even greater stimulation in colleges for Negroes, that is, if one has realistic faith in the efficacy of the doctorate.

Howard University had in 1943 more holders of the degree (19 per cent) than any single agency which employs them. That institution had three times more than its nearest competitor-Lincoln University, Missouri. This is not to imply that the former university is three times better than the latter on this item of institutional competence; for the matter of size and services would vitiate any such comparison. And the same is true in respect to any such comparison between the other schools.

When Howard, the public school system, and certain branches of the federal government were considered together, the city of Washington had in its employ (1943) over one-fourth of all such workers. 

The city of Atlanta, Georgia, is second, but not a close second. Another interesting aspect of this phase

of the study was the employment by white institutions and industrial enterprises of a few persons of the sort. 

Salaries 

The salaries received by the majority of the teachers and administrators were found woefully low in light of the quality of their training and productive experience. The typical Ph.D. received in 1939 an annual salary far below $3,000. It was pointed out in the study that the institutions which paid the most attracted and held the best. Indeed the salary that a college pays its best teachers is one reliable measure of the quality of that college's service to society. 

It proved interesting to note that the Ph.D., per se was not being paid for, the popular conception to the contrary, notwithstanding. Perhaps it is more accurate to say that the evidence, such as presented, was not sufficiently clear and positive on that point. But the data were crystal clear that the comparatively large salaries went to those Ph.D.'s who were presidents, deans, and productive scholars. Rarely was the salary of an unproductive Ph.D., except that of an administrator, found among those in the so-called high salary brackets. 

7. Affiliation with Organizations, Professional and 

Learned Societies

In evaluating adequate salary needs for teachers of the kind, modern higher education takes into account the necessity of a teacher's participation in the work of learned, professional and other social organizations. 

The present study disclosed that some participation was indicated. And it revealed also the nature and

character of the societies which representatives regarded as most important to them. The study did not attempt to discover all organizations to which Negro holders of the degree belong. It is admitted that had such been possible, this part of the work would have been strengthened substantially.

Twenty-four learned and professional organizations were found to be of paramount interest to 200 of the doctors thus affiliated. Such affiliation constituted 54 per cent of all living persons of the kind studied. The societies or associations which devote themselves exclusively to a special field of learning were represented most frequently among the data assembled. An appreciable number of the representatives in the latter kind of organization reported themselves as "fellows" therein-indicating not only more than ordinary manifestation of interest, but possible recognition of scholarly attainments from the respective society. 

Among the organizations of broad scope and function, the names of the American Association for the 

Advancement of Science, the Society of Sigma Xi, and the American Association of University Professors appeared with greatest frequency.

Associations of an avowedly liberal trend or those that claim to sponsor a "progressive" philosophy of education, for example, the American Federation of 

Teachers and the Progressive Education Association, were discovered to be either at the bottom of the list or not represented at all. Significant for their lack of a very good showing in the study were the outstanding 

Negro organizations.

The data assembled forced the conclusion that Negro holders of the degree in question are on the whole a conservative or safe group in matters of purely professional and of broadly social import.

Religious and Political Organizations 

The foregoing statement gained further support when evidence gathered from a study of religious and political organizations in the same relation was considered and treated. The representatives (54 per cent) were affiliated with, or preferred to be members of, 11 church denominations in the aggregate. The denomination shown to have the largest membership in this connection was the African Methodist Episcopal Church. The Baptists brought up a substantial second. 

If the often reported adherence to fundamentalism in religion is factually characteristic of Methodist and Baptist denominations, and this be interpreted in terms of the process of social acculturation, the conclusion that such scholars as a whole are safe in matters of social reform appears definitely sound.

This soundness as here implied seemed the more grounded in truth when tested in relation to the assumed political faith as expressed by representatives in terms of their party affiliation or preference.

The study disclosed that the Republican organization excelled all others as a party of preference for most doctors of philosophy approached on this point. 

The Democrats had appreciable representation in comparison, but the "New Deal" wing of the party did not show up well in the count. In all parties—including the New Deal as a separate phase-some adherents might have been concealed under the registered label of "Independent."

In the face of this traditional political alignment, it was thought significant that names of party organizations, popularly referred to as Leftists, barely appeared in the study. The Socialist Party did have some representation, but the Communists were not

chosen (overtly) by a single member of the group. 

These data, when viewed in their composite relationship with other pieces of evidence yielded by the total study, would seem to indicate that the typical scholar represented in this book is not essentially different in patterns of general educational and social behavior from other Negro intellectuals. The same is true perhaps of Ph.D.'s of other races, namely, that typically, they are able but not pioneers, do not enjoy the so-called thrills of intellectual or creative adventure, and can rarely be identified in name or fact with those movements directed toward social reform. 

8. Need for Further Investigation

The inquiry as a whole was restricted to those aspects of the problem for which data or reliable information could be obtained through the limited facilities and resources available to the author. The questionnaire-check list designed to secure data that might shed light on certain social-background factors did not yield adequate information on all significant items. In the treatment of the several phases of the problem this fact was indicated. It must be pointed out here, however, that certain items of information provided for in the instrument did not materialize, and therefore could not be embodied in the study. Such items, among other things, relate to the date and place of the person's birth; place where elementary and secondary schooling were received; vocation and education of parents; factors related to marital status; age when the doctorate was received; high school graduation honors. It seems noteworthy that many of the respondents who returned the questionnaire-check list left several items unanswered or unchecked for reasons not made known.

These shortcomings militated seriously against the effort at giving a complete account of social-background factors in connection with the problem. In a considerable number of cases the deficiency was made up through a rather laborious examination of eight biographical directories in search of representatives in question. 

There is then (1) the clear need for investigation of background aspects interrelated with the educational and social achievement of Negro holders of doctorates.

Further research (2) is also needed in order to remove the tentativeness of conclusions anent the compensation or salary for the doctor's degree per se; (3) the extent of the membership and participation in learned societies; (4) the affiliation and activity in religious and political organizations. (5) There is also the need for more extensive study on the relation of research output to the possession of the Ph.D. (6)

Likewise there is the urgency for testing the hypothesis that heavy teaching loads vitiate research output. Another problem of value would be (7) a comparative study of the research or creative activity of those 

American Negroes who have the Ph.D. degree and those who have not. And finally (8) the cost in money involved in the task of securing the degree seems of extreme importance in view of the relatively low salaries of the typical holder of the degree. 

These eight indications of problems are only a few among those that have emerged from the study. There are many others perhaps more fundamental and hence of greater significance. But the ones here indicated might prove of challenging value to students in the field, toward the end of effecting a more rounded picture of the achievement of an important segment of the American population.
