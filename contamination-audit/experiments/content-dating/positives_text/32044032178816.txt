JURISDICTION OVER FLEEING DEBTORS PAUL L. WEIDEN 

THE laws prevailing in the United States give, at least at first sight, a good chance to anyone who tries to escape his creditors. 

A gentleman who fears a law suit in New York has only to move to New Jersey. According to what is often thought to be American law, jurisdiction in personam cannot be acquired by the courts of New York in such a case, and the creditor must then find the hiding place of his debtor. When he finally succeeds in doing so, the statute of limitations may have run against him. If the claim is not yet outlawed when his hiding place is found the debtor may continue the game and once more change his residence.

In such and similar cases it seems to be quite impossible to obtain any personal jurisdiction anywhere against the fleeing debtor. 

This paper will discuss whether American law is really as unsatisfactory as these examples suggest.

We are confronted with only one of a group of jurisdictional problems which has not yet been clearly decided by American law. 

There are at least some so-called anomalous rules which are taken for granted and rarely questioned as to whether they are or ought to be the law. Thus, according to the laws of most states, anyone is at liberty to trespass upon land situated in states where he has no property. The evil doer has merely to pass the border line in order to escape but before he is personally serviced in the state where he committed the tort, no matter how serious his wrong may have been.¹ This astonishing license granted by the American law of procedure is, we learn, based upon certain fundamental principles of the law of jurisdiction which cannot be changed. Trespass is a local action and can be prosecuted only in the state where the land is situated. The courts of such state cannot obtain personal jurisdiction unless the wrong-doer can actually be served within

THIS astonishing state of the law is generally based, in last resort, upon the supposedly axiomatic truth that jurisdiction must be based upon power and, furthermore, upon what is supposed to be the historical development of Anglo-American law. This history seemingly shows that the common law courts never assumed jurisdiction without personal service. Over and over again the members of the Supreme Court of the United States have emphasized that common law courts cannot exercise jurisdiction in personam without personal service.3

Yet even the statement of the most venerated judge of modern times, delivering the judgment in a leading case before the United States Supreme Court cannot possibly have the force of restrospectively altering the course of legal history. It would be most astonishing indeed to anyone who knows what is called the English legal mind, to learn that the judges at Westminster let anyone slip through their fingers merely because he crossed the seas. In fact, at no time since the Norman Conquest was there any doubt that the English courts did exercise jurisdiction although the writ could not be served personally and although the defendant was not domiciled in the kingdom so that his property could not be attached therein.

The cumbersome character was not the only disadvantage of the proceedings in outlawry. Above all it did not give the defendant the best possible notice of the proceedings against him. Sir Holdsworth has this to say about the civil writ of outlawry:⁹ "In its original form outlawry was intended to give the most ample and reiterated notice of the suit, and its penal operation attached only to the contumaceous or fraudulent ... but in its modern form it can scarcely be said to have any tendency even to apprise the defendant of the action . . . unless the defendant should reside at the time in the parish in which the proclamation was made. . . . But this can seldom happen, for at the time of awarding the exigent he is commonly abroad or is in England, the place of his abode, and is unknown. . . . And it is quite possible that even his property may be seized and sold and the proceeds paid over to the plaintiff before he is aware that any action is pending against him."

No wonder that in the course of the Benthamian reforms, the civil writ of outlawry was supplanted by the more modern constructive or substituted service of summons. A great English judge of the nineteenth century, Brett, L. J., described the process of reform:10 "According to a familar canon of construction it is first desirable to consider what was the law at the time the statute was passed. A British subject resident abroad could not be served with a writ of summons. By a process somewhat intricate and tedious, but well established, he might be sued nevertheless to judgment and execution in respect of any cause of action over which the English courts had jurisdiction. The courts permitted a course of procedure against him which ended in his outlawry, and, that being once established, the plaintiff proceeded to judgment and to an equivalent for execution for any property of the defendant in England. So with regard to a foreigner and an alien, the courts, by permitting a writ of distringes to issue against any property, compelled him to appear or pursued him to outlawry and judgment. 

Such being the state of the law with regard to jurisdiction and procedure, the statute in question was passed.” 

The United States, as usually, anticipated the reforms introduced by Bentham into the laws of England. The writ of civil outlawry was, like its criminal cousin, not liked and yet existent

II 

BEFORE we consider the jurisdictional background of the present law, we may be permitted to very shortly survey the state of the law in other countries. 

The continent of Europe adheres to a completely different doctrine of jurisdiction. As ancient as the science of the conflict of laws is the doctrine of nationality as base of jurisdiction. Little purpose would be served by even outlining the history of this doctrine. It suffices to say that it originated in the Italian city states 1885 U. S. 350, 369 (1832).

After a short interval under the influence particularly of Dutch jurists such as Huber, it became again the leading doctrine of the continental conflict of laws. The Continental nations claim therefore jurisdiction over all their citizens and subjects whether they are domiciled or resident within the state or not. On the other hand, they recognize the principle that other countries have a similar type of jurisdiction with regard to their nationals. Here we have to deal with a principle that is automatically reciprocal; jurisdictional rights are not only claimed but also granted, even without treaties, among Continental nations.

On the other hand, most European countries have enacted statutes that particularly endeavor to protect their own nationals against foreign debtors. Thus Article IV (c) of the French Code 

Civile, gives as ultimate resort the right to every Frenchman to sue any foreigner in the domicile of the plaintiff. In Germany judgment may be had against foreigners neither domiciled nor resident within the jurisdiction of Germany, wherever they have assets.22 The judgment thus obtained is not a judgment in rem, but in personam. 

It is not restricted to the amount of the assets available within the jurisdiction at any time of the proceedings, but it covers the whole amount of the debt.

The lawyer trained in America, whenever he considers Continental and even English law, must make himself conversant with the distinction which is fortunately absent from American law. 

The foreign legal systems distinguish between certain fundamental legal principles of the conflict of laws and a body of rules called Fremdenrecht, lois d'étrangers or law pertaining to aliens even in the field of jurisdiction. The genuine principles of the conflict of laws are deemed to be reciprocal by their very character and nature, without any treaty or statute. Such is the doctrine of nationality as a base of jurisdiction; this doctrine is honored as a base of domestic and foreign judgments alike. The law pertaining to aliens is, however, governed by unilateral principles; they endeavor

This distinction between fundamental principles of the conflict of laws which are deemed to be automatically reciprocal and certain rules which favor the domestic jurisdiction or discriminate against foreigners is of particular importance in considering modern English law. Yet the doctrine would hardly have survived in the United States had it not been for important practical considerations and for genuine social differences in the life of the European nations and in the United States.

Story knew well what many of the later American jurists have not quite clearly in mind; that the territorial doctrine of power is by no means the only doctrine on which a system of the conflict of laws can be based. He considered at some length the principle of nationality 38 according to which a person carries his law around with him. He refers to the doctrine of "personal statutes" which govern the great majority of all legal relationships in which a person may enter during his life and which are all governed by the law of his nationality. Story, and all the other jurists of his time, must have clearly realized that a system based on nationality was not inducive to the best interest of the United States. It would have increased the importance of being the citizen of any one particular sister state; it might have created jealousies between the states. 

The territorial principle corresponded to the rule of constitutional law that state citizenship is governed by residence and not by birth. 

For the same reasons it had to be adopted. It alone gave a simple and adaptable test to questions of jurisdiction. Such reasons, important as they were in Story's day, are of even greater consequence today.

The considerations apply only to the conflict of the laws of sister states, and not of foreign countries. Yet there is small wonder that a dual system of the conflict of laws was not adopted in the United States. With the more static conditions in the United States of the twentieth century such considerations protecting migratory debtors may have lost some of their force. We will assume, however, that even today the territorial principle of jurisdiction is based on reasons as strong and fundamental as they existed at the time when 

Story laid down the rule.

Yet what is the territorial principle of the law of conflict? It is based upon physical power. It is a rudimentary doctrine: exercise power wherever you actually have it. There is no reliance on comity or public international law or convenience. 

This is, no doubt, its main advantage. There can be no doubt who was legally entitled to do a certain act: only the one who was physically able to do it. And on the other hand whoever is physically able to exercise the function is entitled to do so by law. 

Yet looking at American law we find that the doctrine is rigidly adhered to only in cases of jurisdiction in rem. In the province of jurisdiction in personam only lip service has ever been rendered. 

Whenever jurisdiction in rem is to be exercised, a state, according to the doctrine, must have the power to do with the res as it pleases. The state must have this power at all stages of the proceedings, not only at the time of execution or at the time judgment was had, but also at the very commencement of the proceedings. 

This is actually the very gist of the decision in Pennoyer v. Neff, which marks the victory of the doctrine of territorial power within the province of the law of jurisdiction in rem. Before this decision was rendered, it was generally assumed that a court had only to exercise physical power over a res at the time of the judgment, or even at the time of the execution. It was exactly this practice that was declared to be unconstitutional. 

In the field of jurisdiction in personam it is difficult to see why the doctrine of physical power should not mean exactly what it says: a state must exercise physical power over the defendant at all stages of the proceedings. This must be so particularly at the time execution is had and judgment is rendered. Yet following the line of thought that found its expression in Pennoyer v. Neff, this power must also exist at the time the proceedings were initiated. 

The physical power must be exercised at all relevant times. If however any choice had to be made between two periods of the

proceedings, its end (execution) or its beginning (issuance of the writ and service of summons) the doctrine of power must give preference to the far more important time of judgment and execution rather than to the time of commencement and service. What actual physical power has a state to execute against the person of a defendant if and when he is available within the territory of the state at the time of judgment and execution? All that is necessary. No help by other states is required. No conflicts are possible. On the other hand, what physical power, without the help of other states, can be exercised if the defendant is present at the time of the commencement of the action and at the time of service, yet not at the time of execution? None whatever. According to a territorial doctrine of the conflict of laws, based on actual physical power, jurisdiction against a person can only be exercised if the person is present and actually available within the state at the time of execution, or even at all vital times. Presence at the time of the service without presence at the time of the execution must necessarily be without any consequence. 

It is quite obvious that such a doctrine does not represent the American law of today nor of any previous period of American legal history. Presence within the state at the time actual service is had is a sufficient base of jurisdiction in personam in the United States of America. This means that actual and physical power to impose the command of the court upon the individual is not necessary in 

American law to obtain jurisdiction. 

What Mr. Justice Holmes called the "decencies of civilization"45 or to use a more realistic phrase, the necessities of commerce, never permitted the acceptance of the territorial doctrine as the base of personal jurisdiction. Two principles constitute the theoretical fundament of the present American law of personal jurisdiction: Firstly, fair notice has to be given to the defendant that proceedings are pending in a court; secondly, there has to be some contact between the state in which the proceedings are pending and either the person of the defendant or the transaction in issue. The question when such contact exists between the court and either person or transaction, is answered by a body of rules.46 

Thus, a court has jurisdiction whenever a defendant has consented

Again, as will be seen later, a defendant has to submit to the proper exercise of jurisdiction by the state of his domicile, although he cannot be found and although it is possible for him at any given minute to cross the state border line. In all such cases the contact between the state and the person is not based upon physical power, but upon something else: a relationship sufficiently strong to make it convenient that the state should exercise jurisdiction. It is therefore not physical power but, on the contrary, rules of convenience on which personal jurisdiction is based in American law. In theory the base is therefore the same as in continental law: not physical power, but a body of rules recognized as binding upon domestic and foreign courts alike, restricting the one and empowering the other. In practice the American system is better: it is not based upon any axiomatic truth as the principle of nationality, but rather on rules of convenience gained by experience and adaptable to the everchanging conditions of national life.

Let us be clear as to the use of the term jurisdiction. Unfortunately, this term denotes various conceptions of American law and jurisprudence. Here we are confronted with two distinct meanings. 

The term "jurisdiction" may mean the power of a state to proceed against a person or thing in a certain way. The state of New 

York has the power to adjudge matters relating to property within the state of New York or persons present therein. The state of 

New York has, at least ordinarily, no power to render judgments in rem concerning lands in New Jersey or Kansas or China, nor concerning persons domiciled and resident within such states or countries. This power will henceforth be called "sovereign jurisdiction".

The state of New York may in a given case have "sovereign jurisdiction". In such a case it is for the state of New York to say who and in what manner such sovereign jurisdiction be exercised by its officers and courts. It is for the state of New York to establish whether certain matters should be adjudged by the Surrogate's Courts or by the City Courts or by the Court of Appeals.

We have to deal with cases in which the state has the power to proceed on the strength of the rules of private international law. It has exclusively to be considered whether a certain judge has the power to proceed in accordance with the rules of his municipal law. If we inquire after what preliminaries and according to what formal acts a judge of the state of New York may proceed to enter a default judgment against a defendant, we have again to deal with questions of "judicial jurisdiction". "Judicial jurisdiction" is meant if we say that jurisdiction can only be acquired by personal or substituted service, or that substituted service cannot give jurisdiction to a court unless certain formalities have been observed. 

If the state of New York would be a completely sovereign state, its municipal law would in principle be decisive as to all questions involving "judicial jurisdiction". Yet the state of New York has to comply with the commands of the federal constitution. In certain fields, no such commands exist. The state of New York has an almost unlimited amount of freedom in deciding what particular type of judge should decide particular types of cases. In other fields the state of New York is more restricted. Thus in all cases in which "judicial jurisdiction" is based on substituted service, such substituted service has to be the best available under the circumstances.

While there is very little theoretical discussion in American jurisprudence about the fundamental distinction between sovereign and judicial jurisdiction, it is clearly recognized by American au thorities. Thus Section 75 of the Restatement of The Conflict of Laws reads as follows: 

"A state cannot exercise through its courts judicial jurisdiction over a person although he is subject to the jurisdiction of the state, unless a method of notification is employed which is reasonably calculated to give him knowledge of the attempted exercise of jurisdiction and an opportunity to be heard."

A decision of the United States Supreme Court may illustrate the distinction. In re Connoway73 suit was commenced and writ issued by the Supreme Court of California against Overton, who died as a resident of California without service having been made upon him. Overton's executor was neither domiciled nor resident within the state of California. He therefore maintained that he could not be summoned by scire facias, a form of substituted service. Nor, the courts of the state of California decided, was "jurisdiction acquired over the person of Overton as he was not served prior to his death." The Supreme Court overruled the California courts and held, in effect, that California had acquired what is called in this paper "sovereign jurisdiction" by and with the commencement of the suit in California. It was therefore within the power of the California state courts to acquire "judicial jurisdiction" by the means available under the laws of the State of California, such as scire facias or substituted service.

A similar distinction between sovereign jurisdiction and judicial jurisdiction has been made in another group of cases. A divorce action may have been brought by the husband in state A and by the wife in state B. Writ may have been issued in state A prior to the commencement of the action in state B, yet service of summons may have been made in state B prior to service in state A. In such situations the problem arises: when does the "jurisdiction" of either state A or B come into existence, so that the proceedings in the other state are subject to a plea in abatement? Two distinctions have therefore to be made to discuss and answer clearly the question whether "jurisdiction" may be obtained in the state of the old domicile against a person who changed his domicile after writ was issued.

This is firstly, a distinction between sovereign jurisdiction and judicial jurisdiction. It is secondly, the distinction, discussed above, between the Conflict of Law principles dealing with matters of jurisdiction, and due process considerations.

According to the principles of the conflict of laws, domicile is a sufficient base for jurisdiction. "Jurisdiction" in the meaning of this rule, can only refer to sovereign jurisdiction. The conflict of law is, in general, exclusively concerned with the power of the state to proceed with a matter, and not with a power granted by such state to an individual officer or court which is naturally a matter of municipal law only. The conflict of laws, whether it be based on a constitution or on comity, consists of a body of rules which give certain rights of jurisdiction to certain states. Such rules cannot possibly depend on municipal law governing the assumption of jurisdiction by anyone; before such foreign municipal law has to be taken into consideration, it has to be decided on the strength of the principles of the conflict of laws whether such foreign municipal law at all, in its entirety, can be applied or not. 

It is universally agreed in the conflict of laws that the initial ground or base of sovereign jurisdiction need not continue to exist during the whole proceedings. It is sufficient if such base exists at the beginning of the proceedings. If the defendant was therefore domiciled within a state at the beginning of the proceedings, such state has obtained sovereign jurisdiction in the matter, even without any of its officers or courts having yet acquired "judicial jurisdiction".

As far as due process considerations are concerned, we have again to consider whether in each particular case under consideration the defendant was fairly treated and whether he was given the best possible opportunity to learn of the proceedings and to be heard.

Let us now turn from logical to practical considerations. The test of practicability is under the Due Process Clause of even greater importance than ordinarily. The debtor who changes his domicile and residence does so, presumably, to further his own interests. He will obtain the benefits of the change. It is therefore only just and equitable that he has to take upon himself certain burdens and disadvantages connected with such change. The creditor, on the other hand, cannot prevent the debtor from changing his domicile. A creditor may well know that his debtor has the intention to move to a distant state, or even country. Unless he knows where to find the debtor within the state, the creditor cannot do anything but have a writ issued and trust to luck that the debtor may not establish a new domicile somewhere else before service is completed.

This is not only so in cases of fleeing debtors, but also in many cases of debtors who are above reproach or cannot be proven to be dishonest. Even if the creditor should obtain judgment in the old domicile of such persons, he still has the additional costs and burden of suing upon such judgment in the state of the new domicile, or in such state where the debtor has property. Such burden is incumbent upon the creditor in any case, as a natural consequence of the debtor's change of domicile. A debtor who is about to change his domicile can very easily inquire whether any suits are pending against him before the courts of his old domicile, before he leaves. 

On the other hand, there is no register which can tell creditors about prospective moves of their debtors. 

It is therefore submitted, as a general rule: a state has jurisdiction against persons who were resident and domiciled at the time the action was commenced, although they were neither domiciled nor resident at the time the substituted service was completed. 

As under the Due Process Clause each case has to be considered on its own merits, an exception will prevail where the defendant was not granted due process. An extraordinarily long period of time may have elapsed before service of summons was completed. It is possible that a better type of substituted service could have been effected in a particular case. Yet in general, domicile combined with residence is a sufficient base of sovereign jurisdiction. 

The same is true, in general, for domicile not coupled with

JURISDICTION OVER Fleeing deBTORS 

31 residence. In the average case, an absentee retains strong bonds of commercial as well as social character with his domicile. This is ordinarily the reason why a state may be deemed to be an absentee's domicile, although he is absent therefrom. Hence, it is practical to give jurisdiction to the state of the old domicile whenever a writ has been issued therein. Let us assume that a person domiciled in California goes east to look for business in New England, and finally makes up his mind to start a new business in New York. 

He may not have informed his home town creditors about his moves although he had no dishonest intentions. Creditors who did not accidentally obtain his ever changing address or who did not hire a private detective to find it out, could sue the debtor only in his California home town. It is too much to require that the debtor should find out whether any suits are pending against him in his domicile? Should the creditors, who could not anticipate at the time of filing suit that the debtor may later make up his mind to settle down in New York, have to start all over again in New York?

In the fair interest of both parties, substituted service should be effective. Yet exceptional situations may be more numerous in cases of absentee domiciliaries. The old domicile may continue merely because of certain presumptions or merely because of the intention of the debtor to return. In such and similar cases of bona fide absentees who changed their domicile between the time the action was commenced and the completion of service, service by publication may be unconstitutional. Each particular case will have to be considered on its own merits. It may be of importance whether the creditor knew of the new abode of the debtor, whether the debtor knew of the creditor's intention to sue him, whether the debtor did anything to hide his whereabouts or whether his conduct was beyond reproach. It is the absence of a hard and fast demarcation line that can alone work justice in borderline cases. 

Let us now turn to presence within the state as base of sovereign jurisdiction. In spite of the custom of American writers on the 

Conflict of Laws, presence and domicile cannot be coördinated as equally effective bases of jurisdiction. The power of a state acquired by temporary and passing presence over a person is inferior to the power acquired by a person's domicile. It is, therefore, domicile and not personal presence that is the genuine test under any

doctrine based on territorial power of jurisdiction,75 as it is the governing test of the Anglo-American system of the Conflict of Laws. A person who is merely temporarily present within a state can have no burden or obligation to inform himself of any law suits that may be pending against him by looking into court registers. 

On the other hand, his creditors cannot possibly have the right to start actions without personal service in any place in which the debtor happens to be present. Any other rule would greatly interfere with the modern amenities of commerce and social intercourse. 

As a general rule mere temporary presence is, therefore, not a sufficient base of sovereign jurisdiction unless it be accompanied by actual service.76

We arrive therefore at the following conclusion: a creditor may obtain a valid judgment against his debtor if he started the suit at a time at which the debtor was still present and domiciled within the jurisdiction even if the debtor left the state and established a new domicile elsewhere before substituted service of summons was completed. This rule applies not only to debtors who flee the jurisdiction to avoid service of summons but also to others. 

Only in exceptional cases such proceedings may infringe the Due Process Clause. The same rules have to be applied when the debtor was domiciled within the jurisdiction at the time the action was commenced, although he was not present therein. Yet in this group the exceptional case may be more numerous. Mere presence within the jurisdiction is not sufficient unless accompanied by personal service.

VI THERE is only one group of fleeing debtors which cannot be proceeded against on the strength of the rules above stated even by vigilant creditors: debtors who not only crossed the state line but also established domicile elsewhere before the creditor could commence action. The group of cases here under consideration may be relatively small. We have to deal with defendants who committed some breach of contract or tort which could not be detected by the wronged party before some time had passed; just

this interval, sometimes quite lengthy, may have been used by the debtor to proceed to points unknown because he not only knew that he had committed a wrongful act, but also because he anticipated a suit by the wronged party.

A creditor who maintains any jurisdictional rights in such situations has the full burden of proof that exceptional circumstances exist. He has, therefore, to show not only objective facts but also the debtor's state of mind at the time of his flight. If the creditor sues upon a judgment in personam obtained in the debtor's old domicile in another state where the debtor may have property, the debtor is given another opportunity to be heard; he may attack the old judgment and show that the jurisdictional facts which it was based upon did not exist. 

There is no American authority for the protection of creditors in such cases. There is, on the contrary, a wealth of dicta which tends to support the assumption that such type of protection is not recognized in American law. The common law as interpreted in 

England provides expressly for jurisdiction against debtors who crossed the sea for the avowed purpose of avoiding service of summons, even if they accomplished their purpose before the writ was issued. This has expressly been held to be the law of England in the decisions of Wilding v. Bean and Jay v. Budd, referred to above,77 Anglo-American judges, furthermore, did not decline to assume jurisdiction in such cases at any time in Anglo-American legal history, at least up to the nineteenth century.78 Nor has there been later any decision of the United States Supreme Court which would have expressly held that substituted service cannot be effected against debtors here under consideration. We find only statements of the general principle that the Due Process Clause forbids substituted service against non-residents. We do not find any rule dealing with such special cases in which a due consideration of the interests of both creditors and debtors strongly suggests a contrary result. While we, therefore, have no American precedent in point, there is no reason evident why the common law as interpreted in the United States should not be identical with the English rule.

The form of process of substituted service takes place exclusively within the border lines of the state assuming jurisdiction. The sovereign jurisdiction of such state can indeed not be based on either presence or domicile at the time of the commencement of the action. Yet at least one base for sovereign jurisdiction recognized that American and probably all other conflict systems are brought about by facts which took place before the commencement of the action. This is jurisdiction based on consent. 

It is generally acknowledged in America and elsewhere that such consent may not only take place after the commencement of the action, by such acts as may amount to general appearance, but also by express contractual agreement entered into before the action is commenced.70

At least to a conservative lawyer, therefore, the fiction of an implied consent, so often used in the progress of the law from archaic rules to more elastic principles, may be of some consolation. 

The genuine ground of jurisdiction is here the fraud of the defendant. To look at it from a technical point of view, we may argue that such fraud contains an implied consent. Such line of argument is not unknown to Anglo-American law; it is the very base of the law of waiver of tort. More adequate it is to simply refer to the rule that the defendant cannot be the beneficiary of his own wrong and that such consequence must be deemed to have come into existence which he willingly and intentionally prevented to be brought about. There is nothing new and striking about such effect of fraud; it is well known even in this very limited sphere of the American law of jurisdiction: actual service of summons is not recognized whenever it has been brought about by such fraud as luring the defendant into another jurisdiction.80 

The American law of jurisdiction is based, in its last resort, on convenience and practicability. The question before us is, therefore, simply this: is it convenient and practical, in principle, to give a creditor a right to proceed against fleeing debtors who knew that they had committed a breach of contract or a tort, and who flew in anticipation of the creditor's civil action, before the creditor

could possibly detect the wrong and commence suit? From a practical point of view there is no ground why such debtors should be given a right to proceed. Nor can the Due Process Clause be of any avail to the debtors here in question. They intentionally avoided personal service, they willingly escaped, and, therefore, renounced their right to personal service, which due process ordinarily requires.

Nor can it be said that the proceedings suggested here, while proper against those who actually are evil doers, are fraught with danger to innocent persons who may be the victims of a frame up. 

Quite apart from the strict limitations of the exceptional rulestated above we may benefit by the practical experience of the United Kingdom where no case occurred during the last fifty years in which the rule in Jay v. Budd and Wilding v. Bean has led to malicious conspiracies against absentees. 

A summary of part IV finds: that jurisdiction may be obtained by a state by the fraud of the defendant who fled from the state even before commencement of the action, with full knowledge of his debts and with the intention to escape service of summons. It must be stressed that this rule cannot be based on American, but exclusively on English authority. 

VII 

IT APPEARS, therefore, that a creditor has sufficient means and ways by which he can hold responsible a fleeing debtor in almost any situation. This is as it ought to be. There is no reason why a debtor who is shown to have fled his creditors and, thus, impliedly and expressly admitted the existence of a claim against him, should reap the benefits of his evasion of the law. It has been endeavored to show that American law as it stands presently, offers sufficient remedies in all but one situation. The protection seemingly granted to fleeing debtors is simply based upon a misconception of certain leading cases.

It is sincerely believed that almost all so-called anomalous rules of the law of jurisdiction, some of which were referred to in the introduction to this paper, are similarly influenced by the erroneous construction of certain technical legal principles and leading cases; scrutiny of the historical development and analysis of principle and leading cases will lead quite generally to satisfactory results.
