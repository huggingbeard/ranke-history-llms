FOREWORD PARAPHRASE GILBERT AND SULLIVAN, the lot of a budget policeman today is not a happy one.

Toud Because the desire for a strong national defense is a proper manifestation of patriotism, many people are inclined to jump over a gap in logic and feel that the mere expression of concern over the question whether defense billions are spent wisely is somehow unpatriotic. 

In announcing a series of studies on defense finances last May, The National Economy League declared its support for "every measure of defense conceived in calm survey of actual need and useful protection," but its opposition to unsound financing and faulty management.

In this fourth study of the series, The League has assembled evidence to show the need for an impartial advisory planning group to review the defense program as a whole, to make recommendations which will assist in allocating priorities, and to aid executive departments in the most advantageous use of funds which are now subject only to broad limitations. In view of recently announced plans from Washington for a four-man board, The League's findings contained in this report seem now an even more timely contribution to a clearer understanding and solution of the problem of defense organization. 

Skilled labor is the ultimate productive resource of the nation. 

We should make it go as far as possible. If we wish to look at economic facts in real terms, instead of in terms of financial trimmings, we may think of getting the most defense out of an hour of skilled labor, instead of the most defense for a dollar. From either standpoint, carefully coordinated defense production and management are imperative.

DEFENSE PLANNING: 

A NATIONAL NEED 

By WILLARD D. ARANT* Research Director, The National Economy League THE COST OF THE DEFENSE PROGRAM as now projected will be equal to at least $15 per month (a total of $900) for the average American family over the next five years.

This financial burden, imposed by a sudden realization of this country's stake in swiftly changing world events, comes at a time when the ordinary running expenses of the federal government, including aids to distressed sections of the population, are at record levels, and when the federal debt is nearly twice as large as in any previous period. Although the average citizen does not yet fully comprehend the cost of defense to himself as a federal taxpayer, he is willing to pay for whatever means of protection the changing situation requires for the "adequate defense" of this country. 

Modern war, or preparation for possible war, requires elaborate and expensive machinery. The cost of a single airplane engine, for example, is greater than the annual salary of a cabinet officer. A single cruiser costs more than the salaries and expenses of Congress for a year. A single battleship costs more than the combined expenses of the State, Commerce and 

Justice Departments.

Defense expenditures over the next few years will equal or exceed expenditures for all other purposes. Of the $15.1 billion of direct appropriations for the current fiscal year (1941), more than half, or $8.8 billion, are for national defense. Although this amount is greater than will actually be spent in the fiscal year, annual expenditures in 1942 and 1943, when defense production will have reached its fullest stride, may approach this level. With few exceptions, funds unspent in the present year will carry over into the fiscal year 1942, and in addition Congress has authorized the executive departments to enter into contracts or to make commitments in the name of the government for a total of $9.0 billion subject to future defense appropriations.

The problem of financing the huge defense and non-defense expenditures has been met only to a relatively slight extent. The First Revenue Act of 1940 increased the personal and corporation income tax rates and added a 10 per cent "defense tax" to the amount due under the income tax and most federal excise tax schedules. The act also raised the limit on the direct debt from $45 billion to $49 billion. The Second Revenue Act of 1940 added a further 3.1 per cent to the levy on large corporations and imposed an excess profits tax. 

Revenues under both of these acts were presumably to contribute toward the defense bill. The fact is, however, that total revenues including "defense" taxes will be sufficient only to meet the non-defense expenditures at the current $7-billion level. Provision must soon be made, through reduction of expenditures and levying of additional taxes, to bring the budget more nearly into balance. The National Economy League said in June, 1940, "Not only must the defense funds themselves be spent with the greatest prudence, but other catagories of expenditures must be re-examined and substantially reduced if the federal finances are not to be thrown completely out of control." 

The League is seriously concerned over the financial implications of the defense program. That concern is based on evidence, here presented, that the program to date has been evolved without sufficient broad planning or

coordination, that it lacks the kind of balancing of immediately possible, most useful needs that must precede an efficient program of production and training, and that, in consequence, the spending of funds is likely to achieve less than the maximum possible defense.

Although men in official and advisory capacities are doing excellent work, as individuals, the comprehensive defense planning so urgently needed has not yet been provided. Contrary to widespread belief, neither the Council on 

National Defense nor its Advisory Commission¹ is a planning agency. Their duties are primarily to facilitate the procurement of war materials after the appropriations have been made. In the words of Edward R. Stettinius, Jr., one of the members of the Advisory Commission, "I think that on the industrial side we must serve the Army and Navy the way the Army and the Navy wish to be served," and again, "It is not our assignment to cover the national defense program in its entirety."2 The broad questions of defense policy and implementation of that policy are outside the jurisdiction of the Advisory 

Commission. 

Although seven months have elapsed since the program was initiated, the need and opportunity remain for inquiry into matters involving the balance, ultimate size, and orderly execution of the defense preparations. Recommendations on these matters should be made by an over-all advisory planning commission—which does not now exist.³ The central purpose of this study

DEFENSE OBJECTIVES 

WO MAIN PROBLEMS ARE INVOLVED in determining the objectives toward which the defense effort must move. One is the determination of basic questions of foreign policy; i.e., our relations to the rest of the world. 

These are matters of high national policy to be determined by the joint action and interaction of the Executive, the Congress and public opinion. The other is a question of implementing these policies with guns, ships and planes in the proper balance. Aid in solving the latter is the function which we visualize for the proposed planning commission. 

Foreign policy is within the province of the President, the Secretary of State, and Congress. As to this, it is our purpose here only to urge a broad public restatement of foreign policy from time to time, in order that the people will know where they stand and will be able to form an intelligent opinion of the defense preparations. The necessity for some degree of secrecy in diplomatic matters is conceded, but the broad outline of policy is something which concerns all the people, both as to possible fighting and as to paying the bills, and they are therefore entitled to periodic reviews of our position. with relation to other nations from authoritative sources, such as the President and Secretary of State. The fact that foreign policy is continually changing to .8.

keep pace with dynamic world conditions is one of the strongest arguments in favor of a defense planning commission to help the executive departments and Congress to keep step.

Working within the framework of foreign policy as determined by our constitutional procedures, the proposed planning commission should review the defense problem as a whole, promote the establishment of a defense plan -modified from time to time-and ascertain what roles the Army and Navy should play in that plan. Beyond that, it should determine the balance of different types of weapons within each service. Recognizing the limits to immediate productive capacity, it should inquire whether ships are more important than planes, whether destroyers are more important than battleships, and whether tanks are more important than coast defense guns. The recommendations of Army and Navy heads should not be the last word on these matters. Congress must, of course, rely upon trained military and naval officials for professional advice on many technical aspects of defense. But they cannot always rely on them for advice on cooperation between defense forces, nor on matters which are predominantly economic. 

The League is concerned only with obtaining the full measure of defense without waste which obstructs and weakens. We believe that a planning commission, working with the defense departments and with Congress, can achieve that goal more quickly with a given sum of money, or alternatively, just as quickly with a smaller sum. Whatever the ultimate scale of defense spending, there is imperative need for economy in the sense of "getting the most for our money."

Extravagance in defense may take three forms. First, funds may be spent for projects which, although they are administered by the Army and Navy, are not vital to an emergency program. An example is the rebuilding of 

Quartermaster manufacturing depots without increasing their productive capacity. Second, the existence of appropriations in excess of what can be spent to advantage in the fiscal year may be conducive to waste. Experience proves that spending agencies, in such circumstances, are likely to regard the mere spending of money as the criterion of success, and in an effort to make a showing may be less discriminating in selecting objects of expenditure and pay less attention to prices and costs than if the appropriations had been scheduled on the basis of thorough analysis and planning. Third, even though certain pieces of physical equipment are obtained with all possible skill and at lowest possible cost, they may represent extravagant expenditures if they are not essential to our particular problem of defense. •9.

The vital concern of the nation is not to spend money (although the defense program fits neatly into theories of public spending.) The need is to obtain whatever numbers of trained men and the amounts of physical equipment determined to be necessary-and to obtain them in the proper sequence and at the lowest cost consistent with efficiency. 

It would be possible, of course, eventually to create an enormous defense establishment by spending money for every conceivable defense purpose. If enough money is spent in enough different directions, some of it is bound to hit the right spots. That was the method used in the early period of the 

World War. It was an unwise method at that time, and is manifestly a threat to national credit today, when public expenditures, apart from armaments, are equal to nearly one-fourth of the national income. 

PRODUCTIVE CAPACITY ITH THE GRADUAL TRANSLATION of appropriations into orders, and particularly since Commissioner Knudsen called attention on December 13 to the production problems facing the country, many Americans have realized that, in spite of oft-repeated assertions, productive capacity is not unlimited and there is need for careful allocation of our resources of raw materials, labor-power, factory equipment and management. Especially is this true in the short run, when we have to get along temporarily with existing plants, materials and skilled labor. Over a longer period it is possible to increase these productive resources and to transfer an increasing quantity of existing resources from peace-time to armament uses. 

Industries capable of producing armaments are now largely occupied and will be further occupied in supplying war materials to Great Britain, and it is the recognized policy of the nation to give British orders a large degree of preference. The capacity available to fill our own immediate requirements is undeniably limited. This is why productive power must be so carefully channeled into the most essential uses, particularly during the early period of re-armament.

The economists' phase opportunity cost is useful in describing this problem. From this point of view, the cost of producing one thing is the lost opportunity to produce something else. The cost of producing a four-inch naval gun may be the lost opportunity to produce a four-inch anti-aircraft gun. The cost of producing a tank, which uses an airplane type engine, may be the lost opportunity to produce engines for a long-range bomber. • 10 •

Over a longer period, the cost of producing a fleet of airplanes may be the lost opportunity to produce additional naval vessels. The goal of an adequate defense in the shortest time requires an answer to the immediate question, whether workers shall be trained for aircraft construction or shipbuilding. Worker training will take time. W. S. Knudsen, a member of the 

Advisory Commission, recently said, "We have plenty of unskilled and semiskilled labor, but we are going to have a tight squeeze on skilled labor, for regardless of the efficiency of our training systems, and they are very good, no system is going to turn out a tool-maker in a couple of months." 

Questions involving the balance of different parts of the defense program, one against the other, cannot be satisfactorily dealt with under the present administrative set-up. They belong in the province of a group of men who are not handicapped by heavy administrative duties, who are not bound too much by tradition, and who can determine and convincingly demonstrate to other agencies of the government that this piece of equipment is needed now and that piece of equipment must be deferred. 

The administration has recently taken a step which, in spite of many manifestations of wishful thinking by both government and business, was inevitable as soon as the Second Supplemental National Defense Appropria tion Act was passed. This step was the establishment of a Priorities Board with authority to require manufacturers to fill Army and Navy contracts ahead of orders for private account or export. While the power may be exercised gradually, it will, of necessity, extend over a considerable section of industry as the program advances.

The very essence of a priorities administration is centralized authority. 

Yet we do not have it. The problem of who gets deliveries first. the Army or the Navy, is fought out in the Priorities Committee of the Army and Navy Munitions Board. (This board has two chairmen, one from the Army and one from the Navy, with equal authority.) The new Priorities Board, which is a part of the Advisory Commission on National Defense, does not determine Army vs. Navy priorities, but acts only "to adjust differences that cannot be settled by contractors themselves dealing through procurement officers of the War and Navy Departments and the Priorities Committee on the Army and Navy Munitions Board," and "to handle such priority problems as may arise outside the jurisdiction of the Priorities Committee of the Army and Navy Munitions Board." Clearly some coordination is needed here, and the more so if it becomes necessary in the future to establish priorities not . 11.

only between the Army and Navy and between the government and consumers generally but between different groups of private consumers. 

Recognition of the priorities problem makes all the more pointed the recommendation for a planning group to ascertain what equipment is most essential, "Some agency must be created to define what are first things," said the New York Times on October 25, 1940; "only when that is known can any Priorities Administration wisely exercise priority."

The Increasing Cost of Armaments 

Priorities control, if extended far enough, may retard the rise in war material prices. It is likely, however, that direct price control over certain products will have to be used concurrently. A planning commission, acting through the control agencies already established, can lessen the need for price control by carefully scheduling the demand for defense materials. 

Although a sharp advance in the general price level is not to be expected until resources are fully employed, the pressure of demand in certain lines will cause particular prices to rise much earlier. Prices of several industrial materials have already risen, according to the Federal Reserve Board, including steel scrap, copper, zinc, lumber, hides and wool. 

The rising trend of prices in industries important to national defense has already been reflected in the cost of construction and armaments to the government. The War Department is reported to have increased its estimates of the cost of cantonments for trainees because the cost of leasing land and of installing water, sewerage and other services was "unexpectedly higher." 

In June when the government decided to purchase 3,000 Rolls-Royce Merlin liquid-cooled airplane engines, the cost was estimated at $14,500 each, and an appropriation of $43,500,000 was added, just before final passage, to the 

First Supplemental Appropriation Bill. When the contract was finally let in September the United States' share (still for 3,000 engines) was set at $62,000,000 or $20,667 each. The Navy Department had to ask Congress in July for additional money for guns to be used on auxiliary vessels. According to Admiral W. R. Furlong, Chief of the Bureau of Ordnance, “We estimated that $10,000 per gun would be necessary. Bids came in from three firms and they ran about $14,000 apiece." Several appropriations for "speedup" on construction of naval vessels and aircraft have been necessitated in part by rising costs. As far as naval vessels are concerned, costs have been increasing over several years. "Shipbuilding costs have gone up enormously," declared Admiral Robinson, Chief of the Bureau of Ships. • 12.

The speed of the defense program is increasing costs 20 to 30 per cent above peacetime levels, Army officials told a Senate committee on October 3. 

The increases, it was explained, are due to higher manufacturing costs and not to profiteering.

Prices mentioned in currently published statistics of contracts awarded are, in the largest cases, only estimates, and the final cost of materials ordered today cannot now be determined. Contractors cannot usually undertake to guarantee fixed prices when costs are rising to an unknown degree. The extent of the price rise will depend not only on the rate of spending and the orderliness of spending, but on the government's taxation and monetary policies, the labor training program and other efforts to reduce the demand or increase the supply of goods which the government needs. 

COORDINATION THE HE DEFENSE DEPARTMENTS have two rather diverse functions: first, training in, and on occasion the practice of, the arts of warfare; second, administration of vast organizations which have many characteristics of large business establishments. Under each of these there are problems of coordination demanding the attention of an impartial planning commission with a breadth of view transcending departmental or bureaucratic limitations. 

Coordination in Fighting

Top-ranking Army and Navy officers have gone through a long period of training and selection and are experts in their own fields. Due in part to this very training, however, they are not experts in coordinated operations involving both the Army and Navy. A sort of rivalry, much like the Army-Navy athletic rivalry carried to extreme, is a further handicap in attaining the smooth balance and integration that effective national defense requires. 

A few formal means of cooperation between the forces exist by virtue of voluntary agreements between the War and Navy Departments, but their effectiveness in bringing about coordination of military and naval operations is, at the very least, open to serious question. There have been no recent largescale rehearsals of plans for joint operations, if such plans exist. In the words of the New York Times of May 16, 1940, it is "essential that we create a • 13.

defense organization to assure far more cooperation between our armed services than exists at present. The jealousies and frictions existing between our 

Army and Navy, and between air and ground officers, must be eradicated." 

Study of Army-Navy coordination is a job for experts, and by this term is meant, not specialists in any particular type of military or naval operations, but men with a broad understanding of national defense. These men may be either military or civilian.

A military or naval officer of an imaginative type who is detached from all duties with his service may quickly adjust himself to the necessary viewpoint. 

The ability of intelligent civilians to draw the broad lines of military, air and naval coordination is indicated by a statement which Rear Admiral Yates Stirling, Jr., retired, recently made in another connection, "Oftentimes a specialist has a narrow outlook and may be of less value than a man, not a specialist, having a wider experience, and more capable in his understanding of how to coordinate specialists and cause them to act in unison." 

Coordination in Business Management 

The Army and Navy, two of the largest business establishments in the country from the standpoint of designing, purchasing and warehousing of materials and supplies, pursue their independent courses as business organizations as well as fighting units, with very little cooperation. Complete integration in business matters may be impossible, since each service requires many kinds of materials the other does not need. But much cooperation which is possible has been resisted because, as General Marshall has said, "each service naturally desires to look after its own affairs." Duplicate facilities are constructed and maintained in many places where one would suffice. 

Under the present program and in the future, the most important savings can be made through coordination of the air forces. Whether or not they should be placed under a separate cabinet secretary and operated as a unified military unit—and this should be studied by our impartial planning boardthere is an obvious opportunity for coordination in design. Congressional committeemen have often suggested this, but they have been too easily satisfied by the excuse that different purposes require different types of planes. 

Admirals usually point out that an Army type plane could not be used on an aircraft carrier, and that the Navy requires seaplanes for patrol purposes, but they do not point out that such planes are only a small part of the current Navy plane program. The Navy has, or will have, thousands of land planes, 14

and the Army and Navy could just as well determine upon the best designs for joint use, so that both could have the benefit of mass production. Of equal importance, financially, are the savings which would result from joint use of air fields and repair shops. The fact that land planes of the two forces were of different design has been used to justify the construction of duplicate Army and Navy bases in the same localities. 

The Advisory Commission on National Defense aids Army and Navy procurement by furnishing information on plant capacities and labor and material supplies, and in serving as a clearing house and recording bureau for the largest defense orders, but it does not have authority to eliminate functional duplication between the defense departments. 

Within the departments, there is need for coordination of bureaus. Like bureaus in other governmental departments, they have "just growed." They pursue their independent ways and find it difficult to get together on a common job in time of stress. The New York Times on May 16, 1940, said "there is still a clear need for reorganization in the interest of unity and the elimination of overlapping functions. There are still too many separate chiefs of equal rank working in watertight compartments." 

The Navy Department has recently combined two of its most important bureaus, the Bureaus of Engineering and of Construction and Repair, into the single Bureau of Ships. The consolidation, according to Lewis Compton, Assistant Secretary of the Navy, "is highly desirable because, after all, the construction of a ship should be a single, coordinated function." The hearings on the reorganization bill throw much light on the confusion and divided responsibility existing in Navy bureaus which the consolidation has only partly corrected.

The two old bureaus had existed side by side since 1842, when the Bureau of Engineering was set up to install relatively simple machinery in the first steam vessels. The suggestion for bringing them together had been studied many times, according to Mr. Compton, "but it has been difficult if not impossible to attain in the past any unanimity of opinion on this subject of reorganization. certain morale and esprit de corps is built around a bureau, and until now they have not been able to get together on it." suppose 

Admiral Robinson, Coordinator of Shipbuilding, said, "The reasons it has not been done before are two: The first one is that you did not happen to get people who were willing to make the necessary adjustments and sacrifices, and so forth, to do it that we have at the present time; the second is that you seldom do anything until you have to. [Italics supplied.] Conditions have 15

been getting worse, and worse and ships were getting more difficult to design all the time until we got to the point where we just had to do it." 

The new bureau is headed by a Chief and an Assistant Chief, both rear admirals receiving the highest pay and allowances of that rank. One is an engineer and the other a naval constructor. "That is quite necessary," said 

Mr. Compton, "Otherwise you would ruin the morale of the naval constructors if you put two engineers in, and vice versa. 

The consolidation is not, immediately, much more than bringing the two bureaus physically nearer to each other. Furthermore, the consolidation does not include the Bureau of Ordnance, which purchases and installs armor and guns on ships. The fact that armor is under a separate bureau has caused some delays and extra expense in the past. 

The Navy has six planning and purchasing bureaus: Bureau of Ships, Bureau of Ordnance, Bureau of Aeronautics, Bureau of Yards and Docks, Bureau of Supplies and Accounts, and the Marine Corps. 

The Army has seven purchasing groups: Ordnance Department, Quartermaster Corps, Air Corps, Corps of Engineers, Medical Department, Signal 

Corps, and Chemical Warfare Service.

Many of these groups must work together in planning and procuring war materials. For example, an airplane and its engine are purchased by the Air 

Corps, but the radios are purchased by the Signal Corps and the aircraft cannon, machine guns and ammunition by the Ordnance Department. All must cooperate in the design of the ship and in scheduling deliveries of the component parts.

The question of which bureaus shall have "cognizance" over specific matters is like the question of which trade union shall have "jurisdiction” over certain types of work. In discussing appropriations for motor vehicles, a committeeman inquired about progress on puncture-proof and bullet-proof tires. General Gregory of the Quartermaster Corps, acknowledging that experimental work on puncture-proof tires was under his bureau, reminded the committe that "the development of bullet-proof tires is under the 

Ordnance Department."

APPROPRIATIONS FIRST, THE PLANNING SECOND 

HE LEAGUE has previously pointed out that hurried voting of appropriations without sufficiently detailed study of the objects of expenditure prepares the way for unwise use of public money. While Congress has estab- 16.

lished the main categories of defense expenditures in the appropriations acts, the methods used in arriving at the amounts of those items, and of the total, have been scarcely more satisfactory than those used in determining the totals. in the blank-check relief and public works appropriations a few years ago. 

The method not only robs Congress of an effective voice in controlling expenditures, by delegating such control to the executive departments, but in the case of defense it promises confusion which may prevent the accomplishment of "first things first."

In its desire to have whatever defense is necessary, the public has accepted the estimates of the Navy and War Departments in the belief that definite plans had been prepared with due regard for actual needs and with some regard for cost. The published hearings on successive appropriation bills unfortunately do not bear out this view. Testimony of admirals and generals as to needs of their departments has been sketchy, and there are numerous inconsistencies between the statements of different officers at the same time and the same officers at different times. Congressional committees have not taken time to inquire closely into the justification of estimates, and some items which proved to be highly doubtful upon examination were nevertheless allowed to go through.

When committee members, who had the advantage of official testimony on and off the record, were admittedly unable to pass adequate judgment on the appropriations, it is obvious that other members of the House and Senate had little idea what they were voting for when the bills reached the floor. 

The Second Supplemental National Defense Bill of over $5 billion, the largest peace-time appropriation in our history, was passed by the House after less than three hours of debate. Fewer than 100 members bothered to attend the session. In the Senate the bill was passed after five hours of debate. 

Neither house took a record vote on the measure. A smaller appropriation— $338 million for military posts-was passed by the House in less than one hour and by the Senate in two minutes.

Such haste could be defended only if Congress knew with confidence that the expenditures were actually required, that they had been well planned in advance and that they had been justified to the committees in proper detail. 

The evidence gives no such assurance. It is important that there be a fuller public understanding of appropriation methods involving such huge amounts. 

In presenting estimates for Army munition plants on a wartime scale, Col. James E. Burns, Executive to the Assistant Secretary of War, testified on . 17.

June 4, 1940, that he had not submitted the program to the Advisory Committee on National Defense "because the breakdown was only finished this noon."

General Gregory, the Quartermaster General, in requesting funds for the purchase of land, did not know what sites, or how many sites, would be selected. "We had a very short time to prepare the estimate. . . ." Some light is thrown on Army estimating technique by the explanation of how, in the absence of plans, it was possible to submit acreage figures carried to three decimal places. "The only explanation I can make to that," said General 

Gregory, "is that when the original estimates were submitted it was a larger figure and it was cut down on a percentage basis." This is one of many indications that estimates were prepared by dividing up a sum of money, rather than by adding together a list of carefully determined specific requirements.

In discussing expenditures for Quartermaster depots, General Gregory said, "These estimates were prepared almost overnight, and the detailed breakdown was supposed to be submitted later.” 

An appropriation of several millions for docks, dredging and facilities at Army field service establishments was to be used in part for "long lists of individual items pertaining to power and lighting systems, water and sewer lines, roads and railroad trackage which are very badly out of repair." It is difficult to understand why these funds were needed in view of the large amount of money spent by the Army and WPA on military posts and establishments in recent years, and if needed, why the request should have been postponed until the third appropriation bill.

Testimony in connection with ammunition storage provides an example of how the defense departments think first of money, and second of plans.

One evident reason for requesting huge sums several months in advance of the time they can be used is the desire of the War and Navy Departments to take advantage of Congressional willingness to approve any and all sums asked in the name of national defense. They have been indisposed to risk the chance that Congress may be in a more business-like mood at the beginning of the next regular session in January. For example, when Admiral 

Moreell, Chief of the Navy Bureau of Yards and Docks, was asked whether January would not be early enough for an appropriation for constructing an ammunition depot (for storage of ammunition for ships yet to be built), he replied, "If we were assured of the money in January, that is probably so; yes, sir." 

Admiral Stark, Chief of Naval Operations, in requesting funds not approved by the Budget Bureau, said the Bureau of Ships "could use" a certain additional appropriation and the Bureau of Yards and Docks "would like" certain sums.

Army and Navy officials had difficulty at first in scaling their minds up to the gigantic expansion which the current temper of the nation makes possible. Early estimates of requirements were set forth as final, thus necessitating embarrassing contradiction later. Testimony on more recent bills has been phrased in a manner calculated to prepare the way for even further requests in the future. The War Department, for example, has announced a list of "deferred" items totalling about two billion dollars. Admiral Stark of the Navy Department, upon leaving the closed hearings of the Senate 

Naval Affairs Committee on the "two-ocean Navy" authorization bill, was asked by reporters if he had recommended another 100 ships to be added to the 200 fighting vessels provided in the bill. According to the New York 

Times report, he replied, "Not yet, but I left the door open."

The above evidence is more than sufficient to establish the need for careful, coordinated planning in the preparation of requests for appropria- 19

tions-planning which will act as a check on the natural desires for expansion in every Army and Navy bureau, and which will make certain that every dollar requested has a definite place in an emergency defense program. 

The Cost of Future Maintenance 

Maintenance of our augmented defense machine is an item that has received much less attention that it deserves. The annual costs to be expected after the expansion program is completed cannot now be determined. Some estimates have been made on the basis of the round figures of $1,500 per man for the Army and $500 per ton for the Navy, the approximate cost in recent years. On this basis an Army of 1,400,000 men in training at one time would cost $2,100,000,000 per year and a Navy of 3,500,000 tons would cost $1,750,000,000 per year, or a total of $3,850,000,000 for the two services. 

These estimates may be too high, however, since they include the average annual cost of capital outlays such as construction at military posts and naval shore facilities and the replacement of ships and ordnance. Most of this capital equipment—if the term "capital" can be applied to war equipmentwill be practically new at the end of the current expansion program, and, with the exception of aircraft, should require little replacement for many years. 

Regardless of the turn of the world events, the annual defense cost will be far above the levels of recent years. Army and Navy officials have stated their beliefs that the current program should continue, no matter what happens elsewhere, and it is axiomatic in public finance that an expanded service, even though it be cut when the emergency ends, never returns to the former level of cost.

Apart from the "house-keeping" expenses of the regular defense forces in the future, expenditures will be required for maintenance of the public works now being constructed at military posts, naval stations and air bases. 

The new ships, tanks, guns, automobiles, etc., that are now being purchased will have to be stored or maintained, whether in active use or not. New government munitions plants and expanded facilities at Navy yards will require annual expenditures for upkeep, and according to present plans, many of the facilities being constructed in private plants for defense production are to be maintained by the government as stand-by plants. 

An important but unpredictable cost, which may extend over many decades, will arise from hospitalization and the granting of other benefits to the men who will serve, even temporarily, in the Army and Navy. According • 20 •

to present law, these men will be "veterans" and entitled to many privileges at government expense.

In determining the defense program needed to implement a defense policy, a planning commission must recognize not only the tremendous initial cost of expansion, but also the commitments for huge expenditures in the future which this expansion involves. 

THIS THE PLANNING COMMISSION 

HIS STUDY has raised questions of industrial capacity, finance, and the balance of arms between and within the Army and Navy. All these matters are parts of a defense program broadly viewed. In order to minimize wasteful duplication of effort, extravagant spending and possibly dangerous delay, all must be brought within the scope of an expert planning board or commission. We do not insist that the following suggestions represent the only form which such a commission may take, but we do urge that the recommendation be looked upon as a basis for discussion leading to improvements in defense planning machinery in the shortest possible time. 

The planning commission should be advisory only, having no executive or administrative power. The reputation and character of its members should be relied upon to carry its recommendations with the President and Congress. 

Legal authority when needed-for example, for consolidation of bureaus or shifting of appropriations—should be requested from Congress in each specific instance. The commission should not be granted blanket authority to make such administrative changes as it wishes. It should, however, have full power to summon witnesses and to obtain from government agencies whatever information it may require, including all existing reports and compilations. 

The commission should be small, in order to be efficient, but should have at least as many members as there may be major subjects of inquiry. The division into subcommittees should be, not according to existing departments of the government, but according to the categories into which defense activities logically fall; e.g., aircraft design, airport construction, seacoast defense, workers' training-each of which is now under the control of several different, and in many respects rival, authorities. Coordination of effort within each of these fields would be the job of the subcommittees, but the proper scale of their operations in relation to the whole program would be determined by the full planning body. • 21.

The finance division of the commission should make a thorough study, not only of taxation, but of possible economies in expenditures. This section of the commission would correspond to the Select Committee on War Expenditures which the English Parliament has established, according to the London Times, "critically to examine current expenditures and contracts and to indicate where, without delay, savings can be made and the utmost value obtained for expenditures. . . ."

The planning commission could be set up by Presidential appointment or by Congressional legislation-preferably the latter, since its purpose would be to recommend controls over the use of funds by the executive departments. 

Its membership should be predominantly civilian, so that the requests of the Army and Navy for appropriations could be held within the bounds of demonstrable necessity and industrial capacity. The commission should include military and naval experts, selected from among active officers (who would be detached from all other duties), from among retired officers (including perhaps some who disagree with departmental organization and policy), or from among civilians who have devoted many years to study of the strategy of warfare. The field of selection might well include also members of 

Congress who have long been connected with national defense legislation, but whose efforts toward a sound defense plan are hindered by the cumbrous procedure of numerous uncoordinated committees. 

The planning commission visualized here would not usurp the functions of the Advisory Commission on National Defense, which are essentially those of procurement, nor of the War Plans divisions of the War and Navy Departments. None of these existing agencies is broad enough in purpose to perform the overall planning necessary to a sound and economical defense. 

They would continue to carry out the tasks they now have, but would necessarily work with, and supply information to, the planning group. 

It is not too late to establish such a commission, and its work would not delay the essential parts of the defense program in the least. On the contrary, by eliminating or deferring those parts judged not to be important, but which compete economically with those which are, the commission could speed the attainment of the most vital goals. The program still has a long way to go, and the public will not have confidence in its efficiency until an impartial group of experts is named to investigate the whole problem, to recommend changes in organization and procedure where these may be indicated, and to examine critically new proposals and new situations as they arise. 22.

THE CONCLUSION HE NATION'S DESIRE for all necessary defense has resulted in a series of large appropriations, based upon the constantly expanding requests of Army and Navy officials, and voted without a sufficiently well-defined and coordinated plan, so far as Congress and the public are aware. 

In determining the magnitude of the defense program, two main questions are involved: "What do we need?" and "What can we obtain in a given state of our industrial and technical resources?" Neither question has been answered satisfactorily.

Congress has relied almost exclusively upon the advice of Army and Navy officials as to what funds are needed for defense. The resulting program is likely to involve an undue amount of waste, both of time and money. 

Civilian control, exercised upon the advice of a small planning commission in which civilian members predominate, is necessary to aid Congress and the President in evaluating the needs of the separate departments and bureaus and to provide a critical examination of the requests for funds. Without such control, the Army and Navy, like other departments of the government, may be expected to take advantage of an emergency in their own fields of action to expand beyond actual needs. Furthermore, certain traditional kinds of armaments are likely to receive too much emphasis at the expense of others which may be more effective for our particular problem of defense. 

The ability to spend defense money advantageously has been stepped up considerably by the Advisory Commission on National Defense (which is primarily a procurement agency for the Army and Navy and not a planning agency), but serious industrial and skilled labor bottlenecks have already appeared, the removal of which may require stern measures of control over management and labor.

With so many billions of dollars available, there is danger that the criterion of success will be the speed with which that money can be expended. 

Defense is, however, not a matter of dollars, but of trained men and physical equipment. Too many dollars sent after the same limited supplies of materials and skilled labor will lead not only to confusion in production, but to waste and high costs. The rise in prices of armaments and construction, already evident, means that the money appropriated will not go as far as expected. 

The defense program will have a greater pump-priming effect than any program undertaken during the depression. To minimize economic dislocation • 23 •

-with its promise of insistent demands for continued federal spending in the future-will require both orderly administration of expenditures and a heavy increase in taxation beyond the present level. 

The machinery for evolving a comprehensive plan of defense does not exist today. A qualified planning board, made up of experts in all fields related to defense, is urgently needed to study and ascertain our defense needs, to adjust the rate of spending to the limits of industrial capacity, to aid in consolidating and coordinating the defense effort, and to act as a check on the judgment of officials whose experience and duties are confined to narrow fields.

It has been said in extenuation of present methods that no team is coordinated or properly drilled for its first practice game. The obvious answer is that no team can ever become effective without a coach to coordinate the efforts of the line, the backfield and the aerial attack. • 24 •

APPENDIX A BRIEF HISTORY OF DEFENSE SINCE SEPTEMBER, 1939 

VENTS HAVE MOVED SO RAPIDLY since early summer that the public has lost perspective on the defense problem. A brief review of the past year's occurrences will be of value in showing how the government responded to changes in the situation abroad and in public opinion at home, and will aid in evaluating present plans and future requirements. 

September 8, 1939-Within a few days of the outbreak of the European 

War, President Roosevelt declared a national emergency "to the extent necessary for the proper observance, safeguarding and enforcing of the neutrality of the United States and the strengthening of our national defense within the limits of peacetime authorizations." Executive orders were issued increasing the Army from 210,000 to 227,000 men, the 

National Guard from 190,000 to 235,000, the Navy from 120,000 to 

145,000 and the Marine Corps from 18,250 to 25,000. Orders were also issued for the recommissioning of 100 World War destroyers for patrol duty, and for increasing the force of the Federal Bureau of Investigation by 150 men. The declaration of national emergency was necessary in order to make possible the expenditure of money for these purposes without a previous Congressional appropriation.

January 3, 1940-The federal budget for the fiscal year 1941 proposed $1,840 million for national defense, of which $300 million were segregated as emergency items. "This segregation," said the President, “will, I hope, help to focus attention on our emergency defense expenditures, and the problem of financing them. It will also facilitate the return to a normal defense program when the emergency has passed."

February 23-General George C. Marshall, Army Chief of Staff, urged an 

Army increase of 15,000 men, to bring the Regular Army to 242,000 men, and an increase of $279,000,000 over the budget estimate to procure ammunition, rifles, artillery, tanks and other critical items for a Protective Mobilization Plan force of 750,000 men (including the National Guard). 

General Marshall warned, "As to the existing crisis abroad, we must face the facts. Any major developments there should be paralleled by added precautions in this country. If the situation grows more desperate, we should add to the numbers of seasoned troops in the Regular Army and to the strength of the National Guard. If Europe blazes in the late spring 25.

or summer, we must put our house in order before the sparks reach the Western Hemisphere. These should be but temporary measures, but they should be taken definitely, step by step, to prepare ourselves against the possibility of chaotic world conditions. To put it another way, I am opposed to plunging into a sudden expansion of personnel to the limit of present authorizations, and I am equally opposed to the policy of waiting until the last minute and then attempting the impossible, from the viewpoint of the dominating time factor." 

March 15-Admiral Harold R. Stark, Chief of Naval Operations, testified, "If the ships which we have under contemplation [a 25 per cent increase, but not a 70 per cent increase, was then contemplated] . were completed today or within the next few years, taking into consideration the building programs of other nations, I would say that we probably could defend the Hawaiian Islands and Alaska and the Canal and the coasts of the United States, and the Gulf Stream and the Caribbean. I don't mean that that would be a tight defense."

May 3-President Roosevelt at a press conference declared, according to the New York Times report, "the nation's productive facilities were being taxed to capacity in most instances, so that an immediate outlay of additional money would not be reflected in a corresponding outlay of material." 

May 16-Germany had invaded the Low Countries and was entering northern France. President Roosevelt, in a special address to Congress, detailed the time it might take for bombers to reach our midwest cities from the islands off the west coast of Africa. He requested appropriations to procure the essential equipment for "a larger and thoroughly rounded-out army,” to replace or modernize old Army and Navy equipment, to increase production facilities, and to speed up existing production. For these purposes he asked an immediate appropriation of $896 million, together with contract authorizations of $286 million. These sums were in addition to these already pending in Congress, thus bringing the total requests to approximately three billion dollars. The resulting appropriation, passed in early June and based upon recommendations of the Army and Navy officers as to requirements, provided for an Army of 280,000 men and for the laying down of 24 new combat vessels for the Navy, in addition to the 28 vessels already under construction. 

May 20-General Marshall, testifying before the House Military Affairs Committee, said, "It is becoming more apparent every day that the big question 26.

is, not how much you appropriate, but how much can we spend at the present time."

May 31-With the British forces being evacuated from Flanders, the President announced to Congress that he would transmit Army and Navy requests for additional funds-"over a billion dollars."

June 4-The President, at his regular press conference, said he saw no reason why Congress should not adjourn after it had passed pending legislation. 

June 4—At hearings on the First Supplemental National Defense Appropria tion Bill (which embodied the President's requests of May 31 and a miscellany of other items) General Marshall testified that "there is a requirement for a further increase in the strength of the Regular Army of 55,000 men on a voluntary basis pure and simple," which "would mean that we can develop rapidly, trained and seasoned units out of that force, to enable us to avoid, we hope, the necessity of mobilizing the National Guard," and that in addition 40,000 men should be recruited over a period of time for the Air Corps. He made it clear, however, that he was not making a formal request for these increases, since he had no authority to do so from the Bureau of the Budget or from the President. The purpose of the increases was to prepare for "any eventuality" in the Western Hemisphere. "Not the continental United States. We do not visualize any invasion of this country. An air raid or something of that sort is possible, but, frankly, we do not see it in the offing. But we see all manner of possibilities in the Western Hemisphere." General Marshall said the bill carried the program forward as rapidly as desirable during the fiscal year 1941.

June 5-In connection with the same bill, Admiral Stark was asked whether the appropriation would give the Navy what it should have to meet the present situation, and he replied, "It will either give it to us, or put us in such condition that by the time Congress meets again, if there is anything further we have immediate need for, we will have time to request it.” 

He said the appropriation was designed to "(a) Speed up the construction of ships already building and to be laid down in 1941. (b) Carry out the expansion of the naval air bases as provided for in the so-called 

Hepburn board report. (c) Correct all deficiencies in guns, ammunition, equipment, and other items necessary to get the Navy and naval bases ready for business." The bill provided funds for starting construction of 

68 combatant naval vessels under the 11 per cent expansion program. • 27 •

June 18-Admiral Stark officially recommended a 70 per cent, or 1,250,000ton expansion of the Navy.

June 27-The President signed the First Supplemental Appropriation Act, now increased to $1,488 million in cash and $289 million in contract authorizations. It provided for the increase in the Army to 375,000 men which General Marshall suggested on June 4. 

June 27-The United Press, citing the four-billion-dollar estimated cost of the pending Navy expansion bill, said that "the Army is prepared to ask for more money approximately matching the Navy's request." The Associated Press reported that, except for $175 million to start construction. on the new ships, "administration defense advisers are understood to feel that the Navy's requirements had been met for the time being." 

July 10-The President transmitted to Congress requests of $1,662 million in cash and $2,250 million in authorizations for the War Department and $499 million in cash and $437 million in authorizations for the Navy Department-a total of $4,848 million. 

July 31–The House passed the bill (Second Supplemental National Defense 

Appropriation Bill), having increased the total to $4,963 million. 

August 13-Secretary Knox, questioned as to the possibility of requests for additional Navy funds, replied, "Do you want my honest opinion on it?

I think we will have an awful lot of trouble spending all the money we have got now, effectively."

August 15-General Marshall said, "We cannot tell what final strength will be required. If danger threatens this hemisphere we may require 3,000,000 men, 4,000,000 or more, because our obligations are scattered in so many directions."

August 29-The Senate passed the Second Supplemental Bill, having increased the sum to $5,133 million.

September 6-Congress adopted the conference report on the bill, which increased the total to $5,212 million. 

September 14-The President transmitted requests for $1,941 million, including $1,603 million in cash and $150 million in contract authorizations for the War Department to put into effect National Guard and selective service training, although officials of the War Department had previously testified that $1 billion would be sufficient for this purpose. The request also included additional amounts for the Navy Department, the National 

Youth Administration and the Department of Commerce. 

• 28 •

September 16-The President signed the Selective Training and Service Act, providing for compulsory military training and service of not more than 

900,000 men at any one time.

October 8-Congress approved $150 million for housing military and civilian defense personnel. This brought the total defense appropriations and authorizations to $17,842 million.

HE NATIONAL ECONOMY LEAGUE is a THE 

non-profit organization founded and incorporated in May 1932, to revive the American principle of representative government for the common good, and particularly to secure the elimination of wasteful or unjustifiable federal expenditures. 

The League's present objectives are:

3. To push for a balanced federal budget-the only way now to avoid the complete collapse of our national credit, our system of free government, and loss of our civil liberties. 

All publications of The League are distributed without charge and are sent to all members of the 

Congress. Additional copies of this pamphlet may be obtained upon request. Since such distribution is made possible by voluntary gifts, individual contributions will aid materially in wider circulation and a broader, more effective, educational program, as well as make possible special research projects for which funds are not now available.
