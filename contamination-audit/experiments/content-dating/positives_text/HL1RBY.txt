HARDSHIP LICENSING INTRODUCTION 

The driver licensing laws of the various states typically provide in some way for the suspension or revocation of the license of a person who is convicted of serious or frequent traffic person law violations. Such suspensions or revocations often result in substantial hardship for the licensee and his family, particularly where the licensee is unable to continue in his employment due to the loss of his license. A number of states have enacted laws to ameliorate the harsh impact of a total deprivation of the driving privilege in the case of some or all such suspensions or revocations. These hardship licensing laws are the subject of this Commentary.

The Commentary reviews the status of state laws in effect as of January 1, 1976 relating to hardship licensing. The Commentary will not review all the possible approaches to driver improvement which utilize a limited license.2 It will cover only those laws which provide for the restoration or continuation of a driving privilege to a person who, but for the law, would be without a driving privilege due to a suspension or revocation. 

Hardship licensing laws take so many different forms that it is difficult to pull them all together under one umbrella. Nevertheless, hardship licensing is perhaps the best term for referring to all the different approaches, although some of the laws discussed in this Commentary are not clearly based upon hardship considerations. 

The common denominator of the various forms of hardship licensing is the restoration or continuation of a driving privilege, usually a limited privilege, during a time when the privilege would otherwise be suspended or revoked due to the licensee's misconduct. 

Throughout the Commentary we will use the term "hardship licensing" as a generic term which will include any or all of the various types of laws which share this common denominator. 

UNIFORM VEHICLE CODE 

The Uniform Vehicle Code does not contain a hardship licensing provision similar to those found in many state laws. A proposal to amend the Uniform Vehicle Code to allow the department to issue a limited license to a person whose license has been suspended or revoked when that person needs to drive a motor vehicle in order to earn a living was rejected by the National Committee in 1968.3

4944 The report of the Subcommittee which considered and disapproved the proposal included this summary of their deliberations: 

It was felt that occupational licenses generally are unworkable and tend to weaken the entire administration of driver licensing systems, and that they constitute a "backdoor" approach to lessening severe penalties. If penalties are in fact too severe, they should be reduced directly.4/

The Uniform Vehicle Code does incorporate a rehabilitation licensing concept. It is applicable only to a person whose license has been revoked for driving while under the influence. It provides for early restoration of a limited license upon a showing that the person is making progress in an alcohol treatment program. In 1971 the National Committee extensively revised the penalty provisions in regard to the offense of driving while under the influence of alcohol or drugs. Recognizing that the traditional penalty options of jail or a fine did not effectively respond to the real problem of the drinking driver and thus did not effectively bring about driver improvement, the National Committee moved to provide additional options in the nature of rehabilitative remedies rather than penalties.5/ UVC § 11-902.2 was amended to provide for an examination of a person convicted of driving while under the influence to determine whether that person "needs or would benefit from treatment for alcohol or drug abuse." Following such an examination the court may impose normal penalties or may, following an additional hearing, order treatment on either an in-patient or out-patient basis for up to one year's duration. 

As an incentive to the person to respond to the treatment, the section provides that any person undergoing such treatment may apply for a driver's license. The application must be considered along with a report of the progress of the treatment by the department's medical advisory board. The department is authorized, after receiving the advice of the medical advisory board, to issue a license to the person with conditions and restrictions which are consistent with his rehabilitation and with protection of the public. Of the 38 jurisdictions which have hardship licensing laws, many have multiple laws authorizing different kinds of hardship licensing treatment under various specified circumstances or for persons convicted of specified offenses. The laws in each of the 38 jurisdictions may be briefly summarized as follows:

Alaska -- A court, upon convicting a person for homicide by vehicle, felony in which a motor vehicle is used, leaving the scene of an accident, or perjury to the department, must revoke the person's license for up to one year. Upon a first conviction for driving while under the influence the court must revoke the license for not less than 30 days. Upon a first conviction for reckless driving the court may revoke or suspend the license for 30 days. In all of these cases, however, the court need not revoke the license if it finds that the person's ability to earn a livelihood would be severely impaired and - 3 -

that a limitation can be placed on the license which will enable the person to earn a livelihood without excessive risk of danger to the public.

Another section provides that where a license is suspended or revoked under the implied consent law, the court may find that extenuating circumstances exist which would cause extreme hardship, in which case the suspension or revocation may be modified or nullified. 

Arkansas -- When a court suspends or revokes a license following a conviction for driving while under the influence of intoxicating liquor, the court may issue an order for a restricted temporary permit to allow operation under such restrictions as the court may impose, including use of a vehicle to go to work, to attend church, or for other specified travel designated by the court. Where a license is suspended or revoked upon a second or subsequent such conviction within three years, the court may order issuance of such a restricted temporary permit only if the person submits to a rehabilitation program specified by the court for correction of his drinking problem. The person must continue to comply with the terms of the rehabilitation program or the court may revoke the restricted temporary permit. 

California Any person who drives 25,000 miles or more per year is allowed to accumulate a higher point total before being identified as a negligent driver who is subject to being suspended. Such a driver must accumulate six or more points within 12 months, eight or more points in 24 months, or 10 or more in 36 months in order to be identified as a negligent driver. A person who does not drive 25,000 miles per year need only accumulate four or more points within 12 months, six or more within 24 months, or eight or more within 36 months before being identified as a negligent driver. 

The license of a person who is determined under the point system to be a negligent driver may be suspended, revoked or probated. For any such driver, regardless of his annual mileage, the department may, as a condition of probation, issue a restricted license to permit driving a commercial vehicle while in the course of employment, during specified hours. Any other restrictions, as determined by the department, may also be noted on the license.

Colorado 

The law allows a person who is employed for the principal purpose of driving to accumulate more points under the point system than a driver who is not so employed. A driver who is employed must accumulate 16 points in one year, 24 points in two years, or 28 points in four years before being subject to suspension. All such points must be accumulated while driving in the course of employment. Other drivers are subject to suspension upon accumulating 12 points within one year or 18 points within two years. Minor drivers and provisional drivers are subject to suspension at even lower point levels. The law does specify, however, that the license of a person who is employed for the principal purpose of driving and who is convicted for driving while under the influence or for leaving the scene of an accident must be suspended in the same manner as if the offense occurred outside the course of employment.

Following a suspension under the point system the department may issue a probationary license containing such restrictions as the department deems reasonable and necessary. Such restrictions may limit driving to that required to go to and from the licensee's place of employment or to perform duties within the course of employment. The department may cancel or suspend the probationary license for any violation of the restrictions.

A person who has his license revoked or suspended for driving while under the influence is eligible for a hearing for a probationary license as described above only upon satisfactory completion of a course of alcohol treatment in an approved program. Delaware 

When a license is suspended on discretionary grounds, the department may issue an "occupational license" upon proper application by the licensee stating that the suspension has created an extreme hardship in the conduct of his usual - 5 -

business. Such an application may be made only when no prior occupational license has been issued within the preceding 12 months, and only when the licensee has been subjected to not more than two prior discretionary license suspensions. An occupational license may not be issued if the person's license has been suspended because of physical and/or mental disability or if the license has been revoked because of conviction of a charge for which revocation is required. Violation of the restrictions imposed under an occupational license results in an extension of the original period of suspension for an additional like period, and revocation of the occupational license. Florida 

Twelve months following a revocation of the license of a person identified as a habitual offender, and immediately following any other license suspension, cancellation or revocation, the licensee must be given notice of his right to a hearing. Upon such hearing the licensee may show that loss of his license causes a serious hardship and precludes carrying out his normal business or employment, and that use of his license in the normal course of his business is necessary to the proper support of himself or his family. 

The department must require proof of successful completion of an approved driver training or alcohol education course, and may require letters of recommendation from respected businessmen in the community, law enforcement officers or judicial officers in determining whether the licensee should be permitted to drive on a restricted basis for business only. 

Upon such a hearing the department must either suspend, affirm or modify its order, and may restore a limited privilege to drive for business or employment use only.

Where a person's license is revoked for driving while under the influence, the court may order issuance of a temporary driving permit restricted to business or employment purposes and to necessary driving for the completion of a drinking driver rehabilitation course. The court may order such a permit only if, as part of the penalty, the licensee is required to enroll in and complete a driver improvement course for the rehabilitation of drinking drivers. Such a permit may not be used for pleasure, recreational or nonessential driving. Such a permit may be issued only if the licensee has no prior conviction for driving while under the influence. The permit is valid for 45 days.

The law provides for issuance of a limited driving permit to a person whose license has been suspended following a first conviction for driving while under the influence of alcohol or drugs. The applicant for such a permit must have no prior convictions or accepted nolo contendere pleas within the previous ten years. Such a permit is issued on the basis of extreme hardship where the applicant has no other means of transportation and would thus be prevented from going to his place of employment or performing the normal duties of his occupation, receiving scheduled medical care or obtaining prescription drugs, attending a college or school where he is regularly enrolled, or attending a court ordered driver education or improvement course. The permits must contain such conditions as the department deems necessary to insure that the permit will be used only to avoid such hardship. 

The conditions may include specific places between which the permittee may drive, specific routes to be followed, times of travel, the specific vehicle permittee may drive, and any other conditions the department may require. The permits are valid for one year from the date of license suspension and are not renewable. The permits must be revoked upon conviction for violating any of the permit conditions, or upon conviction for any moving traffic violation. The applicant for such a permit must make an affidavit to the department to the effect that the court entering the conviction upon which the suspension was based has not imposed a suspension or revocation of his driver's license or privilege which would be inconsistent with the driving privileges conferred by the limited permit. The law also specifies that department employees and officials may not be held civilly or criminally liable or held in contempt of court for issuing such a limited permit in reliance upon the truth of the affidavit. 

When a license is subjected to mandatory revocation upon a conviction for felony in which a motor vehicle was used, hit and run, perjury to the department, or a third conviction of reckless driving within one year, the department may issue a temporary restricted permit upon recommendation of the court and upon a showing that the regular livelihood of the person requires operation of a motor vehicle. Illinois The laws provide that whenever a license is suspended or revoked the department may issue a restricted driving permit granting to the permit holder the privilege of driving between his residence and his place of employment, or within other proper limits, where undue hardship would result from a failure to issue such a permit. Conviction for any traffic law offense constitutes grounds for revocation or suspension of the restricted driving permit. Otherwise such permits are subject to suspension or revocation just like any other license.

Another law, which authorizes suspension or revocation upon conviction for three traffic law offenses within one year, provides that a license to drive a commercial vehicle as an occupation shall not be revoked or suspended unless five offenses were committed, at least two of which occurred while operating a commercial vehicle in connection with employment. Where under this law the normal driving privilege, but not the commercial vehicle employment driving privilege, is subject to suspension or revocation, a special permit is issued to authorize driving a commercial vehicle as part of the permit holder's regular employment. Indiana 

Whenever a person's license is suspended and the nature of his employment is such that the suspension would work an undue hardship and burden upon his family or dependents, he may apply for a restricted driving permit which will authorize driving to and from work and in the course of employment. 

No such permit may be granted where the suspension is for failure to post proof of financial responsibility when required or failure to satisfy a judgment, nor may such a permit be granted in cases of suspensions for physical, mental or emotional instability, or for convictions for vehicular - 8

homicide, nor may such a permit be granted if the person's license has been previously suspended or if he has failed to avail himself of appropriate appeal procedures. Application for such a restricted permit is made by petition to a court, and such a permit is granted only upon recommendation of the court. The permit is to set out restrictions as to time, territory and route and must be restricted to the essential requirements of the permittee in the performance of his employment duties. Violation of the permit restrictions results in revocation of the permit and extension of the period of license suspension for an additional two years. 

A second law provides that a person convicted of a first offense of driving while under the influence of alcohol or drugs not involving any death may, in the discretion of the court, be placed on probation for at least one year, the terms of probation including no further traffic law violations, and use of the driving privilege only for essential requirements of the person in the performance of his employment duties, and necessary driving to and from employment. Restrictions as to routes and hours of travel are to be specified by the court and placed on the license. Violation of the terms of probation results in suspension of the license. 

A third law provides that when a person is adjudicated a habitual traffic offender on the basis of ten or more moving traffic violations within a ten year period, and where the person establishes by a preponderance of evidence that he operates a motor vehicle for commercial or business purposes, that his mileage for such purposes is substantially in excess of the mileage of the average driver, and that such excessive mileage may have been a factor contributing to his violation record, the court may place the person on probation rather than revoking his driving privilege for a ten year period. Such probation may be for a period of three to ten years, and the person's driving privilege must be restricted to business or commercial use, including driving to and from his employment. Such probation is not available if the person's license was already suspended at the time he was adjudicated a habitual offender. Such probation is also not available if the convictions include multiple specified serious violations. - 9 -

Iowa 

When a license is suspended under the point system or otherwise on discretionary grounds, the department may, upon application by the licensee, issue a temporary restricted license to him if his regular employment is the operation of a motor vehicle or if he cannot perform his regular occupation without the use of a motor vehicle. 

Such a person is prohibited from operating a vehicle for pleasure while holding such a restricted license. No restricted license may be issued following a mandatory revocation. Kansas 

Upon conviction for driving while under the influence, vehicular homicide, failure to stop and render aid at an accident involving death or personal injury, a felony in which a motor vehicle is used, or a third conviction of reckless driving within one year, revocation of the license is required. In all these cases the court, in lieu of revoking the person's license, may order a restricted driving privilege. Such privilege must be restricted to operating a motor vehicle in going to or from the driver's place of employment or when operation of a motor vehicle is essential in performing the duties of his employment. Such a restricted privilege may be granted only upon a finding by the court that the person's continued operation of a motor vehicle under such restrictions is essential to the person's ability to maintain gainful employment upon which the person or his family is dependent for livelihood, and that such operation will not be inimical to the public safety and welfare. Such restrictions must be imposed for a specified period not to exceed one year. Where the person is convicted for driving while under the influence of alcohol or drugs the court may include in the order "such other restrictions as to the times, places, or conditions for the operation of a motor vehicle by such person as the judge deems necessary," in addition to the restrictions described above. The department is required to issue a license bearing the restrictions imposed by the court, or, in the case of a nonresident, to forward a copy of the court order to the nonresident's home state. Violation of the restrictions results in license revocation. Kentucky 

Following a first conviction for driving while under the influence, the department, upon the written recommendation of the court that the license not be revoked, may restrict the license according to such terms and conditions as the department deems necessary, provided the person has - 10 -

Maine

Maryland 

Licensees who are professional drivers (i.e., required to drive a motor vehicle in the course of regular employment) may accumulate higher point levels under the point system before being subjected to suspension or revocation. A regular licensee is called in for a conference at five points; the professional driver is not called in until he accumulates eight points. The license of a regular driver is suspended at eight points and revoked at 12 points; the license of the professional driver is suspended at 16 points and revoked at 19 points. Also, if a suspension or revocation under the point system would adversely affect the employment or opportunity for employment of a licensee, the hearing officer is authorized to not impose suspension or revocation, or to cancel or modify a suspension or revocation.

Massachusetts When a license is revoked under the habitual offender law, it may not be restored for four years, except that after one year the person may apply for and be given a hearing in order to request issuance of a new license on a limited basis on the sole ground of hardship. The department may, in its discretion, issue such a license under such terms and conditions as it deems necessary and appropriate. 

Another law specifies that some persons convicted of operating a motor vehicle while under the influence of intoxicating liquor may be placed on probation for one year and under certain circumstances may be eligible for early reinstatement of the driving privilege after 90 days. During the probationary period the convicted person is assigned to a driver alcohol education program, and, if deemed necessary by the court, to an alcohol treatment or rehabilitation program, or both. A hearing is held by the court not less than 60 nor more than 90 days after revocation of the license. If the court finds sufficient basis to conclude that the person has satisfactorily completed or is completing the program, and that early reinstatement of the license would

If the probation officer reports that the person has failed to satisfactorily comply with the court ordered program or that the person's driving constitutes a threat to public safety, the court may order the license revoked for the balance of the original one year revocation. 

Michigan When a license is suspended or revoked under the implied consent law, or when a license is denied, the aggrieved person may appeal to the court. If the court sustains the departmental action, the court may also issue a restricted license if the following circumstances exist: 1) public transportation is not readily available to such person; 2) operating his motor vehicle is the only means by which the person can travel to and from his place of employment; 3) there are no other persons within the person's household who are licensed to operate a motor vehicle in this state who could transport the person to his place of employment. 

Such a restricted license may be issued only on condition that the person operate his motor vehicle only to and from his place of employment. The court may not issue such a license to any person who has accumulated more than 24 points under the point system in the preceding two-year period, however. Minnesota 

When a license has been suspended or revoked and the licensee's livelihood depends upon the use of his driver's license, the department may, and shall if a court so recommends, issue a limited license. However, no such license may be issued following a suspension of revocation under the implied consent law to any licensee who has been subjected to a prior revocation within the last three years for driving while under the influence or for failing to submit to a chemical test under the implied consent law. The department may impose such limits on the license as it deems necessary in the interests of public safety and welfare, including limitations to operation of particular vehicles, to particular classes and times of operation, and to particular conditions of traffic. Missouri 

When a license is suspended or revoked the aggrieved person may apply to a court for "hardship driving privileges." If the court finds that a person is required to operate a motor vehicle in connection with his business, occupation or employment, and that the person will otherwise suffer undue hardship in earning a livelihood, the court may grant such limited driving privilege as the circumstances of the case justify. Any moving violation conviction (except for a municipal stop sign violation not resulting in an accident) which results in point assessment under the point system terminates the hardship driving privilege. A hardship driving privilege may not be granted if the revocation or suspension is based upon a conviction for using a motor vehicle in the commission of a felony, leaving the scene of an accident, or driving while under the influence of drugs, or upon a second conviction for driving while under the influence of alcohol. Nor may such a privilege be granted to anyone who, disregarding the existing suspension or revocation, would still be ineligible on statutory grounds to obtain a license, nor to anyone who during the preceding five-year period had either held a hardship driving privilege or had more than once refused to consent to a chemical test under the implied consent law.

The applicant must certify that he will attend and complete within 60 days or that he has already completed a driver improvement course presented by the department. If he fails to complete the course he must surrender the permit.

A second law provides that upon a first conviction for driving while under the influence the court must order the defendant to not drive for a period of six months. New York 

The law provides for issuance of a "restricted use license" to a person whose driver's license has been suspended or revoked and for whom a license is a necessary incident to his employment, business, trade, occupation or profession. Application for such a license must be made within 60 days of suspension or revocation. Issuance of such a license is in the discretion of the department, and the applicant may be required to attend a driver rehabilitation program as a condition of issuance of such a license. The license may be issued only after it is established to the reasonable satisfaction of the department that a driver's license is a necessary incident to employment, and that denial would deprive the person of his usual means of livelihood and thereby constitute an unwarranted and substantial financial hardship on the applicant and his immediate family. Such a license is valid for six months or for the period of suspension or revocation, whichever is less. The license is renewable if necessary. 

It is valid only during the time the licensee is actually pursuing or while commuting to or from his employment. 

On conviction for any moving violation or for violating the license restrictions the license must be revoked, and the person is ineligible to receive another "restricted use license" for ten years. A "restricted use license" may not be issued to any person who within the 10 years preceding application for such a license has been convicted of specified serious offenses, including homicide or assault by vehicle, leaving the scene of an accident, reckless driving, and driving while under the influence or impaired by alcohol or drugs, or whose license has been revoked for refusing to submit to a chemical test under the implied consent law.

A second law provides for the licensing of certain persons who participate in alcohol and drug rehabilitation programs. The law limits participation to those persons convicted of alcohol or drug-related traffic offenses who choose to participate and who satisfy the criteria for participation, provided that a judge imposing sentence may prohibit such participation, and the department can reject any participant. Also a person can participate only once in a five year period. The law provides that a participant, in the discretion of the department, may be issued a "conditional driver's license." Such a license is valid only while enroute to and from the licensee's place of employment, or,

if his employment requires operation of a vehicle, then during employment hours, or while enroute to and from a class or activity which is part of the rehabilitation program, or while enroute to or from a class at a school or college, or during a three hour daytime period chosen by the program administrators on a day during which the licensee does not work at his usual vocation. Such a license is valid during the term of the suspension or revocation, but may be revoked for "sufficient cause, including failure to satisfactorily participate in the program, conviction for any traffic violation, or upon a finding at a hearing that the participant is not attempting in good faith to accept rehabilitation. The law further provides that upon successful completion of the program a participant may apply for, and in the discretion of the department may be granted, a termination of the original order of suspension or revocation. 

North Carolina

Conceptual Approaches to Hardship Licensing 

Why do we issue hardship licenses? Is such a license available to any person who suffers a suspension or revocation, or is something more required to justify issuance of a hardship license? 

The laws of 38 jurisdictions discussed in this Commentary incorporate at least four conceptual approaches to hardship licensing. 

The approaches differ in terms of the basic justification for issuance of the license. This portion of the Commentary will examine these different conceptual approaches more closely. 

It should be noted that some of the states have more than one hardship licensing law. In some of these cases the conceptual approach of one law differs from the approach of another law of the same state. Thus in the following discussion some states are covered more than once. States with multiple laws will be identified in this section of the Commentary by an asterisk (*) next to the name of the state.10/ This will warn the reader that the state has more than one law covered in this portion of the Commentary.

Restricted Approach

A second hardship licensing concept is similar to the first. 

As under the first approach, the hardship license is generally restricted so that only that minimum driving which would be necessary to retain employment or prevent other hardship is authorized. 

Unlike the first approach, however, the licensee is not required to show that undue hardship will actually result from loss of his license. Under this approach a person would be eligible for a hardship license even if total loss of his license would not cause loss of his livelihood or other unusual hardship. The pre- mise of these laws appears to be that hardship will inevitably result from deprivation of the driving privilege, and that a limited license should be allowed with suitable restrictions which will allow only such driving as is necessary to alleviate the undue hardship. OBSERVATIONS 

The Pros and Cons of Hardship Licensing The desirability of hardship licensing has been much debated. 

Before offering our own recommendations and general observations, it may be desirable to review some of the arguments which can be made on each side of the issue.

Arguments Favoring the Concept 

1. Complete withdrawal of the driving privilege can deprive the person of his livelihood. Where the licensee's normal livelihood requires driving a motor vehicle, or where driving is the only reasonable way to get to and from the place of employment, a termination of the driving privilege will result in a termination of employment or ability to earn a livelihood. The adverse effect 

- 50 -

of such a termination of livelihood is disproportionate to any benefit obtainable by totally withdrawing the driving privilege. 

Loss of livelihood may increase the person's problems, force him to accept welfare in lieu of performing a productive role in society, and adversely impact his innocent family. 2. 

There is no justification for imposing such unnecessary hardship when it is possible to restrict the driver's license in such a way that excessive hardship can be avoided, while still sufficiently curtailing the licensee's enjoyment of the driving privilege to accomplish the desirable goals of the license sus pension or revocation. 

3. 

Complete deprivation of the driving privilege serves no desirable driver licensing goals. It is only a punitive measure. 

We already have adequate punitive measures (penalties such as fines or imprisonment) to deal with traffic law violators. Taking away a man's job is neither an appropriate nor desirable punishment for traffic offenses. 

4. Total withdrawal of the driving privilege leaves the driver with no incentive for improvement. He may continue to drive anyway, taking his chances on being detected because, after all, things can't get much worse for him than they already are. Issuing a restricted license during the suspension/revocation period gives the driver incentive. He is interested in complying with the law and with his license restrictions in order to avoid further trouble and to retain his limited permit. 

Arguments Opposing the Concept 

1. Of course complete withdrawal of the driving privilege causes hardship. It is intended to do so. A person who values his driving privilege and wants to avoid such hardship should control his driving conduct accordingly. 

2. We need to be more concerned about the hardship imposed upon the innocent traffic accident victims and their families. 

Not infrequently they suffer a good deal more hardship than loss of livelihood. We need to keep in mind that it is not the suspension or revocation of the driver's license which causes the hardship but rather his own driving conduct. His hardship, unlike that of his victims, is self imposed. 

· 51 · 

---

3. Hardship licensing is a product of the one-last-chance syndrome. Why do we continue to believe that the driver who has not in the past modified his driving conduct so as to avoid the loss of his license, will suddenly do so in the future if he is just given one last chance? 

4. We do not suspend and revoke licenses in order to be punitive. Rather the goal is to remove unsafe drivers from the roads, and to serve as a deterrent to other drivers. If a driver is unfit or unsafe, he should be removed from the road. It is just as unsafe for him to drive to work as it is to drive elsewhere. Most of the deterrent value of license withdrawal is lost if drivers believe they can obtain a hardship license for their really essential driving. Hardship licensing undermines the beneficial aspects of license suspensions and revocations. 

5. The restrictions which are generally imposed on a hardship license are virtually unenforceable, and invite the licensee to flout them. A police officer cannot know what restrictions as to route, time of day, and purpose for travel are imprinted on the license in the driver's pocket unless he stops the driver and inspects the license, which he is unlikely to do unless he witnesses some violation. Once the officer has determined that the license is restricted, how does he determine whether the driver is in compliance? How does the driver prove the purpose of his travel? If the officer does not believe that the licensee is on his way home from work, how does he challenge the driver on that point, and how do they finally resolve the question? Restrictions of this nature leave much room for abuse both by the licensee and by the enforcement officer. Recommendations

Rehabilitative Licensing 

In

Although avoidance of unnecessary hardship is desirable, it should not be a primary consideration in driver licensing. many of the existing laws reviewed in this Commentary, hardship is the sole consideration. If hardship can be shown, a license must be granted to alleviate the hardship. More consideration should be given to whether the person is qualified and whether his continued driving will constitute an unreasonable risk to public safety. No driver who would otherwise be subject to a license suspension or revocation should have his privilege restored solely on the basis of hardship. 

52

The primary consideration in driver licensing should be on driver improvement and necessary rehabilitation. Through driver licensing we should strive to promote the safest possible driving environment by continually working to improve driver performance. 

A positive incentive for driver improvement and rehabilitation should always be provided.

Consistent with the above, the extension of a limited driving privilege to a person whose license has been suspended or revoked may be desirable as a positive incentive under circumstances where that person has taken effective steps to correct his problem and to improve his driving performance, and where an evaluation of his progress indicates that limited driving can be permitted without unreasonable risk to the public.

In determining when and how to restore the driving privilege which has been withdrawn on the basis of past poor driving performance, we should strive to balance the need for positive incentives to driver improvement with the need to protect the public from persons who lack the required skill, competence, or attitude for safe driving.

That balance is not well struck by hardship licensing where hardship is the sole consideration, as it is in many of the laws discussed in this Commentary. The balance does seem to be served by some of the laws described in the Commentary. These laws, which we have referred to as rehabilitative licensing laws, allow restoration of a driving privilege only when the person has made satisfactory progress in a program of driver improvement or rehabilitation, or they require him to make such progress in order to retain the privilege. 

Who Should Do It?

Controversy over the appropriate role of the judiciary in revoking and suspending drivers' licenses has raged for years.

Some of the hardship licensing laws described in this Commentary add new fuel to that controversy by allowing the courts to actually issue a hardship license, or to exercise discretion in deciding whether or not it should be issued. Those laws which allow the courts to issue a hardship license independently of the department pose some particular problems which deserve discussion. 

53 -

The most significant problem is the potential for confusion due to absence of communication. Department records, for example, might show the licensee as suspended, but unknown to the department he is driving under authority of a court issued license. 

Or the department records might show the licensee being licensed without restriction, but unknown to the department his license has been restricted by a court order. Those restrictions will have little impact outside the jurisdiction of the court that imposed them, particularly if the licensee obtains an unrestricted duplicate license from the department, which he can easily do by claiming loss of the original. The right hand doesn't know what the left hand is doing, and vice versa. We tolerated those kinds of communication problems in driver licensing for years because of inadequate information retrieval and processing systems. that we are able to deal with those problems using improved technology, it is inconceivable that we would intentionally build those same problems back into the system by giving separate agencies independent authority to issue and withdraw licenses. Yet that is just what some of these laws do. 

The second problem involves the format of a court-issued "license." Just how effectively will a court order serve the identification functions of a driver's license? Will it bear the licensee's photo and his description? Will it be in a standard format which is readily recognized as a driver's license and which is difficult to alter or counterfeit? Will it sufficiently identify the driver to connect him with his driver licensing records at the department of motor vehicles? Will it fit easily into a billfold and survive there, in legible condition, for several months of use?

Of course only a very few of the laws discussed in this Commentary appear to contemplate a license issued solely by the courts without even giving notice to the department. Most of the laws which allow the courts to issue a license do so with some kind of involvement of the department. So the problems discussed above do not exist, or are only temporary, under most of the laws.

The more basic issue is the proper role of the courts in driver licensing. The best interests of driver licensing are served when licenses are issued, and where necessary, cancelled, suspended, or revoked by ce agency and only one agency. That agency should be an administrative agency such as a department of motor vehicles. We believe that driver licensing is an administrative (executive) function, rather than a judicial function. 

54

The courts should have no primary role in the issuance or withdrawal of drivers' licenses. The only proper role for the courts in driver licensing is in review of the final actions of the administrative agency. Such judicial review is provided for under the Uniform Vehicle Code.217 

Although it is beyond the scope of this Commentary to review the constitutions of the various states which have laws which give the courts some authority to issue or withdraw drivers' licenses, it should be noted that many state constitutions utilize the same kind of separation of powers structure which is found in the United States Constitution. Judicial involvement in the administrative aspects of driver licensing may violate the separation of powers concept. 

The Uniform Vehicle Code Approach 

UVC § 11-902.2 provides an excellent example of the kind of rehabilitative licensing program which should be encouraged, although the Code section applies only to persons who are convicted of driving while under the influence and who are determined to be 

The Code sec in need of treatment for alcohol or drug abuse.22/ tion authorizes a court, after the conviction, to order the person to submit to treatment. The court takes no discretionary action with respect to the person's license. Under UVC § 6-204 the court must require the person to surrender his license because he has been convicted of an offense (driving while under the influence) for which license revocation is mandatory under UVC § 6-205. The court must then forward the surrendered license, together with a record of the conviction, to the department. The department must revoke the license as required under UVC § 6-205(2). The period of revocation is one year as specified under UVC § 6-208(a). 

After the person has made progress in the court ordered treatment program, he may apply to the department for restoration of his driving privilege. His application, and a report of his progress in the treatment program, must be considered by the department's medical advisory board, appointed under UVC § 6-118. These medical experts are best able to evaluate his progress in the alcohol or drug abuse treatment program. After receiving the advice of the medical advisory board, the department, in its discretion, may issue a license to the person with conditions and restrictions which are consistent with the person's rehabilitation, and with the need to protect the public. Hardship is not a factor for consideration, although the restoration of a limited privilege will undoubtedly alleviate some of the licensee's hardship. The license may be - 55

issued only after satisfactory progress has been made. Thus it serves as an incentive to strive for such progress. 

The person who does not make progress will have to wait at least one year before he can hope to be relicensed. Even at that point he will have to apply for a new license and the department may deny it if he is still an active alcohol or drug abuser. No limited license may be issued under the Code provision without first evaluating the person's capabilities and balancing them with the need for public protection. Dangerously incapacitated drivers are not returned to the streets, even if they suffer great hardship from their loss of driving privileges. Before a driver's privilege may be restored, he must show that he is addressing his problem in a constructive way, and that his driving will not constitute an unreasonable risk to public safety. Most of the laws discussed in this Commentary do not provide this kind of incentive for driver improvement, and do not place such stress on the need to protect public safety; they only look at the hardship suffered by the person who, through his own driving misconduct, has lost his driving privilege. 1/
