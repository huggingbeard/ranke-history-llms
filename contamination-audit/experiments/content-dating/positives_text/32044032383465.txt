3/26/4 The Good Neighbor Policy, enunciated by President Roosevelt in his first inaugural address, has continued to determine the attitude and actions of the United States toward the other American republics. Public 

Affairs Bulletins nos. 7 and 11 traced the development of this policy from 

1933 to the meeting of American Foreign Ministers at Rio de Janeiro in January, 1942. It is the aim of this Bulletin to present a picture of its development and application from that date through the San Francisco Conference held in the spring of 1945. During the early years of the Good Neighbor 

Policy, comments were practically unanimous in its praise. However, as time went on, various adverse criticisms by both Latin Americans and North Americans appeared. A selection from these is included in this Bulletin along with the predominantly favorable comments. 

Since the United States was at war with the Axis countries during all this period, furtherance of the war effort overshadowed all other activi- Conferences, economic agreements, etc., all tended toward that end, but resulted also in strengthening the bonds of friendship and commerce between the Latin American nations and their neighbor to the North. The elevation of all remaining United States and Latin American Legations in the various American republics to the rank of Embassy gave formal recognition to tho democratic principle of juridical equality that governs the relations between the American nations. ties.

Application of the Good Neighbor Policy 

A. Committees and Conferences Committees

The several Meetings of Consultation of the Foreign Ministers created four organizations to study various problems arising from the war; the Inter- 

American Juridical Committee, the Inter-American Financial and Economic Advis ory Committee, the Inter-American Defense Board and the Emergency Advisory Committee for Political Defense. 

These Committees have proved to be of great value in their respective fields, supplementing the permanent secretariat of the Pan American Union. "... Through this common medium, the recommendations of these Committees provide the American Governments with a constant source of valuable advice, the fruit of sound and thorough research conducted by specialists in their respective fields. The results of these studios and recommendations are contributing very effectively to the promotion of mutual understanding and friendly cooperation; they are making it possible for the American Governments to arrive at uniform points of view and to adopt uniform policies to meet the common problems that confront them." 

Manuel S. Canyes, Bulletin of the Pan 

Imerican Union, May 1943, p. 268, 273–274.

The Inter-American Juridical Committee 

A resolution of the Rio Conference provided that the Neutrality Committee created at the Panama meeting in 1939 should be reorganized under the name of the Inter-American Juridical Committee, to meet the new conditions created by the war. This Committee consists of seven members, designated respectively by Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Costa Rica, Mexico, Venezuela, and the United

States. The members may have no other duties than those pertaining to the committee. The functions assigned to it by the Rio meeting of Foreign Ministers may be classified under 4 heads: Juridical problems arising out of the present war; postwar problems, the development and coordination of the work of the codification of international law; the coordination of the resolutions of consultative meetings of Foreign Ministers. The Committee has devoted its time to problems which appeared to be the most urgent and has had to subordinate research work to issues of more immediate importance. "Looking at the activities of the Juridical Committee as a whole, perhaps the most important problem before the Committee is the coordination of inter-American and international relations... The time has now come to consider what modifications of inter-American organization and law will be necessary and desirable in consequence of the establishment of the international organization contemplated by the United Nations. At the same time the Juridical Committee is giving close attention to the reorganization of the inter- American system itself, with the object of improving the machinery of con-

Inter-American Financial and Economic Advisory Committee 

This Committee was established by the Pan American Union in accordan ce with a resolution of the Panama Meeting of American Foreign Ministers in 1939. 

Its importance in meeting the problems of economic dislocation caused by the war, was recognized by the Foreign Ministers meeting at Rio in 1942, who assigned specific additional tasks to the Committee, which in the three years of its existence has demonstrated its effectiveness as an instrumentality for the protection of the inter-American economic structure. Among other things it summoned a conference of representatives of Central Banks or similar institutions, called the Inter-American Conference on Systems of Economic and Financial Control, which was held in Washington, June 30-July 12, 1942, in accordance with Rio recommendations. Its purpose was to prevent the nations and persons threatening the Western Hemisphere from carrying on any operations in the Americas, by exercising effective control over commercial and financial transactions. The Conference resulted in important understandings to this end. 

Department of State Bulletin, March 27, 1943, p. 260-263. "The Inter-American Conference on Systems of Economic and Financial Control worked in an atmosphere of loyalty and cooperation. Not even the most trifling or insignificant incident sprang up among the delegations. All the recommendations were adopted unanimously. The different footing on which the delegates met... caused no diversity of judgment in approving recommendations referring to common defense, because that defense arises from a uniform belief in the dependence of the Western Hemisphere in order that every citizen may live independently and be fully aware of his honor... All the American Republics can point with pride to that fraternal spirit which inspires them and which they proved at the recent Conference. It can truthfully be said that the Pan American Union has reached maturity. 11 

Manuel C. Gallagher, Delegate of Peru, Bulletin of the Pan American Union, September 1942, p. 482-483.

Inter-American Defense Board 

The Inter-American Defense Board is an autonomous organization under the auspices of the Pan American Union. It was established in accordance with 

Resolution 39 of the Rio de Janeiro Meeting which recommended the "immediate meeting in Washington of a commission composed of military and naval technicians appointed by each of the Governments to study and to recommend to them the measures necessary for the defense of the continent." Its first

-- 
- meeting was held on March 30, 1942. It meets regularly in the city of 

Washington. The Inter-American Conference on Problems of War and Peace at 

Mexico City recommended that this Board continue as an agency of inter-American defense, until the establishment of a permanent body as provided for in the same recommendation. 

Bulletin of the Pan American Union, June 1942, p. 335. 

Emergency Advisory Committee for Political Defense 

The Governments represented at the Rio meeting agreed upon a politicaldefense policy for the individual and collective defense of the continent and created a committee to study and recommend measures and plans for the protection of their security and solidarity against hostile acts of political warfare. "Of the various inter-American agencies established prior to and during the war to advise the governments on emergency problems, perhaps the 

Emergency Advisory Committee on Political Defense represents the most radical departure from past experience and tradition. Although none of its characteristics can be said to be entirely new, their combination in and application by the Committee are in many respects quite novel. The means and manner by which the Committee has carried out the task with which it was charged appear to be of as much interest as its actual accomplishments, which themselves have constituted, during the nearly three years since its creation, a vital contribution to the defense of the Americas. "The Committee is as much an innovation in inter-American relations as is the consultative procedure of which it is a product and an extension. The procedure and its offspring reflect a maturing pan-Americanism. Both have the same basic premise: namely, that inter-American solidarity is a fact; that pan-Americanism has progressed from the realm of inspiration, through the intermediary stage of an inorganic system of principles and techniques of international cooperation, to the point whereby full use can be made of emergency instrumentalities for joint political decision and action." 

 January 7, 1945, 

This Committee is composed of seven members, designated by the Governing Board of the Pan American Union, after consulting the governments of the American republics. These members act as individuals and represent all the 

American States, becoming the first inter-American body expressly required to represent the entire community. The Committee held its first meeting at its permanent headquarters in Montevideo, Uruguay, on April 15, 1942. It has a permanent secretariat appointed by the Government of Uruguay and subcommittees to study various phases of Axis subversive activities and draft proposals for consideration by the whole Committee. 

Dr. Carl Spaeth, U. S. member of the Committee, at a meeting of consul tation in Washington in the summer of 1943, said: "Since the governments of . the 21 American ropublics are represented in the Committee on a plane of

10 absolute equality, they have been provided with equal means and facilities through which to put forward their proposals as to the best manner of carrying out the principles of political defense which were agreed upon by the Foreign Ministers. Both the internal organization of the Committee and the instrumentalities of communication between the Committee and the governments have been planned and developed to insure such participation by all of the republics. Through liasion officers appointed by each of the republics to maintain continuous contact with the Committee, through national committees for political défense, through regional meetings of national officials and finally, and most importantly, through the consultative visits, the Committee has been able to take into account the institutional, legal, and factual conditions of the individual countries."

Inter-American Congress on Social Planning "The Inter-American Congress on Social Planning was held at Santiago, Chile, from September 10 to September 16, 1942 at the invitation of the Chilean Government. The meeting was called in accordance with resolutions of the Inter-American Committee on Social Security established at Lima, Peru, in December 1940 by a group of persons who were guests of the Peruvian Government at the dedication of the Bank of Social Security Funds and the opening of the Workers' Hospital. The Committee was organized to make possible a systematic and continuous exchange of information among the social-security institutions of the American republics, and it was planned that it would cooperate with the International Labor Office in attaining its objectives... "The principal results of the Congress were (1) the adoption of a declaration consisting of 16 resolutions concerning the extension and development of social security; and (2) the adoption of a resolution providing permanent statutes for the 'Inter-American Conference on Social Security'. Conference of Ministers and Directors of Education of the American Republics. 

The first Conference was held in Panama City, Panama, September 27, to October 4, 1943 and was attended by representatives of the 21 republics. 

More than 50 resolutions were passed, covering a wide range of subjects of great interest to American educators and stating the fundamental principals of education in America. One of the principal subjects before the Conference was the establishment of an Inter-American University, which had been proposed many years before and endorsed by the Governing Board of the Pan American Union in March 1943. A Charter for the University was approved by the Conference, in which it was defined as "a community of culture at the service of the American nations" that will work to sustain the "principles of democracy and of the freedom of teaching and research." A ten-year convention for the organization of the University was signed, subject to ratification by the American governments. .. 

Bulletin of the Pan American Union, January 1944, p. 13-15.

"The recommendations embrace many subjects connected with enforcement work. Included was a suggestion that political objectives be excluded from police organizations. Others advocated greater development of criminal and civil identification systems; regular exchange of information between police organizations of various nations; establishment of adequate training programs; and simplification of extradition proceedings without infringing on constitutional rights."

Part II B. Economic Policies, Trade Agreements, etc. 

"The plan for the mobilization of resources which has taken place in this hemisphere during the war years was blueprinted by the Third Meeting of American Foreign Ministers which convened at Rio de Janeiro a few weeks after Pearl Harbor and formulated a program for full participation of the American republics in the war. At the Inter-American Conference on Problems of Peace and war recently concluded at Mexico City, the nations of the Western Hemisphere culminated their common wartime determination to protect their freedom by adopting far-reaching measures to continue participation in the United Nations' war effort and to ease the transition from a war to peacetime economy... "The collective voice of the 260,000,000 people of the Western Hemisphere spoke clearly and boldly at the Rio meeting, and immediately following it the people of each individual republic acted to translate the agreements into action. As a result of this cooperative action, the 

United Nations have had the use of military, naval and air bases throughout the hemisphere which have enormously simplified problems of wartime strategy. Hemisphere ports have been thrown open to United Nations ships carrying men and supplies to war fronts... Other republics have set up coastal defenses and troop concentrations at strategic points. Twenty hemisphere countries have now declared war on the Axis. "In addition to these many essential factors of military cooperation, there is another aspect, perhaps not so well known, but equally significant and dramatic. How the economic might of the Americas has been mobilized since Pearl Harbor is a story which has sometimes been obscured behind more spectacular war news. During these critical years, the development of raw materials to supply United States industry has proceeded in the other Americas at a rate unmatched previously. "United States war industry has turned chiefly to the other Americas for replacement of tropically grown materials formerly imported from the Far East-such essentials as manila hemp, rubber, quinine, vegetable oils. 

Bauxit for aluminum, manganese, chrome, nickel and other alloys used for armor plate, mahogany and hardwoods for the hulls of PT boats, quartz crystals and tungsten for radio communications, industrial diamonds for cutting tools, nitrates and mercury for manufacturing explosives- for these and many other critical materiais the other republics have been our chief source in recent years... "The Good Neighbor policy has become a powerful force in this hemisphere, deeply rooted in popular support. It is to the interest of all the Americas to let this force continue toward the great goals of the future- toward the solution of common problems in the field of industrial development, health and sanitation, food supply and toward achieving full employment after the war. Peace and security against aggression, expanding markets and trade, new industries and rising standards of living for all

- the peoples of the Americas- these are the long goals of inter-American cooperation." 

Wallace K. Harrison, Director, Office of

Inter-American Affairs. The Journal of Commerce, N. Y. April 16, 1945, p. 2A, 5A.

Inter-American Development Conference 

The Inter-American Development Commission was created on January 15, 1940 and before the end of 1941, National Inter-American Development Commissions were established in all the Latin American republics. These 

Commissions, made up of representatives of finance, industry, agriculture, mining, transportation and government, serve as channels of cooperation between business and government in the development of the hemisphere's resources for the welfare of the American peoples. The Rio de Janeiro 

Conference specifically charged these Commissions with the formulation of plans for the utilization of natural resources and the improvement of industrial economics in order to raise the standard of living of the nations. The first joint besting of these Commissions was held in New 

York, May 9-18, 1944. The recommendations of the Conference plan for the future welfare of the republics of the Western Hemisphere, but they also recognize the role of these countries in the rehabilitation of Europe. 

In general, discussion concerned the world-wide distribution of surplus production; improved standards of living; banking and exchange; transportation and communications. In a summary of the accomplishments of the Conference Mr. Nelson A. Rockefeller referred to the recommendations as guides and incentives "for an economic constitution within which we can all grow and expand and find greater wealth and happiness for all the peoples of the world." 

Julian G. Zier, Bulletin of the Pan 

American Union, July 1944, p. 382-385.

Inter-American Institute of Agricultural Sciences. 

A convention establishing the Inter-American Institute of Agricultural Sciences was approved on December 15, 1943, by the Governing Board of the Pan American Union, which had been incorporated in Washington, June 18, 1942, where its executive headquarters are located. Field headquarters are to be at Turrialba, Costa Rica. Provisions have been made also for regional offices in the various republics. The purpose is "to encourage and advance the development of agricultural sciences in the American republics through research, teaching, and extension activities in the theory and practice of agriculture and related arts and sciences." 

There will be many advantages to the United States in supporting this Institute. A basic aim of the Institute is a rise in the standard of living of the other American republics, which should increase both in amount and diversity their demands for the products of the United States. 

Research in agricultural diseases conducted by the Institute should also be of benefit by removing the menace of their spread to the United States. 

An opportunity is also provided to the United States through participation in the Institute to demonstrate its interest in the other nations of the hemisphere.

Edgar S. Furniss, Jr. Department of

Hemisphere Defense and Declarations of War 

Cooperation in the defense of the hemisphere has been the motivating force during the war years of all Inter-American conferences and agreements such as those described in previous sections of this report. Besides these, such agencies as the Joint Brazil-United States 

Defense Commission and the Joint Mexican-United States Defense Commission, each composed of military delegates from the two countries concerned, were created in 1942 to make plans for the common defense of the hemisphere. 

The United States also made agreements for military collaboration, as for example with Cuba, whereby the Cuban Government offered facilities to the United States War Department for training aviation personnel and for operations against enemy underseacraft. It was understood that after the termination of the emergency the facilities would become a training center for the Cuban Air Force. Military and naval officers or missions were sent to the various countries at the request of the government concerned.

Declarations of War.. 

A group of the Latin American nations declared war on the Axis.. soon after Pearl Harbor and most of the others severed diplomatic relations or declared war within a few months. Argentina severed. diplomatic relations with Germany, and Japan in January 1944, but did not declare war on them until March 27, 1945, after the Mexico City conference. When the war with Germany ended, every Latin American country had declared war on Germany or a state of belligerency, except Ecuador which had broken diplomatic relations with her on January 29, 1942. In July, 1945, Colombia and the Dominican Republic were the only states which had not declared war with Japan, though both had broken diplomatic relations early in the war.

Part II D. Cultural Relations 

Development of cultural relations between the United States and the Latin American nations has been wide-spread and varied. The following notes give an indication of the nature and extent of these relations. 

Inter-American Centers "Across the country there are at present sixteen regional Inter- American Centers and numerous sub-centers. They are clearing houses, services bureaus, and sources of information for individuals and organizations who can contribute to inter-American understanding and cooperation. 

They are private, non-profit bodies, dependent upon private contributions for their support, their aim is to encourage, strengthen and assist the innumerable local and regional groups whose programs include the promotion of inter-American cooperation. They undertake programs of their own only when that is clearly the most effective way of procuring the cooperation of the largest number of groups and individuals..." 

These Centers were devised by the Office of the Coordinator of Inter- American Affairs. The program was approved by the State Department on 

November 3, 1941 and from that time until the end of 1942 existing organizations were encouraged to expand their Inter-American activities and financial assistance was granted to them for this purpose. After 1942 a new policy was adopted and the formation of new Centers was encouraged. "Business, civic, and educational leaders alike have agreed that a primary function of the Centers must be to increase popular understanding regarding Latin America in order that there may be a more interested and informed public opinion, ready to uphold wise national policies toward the countries that are our neighbors... "Thus far the popular educational work of the Centers has been chiefly of a general character. In the future, however, many Centers are expected to devote additional attention to activites demonstrating the importance of inter-American trade to full employment in the United States; the value of imports; the importance of increasing the purchasing power and raising the standard of living in the other American nations; and the reasons why the development of industries in Latin America not only enhances the well-being of our neighbors but also gains long-term advantages for this country irrespective of temporary dislocations and necessary readjustments."

These Centers also provide hospitality and facilities for visitors from Central and South America brought to this country by the Coordinator and the State Department. The Centers hold conferences for economic leaders; are sponsoring or planing courses to train business men to deal intelligently with their problems in the other American republics. 

American citizens residing in Latin America state that the work of the Centers carries great weight in convincing Latin Americans that the Good

Cultural-Cooperation Program of the Department of State 

In a speech at Loyola University Forum, New Orleans, May 8, 19449 

Assistant Secretary of State Howland Shaw, said: "We are no less interested in centers of American culture abroad. 

Not long ago there came over my desk in the Department of State the annual report of the Benjamin Franklin Library at Mexico City. This library was set up on a modest scale in 1942 with funds from the United States Government. It now has 8,000 volumes concerning the 

United States, of which over 1,000 were donated by private citizens during the past year, Nearly 60,000 loans of books were made in 1943. Interestingly enough, about half the readers were Mexican children who crowded into the small reading-room specially reserved for them, and overflowed into the halls. The auditorium in the library is used for weekly motion pictures on the United States and for lectures by visiting Americans. English classes, also given in the building, showed a remarkable growth last year. Whereas in

E. Ideological Ties 

In general, the American republics have agreed among themselves in upholding the principles of the sanctity of treaties, sovereign equality, human rights and the dignity of man; in expressing faith in the democratic process and in condemning fascism, war and the use of force. At the Mexico City Conference the American Foreign Ministers reaffirmed their solidarity in adherence to these ideals. In the preamble to the resolution designated the "Act of Chapultepec", it was declared that the American states have been incorporating in their international law, since 1890, by means of conventions, resolutions and declarations, the following principles: The proscription of territorial' conquest and the non-recognition of all acquisitions made by force; the condemnation of intervention by one State in the internal or external affairs of another; the adoption of conciliation, unrestricted arbitration, or the application of international justice, in the solution of any dispute between American nations; the recognition that respect for the personality, sovereignty and independence of each American State constitutes the essence of international order sustained by continental solidarity; and the affirmation that respect for an the faithful observance of treaties constitutes the indispensable rule for the development of peaceful relations between States, and that treaties can only be revised by agreement of the contracting parties. 1. 2. 3.

Part III The Recent Conferences. 

A. 

The Mexico City Conference.

The Mexico City Conference was precipitated by Argentina's request for a hearing on her foreign policy. Tension in inter-American relationships had been developing for some time, so that a conference was long overdue. Its results may be summarized as follows:

The Act of Chapultepec obligates the American states for the duration of the war to consult together whenever an act of aggression by an American or other State is committed or appears about to be committed and to take action against that aggressor if his actions are hampering the war effort. Action against such aggressor is to be taken in accordance with the constitutional provisions of the individual countries and a treaty is to be drafted and submitted to each country extending the guarantee after the war.

Argentina. The Conference deplored the fact that Argentina had not found it possible to take the steps which would permit participation in the conference. It reaffirmed its belief that the unity of the Nations of America is indivisible and declared that complete solidarity and a common policy are essential for the security and peace of the continent. 

It expressed the hope that Argentina will cooperate with the other American nations by identifying itself with their common policy and by orienting its own policy until it achieves its incorporation into the United Nations as a signatory of the joint declarations drawn by them. The final act of the conference is open to the adhesion of Argentina, provided that it be in accord with the criteria of this resolution. 

Reorganization, consolidation and strengthening of the Inter-American system. The International Conference of American States shall meet ordinarily every four years and the Foreign Ministers every year at the call of the Governing Board of the Pan American Union. This Governing 

Board shall be composed of one delegate from each Republic, with the rank of Ambassador, but shall not be part of the diplomatic mission accredited to the Government in whose territory is located the seat of the Pan American Union. Additional functions are assigned to this Board. 

Economic Charter of the Americas. The American Republics declare their firm purpose to collaborate in a program for the attainment of the following: the continuation of mobilization of their economic resources until total victory; the orderly transition of their economic life from war to peace-time conditions; and a constructive basis for their sound economic development. The ten principles listed, which will govern their policy, include equality of access on equal terms to the trade and raw materials of the world; elimination of excesses of economic nationalism; restriction of private business arrangements that obstruct international trade; just and equitable treatment for foreign enterprises and capital, and the promotion of the system of private enterprise.

Dumbarton Oaks and San Francisco. The Dumbarton Oaks proposals were endorsed as a basis of future negotiations. The Conference resolved to transmit to the nations invited to the San Francisco Conference the reports containing the views, comments and suggestions of the American republics concerning the Dumbarton Oaks proposals. 

Other resolutions related to a permanent military agency, war crimes and elimination of subversive activities, control of armaments, free access to information, social principles, Atlantic Charter, racial or religious discrimination, equal rights for women, etc. The Declaration of Mexico reaffirms the principles governing the relations between American states.

Part III B. The San Francisco. Conference 

One of the most important problems confronting the San Francisco Conference was the position of such regional organizations as the Pan American Union in relation to the world organization. This question dominated discussions during the first three weeks of the Conference. 

It was finally entrusted to a committee of the Commission dealing with the Security Council. While some believed that the inter-American system, or specifically the Act of Chapultepec, should be allowed to function independently of the World Security Council, others thought that such an exemption would break down the whole concept of maintaining peace on a world basis. A compromise plan was reached which amalgamated about sixteen proposals, including the Latin American viewpoint. This plan is embodied in Articles 33, 51, 52, 53, 54 of the United Nations Charter.

From the principles stated therein, the following conclusions can be drawn: "1. Upon Regional Agencies or Arrangements rests the primary responsibility to seek a pacific settlement of disputes before they are referred to the Security Council. In fact, the Security Council is expected to encourage the solution of local disputes through regional arrangements or by regional agencies, either on the initiative of the States concerned or by reference from the Council. "2. The foregoing provision, however, is subject to two limitations: (a) The Security Council may investigate any dispute, or any situation which may lead to international friction or give rise to a dispute: and (b) any State, whether member of the International Organization or not, may bring any such dispute or situation to the attention of the General Assembly or of the Security Council. The jurisdiction of the Council within these limitations, however, has been construed to extend only to the investigation of a question, and not to the replacement or duplication of the efforts of the regional agency in seeking a peaceful settlement. "3. The right of any group of nations to enter into agreements for self-defense is recognized. Consequently the Act of Chapultepec, or the treaty that may be concluded to convert this wartime measure into a peacetime agreement, is entirely in harmony with the World Charter. 

14. Should any nation party to such a defense agreement be attacked, the other contracting States may carry out their obligations to join in its defense, as an emergency measure and until the Security Council has taken the measures necessary to maintain international peace and security. 

115. The Security Council retains the right to intervene directly whenever it may deem it necessary in order to maintain or restore international peace and security. Action by the Security Council, however, in connection with the investigation of a dispute or the use of force, may be prevented if one of the permanent members exercises its veto power. 

This veto power does not extend to discussion in the Council or to the preliminary phase of peaceful settlement. If a permanent member is a party to a dispute, such member must refrain from voting in any action of the Council directed toward peaceful settlement. In such a case the permanent member can only exercise its veto power with regard to the application of sanctions and enforcement measures directed against it.

"With reference to the right of a nation or a group of nations to enter into agreements for self-defense, the Secretary of State of the United States informed the Latin American delegates at San Francisco on May 15, 1945, that it was the intention of the United States Government to invite the other American Republics to undertake in the near future the negotiation of a treaty which, as provided for in the Act of Chapultepec itself, would be consistent with the Charter of the World Organization and would support and strengthen that organization, while at the same time advancing the development of the historic system of inter-American cooperation."

Roland Hall Sharp, Staff Correspondent on Latin American affairs for the Christian Science Monitor, in his book, "South America Uncensored", wrote: 

"Application of the Good-Neighbor Policy has fallen far short of its possibilities because sound principles have at times been abandoned in favor of delusions that inter-American friendships could be bought for a price, although that price undermined the policy's foundations.. When President Roosevelt in his first Inaugural 

Address launched the Good-Neighbor Policy as a phrase and a going concern, he based it on mutual self-respect. "...Washington's betrayal of the Good-Neighbor Policy began with partial self-betrayal. The State Department adopted as fixed policies toward Latin America such evident inconsistencies that the mere statement of them makes them seem incredible. Yet they are solid fact. 

Their impact has not been lost on the logical mentalities of South 

American peoples and their astute leaders. "While nearly 12,000,000 young North Americans were fighting to free the world from fascism in its German, Italian and Japanese phases, the State Department not only winked at fascism in South America, but strengthened it. Instead of adopting a clear-cut ettitude of opposition to fascism in all its forms, Washington has pursued a course of expediency. This may have been inescapable in some instances, due to urgent military necessities of the war against the Axis. Expediency, however, has gotten out of hand, with unfortunate consequences. The most dangerous single departure from a consistent democratic diplomacy has come in dealings with Brazil and Argentina. 

Washington's faulty diplomacy in South America goes on unchanged in essentials, but intensified in harmful effects, three years after dictators' collaboration had to be bought--if ever it had to be-at the expense of the self-respect of the United States of North America."
