A NATIONAL POLICY FOR LAND AND WATER by Le CGray, Director Division of Land Utilization Resettlement Administration 

A paper read before the National Reclamation Conference, Salt Lake City, Utah, November 15, 1935,

We are now witnessing drastic changes in national land policy. 

Outworn policies based on laissez faire ideals are being discarded. are rapidly adopting the view that land should be devoted to the realization of the maximun social welfare, and this point of departure has resulted in revolutionary changes in land policies. We

The Taylor Act, for instance, has reversed the traditional American land policy and closed the public domain to further settlement, pending a complete examination and classification of all available lands to determine what their best use might be. For some time it had been recognized that the areas of good farm land in the public domain were practically exhausted, and that further homesteading of these lands could not be encouraged without increasing distress on the part of families attempting such settlement.

Other conservation programs have also received renewed impetus during the past three years. Purchases of the United States Forest 

Service have been extended by several million acres in order to increase the national forest holdings, particularly in the eastern United States. 

State forest programs have also been prospectively forwarded by the passage of the so-called Fulmer Act, which makes possible Federal cooperation with the States in the acquisition of forest lands, the administration of which comes more nearly within the limits agreed upon for State VS conservation work. The past several years have also witnessed renewed interest in the conservation of wildlife, and a new point of view with reference to Indian tribal lands, As a result we have witnessed increased land acquisition programs on the part of the Bureau of Biological Survey and the Office of Indian Affairs. During the past two or three years there has also developed an attempt to bring to these various conservation programs a new point of view that would harmonize their separate objectives and form the basis of a well integrated national land-use program. Heretofore our conservation policies have been largely dictated by the objectives and plans formulated within the limits of each field of action, whether forestry, wildlife, conservation, or other. The broader these programs have become, the more they have begun to overlap and conflict, and the clearer has become the need for a well thought-out unified program of land acquisition which would accord to each separate program a degree of emphasis determined from the broader viewpoint of national social and economic welfare. ·613032

3The first step in proceeding along such a line was to gather all available information on land prolong and land programs in the United States. It may perhaps appear strange that no such fact-finding study had ever before been undertaken to bring together the voluminous reports issued by the various Federal agencies concerned with land use. The report which the National Resources Board made to the President in 1934 contained the first comprehensive survey of land resources and land problems in the United States, and set forth the basis for a national land program designed to accomplish those adjustments and changes which existing conditions indicated as desirable.

The survey of the several States, undertaken previous to the writing of the National Resources report, disclosed tremendous economic problems resulting from the misuse of land. We have long been aware of the destruction of forests, wildlife, soil fertility, and grasses as concomitants of the improper use of land. The National Resources report pointed out that there were other no less important results. Primary among these was the depressed, even destitute, standard of living to which large portions of our rural popu lation have been subjected because of their mislocation or because of the wanton exploitation of land and its resources. "Stranded populations" gg they are called, these families whose source of livelihood has been destroyed by the exhaustion of timber or mineral resources, or who from the start have been slated for hopeless failure because of the relative poverty of their land.

The dislocation of public finance due to misdirected settlement and the wasteful use of natural resources was another problem stressed by the Board's land report. Instances can be cited where the whole system of local government is threatened because of the misuse of land and the breakdown of taxation systems. Poverty-stricken families, living on poor land

-4- that will not support them, and unable to pay taxes to the local governments, have become tremendous burdens upon their less poor neighbors, because of the necessity for providing roads, schools, and other public services in areas where tax returns are in no measure commensurate with the expenditures incurred.

To meet some of these circumstances, a new conservation program was initiated in 1934 which aimed at the public acquisition of land primarily to correct maladjustments, either social or economic, which in the past had arisen from the unwise use of land. A series of projects have been undertaken to demonstrate methods of converting misused lands to public uses consistent with general welfare. 

In concrete form this program means the public purchase of certain overgrazed areas of range land, cut-over arcas, and blocks of submarginal farms which have proved consistently unable to support the operators, and the return of these lands to grazing, forests, wildlife sanctuaries, recreation, and multiple uses, under sufficient control to enlarge the capacity of such lands to serve humanity now, and to conserve them for future years. 

Farmers living on such lands are helped to move to better areas, where they may achieve a satisfactory standard of living and contribute their share of taxes to the support of local government. 

Thus, for the first time in our history there is being inaugurated a positive land-settlement policy, which is at the same time a resettlement policy aimed at correcting the result of misdirectod settlement of the past. 

The problem of tax-delinquent land is met by putting it in some use where with proper management it may become once more an economic asset rather than a liability to the local community. The significance of this program is that it is directed towards more basic social and economic ends than the protection of natural resources alone, highly important though that con-

or sideration is. It is aimed at improving the standard of living of the people on the land, the pattern of community organization, and the condition of public finance, as well as the physical state of the soil, grass cover, forest. The basic purpose of the program of land acquisition which the Resettlement Administration is now carrying out through its Land Utilization Division is to help put land to its best use, whatever that use may be. 

It is resulting in the increase of public forest lands, public game areas, and public recreation areas as well as in the development of erosion con-trol and the replenishment of both forests and grasses, I am particularly anxious to stress the fact, however, that we believe the development of other forms of controlled land use, such as rural zoning and the intelligent application of land bank loans on the basis of adequate land classifications, should have precedence over public land purchase whenever possible. Public acquisition should be a last, rather than a first, resort.

This brief sketch of some of the far-reaching changes in national land policy will serve to emphasize the necessity of reorienting our reclamation policy in order that it may have a proper relationship to the redirected land policies of the Nation. 

Although I do not come to you as an official spokesman of the Department of Agriculture, since I am at present in the Resettlement Administration, many of you, because of my long association with the Division of Land Economics of the Department of Agriculture, may suspect that I reflect the attitudes which in the past have been ascribed to the Department. It is true that both the Department of Agriculture and the Resettlement Administration, in which Federal land-use planning is now centered, have positive attitudes toward reclamation. These attitudes, I suspect, have been misunderstood by some western reclamation enthusiasts. I am especially glad, there

fore, for this opportunity of meeting with you and entering into a frank discussion of reclamation problems from the viewpoint of national land policy. 

It has sometimes been felt that the Department of Agriculture is antagonistic to reclamation. I wish to state most emphatically that this view is incorrect. Neither the Department of Agriculture nor the Resettlement Administration is opposed to reclamation developments as such. On the contrary, reclamation is recognized as one of the essential segments of land. policy. The actual development of reclamation in the past has frequently been characterized by certain unfortunate aspects which the Department of Agriculture has strongly opposed as being in the long run detrimental to a successful reclamation program, and contrary to the welfare of all farmers, including those on reclamation projects. The basic desire of the Division of Land Utilization is to see reclamation assume its proper place in a sound national plan for land use, and to eliminate from it those features which impose impossible handicaps upon its successful development. 

At the outset let us have an understanding of the term "reclamation". 

In the West "reclamation" is usually thought of as the process of bringing new lands into cultivation by irrigation. the only form of reclamation.

Irrigation, however, is not An evon larger acreage has been reclaimed by means of drainage. A good deal of land has been subjugated by clearing or jointly by draining and clearing. A national reclamation policy should comprise all the various methods of rendering land suitable for cultivation. 

Irrigation and drainage, however, are peculiarly dependent on collective action, and therefore represent the two major segments of reclamation policy. 

When the additional agricultural lands are needed, the relative merits of both irrigation and drainage projects should be carefully compared to determine which may best be able to meet the Nation's requirements.

It is hard to find valid arguments for Federal participation in irrigation that do not apply equally to other methods of reclaiming land for agricultural use. In the earlier years of Federal reclamation policy, its association with the public domain and the homestead system appeared to provide an excuse for special treatment; but we know that much private land was reclained and that much of the land that now awaits irrigation is in private ownership.

A more valid argument, to my mind, is afforded by the extensive unoccupied or sparsely occupied spaces in the West and the importance of providing a more ample agricultural foundation to support the structure of State and local governments and the transportation and commercial nechanisms of this section. There are many other parts of the United States, however, perhaps somewhat more restricted in area, whore similar problems exist and where a policy of subsidizing the expansion of the agricultural area could be as easily justified.

I am convinced that as long as Western irrigation demands special treatment as compared with other forms of reclamation, it will be in a false position and necessarily on the offensive. 

The West, no less than other sections, should be interested in the development of a national policy for reclamation.

Such a policy, obviously, would cease to be merely sectional in scope and concern.

This is all the more important because both irrigation and drainage are destined to depend in the future almost entirely on public rather than

private enterprise.

It is true, the latter has played much the larger role in the past both in drainage and in irrigation, but the results are sorry in the extreme, as witness the mulcted bond holders, the numerous illconceived enterprises, the wasteful and poorly distributed use of water in arid regions, the scrappy, unsystematic pattern of drainage districts, the abnormal capital structures of farms and districts being painfully liquidated with the aid of Federal loans, and the destruction of public confidence in reclamation bonds.

This last result, if there were no other reason, justifies the conclusion that future reclamation will be dependent on public enterprise. 

There are very positive reasons, however, why this is in the interest of the general welfare. Some irrigation and drainage projects not only are too large for private capital to undertake (even if the market for such securities were better) but are inextricably interdependent with such purely public functions as flood control, navigation, municipal water supply, and the more recently assumed function of power development. Even when the specific project is on a small scale and not directly associated with the public functions just mentioned, there is still a paramount public interest-especially in arid regions--in the use and conservation of water. In humid regions there is a large public interest in the relationship of the aggregate of small drainage projects to the obvious public responsibility for navigation and flood control. This is another way of asserting the point so strongly emphasized in the Report of the National Resources Board --namely, that the national interest requires the development, utilization, and control of the waters of entire watersheds insofar as these ends can be accomplished on the basis of the pattern of water rights created in the laissez faire period of our land and water policies, and the traditional doctrine of State's rights which still constitutes an important factor to

be reckoned with. I find an excellent statement of this point of view in the Foreword to the Progress Report of the Pacific Northwest Regional Planning Commission for 1934, probably written by your President. "In the Pacific Northwest it is particularly true that no State can plan for itself without planning for others and without the help of others. 

Each Northwest State must participate in regional planning. Each represents a part of and is concerned with the whole of the Columbia Basin not only as a drainage area but as an economic zone. "Therefore, each State planning Board, each State consultant, each advisory-technical committee, and every other auxiliary agency in planning for States must plan for the region; and that combination of State plans will form a plan for the region--not only as a mosaic of geographic areas and organization, but of thought, concept and action". 

This is true in general not only for the Pacific Northwest but for every large economic area in the country. And if the combination of State plans must fit into a mosaic of plans for an entire region, it is also necessary that these regional plans be considered in their relation to a larger national plan. This does not mean that a project must be planned and controlled by a central agency in some remote part of the country, but rather that no project be initiated until its purpose coincides with a predetermined and consistent land policy for the entire Nation. reclamation.

Over and above these considerations, however, there are other less obvious considerations justifying the development of a national policy for The New Deal has given birth to a policy of production control in agriculture--a policy long followed by big business, but possible for 6,500,000 farmers only through the intervention of the State. It goes without saying that such a policy implies a deep concern with the bringing of new lands into cultivation, especially when this is done with governmental finan-

cial assistance involving a greater or lesser element of subsidy. 

At this point I should like to deal with the traditional opposition of the United States Department of Agriculture toward the expansion of the cultivated area through reclamation. Although this period--which has now extended over a decade and a half--when American agriculture has suffered from overproduction in relation to available market outlets, has naturally made the Department sensitive about the expansion of the cultivated acreage in any section of the country, this is far from implying an unqualified opposition to the bringing in of any new acreage, whether by irrigation, drainage, clearing, or the plowing up of pasture land. Our 350 million acres of harvested crop land is of varied quality, ranging from excellent to distinctly submarginal. 

Moreover, lands originally of fair quality are continually deteriorating through erosion and overcropping until they finally sink into the submarginal class. Some of these lands are continually being abandoned and others should be. It follows that to maintain a constant national cultivated acreage it would be necessary to bring in some new lands. 

Even if this results in displacing and forcing from cultivation lands already in cultivation, this displacement cannot logically be gainsaid, provided the new lands are distinctly superior and capable of defraying the proper costs of reclamation and operation. I do not believe the 

Department of Agriculture or the Resettlement Administration would oppose such local expansion of the cultivated acreage provided the aforementioned conditions are fulfilled. I certainly would not.

This conception of national policy is reflected in the plan referred to in the call for this meeting, to retire ten acres of submarginal

land for each new acre brought into cultivation by Federal reclamation projects.

The first announcement of the policy was made by the President on July 27, 1933, more than two years ago. It was made in connection with an announcement concerning the Grand Coulee and the Casper-Alcova reclamation projects. One interpretation of the reason for it was that the products of newly reclaimed lands would force poor lands out of production, and that if the Government were to finance the reclamation of these new lands it should also offer some assistance to the marginal farmer forced out of operation. 

On July 24, 1935, two years after the first announcement of this policy, a statement was published to the effect that, "10 acres of land would be taken out of production for each acre put into cultivation by reclamation and irrigation projects", the figure being arrived at as a rough estimate of the relative productivity of irrigated and submarginal lands. While the formula sounds simple, its application bristles with complexities. Formulation of the detailed plan of application of this policy has been placed in the hands of an interdepartmental committee with four members representing the reclamation interests of the Department of the Interior, three members representing the Department of Agriculture, and the present speaker representing the Resettlement Administration. 

This committee is now considering a detailed report of the acreage which is being reclaimed in order to recommend the proportionate purchases of submarginal land which should he made.

While it may be justifiable to permit local expansion when such expansion can be justified in terms of economic feasibility, it is quite

another thing to justify such expansion in a time of severe depression when the new lands can, under no reasonable expectations, defray the cost of reclamation, improvement, and operation properly chargeable to them. It is even less defensible when public subsidies are employed to make possible such expansion. It is still less defensible when even with the employment of such subsidies lands are brought into cultivation which are utterly incapable of defraying the charges they are expected to meet.

The practice of looking to the Federal Government for the relief of districts which fall into financial distress (which has already led to loans by the Reconstruction Finance Corporation aggregating $115,000,000 to 628 reclamation districts, companies, and associates), as well as the inter-relation of such distress with the development of financial distress in other segments of our economic mechanism, constitutes another, though perhaps less cogent, reason why we should devolop a national reclamation policy grounded on a firm foundation of economic feasibility. 

A final reason for a national reclamation policy lies in the fact that the Nation has at last assumed definite responsibility for rural land settlement and resettlement, Long after it was recognized in the majority of civilized nations, we have awakened to the fact that the economic and social considerations involved in land settlement are too serious to be left to the haphazard processes of real estate promotion and speculation or the undirected efforts of individual settlers. The assumption

of this responsibility has been further necessitated by the need for correcting the serious maladjustments in the settlement policy of the past, and by the recognition of the evident inability of our industrial civilization to absorb the surplus population of the countryside, a population having a natural increase estimated at a half million per year. While it is to be hoped that a way will ultimately be found to solve the anomalous problem of a vast unsatisfied power of consumption confronted by an equally unsatisfied desire of millions to find a place in the industrial order, there appears little prospect of an early solution. We know, in fact, that even amidst the transitory super-prosperity of 1929, there were 1,500,000 to 2,000,000 unemployed, millions more who were underemployed, and millions whose employment in the short period of feverish industrial activity was destined to prove essentially transitory.

It is evident that our national safety lies in the direction of making it possible for a larger proportion of our population to find a secure foothold on the land. This does not necessarily mean a further amplification of an already embarrassing surplus of commercial agricultural products. It has been estimated that 50 percent of our conmercial product comes from little more than 10 percent of the population, while more than a quarter of our population contribute only 3 percent of the commercial production of our farms. By a better distribution of our commercial production, combined with the development of a scientific type of commercial farming accompanied by family self-sufficiency, and a further integration of agriculture and industry in what is called parttime farming, it is possible to give a much larger number of families

access to the land without further complicating the problem of commercial agricultural surpluses.

In the extensive program now being evolved along these lines by the Resettlement Administration, we are already beginning to look for additional areas of fertile land which may provide a suitable basis for such a program, replacing other areas of poor land destined to be gradually abandoned, In this process a rational and truly national program of reclamation, divorced from the all too prevalent speculative influences of the past, can make a considerable contribution.

As pointed out in the Report of the National Resources Board (Land Section), the most promising opportunity lies in the development of numerous small irrigation enterprises and the improvement of the crude and inadequate water supply facilities set up by individuals or small associations with inadequate capital. Although these enterprises are not of a magnitude to appeal to the imagination and to challenge the skill of the engineer, from the viewpoint of agriculture they are important. There are many opportunities for small low-cost irrigation enterprises to be established for the purpose of growing hay or other stock feeds to supplement the economy of adjacent grazing areas. 

There are enormous possibilities of stabilizing the reclamation economy of the west, not only through the development and improvement of numerous small irrigation systems, but also in scientifically redistributing the water supply in better relation to the quality of the land. It has long been obvious that a reclamation policy would be inadequate and incomplete without an associated policy of settlement. 

Meade himself has plead for such an association. A settlement policy is needed not only to insure rapid settlement so that the construction burden may be distributed among an adequate number of families, but also to nake available to settlers suitable improvements and equipment at low interest rates, an adequate system of farm organization, a div~ ision of the land that will permit the support in reasonable comfort of a maximum number of families, and above all suitable safeguards against the emergency of land speculation, overcapitalization, and absentee landlordism. It is obvious that either informally or formally the reclamation policy and the settlement policy of the Government must become closely associated, especially as our reclamation policy is

broadened, and that in the selection of reclamation projects settlement considerations should have a major influence. 

If we are to embark on a national policy of reclamation, however, it is essential that such a system be divorced from politics, otherwise it would merely surve to amplify the serious mistakes and abuses which have been all too evident. I do not stand alone in recognizing these tendencies.

The Water Planning Committee of the National Resources Board made a comprehensive study of the principles and policies of use and control of water resources at about the same time the Land Committee was preparing its report. It is interesting to note that the conclusions arrived at by these two independent groups, insofar as irrigation is concerned, are strikingly similar. The Water Planning Committee in its discussion of irrigation states: "The promotion and management of irrigation in the United States have not been rational--in many instances its need has not been rationally determined and its methods have not been properly established. It is the judgment of the committee that for the present new irrigation projects be not promoted, at least pending the development of a carefully studied, comprehensive reclamation policy, whether the projects be Federal or private, and that present irrigation activities be devoted to establishing existing projects on a more serviceable and profitable basis".

Other public agencies interested in irrigation have supported this view. Among them are the Western Chambers of Commerce which met in Los Angeles in December 1934, and a Special Committee on Reclamation

Policy appointed by the American Society of Civil Engincers. 

In calling attention to these conditions, I am not undertaking to criticize either persons or parties, but rather, the political character of the system. These conditions and tendencies are not peculiar to reclamation under the New Deal. They have existed since the Federal Government first launched its reclamation policy. We can escape from these conditions and justify the broadening of our reclamation program only by putting the program under the complete control of a nonpartisan board. In a democratic system of government there is no other means of avoiding the inevitable abuses growing out of a political system of reclamation. Nor is the problem peculiar to reclamation.

It applies to other forms of public works as well. 

To summarize--I advocate a truly national system of reclamation which would have the following characteristics: 

1. First, and foremost, a complete divorcement from politics through control by a nonpartisan board. 2. 3. 4. 5. 6.

Inclusion of both drainage and irrigation. 

Development on the basis of plans for water use and conservation covering entire watersheds. 

Close integration with agricultural and settlement policies of the Nation.

Substitution of a low interest rate for the subsidy in-volved in exemption from interest charges on capital advanced for construction. (The only logical alternative would be the extension of such exemption not only to non- Federal irrigation projects, but also to other forms: of reclamation, and, indeed, to all farmers).

Due allowance for costs properly allocated to power, flood control, and other elements in multiple-purpose projects, and also for measurable indirect benefits to other private interests as primary conditions for the development of projects.

These proposals may appear so radically different from existing policies as to seem utopian; nevertheless, these steps are necessary to a sound national reclamation policy, and sooner or later we must take them.
