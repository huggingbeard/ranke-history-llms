FOREWORD 

The discussions comprising this volume were given as a series of radio talks originating in the Tabernacle in 

Salt Lake City on the weekly Sunday evening Church program presented over Station KSL. They were so generously received by listeners over a vast area, and the requests for reprints so numerous that the series is now presented in book form.

As such it becomes another outstanding contribution to the literature of Mormonism. The subjects dealt with are many and varied, and they reveal the depth of perception and the broad understanding of the man who prepared them.

Stephen L Richards has long been admired for the simple, yet profound beauty of his language. Elder Richards distinguished himself as a lawyer and business leader, as well as a Church worker, before his call to the Council of the Twelve. His rich background is evident in the clarity of judgment he brings to problems perplexing men and women in a world torn by war and strife.

INTRODUCTION OUR BALANCE SHEET at the CLOSE of the YEAR 

I am honored in the privilege to address you and to take a little of your time in discussion of things which I hope have interest for all of us. If I shall be fortunate enough in this or subsequent talks to say anything which, even in small degree, may be helpful to anyone who listens, I shall be grateful indeed.

The end of an epoch-making year is almost here. 

Its momentous events and the lessons it has taught are perhaps too near to us to permit deliberate appraisal, but we are moving and living so fast that we must evaluate and learn as we go. In the year since our country entered the war, America has suffered many defeats and has seen some victories, both on the battle front and the home front. In the main, perhaps, internal affairs have been the darker side of the picture. Evidences of disunity, confusion, and selfishness have at times been quite discouraging. Signs of hope, however, for better organization and order and more unified effort lately appear. 

With these signs come also more conscious recognition of the stern reality of war and more deprivations and sacrifices on the part of the people. Anticipation of victory sooner or later has brought rather timorous talk of the peace and what it shall be when it comes. Under-

neath this over-all picture has been the steady, persistent induction and training of military forces, the constant clangor of production and the swift exaction and expenditure of moneys in prodigious amounts, the like of which the world has never known before. 

What are the lessons the year has taught us? How will they help us for the future? Of course they are many. I shall mention only a few. First, we have learned that shock, however violent, cannot be counted on to permanently change deeply ingrained habits, thought, and action. We thought that Pearl Harbor had united the nation. And it did, perhaps, for a time, but it was not long after this dreadful catastrophe before selfishness of groups and individuals began to assert itself in costly disputes and dissension which slowed down most pressing production and created bitterness between factions that almost precipitated battle and bloodshed. Second, we learned too that partisan prejudice and allegiance are all too often more important in the hearts and minds of men than patriotism; that there are altogether too many citizens of America who would sacrifice country for party and political preferment. And thirdly, observations and experience have taught us, and perhaps it is the most painful lesson of all, that the youth of America who have been called to the colors,-grateful as we are for their sacrifice and service and much as we love them, have not lived such lives as to bring to their country in its time of need, in a degree we should all like to have seen, that physical fitness and personal strength which is the rich heritage and native endowment of these sons of America.

Now if this very brief and perhaps much too super-

ficial summation of the debit side of our annual balance sheet is at all accurate, I hope you will indulge me in the presentation of a few suggestions, not all by any means which might be offered,-which in their application should be helpful to our country and the noble cause it has set itself to sustain. 

Unity can be achieved through fraternity, the brotherhood of man. Blood ties are among the strongest cohesive factors known to society. It is possible for men of different groups and races to establish bonds of relationship of substantially the same quality and endurance as within the family itself. These bonds are spiritual bonds. Every man is inherently blessed with the native attributes of brotherhood and friendship. Someone has well said, "God is a Father; man is a brother; life is a mission and not a career." All that is required is conscious cultivation of this precious endowment.

But the concepts which men hold vitally effect the development of this great virtue. If men do not recognize common origin it is difficult to establish kinship, but if they acknowledge the Fatherhood of God, brotherhood then becomes not only an ideal but a reality. 

It would seem that the ideal alone has proved not to be sufficient. While much credit is undoubtedly due to fraternal and other organizations which have sought diligently to promote this ideal, the strength of the ties established by them has not been adequate to withstand the impact of other forces. There is ample support in both Christian doctrine and human experience as well for the statement that there is no lasting efficacy in a brotherhood of man without the Fatherhood of God. 

Jesus said, "The Father and I are one," (D. & C. 93:3-4,

John 10:30) and He told His disciples, "If ye are not one, ye are not mine." (D. & C. 28:37) "Love the 

Lord, thy God, with all thy heart," and "thy neighbor as thyself" is not only the primary law of the gospel, it is also the only firm foundation for the establishment of human relations,—unity through brotherhood. 

In its broadest implications this would be the answer to all our problems, but it would be too much to hope that everyone would make the application to life and conduct. So, it may be profitable to attempt a little further analysis of the other major items I have noted as coming out of the experiences of the past year. 

Why do men place party above country? One of the answers is obvious, namely, for selfish personal interest and the emoluments of office. The other answer is not quite so apparent. It is, so it seems to me, a lack of an adequate concept of the place and function of government in human affairs. Now the only remedy 

I know for the eradication of the misconception is the inculcation of the Savior's doctrine of altruistic service: "For whosoever will save his life shall lose it; and whosoever shall lose his life for my sake shall find it." (Matt. 

16:25) This unselfish doctrine is the true foundation for neighborliness and friendliness among men and for the great principles of charity and love. Contraction of the heart is perhaps the most malignant malady afflicting the world. I know of no prescription nearly so effective as that of giving,—giving one's substance and giving one's self. You may well measure the patriotism and real devotion of citizens of this country to its institutions and lofty ideals by the extent to which selfish ends are subordinated to the common good. It is a very

definite perversion of the rightful place and concept of party organization and procedure in our government to subordinate the nation's prestige and welfare to partisan preferment and personal aggrandizement. I am pleased to observe that there are indications which point to better conditions in this respect. 

I would like now to give you some lofty concepts of government. I read from modern revelation: "Governments were instituted of God for the benefit of man... 

He holds men accountable for their acts in relation to them, both in making laws and administering them for the good and safety of society." (D. & C. 134:1) And again from the same source this pertinent admonition: "When the wicked rule, the people mourn. Wherefore, honest men and wise men should be sought for diligently, and good men and wise men ye should observe to uphold; otherwise whatsoever is less than these cometh of evil.” (D. & C. 98:9-10) If only such wholesome principles might underlie the conceptions of the rights and obligations of citizenship and official position in our beloved country, a very different story might be told of its performance in times of stress such as those through which we are now passing.

We live under a beneficent constitution which commands not only our loyalty, but our reverent veneration as well. In the sight of God America is a "choice land," and its constitution a divinely inspired document, for God has declared, "For this purpose have I established the constitution of this land by the hands of wise men whom I raised up unto this very purpose and redeemed the land by the shedding of blood." (D. & C. 101:77-80) Such divine pronouncements not only enhance our love of

country, but they invest patriotism with a religious fervor and sanctity that make the preservation of our national institutions and ideals an integral part of the establishment and maintenance of the Kingdom of God on earth. 

My people believe, and so declare, "that Zion will be built upon this, the American continent," and they merge her destiny into the glorious future forecast by the Savior of the world for the good, the just, and the faithful. 

What a boon it would be to our government and all our people if such concepts were universal! 

And now a word as to the last debit item on the statement. I discuss this item with reluctance, and I would not mention it at all except for the hope I have that drawing attention to it may help some young man. I do not know that reports are available to indicate just what the physical examinations of inductees reveal on the causes and forms of physical unfitness and disability. I do know that I have seen figures in the press which show that almost incredibly large numbers of men have been rejected, and I have also read that among the major contributing causes of disability are those that arise out of failure to lead clean, wholesome, circumspect lives.

When we contemplate the irreparable loss that so comes to the nation and the even more regrettably sad plight of the unfit themselves, it would seem that there is ample justification for trying to say something and do something about it. It is only an infrequent condition, such as the present emergency, which brings these matters forcibly to our attention. In the last war we had a similar shock and we got the Eighteenth Amendment. We did not get a lot of other things we should have had; we did not get public opinion as solidly behind some re-

forms as it ought to have been, but we did get Prohibition and we know now definitely that it was a salutary thing for the nation and our young people in particular. 

Since Repeal, an orgy of excesses has set in, of which the youth again are the chief victims. I cannot say, of course, -and perhaps no one can,-what may come out of our present war experience. I am firmly convinced that if the conflict lasts a long time the absolute need of effective manpower in the war effort will of necessity dictate some prohibitory measures. 

I hope with all my heart they may come before it is too late, and I take occasion here to voice again the appeal that was made by resolution in the last General Conference of our Church to the Commander-in-Chief of our armed forces that he give early consideration to measures designed to reduce the evils attendant on unchastity and liquor in and around army camps and establishments. Our boys are making a big enough sacrifice to serve our country without being forced into environment which jeopardizes virtue and health. I am sure I speak for the good homes of America when I renew appeal for action in this matter, and I know that I speak for efficiency in the service at the same time. I am aware that the problem is not easy of solution, but there is no doubt if our leaders and officers were disposed to do it, they could find a way.

Now I told you I was reluctant to speak of this matter because I fear that some of the fine boys and men who are serving their country, of which service I am a beneficiary, might feel that I was ungrateful and inappreciative. I assure them and you that that is not so. I am grateful and I am trying to do my part,-not

as big a part as theirs,—to sustain them in the great cause. 

I want only to help them and I can think of nothing that will help them more than to try to throw around them safeguards so that they will come out of the conflict as clean and wholesome and pure as they were when they lived in good homes under the uplifting influences of devoted fathers and mothers, wives and children.

I most earnestly hope, too, that the religious faith. of these boys in the service will not be injured during their military experience. Now is the time they most need spiritual comfort and support. Any officer who by word or action knowingly injures the religious faith of a boy in the service should be held accountable. I cannot permit myself to believe that we have any appreciable number of such men in the forces. I am fearful, however, that there are many who thoughtlessly and negligently contribute to religious and spiritual delinquency. 

Now, my friends, you have such of my impressions at the close of the year as I have been able to crowd into these few minutes. To brighten the picture, I would like to say in closing that I observe a number of heartening and encouraging things among our people. There is surely evidence of a disposition to bear hardships patiently, much volunteer help is available, drives for funds are prosecuted quickly and successfully, and there is abundant evidence of real patriotism everywhere. I think, however, that which heartens me most is what

I believe to be rather a pronounced tendency on the part of scholarly, thoughtful men in their exploration of causes and effects to make, for them, bold assertions to the effect that the need of our country and the world is more certain

and unchanging standards of life and thought to which men may cling in times like these. They don't always say it in so many words, but reading between the lines one can at least hope that in the back of their minds is a recognition of the necessity of authoritative religion and the kind of life it inspires. Then, too, how refreshing it is to hear the simple story of faith told by the national hero, Eddie Rickenbacker. There would be tremendous gain for the spiritual power everywhere if only men had the humility and the courage that he had to acknowledge the answer to fervent prayer.

So for one, I do not propose to be discouraged by the depressing aspects of scenes about us. 

There are innumerable things to be grateful for, foremost of which is the word of God that His work is set up in the earth never again to be destroyed, that righteousness and liberty and justice will triumph, and that ultimately peace and brotherhood and good will will envelop the earth.

CHAPTER I HOME, THE FOUNDATION 

A noble nation of ignoble households is impossible. 

In war or peace the cooperation of the home is indispensable. Just as the home is the foundation stone for the structure of government and society, so too are religion and the church the firmest props supporting this ancient and sacred institution.

It is because I am convnced that these things are true and vital that I propose to discuss the home as the first subject in this series on "The Church in War and Peace" In my judgment the formulation of any program for either war or peace which does not take into consideration the influence of and the effect upon this basic institution would be inadequate, if not entirely futile.

According to sacred history, the government initiated in and growing out of the home was the first known form of human government. The head of the family came to be the chieftain of the tribe or clan and his lineal descendants were accorded the inherited right of sovereignty. The patriarchs were not only prophets, they were law-givers to whose will their peoples were submissive. In this respect earthly governments came to be prototypes of divine government, for the very genius of divine government is fatherhood and a recogni-

tion of the family relationship. God, the Creator, is the Father, the Proprietor, and the Ruler; men, the children, bound by the ties of filial obligation, are the subjects who should ever be amenable in all respects to the will of the Father.

Then too, throughout the history of civilization, blood ties and race have been the strongest cohesive factors in the grouping of society. Many of the greatest nations have been but enlarged families with blood strains of remarkable purity.

The home has ever been the center of economic interest. It has undoubtedly produced a greater part of the wealth of the world and it has also spent it. 

It is the primary educational institution. Important as schools have been, they have never occupied a position more than complementary to the home, which is the nursery not only of all human beings but of all virtue. 

Governments which have attained high place in the world's history and affairs have, I think without exception, been those which have given due recognition to the home as a fundamental institution of society. They have enacted laws for its protection and advancement, and crimes against the home and its sanctity have been regarded as among the most heinous offenses. 

In this connection I recall the statement of an eminent man who at one time, speaking in the British House of Parliament against the imposition of a tax on the homes of the poor, said, in substance, "My home may be a poor and rude one; the roof may leak; the wind may enter; the rain may enter; but the King of England with all his army cannot enter. My home is my castle, sacred and inviolate to me and my family." Such a conception

of home has lain at the very foundation of English and American law, and that conception is in no small way responsible for the rights and liberties which we now enjoy.

What is its prospect in this dramatic evolution of persons, things and institutions which is now in process? 

I would not venture a sure prediction, but I do agree with 

Dr. Henry Van Dyke who said that "If old-fashioned

American family life vanishes nothing can take its place." 

What was an old-fashioned American home, or rather 

I should say, what is it, because I am thankful to note that there are still some such homes left in the land. You know what it is. You know that it is not just a house, however grand and imposing the house may be and however embellished it may be with costly furniture, rich hangings and floor coverings woven of the toil of far-off Persia. You know that it is not a mansion wherein reside a man and a woman, fretting under the bonds of a marriage contract, a poodle dog and a retinue of servants whose chief function it is to see that the three chief occupants of the house, the man, the wife and the dog, enjoy equality of right and privilege. And you know that such an old-fashioned home is all too seldom located among the costly residences of the rich. You know that it is usually to be found among the modest and humble; but not among the poor of the land, for they are not truly poor who maintain a real home. You know that in an old-fashioned American home you will find a large family of happy boys and girls, for whom father and mother willingly, patiently and lovingly devote lives of toil and service; not for ostentation and

pride and gratification of selfish desires, but to fulfill high conceptions of duty and the laws of God. 

The cry of the world is for real men and women. 

I know of no place where they can be found except in the homes of the people. The homes which produce real men and women must be presided over and maintained by men of strength and courage, of virtue and of vision, and by women of tenderness, unselfishness and infinite patience and love-endowments of God for the motherhood of the race. Good living is the first requirement of every parent. God pity the unfortunate parent who comes to the realization, as some day all must surely do, that the sins of the child are chiefly attributable to his or her own bad example or neglect.

Criminologists tell us that most of our delinquencies originate in bad or neglected homes. Economists say that the training of the home is largely responsible for the thrift, industry, and prosperity of the nation. Doctors advise us that the health of the people depends on its care and teachings, and the eugenist assures us that the whole trend of human happiness, intelligence, goodness and endurance depends on it. 

Do you know that statisticians have scientifically calculated that the United States will support a population of not to exceed two hundred million people, and that we are very rapidly approaching "this point of saturation"? The character of the nation and its destiny depend almost entirely on the families who shall make up the two hundred million. Will they be families descended from the old stocks of America who set up her great institutions and who have fought for and fostered her liberty, her equality, and her justice, or will they

be families in the stream of whose blood does not course the great impulses, the indomitable will, and idealism which have been and are the genius of our democracy?

Such questions must give pause and concern to every lover of America.

To the members of our Church the home has an enlarged significance that is subordinate to nothing else in life, for it constitutes not only the source of our greatest happiness here in this life, but also the foundation of our exaltation and glory in the life to come. After all, it is essentially a religious institution. It has its origin in religious ceremony. It is the fulfillment of divine command. Its government is of a religious nature, and the finest of its products are spiritual. 

So it is here in the humble and yet exalted institution of the home that I find the greatest opportunity and mission for men and women. I am sorry to say, however, that the record does not in all cases disclose a very creditable response to this big opportunity and obligation. Modern education has not always produced good home makers. Unfortunately the degree M. A. rather seldom spells Ma. Published data inform us that the average number of children in the families of the boot-blacks of America is slightly over four, while the average number of children in the families of school teachers is slightly under two. Now it may be that two school teachers exercise more and better influence than four boot-blacks, but how long will it take on the present respective rates of increase for the boot-blacks to crowd out the school teachers? I present this illustration from a popular scientist, not in derogation of people who follow humble vocations, but to empha-

size the fact that the world supply of intelligence, goodness and beauty is largely a matter of propagation. 

There is in this respect a traditional and rather well advertised distinction which our people enjoy. They have been noted for their large families, and had they been better understood they would be famous for their good families. Children have been our best crop and in the good old homes there has been an abundance of them. 

Eight, ten and a dozen in a family were common numbers. 

What families they have been! In days of privation and striving how they have stood together! The sacrifices which they have made, one for another; the love, the service, and nobility which have come from these great homes will probably never be known to many, but those who know of it and speak of the accomplishments of our church in the first century of its existence, mention first the noble fathers and mothers who in log cabins of the frontier or mansions of luxury have served faithfully as priests and priestesses in the temple of the home. 

Our church calls to its members and to all people to maintain the integrity, the purity, and the high purposes of this sacred institution. I trust that our people and our countrymen will not so yield to the insidious appeals of selfishness, vanity, and the world as to be swerved from so doing. 

To warn of a great danger I must speak of it more specifically. I do so most reverently. If it shall please the Lord to send to your home a goodly number of children, I hope, I pray, you will not deny them entrance. If you should, it would cause you infinite sorrow and remorse. One has said that he could wish his worst enemy

no more hell than this, that in the life to come someone might approach him and say, "I might have come down into the land of America and done good beyond computation, but if I came at all I had to come through your home and you were not man enough or woman enough to receive me. You broke down the frail footway on which I must cross and then you thought you had done a clever thing." 

I said that for our Church the home has a great religious significance. We believe that the marriage compact is not for life only or "until death doth part,' but for all eternity; that when the covenant is entered into in the proper manner and place and sealed by the power of the Holy Priesthood, which is the delegated authority of God to man, it becomes an everlasting union, an eternal institution into which there shall enter all children born in such wed-lock, and that the ties of kinship so created are eternal ties recognized in heaven as on earth. Indeed our heaven is little more than a projection of our homes into eternity.

The spirits of men, which are the literal children of the Father, are by Him permitted to take on mortality through a home, it being the chief purpose of the administrators of the home to guide the spirits so entrusted to their keeping back to the eternal presence whence they came.

So it is that we strive so diligently to maintain our children in the bond of this eternal covenant and union. 

While we mourn at partings, we do not fear death because death does not break this bond. We must all go by way of it to find place in the eternal family circle.

But we do fear sin that may deprive us of the presence of a loved one when we meet in our future homes. 

We deplore divorce. It strikes at the very foundation of the home. Fortunately the number of divorces among our people is relatively low.

Perhaps this mere glimpse into our philosophy of life and heaven and exaltation will serve to justify our undying interest in the homes of the people. 

We rely on these institutions to produce the manhood and the womanhood for the Church and the nation. 

Respect for law, order and established institutions must come from good family life if it comes at all. Boys and girls who grow up to call father "the old man" and mother "the old woman" are not likely to be easily amenable to the necessary restrictions which society imposes. If they cannot respect and love home and parents, their affection and regard for any worthy cause and institution are doubtful.

So I do not question the dominant place of home in our civilization, but I am concerned about the kind of homes we shall maintain. I believe the nation would be infinitely more secure if the influence of pure religion could come to every home. I have confidence in family prayer.

It would be far better if the Sabbath were observed as a holy day rather than a holiday. The enemies of society are not reverent, God-loving people.

We live in a Christian nation, founded on Christian principles. The only real hope for the world lies in the democracy and altruism of Christ. Christian homes are the answer.

Years ago it was a common thing to see in houses placards or framed mottos, worked in attractive designs

and colors, hung over windows and mantels, bearing the inscription: "God bless our home." It is not fashionable to display these mottos now. One never sees them any more. I trust, however, that if they may not hereafter hang on the walls of our houses, they may be deeply inscribed in our hearts. God bless our homes! 

And God bless our boys who have gone to protect them and the mothers, the wives, and loved ones they have left behind. These are the days when the ties of home and family reach deep into the heart. Out of such ties arise the incentive, the resolution, and the courage that will win this war and bring surpassing joy in happy reuniting in the homes of people.

CHAPTER II  

It is not my purpose to attempt a theological presentation, but rather a practical discussion of this spiritual theme. I feel that any consideration of the place of the 

Church in our lives and affairs would be wholly inadequate without some understanding of the nature, value, and acquisition of what we of the Church call a testimony.

It is probable that among Christian people there is a rather wide variety of interpretation on this subject. 

Indeed, in the Scriptures themselves a number of different expressions are used to designate this all-important and vital thing in religious life. But for my purpose it is not material whether it be referred to as "the testimony of our conscience," "the testimony of Jesus," "the Comforter," or "the Holy Spirit." What I wish to talk about is that something which brings abiding conviction and complete assurance of the verity of things divine. 

This personal conviction, which we call a testimony, is more than belief, more than rational deduction, even more than faith as ordinarily understood, yet definitely founded in faith. Perhaps the best definition of the “testimony of God" is that given by Paul when he characterizes it as "the wisdom of God in a mystery, even the

hidden wisdom, which God ordained before the world unto our glory." (I Cor. 2:7) Undoubtedly there is mystery in this "hidden wisdom which God ordained" for, as Paul further says, "that natural man receiveth not the things of the Spirit of God: for they are foolishness unto him: neither can he know them, bcause they are spiritually discerned." (I Cor. 2:14) But these scriptural statements are not so abstruse as they might appear to be. 

The simple meaning is that a testimony of divine things comes from divine sources and not from human rationalizing. It should be noted that there is no statement nor inference that a divine testimony is not reasonable, but only that it does not arise wholly out of the reasoning faculties.

I think perhaps it is because of this latter aspect of the case that men who are inclined to pride themselves on their reasoning faculties find it so difficult to tolerate a conviction which does not lend itself to the tangible proof of the five senses. They do not acknowledge that there is a very real difference between conclusions of the mind and convictions of the spirit, which differentiation 

Paul made so clear when he said, “For what man knoweth the things of a man save the spirit of a man which is in him? Even so, the things of God knoweth no man, but the Spirit of God." (I Cor. 2:11) I am very much inclined to believe that the failure to make this distinction is in large measure responsible for a great poverty of faith and testimony in the world.

Most people these days are reluctant to place limitations on the discovery of secular knowledge and the achievements of science. You will seldom find anyone who, in forecasting the development and utilization of the

forces of nature or some invention to meet a given need, will say positively, "It can't be done." We have come to have that much faith in human resourcefulness. Why is it that intelligent men will admit the probability of the scientific discovery even of the fourth dimension, and yet obstinately set themselves against acceptance of spiritual reality and brand as fanatics those who lay claim to spiritual experiences and conviction? I can give theological reasons for such a state of thinking but I will not do so now because, for the present at least, I would like to have you consider this situation in terms of what seems to me ordinary reason and common sense. 

I gratefully acknowledge the contributions which science and scholarly men have made to an understanding of the universe and its physical and astronomical laws and phenomena. What a boon to humanity these contributions have been! But with all his long quest, and the knowledge and control of the great forces of the universe he has attained, does man yet understand the real inner essence of these hidden forces? What about gravitation, heat, light, and electricty,— these physical forces which we all know and around each of which an elaborate science has developed? Intelligent man has discovered how to measure them, many of their properties and attributes and how to utilize them, but has he yet found out just what the basic primal element in each of these forces is? I think the greatest scientists have sufficient humility and frankness to admit that the ultimate has not yet been achieved and that these basic facts are still beyond them, and the search goes on and on. 

Now if we admit our inability to fully comprehend and define these material forces about us, which we know

so tangibly and definitely by the effects which they produce, why, I ask you, should we be so reluctant to acknowledge the existence and potency of other hidden forces which affect the lives and conduct of men and women but which, like these other manifestations of nature, we cannot fully understand and define with human faculties? It seems to me this is a legitimate question to ask a man of reason and logic. If he should reply, "I have no knowledge of spiritual forces because I have had no experience with them, therefore, for me, they do not exist," I should have no quarrel with him. 

That may well be his status. But if he should say that such spiritual forces have no reality for others and that they are mere imaginary concoctions of the mind, then I should brand him as unscientific and illogical,-first, because he presumes to say that a thing may not be because he does not understand it; and secondly, because without experience himself, he denies the validity of the experiences of others. I think that fortunately there are not a great many who take such a pronounced position, but I regret to note that there are many who exhibit such an indifferent and disparaging attitude toward spiritual concepts and those who hold them as to mark them with an intolerance and presumption they would not relish in others.

So, my friends, I submit the conclusion that a testimony of spiritual things is not unreasonable and that it is a great mistake to impugn the intelligence of anyone who has such conviction. We take the position in our 

Church that there is no higher order of intelligence than that which comprehends and accepts spiritual truth, and that in its final analysis all truth is spiritual. Our

25 scriptures declare that all things were created spiritually before they were created temporally, and that "truth is knowledge of things as they are and as they were and as they are to come, . . . the spirit of truth is of God." (D. & C. 93:24, 26)

Now I would like to tell you about the desirability of testimony and its effect on individual and church life. 

I am not able to explain the inner processes by which it is acquired, but I know that a testimony of divine truth does for a person what nothing else can do for him. It brings comfort, contentment, and purpose into life. It is resistant to evil. I have often wondered if there is not within its essence something akin to the serums which are injected into the blood to procure immunity from disease. Perhaps the comparison is not an apt one, but certain it is that there is an element in it that wards off the microbes of sin and makes the man of testimony more impervious to the repeated attacks of the adversary of truth. Be assured that this protection so afforded is no inconsiderable advantage in the living of a life. 

The acquisition of a testimony often completely transforms the person who secures it. When Saul of Tarsus miraculously received into his heart and soul the testimony of Jesus he was a changed man. All his conceptions, his ideals, and the works of his life were, so to speak, set in reverse. The transition came about in a moment, not gradually, but suddenly, when something of real essence entered him. However we need not go to the long past for illustrations. Many have come within my own observation and experience, and innumerable other transformations of lives have come within the history of the Church of Christ.

I remember being in one of the missions some years ago when a man asked that he might drive me to my next appointment, a distance of sixty or seventy miles.

During the course of the journey he told me the story of his life. He was a strong, robust fellow, but he shook with sobs as he confessed his errors and his sins, and then said, "Do you think I can be forgiven? Until the missionaries brought the gospel to me and until I received a testimony of its truth, I never realized the kind of a life I was living. My whole understanding of life has now been changed. I would like to spend the rest of my days making amends for what I have done."

That man was a different person than he had been. 

When I explained to him that the gospel held out forgiveness to all who would truly repent, the light of hope and courage came into his eyes and I am sure that a firm resolution took possession of him to make his life conform to the light that had come.

On another occasion I remember shaking hands with a number of people at the conclusion of a missionary meeting. In the group I met an old woman,-so very old that I took the liberty of asking her age. Of course 

I would not have done that with a younger lady. I said, "How old are you, sister?" She replied tremulously, "Twelve years old." "Twelve years?" I said, "How do make that out?" "Twelve years ago," she replied, "the missionaries brought the gospel to me. Only since then have I really lived." That was her tribute to that marvelous something which had come into her life which we call a testimony. you

members. Indeed no one is asked to come into the

At times it is something of a shock to applicants for admission into the Church to be advised that the evidences of their real conversion are not adequate. Such persons are not infrequently urged to further investigation and more supplication that they may know of a surety that it is the truth which they embrace. 

A young lawyer once told me that he would like to join our Church. I knew him to be a fine fellow and I told him we would be glad to have him as a member. I told him also that it was necessary to do something more than merely to indicate his desire for membership. 

I advised him that he should make careful study of the gospel, that the principles taught by the Church would seem reasonable and desirable to him, but that that was not enough. I then told him that in his studies he would be expected to supplicate the Lord for a divine impression of the truth and divinity of the work, which we call a testimony. He was much surprised and, I think, disappointed. He said that he had assumed that all that was necessary was a willingness to join. He did not know that the true disciples of Christ are they who have no doubt as to His divinity and His Lordship. 

A firm testimony of Jesus is the greatest comfort which may come to the human heart. There is great need for that comfort today, and greater need will arise as we endure more of the sacrifices and deprivations that this terrible war is bringing. There will be scarcely a family that has not someone in danger, and the cold hand of death will reach into many a home

and take away loved ones whose companionship is more precious than anything else in life. In such cases the kindly ministrations of friends and neighbors will help, but neither they nor anything else can bring the comfort, the lasting consolation, and the hope that come from a sure testimony in the reality of spiritual life and the immortality of the soul of man. Wives who lose husbands and mothers who lose their sons want to know that they are not gone forever and that they will meet them again, and the only place in the wide world they can obtain that blessed assurance is in the testimony of 

Jesus and His holy gospel.

What a boon it would be to every soldier facing death, either on the vast expanses of the ocean or in the battlefields of the jungle, the desert, the mountain, or the plain, to know deep down in his heart, with a conviction beyond question, that the Savior of the world is his Lord and King, that He gave His own life to make life everlasting, and that whatever happens, a good man will not ultimately lose his life, but will find it! 

People having such convictions never think of loved ones as lying in graves on land or sea. They know that death is a transition to life. They envision their loved ones as having gone on just a step ahead to await their coming, "out there" to continue together the eternal mission and destiny of their lives. 

If any man thinks that this comfort and assurance cannot come in these days, I would like to direct him to a people nearly a million strong, and if it were feasible, ask him to consult these men, women, and children to ascertain their views on these matters of life and hereafter. I can assure him that he would find a unanimity

of faith and concept that would hearten and sustain him. 

He would find, too, a people who are proud to bear their testimonies of truth and not ashamed to acknowledge the divinity and Lordship of the Christ and the miracles of His power.

It would be a great gain for the world if all who profess to take upon themselves His name had the courage and the conviction to do this. The humanistic interpreters of the Christian message are making but doubtful contributions to the advancement of the Christian cause. When they rob the doctrines and the history of their supernatural divinity they take from them their true genius and vitality. It is the humble, direct testimonies of humble folk which support the cause of Christ in the earth. 

Personally I am grateful to be one of those who enjoys such conviction. Every interpretive faculty which 

I possess gives me assurance of the divine truths which my people proclaim to the world. These truths provide the purpose and pattern of my life. They are of the very essence of existence. I wish I could impart the confidence and security which I and my colleagues feel to every man, woman, and child. It would be the greatest gift that could be bestowed. But I cannot bestow it. All

I can do is to testify of its worth and hope and pray that people everywhere will recognize its value and live for it and pray for it and receive it. God speed the day when the testimony of Jesus shall dispel fear and uncertainly in this stricken world.

CHAPTER III  

I am proud to represent a people who have always dignified work and reproved idleness. Our books, our sermons, our leaders, and particularly our present President, have glorified industry. The busy hive of the honeybee, Deseret, has been our emblem. Work with faith is a cardinal point of our theological doctrine, and our future state, our heaven, is envisioned in terms of eternal progression through constant labor.

This fundamental principle of the honor of work is sorely needed in application in our country today. All the fraudulent schemes, the rackets, governmental corruption, and most of the factors which so seriously retard unity and efficacy of effort in our war projects are chiefly attributable to the failure to recognize the integrity and dignity of work and the satisfaction and happiness that flow from honest toil. 

What is honest work? I believe it is rightly interpreted only in terms of service, and the value of true service is measured by someone else's gain. Such a concept is not only Christian, but it lies at the foundation of all sound, profitable business. Illustrations of this fact are so innumerable I shall not mention any. 

There are at least two factors indispensable to ef-

ficient work: first, integrity, which includes loyalty; and second, ability. No one can succeed unless he is dependable. If he works for another, he must make his employer's interest his own. If he works for himself, he must be faithful and true to his enterprise or he will fail. The "watch-the-clock" variety of service, with no interest in the job but to get the per diem, is as much responsible for failure as any other item which I know. 

To succeed not only must your workers be loyal but they must also be able. In the highly competitive markets for goods and services, as with our war industries, the skills are usually the deciding features. Ability is generally the product of training and education. The most skillful not only have the best chances themselves, but they contribute most to the success of the enterprise with which they are associated. It is a notable fact that during the years of depression, with some exceptions of course, particularly in industrial centers, the really skilled worker has been little in evidence among the unemployed, and today it is the man of skill only, who in certain specified lines, is exempted from military service because of his work.

On the other hand, our recent history demonstrates that the unskilled laborer has a hard time of it. His service is the first to be dispensed with. He is the least wanted. And, I regret to say it for fear of hurting feelings, he makes the least contribution to economic progress. In saying this, I want you to understand that 

I do not limit skilled labor to the professions, the artisans, or any white-collar or other group who may consider it beneath them to do any honest work. I class any man as a skilled worker who has cultivated the

ability to do his assigned job exceptionally well. 

It is my privilege to travel around the country a good deal. I see many farmlands. I often note that on one side of a fence is an area of stunted crop growth, weeds, barren patches, and unproductive desolation, while on the other side of the fence, with nothing but a dimension line between, with the same potential soil, equal water right, the same air, the same sun, the same climate, lies a field beautiful beyond description with the abundant productivity which nature yields to the skilled husbandman. I see fat cattle and lean, good homes and poor ones. I go into stores. Some are attractive,-merchandise is so arranged, service so efficient and courteous that goods sell, while in others, on the same block perhaps, there is evidence of slovenliness, poor buying, lack of the art of proper display, and the proprietor wonders why customers do not come.

Now what is the determining factor in these contrasted results and a world of others comparable to them? 

Why of course, with relatively few exceptions, it is the personal equation, the skill and the energy of the proprietor, or his employees, or both.

This doctrine of work lies at the very foundation of the capitalistic system, or what we choose to call "the American way." Many people misinterpret capitalism. 

They think that because the word capital is used to designate the system that its chief purpose is to make wealthy men who are usually called capitalists, and whose wealth it is feared is too often accumulated at the expense of the poorer classes. I admit that there are instances, altogether too many, when this comes about. But this is not the true concept of capitalism. In its inner essence

it is little, if anything, more than a man's free right to work, to choose his work, and to enjoy the rewards of his efforts. In my estimation it is a most precious thing, and it is indispensable to the liberty and freedom of which America boasts. It is the only tried and tested system of free enterprise in this world and every other opposing system is built on an abridgment of personal liberty. We are fighting for it, and we are determined we will not lose it.

But we will lose it some day if we do not understand it and recognize its virtues. It is not the capitalistic system itself that makes some men rich and some men poor. 

The men themselves do that, again with some exceptions. 

The system merely offers the opportunities. There are, of course, abuses within it, as there must always be when humanity is involved. It does not guarantee that all men will be rich, and it is worthy of note that all systems which do, usually succeed only in making all poor. To eradicate abuses within the system is almost as difficult as to reform humanity. But who would advocate killing humanity because they are slow to reform?

We must be patient with our American system. It will take a long time to eliminate the imperfections in its operation. To be patient it is necessary to be tolerant,tolerant of other people and their views. Everything we do in America is a composite of many opinions. The unifying element in our national affairs is common purpose and desire. I believe that the war effort, however discouraging some of its aspects may have been, has certainly indicated beyond question a common desire to maintain our fundamental liberties, one of the most important of which is our system of free enterprise. The first essential step is implanting the concept of useful service for others. This is the doctrine of altruism and basically it is a religious principle. What a spectacle we must be making of ourselves in the eyes of the people to whom we propose to open the democratic way of life! I am sure that I voice the sentiment of the great majority of our citizens when I say that this thing should stop, and stop now. Our national ideals and principles demand it, and ultimately it will be requisite and indispensable to the preservation of our union.

I do not wish it to be understood from these statements that I am appealing for the triumph of any faction in the great controversy,—in fact, I think that a factional victory only would be disastrous. I am hoping for two things: first, a widespread recognition of the seriousness and grave import of these conditions. I doubt that the questions and problems confronting the country before the Civil War were more pregnant with danger and threat to national unity. And secondly, what I am hoping for most to follow in the wake of a consciousness of the urgent importance of the situation, is a reconciliation of differences in righteous and equitable adjustment. 

Labor troubles in this country, or in any other

country, can be composed if men meet them in the spirit of brotherhood. Mutual cosideration enjoined upon us by our Savior is the key to the solution. Undoubtedly no solution yet presented is perfect, but the most encouraging from a practical standpoint seems to me to come from the composition of violent labor disputes in Sweden many years ago. There the employers and employees suddenly came to recognize the futility of disputation, vindictiveness, and violence and turned from coercion to persuasion. 

This change of heart avoided revolution and laid the foundation for amicable relations which have endured for more than thirty years. With such examples before us it is surely not hopeless to look for peaceful solutions.

Rather frequently in these dark days we are exhorted to prayer. The President sets aside a day or a week in which prayers are to be offered in the churches and in the homes of the people. It is expected that we will pray for unity, for devotion to the ideals and principles of country, for protection and for success. Has it occurred to us what a solvent prayer could be for contention and animosity in our national life? If this sacred Christian practice which has come down to us through all the annals of our republic could be more than a mere perfunctory thing, and could mean something real in tempering men's hearts and feelings and in stimulating kindly and fraternal relations,-if men could only pray together, they could work together and such work would transform this bleeding world. That day will surely come, my friends.

It is so ordained. Be not skeptical or doubtful of it. Work for it with all your mind and strength.

What is this feature which has made these men and boys different from their fellows? It is the Priesthood of the Living God which has been bestowed upon all of them. I hope to explain to you briefly what it is, what it does, and where it comes from. 

The Priesthood is usually defined as being "the power of God delegated to man to administer the ordinances of the gospel." This is a true definition, but I like to add to it an interpretation in terms of service. The power is not static. The extent of it varies with a man's worthiness and capacity to hold and use it. In this respect it is not incomparable to the waters that flow from our mountains down into the valleys below, as these waters are controlled and regulated by the law of irrigation which prevails in our communities. An early settler might have diverted from its natural channel the whole of a mountain stream and thereby set up a claim of right and ownership to the entire flow. It is so with the great stream of power that flows from the fountainhead of God down to His servants in the earth. I desire to speak to you on the subject: Being Orthodox in Religion. I am led to believe that this subject is timely and of some general importance by many expressions which have reached me, coming from both young and mature people, which seem to indicate that there is something of an aversion to being considered orthodox. 

Not infrequently does one hear it said, "Yes, I am a

Church member. I think it is all right, but I don't pretend to be orthdox."

The manner of making such a statement, or others like it, usually carries the implication that the one who takes this position regards being orthodox as something of a stigma or blot on his intelligence. He seems to think that orthodoxy is opposed to broadmindedness and indicates contraction of view. From the technical definition of the word, of course, this interpretation is erroneous. Orthodoxy means, "correct or sound in doctrine; holding the commonly accepted faith." 

I cannot help believing that much of this attitude results from an inadequate conception of Christian doctrine and illogical thinking about it. I feel sure that many who express skepticism are not really as skeptical as their talk would indicate, and that some who think

themselves skeptical could easily resolve their doubts by a little clear thinking.

Now what is necessary to be orthodox, or "correct and sound in the doctrine" of Christianity? To what must we subscribe? In answer to this question, I might, with propriety, quote the long list of Christian principles which are considered fundamental in the Christian doctrine. But I am not going to do this. I am going to try to simplify and condense the answer. The answer I propose is this: Accept Christ for what He said He was. 

If we can really do this, we will be orthodox. 

I have never been able to understand how intelligent, educated men could reconcile the acceptance of that portion of the record of the Savior which sets forth His incomparable teachings and philosophy, and at the same time deny the correctness of the same record which proclaims His divine Sonship and Lordship and miraculous powers. What justification is there for credence in a part of the record and not all of it, or for believing some of the things the Master said and not all that He said? I admit that it may be easier, in that it requires less faith to accept some parts, rather than other parts, but from the standpoint of the authenticity and validity of the record itself, which is admittedly the source from which we obtain our knowledge and information of the Christ, how can one part be true without all being true? To that question I have never been able to discover a satisfactory answer.

CHAPTER VI  

I have heretofore told you of a spiritual power that makes men different. Now I wish to tell you of some new concepts of the Christian gospel. This occasion will permit me to give only a few of these new interpretations and that briefly. I present them because I feel sure that they contribute to a larger appreciation of the principles of life taught to us by the Savior of the world. 

I set forth as the first item of this new interpretation the doctrine of the dominance of intelligence. I believe 

I am correct in the assertion that in all Christian literature prior to the advent of the Restored Gospel there were to be found no such concepts of the origin, function, and place of intelligence in the universe as comes from modern scripture. Here are some excerpts: 

"Intelligence, or the light of truth, was not created or made, neither indeed can be. "All truth is independent in that sphere in which God has placed it, to act for itself, as all intelligence also; otherwise there is no existence." (D. & C. 93:29-31.) "The glory of God is intelligence, or, in other words, light and truth. "Light and truth forsake that evil one. (D. & C. 

93:36, 37.) "Whatever principle of intelligence we attain unto in this life, it will rise with us in the resurrection.

"And if a person gains more knowledge and intelligence in this life through his diligence and obedience than another, he will have so much the advantage in the world to come. (D. & C. 130:18, 19.)

Now since intelligence is co-eternal with God and the very glory of God, it follows logically that it is the chief investiture of man. Indeed, it is man, for it is that part of his constitution that persists, that is eternal. 

This knowing, conceiving, illuminating principle of existence lies at the base of all our powers and potentialities. Without it there would be no virtue and no sin. 

It alone gives to man his free agency, the power to choose, to will, and to act, conscious of the effects of his decisions and his deeds. It accounts for the place of good and evil in the world and justifies their existence,a philosophical problem that has baffled the minds and the thinking of great scholars of all time. 

This conception of intelligence justifies the eternal quest for knowledge. It explains the necessity of acquiring knowledge, for it makes knowledge essential to progression, and progression in the last analysis is salvation. It places a terrific penalty on ignorance. It lays down a new and very definite gospel doctrine that "it is impossible for a man to be saved in ignorance." 

The world in general, and the modern world in particular, with its science, scolasticism, and technology has been prone to scorn and slur religion and the Church for an alleged backwardness and retardation in intellectual processes and in the acquisition of knowledge. 

Whatever justification there may have been in times past

for such an imputation. I present now an interpretation of the gospel that is a complete refutation of these disparaging allegations and inferences of the secular world. 

The gospel of Christ, under such interpretation, offers a stimulus and a reward in the pursuit of knowledge and the development of intelligence that transcend in their appeal and promise anything that the students and philosophers have ever evolved. 

But the knowledge and intelligence that it rewards must be true. "Light and truth" are the words of revelation. It places no premium upon the acquisition of falsehood and error. It distinguishes very clearly between sophistication and true intelligence. In differentiating intelligence from education a man once said, rather facetiously, that intelligence is that which enables one to wisely meet the situations of life without education; whereas, education is that which helps one to meet the situations of life without intelligence. 

This statement may leave the inference that intelligence is largely inherited. The gospel teaches us that it is—that it came as a native endowment of the race, but that the potency of the endowment has been in many instances largely mitigated by the perversion of the race. 

It is encouraging and gratifying, however, to be assured that no man has quite lost this vital and all-important attribute. There is enough intelligence with every man to acquire knowledge and truth if he elects so to do. It is a significant fact, demonstrated by most extensive research and investigation, that there is no people in the earth, even the most primitive, which has not sufficient intelligence to grasp and hold to a conception of God as a Supreme Being.

How to exercise the intelligence we possess for the attainment and utilization of knowledge is a vital matter in the interpretation and application of the gospel, but it is one that I must pass over briefly. The gospel places highest value upon that intelligence and knowledge which contribute most to the enduring welfare of man. “Pure knowledge," it is called in the revelation. (D. & C. 121: 

42.) This pure knowledge contemplates an understanding of the divine plan of redemption and the principles and laws that pertain to the Celestial Kingdom of our Father. In all the learning of the world there is nothing of higher import for the mind of man to comprehend than the eternal principles and truths that pertain to exaltation in the presence of God. There is no higher order of intelligence than that which enables man to perceive these glorious truths.

Since intelligence is a divine essence, it may function adequately only when recognized as divine. In that recognition faith is indispensable. Faith and knowledge constitute testimony and bring that conviction which is epitomized in the declaration, "that to know thee is eternal life." So, I ask, where is the philosophy or school of thought that esteems more highly and ascribes nobler attributes to the mind and intelligence of man than does this exalted concept set forth in the new interpretation of the gospel of Christ?

The next major phase of this new interpretation is a unique and distinctive concept of the family of God. 

God, the Master Intelligence, is the Creator and veritable Father of His children, the lesser but potentially divine intelligences who make up His family and populate His kingdom. As a kindly parent He desires and designs

65 the eternal welfare and happiness of His offspring. In modern revelation His purpose is clarified as never before in recorded scripture. "For behold, this is my work and my glory to bring to pass the immortality and eternal life of man." (P. of G. P., Moses 1:39.) 

And again, "Men are, that they might have joy." (B. of M. 2 Nephi 2:25.) These are the sublime declarations revealed in latter days, forever banishing all doubt and uncertain speculation as to the place and purpose of man in the universe.

In what does the joy of man consist? There are two major things: first, an eternal progression in intelligence, knowledge, and power that leads to perfection, even as 

Christ is perfect; and second, companionship with God in His presence and in the presence of His Son. 

This pronouncement of the purpose of our being, together with additional revelations, brings a new concept of heaven. By the perfection of our lives we achieve joy and come into the presence of God. Where is He? He is in His own kingdom. Are there other kingdoms?

Yes. And in that revealed knowledge we learn the truth about heaven. There are other kingdoms referred to but not fully explained by St. Paul and also indicated in "the many mansions of my Father's house," spoken of by the Savior.

These scriptures have been fully amplified by the new interpretation. Kingdoms of eternity have been defined. Laws that govern the various kingdoms have been set forth and entrance requirements stipulated. 

There are preferential places and conditions in the hereafter, as there are here. The highest and most desirable is the Celestial Kingdom of our God. In that kingdom,

and only there, do intelligences attain their highest state of perfection. Only there do we have assurance of the reunion of families and the perpetuation of family relationship and eternal increase. In that kingdom man may ultimately become divine.

In the mercy of God other lesser kingdoms make provision for just compensation in the lives of men. But the lesser kingdoms are outshone by the celestial as the sun in its brightness and glory transcends the moon and the stars.

Men attain these various kingdoms by election and the work of their lives. We do not know all the conditions precedent to entrance into the lesser kingdoms, but what is necessary to come into celestial glory has been definitely revealed. The principles and ordinances of the gospel of Christ are the laws of that kingdom. Faith, repentance, baptism, the laying on of hands for the bestowal of the Holy Ghost, ordination to the Priesthood,are all essential requirements. Why? Because God has stipulated them and because also they are consonant with the spirit, the purpose and glory of the kingdom. 

Is it necessary for one to be baptized to go to heaven?

That depends on the place in heaven to which one aspires. We have no knowledge as to whether it may be necessary for the lesser kingdoms, but we do know by revelation that it is absolutely essential for the heaven of the Celestial Kingdom of God. The scriptures give us to understand that through the general atonement of the 

Savior all mankind will be resurrected from the grave and come forth in a life hereafter, but we know that the atonement itself is not adequate to place us in the Celestial Kingdom, in which respect we distinguish between the

69 general salvation of the human family brought about by the Redeemer of the world, and the exaltation of the individual accomplished by his own works and faithfulness, through the mediation of Christ. 

From this clearly defined, sublime concept of heaven is devolved the mission of the Church, which is: first, to disseminate knowledge of the Celestial Kingdom; and second, to assist in perfecting the lives of men so they may enter that kingdom.

This mission of the Church, through the revealed justice of God, is extended not only to all the living, but to the dead as well. It is not designed that the accident of death shall circumscribe the free agency of man to choose and attain his ultimate destiny. Since, however, it is in the plan that certain ceremonies and ordinances which are requisite for entrance into the Celestial Kingdom shall be performed in mortality, merciful provision has been made whereby the living may vicariously perform these ordinances for the dead. Hence our extensive temple work wherein all conditions essential for entrance into the Celestial Kingdom are complied with for both the living and the dead.

I think of all Christian service, vicarious work for the dead is the most Christ-like. It often entails great sacrifice. The beneficiary is not here, even to give thanks. 

It is true benevolence.

As another item in this new interprepation of the gospel, I must not pass by the doctrine of the holy Priesthood, of which I have heretofore spoken. Knowledge of God, His family and His kingdom, and reason also, assure us that we cannot do His work and accomplish His purposes without a delegation of His power. The

008 Priesthood is His commission to men. It holds the keys to His kingdom. The gates will not unlock without these keys.

What is the significance of these new interpretations of the gospel? Perhaps I can best tell you by relating the experience of a young man engaged in missionary service. I asked this young man to speak in a meeting of missionaries reporting their labors. It was in Texas, I think.

He said in substance, "While pursuing my labors the other day, I went to a house and knocked on the door. A fine looking, dignified lady came to the door and I told her the purpose of my visit. I explained that 

I was a missionary of the Church of Christ and I asked if I might have the privilege of explaining to her some of the principles of the gospel which my Church taught. 

To my surprise, she immediately became angry and indignantly said to me, 'I resent your bringing the gospel to me. I'm as good a Christian as ever you dare be. I've always been a member of a Christian church and supported the Christian cause. You take your gospel to the heathen.' "I was so confused," the young man said, "by her irritation that I scarcely knew what to say, but it occurred to me to ask some questions. And I said to her, 'Madam, will you permit me to ask a few questions?' She said, 'What is it?' I asked, 'In your study of the gospel, have you ever discovered the way in which work may be done for your kindred dead?' No, she did not know it was necessary. 'Have you ever been told of a power that can bind and your you husband together beyond this life when "death doth you part," and also assure you of the

companionship of your children in the family relationship in the life to come?' No, she had never heard of such a power, although she hoped for a reunion hereafter. 'Have the various kingdoms or gradations that exist in heaven ever been explained to you?' 'No, never.' 'What is the difference between salvation and exaltation? What has God stated the purpose of man's life to be? What is the Christian doctrine of eternal progression?' She did not know. Her resentment subsided. She said, 'Young man, I beg your pardon. I see there is much about the gospel I do not know. Won't you please come in and explain?'

There are hundreds and thousands of good people professing the Christian faith who do not know of this new interpretation and new restoration of the pure gospel of Jesus Christ. When prejudices disappear and hearts and minds are open to receive the "glad tidings of great joy" that have been re-revealed, then will the gospel of Christ find a reception that it has never before known. 

In this new revelation lies the hope of the world. 

When true intelligence shall banish error and superstition and the family of God shall be more than a theological conception; when men shall truly aspire to the highest heaven, and the duly constituted authority of 

God shall be recognized and esteemed, then,—and not until then,—will the long-desired peace of Christ descend upon the nations. God grant the early coming of that day!

CHAPTER VII  

There are biographers and students of Lincoln who maintain that he was a man of God, divinely prepared and inspired to be the liberator of human beings and the savior of the union of free men. I am sure that there is much to support the argument for this concept of Lincoln, although I do not pretend to have been a sufficiently deep student of his life and philosophies to warrant an independent judgment in the matter. I do know, however, that many of his utterances in public and private stamp him as a true Christian, although he did not ally himself definitely with any of the churches of his day. 

It is about his religious nature that I wish to weave this discussion in the hope that we, his fellow countrymen of a later generation, living in troubled and anxious times not incomparable, in gravity at least, to those through which he passed, may derive some measure of that support and wisdom so amply bestowed upon this great servant of the people.

These biographers of whom I speak point out that Lincoln's background was all deeply religious, that the Bible was always his first and best book, that it was taught to him by his two mothers, and that its precepts were ever the criteria by which he determined right and wrong. 

Linguists say that his simple, pure language was unconsciously fashioned by his familiarity with holy writ and that the sublimity of his thought and vision was

definitely a product of the inspiration and influence of the divine prophets.

Those who hold to this thesis assert that he had a forerunner for the mission of his life, even as did the Savior, in the person of Peter Cartwright, the great exhorter and revivalist who helped to mold both Lincoln's spiritual and political life and prepare the soil for the seeds which were to ripen in the heart and soul of the 

Great Emancipator. It is recorded of this fiery preacher that a dinner party was given in his honor in New York City attended by many of the prominent men and celebrities of the day. To Cartwright's surprise, he heard nothing but criticism of President Lincoln. After standing it as long as he could he asked to be excused, but before leaving the party rebuked the critics and upheld the 

President in these words: “Once we were opposing candidates for a seat in

Congress. I went down to defeat, but it was defeat by a gentleman and a patriot. I stand here tonight to commend to you the Christian character, sterling integrity, and far-seeing capacity of the President of the United States whose official acts you have, in blind moneymadness, so critically assailed. your "When you go from here to your homes, I want you to bear with you the assurance of his neighbor and once political opponent that the country will be safe in his hands."

One of his biographers says that in his "first political campaign, Lincoln, always mindful of religion, issued a circular announcing his platform of principles in which he declared that all citizens, however poor, should be afforded an opportunity to acquire at least a moderate

education to the end that they might be able ‘to read the scriptures and other works, both of a moral and religious nature for themselves.' . . . This doctrine contained the germ that flowered into the magnificent public school system.' 

It is, however, from the words of Lincoln himself and his living that his truly Christian character is substantiated. His law partner went so far as to contend that 

Lincoln was not a Christian because he said on one occasion "that his religious code was like that of an old man back in Indiana who said: 'When I do good I feel good; when I do bad I feel bad, and that's my religion,'' and also because at times Lincoln berated the clergymen of his day for what he conceived to be their hypocrisy and insincerity in distorting the scriptures to give support to the selfish and ulterior motives of influential parishioners. 

But when he was presented with a copy of the agnostic Tom Paine's Age of Reason, he said of the book: "I have looked through it carelessly it is true, but there is nothing to such books. God rules this world and out of seeming contradictions all this kind of reasoners seem unable to understand He will develop and disclose His plan for men's welfare in His inscrutable way. Not all of Paine's nor all the French distempered stuff will make a man better, but worse. They might lay down tons and heaps of their heartless reasonings alongside a few of Christ's sayings and parables to find that He had said more for the benefit of our race in one of them than there is in all they have written. They might read His 

Sermon on the Mount to learn that there is more of justice, righteousness, kindness, and mercy in it than in

12 the minds and books of all the ignorant doubters from the beginning of human knowledge." 

Lincoln saw God in nature. A friend of his young manhood said that one night Lincoln asked him to take a walk. The friend said “that he turned his eyes toward the stars and told me their names and their distance from us and the swiftness of their motion. He said the ancients used to arrange them so as to make monsters, serpents, animals of one kind or another out of them, ‘but,' said he, 'I never behold them that I do not feel I am looking in the face of God. I can see how it might be possible for a man to look down upon the earth and be an atheist, but I cannot see how he could look up into the heavens and say there is no God." " 

He once told another friend: "That the Almighty does make use of human agencies and directly intervenes in human affairs is one of the plainest statements in the Bible. I've had so many evidences of His direction, so many instances when I have been controlled by some other power than my own will, that I cannot doubt that this power comes from above . . . All we have to do is to trust the Almighty and keep on obeying His orders and executing His will." Continuing further, he said to others: "I am a full believer that God knows what He wants a man to do,-that which pleases Him. It is never well with the man who heeds it not. I talk to 

God. My mind receives relief when I do, and a way is suggested. If it is not a supernatural one, it is always one that comes at a time and accords with a commonsense view of the work."

It is well contended that this deep religious faith was largely responsible for Lincoln's impeccable honesty

and purity of life. He could make no compromises with wrong and he framed the issues of his time so clearly on the code of righteousness that he straightway won the respect and admiration of honest folk everywhere. 

He always had the courage of his convictions from which nothing could swerve him. It is the only beverage I have ever used or allowed in my family and I cannot conscientiously depart from it on the present occasion. It is pure Adam's ale, just from the spring."

He was committed to the abolition of liquor as he was to slavery, and he said to one of his friends: "We have cleaned up a colossal job; we have abolished slavery. 

The next great movement will be the overthrow of the legalized liquor traffic, and you know my heart and my hand, my purse and my life will be given to that great movement. I prophesied twenty-five years ago that the day would come when there would not be a slave or drunkard in the land. I have seen the first part come true." Would that he had lived completely to fulfill his prophecy!

Another essential doctrine I gather from his utterances is the support and preservation of the Constitution. 

In his farewell address of September 17th, 1796, after two terms as President, he expressed himself as follows: "That the free Constitution which is the work of your hands may be sacredly maintained; that its administration in every department may be stamped with wisdom and virtue; that, in fine, the happiness of the people of these states, under the auspices of liberty, may be made complete by so careful a preservation and so prudent a use of this blessing as will acquire to them the glory of recommending it to the applause, the affection, and the adoption of every nation which is yet a stranger to it." 

He declared that "the basis of our political system is the right of the people to make and to alter their constitutions

of government, but the constitution which at any time exists till changed by an explicit and authentic act of the whole people is sacredly obligatory upon all." He cautioned "that you resist with care the spirit of innovation upon its principles, however specious the pretext." And then with prophetic vision he observed, "It is important likewise that the habits of thinking in a free country should inspire caution in those entrusted with its administration to confine themselves within their respective constitutional sphere, avoiding in the exercise of the powers of one department to encroach upon another. The spirit of encroachment tends to consolidate the powers of all the departments in one and thus to create, whatever the form of government, a real despotism." 

How wisely he called attention to "the love of power" in the human heart and "the necessity of reciprocal checks in the exercise of political power by dividing and distributing it into different depositories and constituting each the guardian of the public weal against invasion by the others." "If, in the opinion of the people, the distribution or modification of the Constitutional power be in any particular wrong, let it be corrected by an amendment in the way which the Constitution designates." But he solemnly warned, "Let there be no change by usurpation, for though this in one instance may be the instrument of good, it is the customary weapon by which free governments are destroyed."

I present next the emphasis to be placed on general education. This was Washington's admonition: "Promote, then, as an object of primary importance, institutions for the general diffusion of knowledge. In pro-

I think it is generally conceded, without offering proof, that religion is a less vital factor in American life in this generation than it was in his, and that a very much smaller portion of the population has been exposed to its influence in our time.

It was because of the general importance of this salutary principle of human life that the ministry of Christ among men opened with His clarion call, "Repent ye, for the Kingdom of God is at hand."

It is true that these statements apply to individuals, but it is also true that national rectitude is but the composite of individual goodness. All that is necessary is to get individuals to repent to secure the national repentance for which Lincoln pleaded. Since, therefore, this is a personal matter, I wish to make a few personal appeals. 

First, I want to appeal to those who have been entrusted with positions of leadership in our national institutions, both civic and military. I appeal to this group particularly, not because they are more important than average citizens, but because their example and influence are more potent in forming public opinion and policies. I most earnestly entreat these men and women in public positions to give serious and solemn consideration to the spiritual and moral welfare of our country and to remember that their constituency, the citizens of America, do not, cannot, and will not disassociate the private lives of their officials from their public utterances and actions however much, they (the officials) may insist on such a separation. The prestige and influence which come to a man with public office should, I am sure, be

considered more as a responsibility, than as a personal aggrandizement. One so elevated to prominence should ask himself seriously, not only how he may best administer the duties of his office, but also how the influence of his life and his conduct may best promote the welfare of his fellow citizens. If all would do this, I am sure that they would become convinced that the latter is not less important than the former. 

We remember Washington and Lincoln today as much for what they were, as for what they did. The symbol of greatness of "the Father of his Country" has descended not alone from his military genius, and his political acumen, but from crude sketches of the colonial general on his bended knees at Valley Forge and the simple and naive story of the boy who couldn't tell a lie. The concept of Lincoln that lives and is most deeply imbedded in the hearts of the people is not that of the shrewd and skilled debater, and astute politician, but it is the picture of a tall, gaunt, solemn figure with deep, sad, mysterious eyes looking out on a troubled world and a firm and sensitive mouth expressive of sympathy, compassion, and charity for all, whom all the world affectionately and lovingly call "Honest Abe." The recorded. words of the Savior of the world can be compressed in a few pages, but what He was and what He has meant to humanity could not be set forth in all the libraries of the world.

Let me ask another question. Why should the offering of prayer in our public school system be restricted, in all grades from grammar school to the university, to graduation exercises? I am sure that to a very great extent it is, and that there are thousands and thousands of excellent people in the teaching profession who are as interested as the parents themselves, and sometimes more so, in developing high qualities of character and spirituality in the student, but if we are to develop national repentance on a large scale, there must be infinitely more conscious effort put into the cause by the educators of our land. CHAPTER XI THE HOPE OF A PRODIGAL WORLD 

A man had two sons. The older was a sober, sedate young man, dutiful, industrious, devoted to his father's interests. The younger son seemed of a different nature. 

He loved play more than work. He was most happy in the company of convivial friends and he was discontented with his home surroundings. This man loved his sons. They were equally dear to him. 

One day the younger son, being unwilling longer to endure the restraints of his home, said to his father, "Father, give me the portion of goods that falleth to me, and I will ask nothing more of thee." When the old man heard this, he was very sad, because he realized that his son was forsaking him and abandoning his home with its standards and traditions, but he yielded to the young man's request and divided his substance with him and his older brother.

When the young son received his portion, he converted everything into cash and rode away to a rich city where he meant to make his home. He secured a fine house and embellished it with costly furnishings. He bought slaves and then invited his friends to partake of his hospitality. He made merry with his guests and he thought he was happy. He violated all the command-

ments, which his father had given him-his youthful training must have given him some compunction—but he told himself he had what he wanted. 

He thought that the money bags he had brought with him were bottomless, that his means would last forever, but he discovered that he was mistaken. No money bags ever are bottomless.

He was soon without funds. He lost his house and his servants, and his friends went with them. He was destitute. To add to his misery a famine came upon the land. There was little food for anyone and none for him who had no money with which to buy. In his desperation, he begged work of a citizen whom he had known in his better days and the man hired him as a swineherd, a keeper of hogs, the most menial and degraded of all positions to which a Jew could come. So reduced was he that he ate the very husks with the hogs he fed and became emaciated, pale, and sick. 

Then he began to realize what he had lost and the mistakes he had made. He thought of his home and he said to himself, "How many hired servants of my father's have bread enough and to spare, and I perish with hunger! I will arise and go to my father, and will say unto him, Father, I have sinned against heaven, and before thee, and am no more worthy to be called thy son; make me as one of thy hired servants."

After he had taken this resolution, with great difficulty, in his weakness, he found his way back to his father's home, but when he reached there, he was so ashamed and humilated that he dared not go in, but "stood afar off," wondering and anxious as to how his father might receive him.

I give to you this somewhat elaborated parable of the prodigal son as an introduction to a few observations which I wish to make on the subject of forgiveness. 

I have always regarded this parable as one of the most significant and encouraging of all the things left to us by the Savior. It breathes the true spirit of the 

Christ. I believe that He gave this story to the hardhearted, rigorous of His day, steeped in the letter of the law, and forgetful of its spirit, to teach them the true genius of the gospel which He had brought. I think that He intended the father in the story to represent the Father of us all and the two sons to stand for the masses of humanity.

He had the wayward son commit the most grievous sins, including the renouncing of his patrimony, and forsaking not only his father's institution, but his way of life as well. Such actions, in the estimate of the lawgivers and judges of that day, would have merited complete ostracism and cutting off. But the Savior wished to teach that there is mercy, as well as justice, in the gospel plan, and that the Father forgives those who truly repent.

It would be a mistake to overlook the fact that this wild boy suffered for his transgressions. There is truly a penalty for the infraction of every law. The Savior, in this parable, did not exempt the young man from reaping the consequences of his unrighteous acts. It was made clear that his degradation almost cost him his life; but when he did acknowledge his wrong, and in abject humility did seek his father's pardon, it was not denied.

The elder son seemed to resent his father's kindly treatment of his brother and reproached him: “Lo, these many years do I serve thee, neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment; and yet thou never gavest me a kid, that I might make merry with my friends." He evidently was one of the people who did not understand the meaning of mercy, but his good old father consoled him with the words, "Son thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine. It was meet that we should make merry, and be glad; for this thy brother was dead, and is alive again; and was lost, and is found."

What encouraging and admirable principles are here stated, that righteousness shall be rewarded; that mercy shall not rob justice, but that through mercy and repentance man may be saved from eternal condemnation for the infraction of law; and that there is great rejoicing when one who has made mistakes turns from his wayward course and in penitence and humility comes again to the fold of Christ.

CHAPTER XII THE LAW OF TITHING 

I propose to discuss one of the oldest subjects in revealed religion. It is a subject which is usually invested with temporal significance, but to my thinking it carries also the highest spiritual implications. I wish to treat briefly both aspects of the subject, which is the law of tithing.

The giving of a tithe, or a tenth of one's income, to the Lord and His work dates back to the very beginning of the sacred records. We are told that Abraham paid tithes to the great high priest, Melchizedek, and mention is made of the law and the practice in many scriptures, both ancient and modern. Taking into consideration the nature of man, it has always seemed reasonable to me that in the very beginning the Lord should have prescribed this test of sincerity in faith and worship. 

It is such an easy thing to make protestations by word of mouth and sing praises that it is no wonder they were not counted sufficient. The real essence of the Christian gospel is giving. God gave His Son and His Son gave 

His life, not only to bring salvation to humanity, but also to teach men and women the transcendent message of service and giving.

Now of course there are many things which may be given, but it is apparent that it is the Lord's view that

there is nothing more important for a man to give than his money and his property. It is said that Jesus had more to say about man's attitude toward money and property than about any other one thing. In sixteen of thirty-eight parables Jesus made this His theme. It has been well said that the measure of a man's Christianity may be determined by the way he gets and spends his money.

Many years ago I read a little volume entitled, "Dealing Squarely with God." In it was this paragraph: "After all, is not money myself? Money is the medium for which men exchange their abilities, ingenuity, and labor. When a man gives his money he is giving himself, and the way a man gives his money is the way he gives himself. Money is myself. I am a laboring man, we will say, and can wield a pickaxe and hire myself out for a week at two dollars a day. (That was many years ago.) At the close of the week I get twelve dollars and I put it in my pocket. What is that. twelve dollars? It is a week's worth of my muscle put into greenbacks and pocketed. That is, I have got a week's worth of myself in my pocket." So, you see, on this hypothesis, when a man gives the money that he's earned, he's giving literally of himself. Giving is worship. We are commanded "not to appear before the Lord empty-handed." Not that the Lord needs the gift, but that man needs to give.

The first principle of religion is recognition of Godfaith. The real test of that recognition is giving. By that test we may judge with accuracy the religious attitude of our country. Years ago statistics revealed the fact that more money was spent for face powders and cosmetics;

117 more for ice cream, soft drinks and chewing gum; more for cigarettes, in each of these items respectively, than the total sum expended to support all churches. May that not be a criterion by which we may safely judge the religious attitude, the deepset religious feeling of the people of the country? Do not the words of the prophet Malachi seem pertinent: "Will a man rob God? Yet ye have robbed me. But ye say, wherein have we robbed thee? In tithes and offerings."

We do not rob God by withholding our gift in the sense that we deprive Him of the substance of earth. 

He always has that substance, never relinquishing it. But we rob Him of the satisfaction and the joy that He must feel when His children respond to His mandates and open their hearts in giving and in worship. Someone has said, "God never gives a quit-claim deed. He only grants a leasehold estate, and he who receives the lease must ever return the rental."

Christ bought us with a price, so we were told in the scriptures. Is it to be thought that we are to gain salvation without a price, without giving and paying for it? Whenever we speak of paying in this sense we do not mean that pay which is given as if in barter, but we mean the return of substance which is committed to our stewardship and which we hold in trust for the one who has so blessed us.

The Church generally is probably the only society in the world where a man is not suspended from membership for failure to pay his dues. I think that in substantially every other organized body of men for social or material gain if a man fails to pay his stipulated con-

tribution he is dropped. While the Church does not drop from membership those who fail to pay, I feel very certain that those who fail to pay their stipulated portion automatically drop themselves from the real advantages of Church participation and the blessings that inure from activity within it.

I like to think of the Lord as a partner, because the essence of a partnership is a sharing of profits. It is, however, indispensable in a partnership that there shall also be a sharing of the burdens of the enterprise. The honor and the satisfaction that come to one in realization that he lives in partnership with God is to me a lofty and exalting thought. One cannot hope to realize the profits from that venture without bearing his portion of the expense-the giving which is requisite. "Prove me now herewith, saith the Lord of hosts, if I will not open the windows of heaven," speaking to those whom He had admonished to pay their tithes and offerings. What comes from the windows of heaven?

Both temporal and spiritual blessings. Temporal blessings accrue largely from the cultivation of thrift habits. The payment of tithes of necessity compels an orderly arrangement of one's affairs. Accounting is indispensable.

Budgeting is necessary. Saving follows. All of which are necessary to financial success. 

It was Victor Hugo who said: "Above all, teach the children to save. Economy is the sure foundation for all virtues." I once heard a banker say that if tithing served no other purpose than to secure an orderly adjustment of one's affairs, a budgeting of the income and expenses, it would be invaluable. I feel sure that he who pays his tithes not only has a better conception of economy, but

119 he is indulging in a practice which will bring him into better thrift habits and enable him to go forward toward financial prosperity. 

One who is honest with God is apt to be honest with his neighbor and with his employer. The need for honesty is attested everywhere, and I can well advocate the payment of tithing in a straightforward, square, honest way as being a safe foundation on which to build those principles of integrity that shall make honest men and women in the community. 

The need of the tithe in the prosecution of God's work must be apparent to everyone. There are so many avenues in which sums may be expended to promote the work that I can scarcely take time to mention them. The building and maintenance of all the churches require large sums of money. While it is the pride of America that every community can boast a church and a school, yet I feel sure that conditions in our country would justify many more such establishments than we now have. 

Not long ago I had the privilege of traveling in one of the missions in which there were many rural sections which are not in the van of our progress and civilization. 

I was happy to note that our Church had caused to be erected a number of inexpensive, but beautiful, small chapels. Many of the people who resided in the neighborhood of these chapels lived in poor and neglected homes. I could well conceive the influence that these church buildings might exert, not only in the furtherance of religious views, but in their effect on the home life, the community life, the habits, and the practices of the people. These little chapels were clean and orderly, and 

I am sure that they bring an inspiration to many hun-

dreds of home-owners to clean up their establishments, to live in a more orderly and more beautiful way. Why, if our own Church were endowed with the means which would come from the full payment of tithing from all its members, such buildings could be extended throughout many parts of our country, and in other lands, when circumstances may permit, with wonderfully beneficial results.

And, in addition to the building and maintenance of churches, the work of our people contemplates the erection of temples and the carrying forward of work therein at very considerable expense. Our whole missionary cause is in large measure dependent upon the financial support that comes to the Church, although the missionaries themselves contribute their time and personal expense. The facilities which are now available for the spread of the gospel of Christ are expensive, so that there are innumerable ways for the necessary spending of money to further the cause of Christ. What a tremendous gain it would be for humanity everywhere if men would put more of their substance into religious endeavor! 

Now I wish to mention another aspect of this subject which is of the utmost importance. It transcends the temporalities of which I have spoken,-vital as they are. 

My observation and experience have convinced me that there is nothing which brings more spiritual growth and power than the honest observance of the law of tithing and generous giving to the cause of Christ. The prime purpose of religion is to bring that power to man. For it the whole organization of the Church is maintained. 

So it is fortunate indeed that he who gives his righteous gift helps the cause and helps himself.

THE LAW OF TITHING 

121 To get the full spiritual value out of this law of God, every man who pays his tithing should enjoy it. 

The gospel of Christ is a gospel of enjoyment. “Men are that they may have joy," is one of the cardinal principles of revealed religion. When one pays his tithing without enjoyment he is robbed of a part of the blessing. 

He must learn to give cheerfully, willingly, and generously, and his gift will be blessed. In order that he may receive more enjoyment, he needs to pay more frequently. 

Why deprive one's self of the joy that comes from this voluntary giving until the end of the year, when by payments throughout the year we may increase and enhance not only the joy of our giving but the ease of it?

I have found it to be a very difficult problem in mathematics to pay one-tenth out of one-twelfth. I commend that thought to those who are receiving monthly stipends and who indulge the practice of paying their tithing at the close of the year. I am sure you will find it very difficult indeed to get the tenth out of the twelfth if your tithing remains for payment until the last month. 

I can heartily recommend to you the payment of your tithes as funds come into your hands, not only because it will be easier, but because greater blessings will inure to you.

In the Church which I have the honor to represent, we consecrate our lives to the advancement of the cause of God. There is no higher evidence of that consecration than this giving which has been enjoined upon us by the Lord. The poet has well said: "Who gives himself with his gift feeds three-himself, his hungering neighbor, and me." So the law of the tithe is the epitome of the gospel. It is genuine worship and true recogni-

tion of the sovereignty of God. It is real consecration, the giving of the muscle and energy of life to the cause; and it begets the abundant life of love and service for which the Christ came. It is a measurement of true religion. By the extent of its observance every man may determine for himself the vitality of his own faith and his love of God.

A prophet has said, "The tenth shall be holy unto the Lord." It will be holy unto you, men and women of Israel, if you give it lovingly, joyously, willingly, to the great cause. And it will be holy to all men everywhere who will make generous contributions to the cause of true religion in the earth. We are sorely in need of 

God's blessings today. It is a mistake to think that they will come without putting ourselves in line to receive them. Supplication in our extremity may not be sufficient. Demonstration of our love and devotion will be more adequate. God help us all to emulate the divine example and grow in the stature of Christian manhood and womanhood by the liberal giving of ourselves and our substance to the great cause, I humbly pray, in His name.

CHAPTER XIII CONFERENCE IN WAR TIME 

Today in the Tabernacle at Salt Lake City, from which I speak tonight, began the One Hundred Thirteenth Annual Conference of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. While attendance at the conference has been restricted because of war conditions, there have been invited officials and representatives of the Church in the number of about twelve thousand, and most of these men are here participating in the meetings. They have come from nearly all sections of the United States, from the East coast to the West, from Canada and Mexico, and some other foreign countries. The usual attendance of members of the Church generally, and the women's organizations in particular, has been discouraged because of difficulties in travel.

The conference, however, in spite of the handicaps which prevail, is an imposing occasion. It was an inspiring sight in the sessions today to see this historic building filled with earnest men intent on receiving instructions and giving consideration to matters which should enable them better to lead their organizations and the people in the accomplishment of the high purposes to which their lives have been dedicated. Mormonism, as people generally call it, had its beginning in the early part of the nineteenth century. In 

1820, a young man fourteen years of age, residing in the western part of New York State, became much interested in, and confused by religious discussions and contentions carried on by various sects in the neighborhood of his home. Religion was popular at the time and everyone was expected to ally himself with one church or another. 

This young man was very serious in his consideration of the choice he was to make. He was careful and he wanted to be right. The investigations he was able to make did not satisfy him. He was not educated. His family was poor and could not afford schooling for him, but he was intelligent and he had some knowledge of the Bible. 

In his perplexity he read the passage from James: "If any of you lack wisdom, let him ask of God who giveth to all men liberally and upbraideth not; and it shall be given him." He believed that he could rely on this promise and he went into the nearby woods to pray. He wrote a rather minute account of his experience. The substance of it only, can I give you here.

He said that when he knelt down and began to pray, he was suddenly seized by an unexpected and terrific force that threw him violently to the ground and seemed to threaten his life. After struggling with it for what seemed to him a long period, and when he felt he was

He was made aware that one was God, the Father, who introduced the other as His Son. These personages communed with him, and he was told that none of the churches seeking his allegiance was the true church, but that the gospel in its purity was again to be restored, and that he was to be selected to be an instrument in its restoration and in the establishment of the true Church of Christ. He was then told to make preparation for the work he should be required to do and promised that he should be visited thereafter. 

Almost completely overcome by this tremendous experience, he made his way, with great difficulty, back to his home and told his parents. During the several years that followed, while he led the life ordinary with a farmer's boy, he had several heavenly visitations, and in 1823 he was directed by an angelic messenger to a hill not far from the place of his residence, known now as the Hill Cumorah, where there was disclosed to him a stone box in which were hidden plates of gold with engravings thereon and an instrument for their translation, all of ancient origin. After a time the contents of this box were placed in his possession and he spent many arduous and eventful months in making a translation of the ancient characters engraven on the plates. He made the translation, as he testified, with the aid of the instruments which were with the plates and with certain spiritual endowments which were given to him. He had assistance in the writing of the translation, and a number of other persons saw the plates and handled them. From

In this book were set forth the records of ancient peoples who had inhabited portions of the western world in both North and South America. Political and religious experiences of these people were set forth, as were also accounts of their origin, migration and ultimate fate. 

Perhaps the most important thing in this book was the record of the visit of Jesus Christ to this hemisphere at a time immediately following His crucifixion, and the establishment of His gospel and His Church in this land under His own personal supervision.

During the time this young man was engaged in the translation of the plates and subsequent to the publication of the Book of Mormon, he was subjected to a great deal of persecution, many indignities, and much public abuse because of the claims and assertions which he made that he had had divine revelation and had received the ministration of angelic beings. He and his family had been of no prominence in the community where they lived, but he was hounded from place to place and obliged to resort to many secret devices in order to complete the work to which he was assigned. 

He confided in his own family and comparatively few other friends, but the whole countryside was aroused in antagonism against him.

The story of his life reads like a history of the Savior whose representative his followers claim him to be.

The fast-growing church which Joseph Smith established did not die with him, but the opposition and persecution which he encountered persisted against it.

CHAPTER XIV  

In the early part of the nineteenth century no such science as we have today had been built around human health and diet. Then relatively little was known about the chemistry of foods, and the learning on physiology and anatomy has since been greatly developed. Calories and vitamins, so common in every day conversation now, were not in the vocabularies of those days, and the large volume of statistical data gathered by insurance companies, governmental agencies, and other sources on overand under-weight, diet, various forms of disease, and their relation to human health was not available and generally circularized as it is today. 

Yet, of course, health was a matter of private and public concern then as it is now. People have always recognized its relation to comfort and happiness and it is instinctive within the race to strive for longer life. 

If in the year 1833, a group of scientists had evolved a set of health rules and principles and had published them to the world with the results of experiments and other scientific data demonstrating the efficacy of these principles, it would undoubtedly have been accounted an inestimable boon to humanity.

Well, there was published in that year a code of

health laws so comprehensive as to include not only physical health, but mental and spiritual vitality as well, and so fundamentally sound and effective that all the scientific research and statistical observations conducted in more than a century and a decade have never found a single flaw in the code.

This code was a very unusual document. Examination of it convinces one that the impelling motive within it is to help people. It is apparent in its design that its author had a good knowledge of public relations and well knew the psychology of approach to persuade and win people. The law was not given by "commandment or constraint," but it was "sent" by way of "greeting" and "given for a principle with promise." The promises which are set forth to induce compliance with the laws are among the most desirable and encouraging that can be imagined. I will mention them in detail later. To encourage all to comply with the provisions of the code, it is also set forth that it is adapted "to the capacity of the weak," but a caution is wisely provided that those who seek to keep the law must beware of the temptations, "evils and designs which do and will exist in the hearts of conspiring men." Then, in the introduction to the code, in order to make sure that although the law. is sent by way of greeting and not by constraint, it is nevertheless essential and indispensable to health and happiness, it is declared to be "the order and will of God in temporal salvation.”

After this appealing and wise introduction, the code enjoins, as inimical to health, the use of three substances which have long been used by man. The first is intoxicating liquors, which are characterized as strong drinks;

the second is tobacco; and the third is "hot drinks," which are interpreted to mean all beverages containing habitforming drugs, such as caffeine in tea and coffee and various forms of colas, etc.

The next part of the code has been characterized as setting forth "positive health factors,"-indicating what foods are good for man and to some extent how and when they should be used. All fruits and herbs are commended with directions that they be eaten “in the season thereof,” the flesh of beasts and fowls is permitted with the provision that "they are to be used sparingly," and that they should be used "only in times of winter or of cold or famine." "All grain is ordained for the use of man and of beasts," but wheat is set apart particularly for man. 

Then follow the rewards, of which I spoke, which are to come to all who obey the code. The promises are threefold. First, for bodily health; second, for "wisdom and great treasures of knowledge;" and third, for long and abundant life. 

Now, perhaps, it would be interesting to discuss briefly a few specific items mentioned in this remarkable code. You will remember that it contained a warning against the "evils and designs" of "conspiring men.'

This prediction was in 1833, long before the development of modern advertising and the inauguration of such gigantic business concerns as we have today. It is true that liquor and tobacco were known and used in that day, but it is altogether unlikely that the wildest imagination ever envisioned the colossal business which has developed out of the exploitation of these two articles. It is probable, although I do not state it as a fact, because

I do not have supporting data, that these two things are more widely advertised than any other product consumed by man. Billions of dollars are invested in their production and distribution, and "conspiring men" have foisted their use on a credulous public, young and old alike. 

Such "conspiring men," praying upon human gullibility and weakness, and prompted only by selfish interests, have glutted the country with the most alluring and seductive advertising the civilized world has ever known. 

In the sales methods employed all the finer sentiments, emotions, and relationships of the human family,-beauty, love, courtship, marriage, fatherhood, motherhood, friendship, and even patriotism have all been perverted to serve the selfish purposes and pad the pockets of the promoters of this nefarious traffic. There is scarcely a newspaper or magazine in the whole country that does not derive a large measure of support from such advertising. Billboards with alluring pictures line the highways from one end of the land to the other and, what is perhaps more regrettable than all, celebrities of stage and screen and the athletic world, the heroes and heroines of youth, are bribed and seduced to lend their names and fame to deceive their admirers and lead them to the adoption of practices inimical to their welfare. Every word of this prophetic utterance of more than a century ago has been literally fulfilled.

Next, the use of intoxicating liquor is enjoined and declared not to be good for man, “neither meet in the sight of" God. I think it is scarcely necessary to tell you that it has long been definitely ascertained that alcohol is not a food, that it has no body-building value. A long list of authorities might be cited to support this statement,

but time will not permit. Any bibliography on the subject will be sufficient to satisfy one who cares to investigate. 

Alcohol is a poison, a racial poison, one author has called it, because its harmful effects are transmitted to the children and the children's children. One of the most unfortunate and regrettable things growing out of governmental control of alcoholic beverages is that in harmony with laws that have long been in operation, requiring ingredients to be properly labeled for the protection of the public, it has been, up to date, impossible to require that the label of "poison" be placed on alcohol. That measure itself might have been of tremendous help to countless persons.

The figures on the production and consumption of alcoholic beverages, like those of our war expenditures, defy the conception of the human mind. I shall not attempt to present any of them, because they do not particularly serve my purpose at this time. Most unfortunately they show that drinking and drunkenness are increasing and the ills and crimes attendant upon them also. The only item that shows a decline is in automobile accidents due to drunkenness, and we all know that rubber, and not drink, is responsible for this decrease.

I do not need to point out the waste in human life, energy, and happiness due to alcohol, and the loss to the nation in the inefficiency, crime and disease which it causes. What about the generations to come if its use is not curtailed? I sat in a train a few weeks ago riding a couple of seats behind two soldiers. The one, much older than the other, was attempting to get the young boy to take a drink out of a flask. I only heard a part of the conversation, but enough to assure me that the

boy had first protested and that the older fellow persisted, made fun of the young man, and finally got him to imbibe. It may have been his first drink. Of course I had no way of knowing, but during the course of the rather long journey the young man was persuaded to take several more drinks 'till finally he was drunk with the older man, joking over him and poking fun at him. 

I had great difficulty restraining myself from some kind of interference. How much of this thing goes on I do not know, but I do know that it is a great pity that many thousands of our young men who have left good homes and good environments to fight the battles for freedom and country should be exposed to such conditions. 

What I just said about alcohol applies very largely to tobacco. The learning of the last several decades has proved beyond question that it too is a poison without food value. Again, in spite of its ill effects on the human system, its use has grown tremendously. The youth are now, perhaps, its chief victims, boys and girls alike. The figures that represent consumption of this noxious weed, like the others I mentioned, are incomprehensible.

Pretty much the same is likewise true of the products containing caffeine, tea, coffee, and the colas. The injurious effects of caffeine have not only been demonstrated, scientifically, but they are even acknowledged in the trade itself by the advertising of the merits of noncaffeine coffee and other substitute beverages. So I submit that the evidence available, although I have not had time to analyze it for you, is sufficient to fully substantiate the correctness and soundness of the injunction against the use of the products I have mentioned given more than a century ago.

Briefly, I wish to call your attention to the positive health factors in this code of health and the substantiation for its doctrines which has developed over the years. 

The comparatively recent enlargement of the knowledge of vitamins and their essentiality in human health, and the now better established laws of nutrition, furnish much corroboration for the counsels given-that herbs and fruits should be eaten in the season thereof, that meat should be used sparingly, that grain is the staff of life, and that wheat is designed especially for man. Our present highly developed dietary principles could all be evolved out of the statements and their connotations set forth in this early code of health. In fact one of my colleagues, a scientific man, has already done just that thing. Here, then, written a hundred thirteen years ago, is the foundation of the health laws of today and of some that may be developed in the future. 

Now, where did this code of health come from? 

Who was its author? Many of you will have rightly guessed before now that my references have been to a document emanating from Joseph Smith, published, as I have said, in the year 1833. Joseph Smith was not a scientist. It is doubtful that he was even acquainted with the literature that might have been available to a student of his day bearing upon the subject of health. In all probability he did not understand the scientific reasons which lay behind this excellent statement of health principles, but he did have an unusual understanding of the nature and constitution of man. He knew that the living part of man is his spirit. It had been made clear to him that the spirit in man is the literal child of God, and he knew from the Bible, as from other sources, that the

body is the tabernacle wherein the spirit dwells. He knew that no man could take poison into his body and thus defile the tabernacle of the spirit without impairing the physical organism and without offering affront to God, the Father of the spirit. He knew, therefore, that this Word of Wisdom which he had received was not only a temporal law, but a spiritual one as well. 

Indeed, that was manifest in the document itself. For the great blessing promised for observance of the law was a spiritual blessing-"wisdom and great treasures of knowledge, even hidden treasures."

This, then, was perhaps the most important deduction that Joseph made from the revelation of the Word of Wisdom, for it was a revelation from God to His prophet that the laws of health have spiritual, as well as temporal significance. Many people have been acquainted with the restraints of the Word of Wisdom,they have been pretty well advertised-but comparatively few understood the true genius and philosophy of this great document. 

I remember many years ago sitting down to a meal with a friend in one of the hotels in Washington. I observed that he did not use tea nor coffee, nor tobacco. He likewise noticed that I abstained from these things and we commented upon it. He said, "I have long been convinced that the items prescribed by your Word of Wisdom are not good for me. I am sure my health is better without them and I think all men would be better without them. I can well understand the physiological reasons for your Word of Wisdom, but I have never been able to understand why you attach any religious significance to it." I then told him what I am now trying to tell

you. That in our estimation the spiritual aspects of this code are more important than the temporal ones. We have long observed that there is a very definite relation between a man's spiritual power and the purity of his life. We have noticed that when a boy begins to smoke, he usually ceases to pray. When he does that, he often absents himself from the Church and its institutions and forsakes the company of good people whose influence upon his life would be helpful. The Church does not ostracize boys and men for this practice, but they often ostracize themselves, very much to the regret of their associates who love them and would do all in their power to help them. I realize that this may not be true to the same extent with those who do not entertain the same concepts as do we with reference to personal wholesomeness. 

So, my friends, I do not hesitate to commend to all the principles, the restraints, the cautions, and the promises of this great code of health, the Word of Wisdom. I know of nothing wiser and more calculated to bring freedom from pain and distress and ultimate contentment and happiness. The fact that it was given as a law of God adds to its force and vitality with everyone who reverences the great Lawgiver. The money that could be saved by the observance of this law would be the difference between success and failure for many men and their families. The conversion of all the industries which supply and distribute these harmful products would divert untold millions into infinitely more profitable channels. Compliance with these health rules would tend to insure sound bodies and alert minds to the populations of the world. Such bodily strength and mental

vigor would guarantee a power in men and women adequate to meet the necessities of this day, and always. 

And transcending all these the acceptance of the divine concept of the body as the tabernacle of the spirit, and the obligations to the divine Creator which go with it, would do more to elevate man to his rightful place as a son of God, “a little lower than the angels," and attain the high spiritual powers with which he is by providence endowed, than any other one thing we can contemplate.

CHAPTER XV FREEDOM FROM BONDAGE OF DEBT 

It seems appropriate at this time to repeat some counsel which has come from the leadership of the Church since its earliest days. This is the counsel to get and keep out of debt. I think I have never known of conditions and circumstances where this advice is more applicable than to those of the present day. We have never known a period of higher wages and incomes for the people generally, nor one of more universal employment. Every one who has health,-old and young, men and women,— can secure work if he or she has the will so to do. When will ever come a better time to pay personal obligations?

One of the present urgent needs is to curb inflation and the ever-increasing competition by private citizens against their government and among themselves for the purchase of goods and services. Of course rationing and price ceilings do something toward that end, but what is most needed at the moment is a voluntary reduction of purchasing power, and it would be far bet ter to be voluntary than involuntary. If everyone would only pay his debts and do his part in the war effort, much would be accomplished to relieve the pressure on the limited supplies of the country and thereby enable

the government to secure its necessities at a lower cost. which, in the end, the citizens themselves have to pay. 

This would also reduce the cost of their own living. 

Recent depressions in our own and other countries, following inflation, ought to be so vivid in the memories of the people as to constitute a warning that would induce immediate action. Fellow citizens, take no chances with inflation; it is much too dangerous.

I am well aware that for many people the high wages of today offer opportunities to satisfy many cravings and longings they have had for a long time. Women who have never had a fur coat, now for the first time in their lives have the money to buy one. 

There are many other items of dress which the more unfortunate have never been able to enjoy; there are conveniences and embellishments for the home which they have long wanted and perhaps envied in the possession of the more successful. Now they have the money to buy these desirable things, that is, to the extent they are available, and I, for one, wish that they could have them.

Debt is a taskmaster to be feared almost as much as the dictators themselves. It has enslaved thousands in its meshes. It has wrecked happy homes. Debt robs a man of much of the energy and support which he is otherwise able to give to the Church and to other good causes. I have known men in high Church positions being compelled to relinquish their offices by such circumstances. Debt, with many people who place before everything else their obligations and opportunity to develop and spread the gospel of Christ, has all too frequently retarded, and sometimes completely inhibited

Even with partial compliance they have achieved over the years remarkable success in this respect. 

Too much cannot be said of the obligations which men have to prepare themselves for something beyond making money. It is safe to say that the world would not be in its present condition if many more of its people had seriously and steadfastly devoted themselves to non-materialistic endeavor. There will never be a better time than now to discharge obligations. Creditors may not now be pressing their demands, but we can rest assured that there will come a day when they will want their money and debtors will not have it to pay. Everyone who experi-

I know of nothing more effective to this end than to get and keep out of debt and establish a reserve. I don't think any of you have ever known a man to "go broke" who was not in debt.

I hope this time-tested counsel will not be nullified by quibbling over what are "legitimate debts" to retain and what are the ones that ought to be paid. I think we can safely say that at this time, where resources permit, all debts should be paid. Long-time mortgages and other kindred obligations should be "whittled down" as fast as possible to prepare for the exigencies to follow, and unsecured obligations particularly should be wiped out without delay where income will permit. Of course, it is recognized that the capital requirements of most businesses and many individuals at times warrant borrowing, but sound business practice has pretty well defined such circumstances and all will do well to keep within the limitations experience has proved to be prudent. It is one thing to incur an obligation to provide a home for a family to be met out of the reasonable expectations of the borrower's income. It is quite another thing to mortgage one's future for years to come in the purchase of non-essential, even though desirable, commodities. It is fortunate that the government has put a check on this practice of installment buying which has brought ruin to so many people. It is a pretty safe rule for individuals, as well as businesses, to expand out of profits.

CHAPTER XVI EASTER THOUGHTS 

Easter Sunday for all who hear my voice is now in its closing hours. During the day we have listened to soul-stirring music and eloquent addresses which have served to focus our attention on the greatest event in all history, the resurrection of the Lord Jesus Christ. 

Some of us, too, may have seen the children in their play with their beribboned bunnies, their fancy baskets and multicolored eggs. Others of us may have watched the procession of new hats and gay apparel, which this springtime season always brings out. To many the day may have been a holiday; to some a holy day; and to most perhaps a little of both. I find myself wondering at the close of the day what are the impressions and lessons it has left.

In the first place, I believe that this day has served to recall to our minds the actuality of the resurrection. 

This is so, at least, with those who accept the divinity of the Savior. We have again been reminded of this central fact in the life and ministry of our Lord. The record of the event has been recited for us again,-how, following His crucifixion and the mutilation of His body, Jesus was taken from the cross and laid in the sepulchre of a friend, there wrapped in burial clothes and His body anointed with embalming spices; how, after such laying

away, a stone was placed to close the entrance to the sepulchre and Roman guards stationed to guard it; how, in the early morn of the third day following

His cruel death, the stone was rolled away by an angel who so frightened the guards that they ran away in great fear and consternation; how His intimate friends came shortly thereafter to perform further kindly offices for Him and in astonishment discovered He was gone; how He first appeared and brought comfort and reassurance to the woman who loved Him; and how thereafter He established to the satisfaction of other friends, and strangers too, some doubting, the reality of His life after death and the literal fulfillment of His remarkable prophecy then so vaguely comprehended that He should be killed, but "He shall rise the third day." (Mark 9:31).

This profoundly impressive story we have heard retold this Easter Day. There is within it more significance for the life and happiness of man than in any other circumstance of which we have record, unless it be in the account of man's creation as the son of God. 

It is out of this transcedent event in the life of Jesus that we derive the supreme blessing of humankind, the immortality of the soul, and eternal life with God. 

I believe, my friends, that the resurrection is as well attested by credible witnesses and records as any other event in history of equal antiquity. It has often been challenged, but never disproved. Millions and millions of people have had and do now have an inner conviction of the truth of this record, and it has brought to them and continues to bring to them more comfort and more enduring hope than comes from any other source

151 in the whole wide world. The story persists through the centuries of time because it is true. Christ lived a mortal life, died, and rose from the grave that you and I and all mankind might have life everlasting.

The inestimable blessing of the resurrection inures to all. Every man, woman and child who has lived or who will live is the beneficiary, "for as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive." (I Corinthians 15:22). It is interesting to note that in the record called to our attention this day, no mention is made of any requirement on the part of man to qualify himself to receive the blessing of resurrection. It will come to all men through the atonement of Christ irrespective of the things they do in life. Everyone will come forth hereafter to receive judgment for the works of his life. It is universal. Mention is made of an order in the resurrection of people, some will come forth before others, but in the end every spirit will be reunited with an immortal body to constitute an eternal living soul.

In this connection, while you ponder the resurrection, you might be interested in a concept of life hereafter which has come to us from a divine revelation which is not universally known. You will remember that the Savior said to His disciples, "In my Father's house are many mansions: if it were not so I would have told you. I go to prepare a place for you. that where I am, there ye may be also." (John 14:2, 3). 

You will remember also that Paul said, "There is one glory of the sun, and another glory of the moon, and another glory of the stars: for one star differeth from another star in glory. So also is the resurrection of the dead." (I Corinthians 15:41, 42).

This revelation, of which I speak, was given in the year 1832 to clarify the scriptural statements which I have just read to you and some others like them. In this revelation, it was made plain that there are preferential places and stations in the life to come, as there are in this life. The kingdoms or glories into which men may go after the resurrection are therein specified as are also the conditions which must be fulfilled to enter the various kingdoms or stations. In this and other modern revelations it is made clear that obedience to all the principles of the gospel of Christ is essential, not as a condition for the resurrection, which comes to all, but as a prerequisite to entrance into the Celestial or highest kingdom of the hereafter. It is pointed out that there are gradations of glory or reward for all according to the lives they lead, but the highest attainment is eternal life in the kingdom where God and Christ dwell. The ordinances and laws of the gospel are essential for entrance into that kingdom, and it is the function of the established Church of Christ to perform the required ordinances. 

The import of this and other latter-day revelations is not that all must belong to the re-established Church of Christ and receive the ordinances in it administered to come forth in the new life of the resurrection and attain what is sometimes called general salvation through the grace and the atonement of the Savior, but that acceptance of the restored gospel and obedience thereto are indispensable to the attainment of the highest glory in the hereafter available to man. On the assumption that it is natural for every man to want the best, he can achieve that ambition only by complying with the

153 rules by which it is attainable. So today we give thanks to Almighty God, not only for the immeasurable gift of immortality through the resurrection, but also for the incomparably beautiful and desirable prospect of exaltation in the Celestial Kingdom through the gospel of His Son.

I will now leave the sacred record which has been revivified for us this Easter Day and present for your consideration a few items which, I believe, this time of commemoration suggests and which we may well and profitably preserve in our thinking.

We are engaged in a great and cruel war. So universal are its demands that there is scarcely a family which has not someone in the country's service, and we are only just beginning to fight. Every day new lists of casualties come,-more boys are killed, wounded, and maimed and more hearts are broken. As the days go on these sorrows will increase. Every week, for how long a time nobody knows, will bring more widows, more orphans, more bereaved mothers and fathers, and sad sweethearts. This is the awful penalty of war. It always has been and it always will be. 

If a great grief comes to your home, you will need help. Nothing is more precious than one's own flesh and blood, and the loss of a manly son, a devoted husband, or father, or a life mate-to-be is not easily requited. 

Kinspeople, good friends and neighbors may come to bring you sympathy, love and kindness. This will help, but it will not be enough. You will tell yourself that he died in a great cause, in the service of his country. 

You will convince yourself that it was a noble sacrifice. 

You will be proud of him, but your heart will still ache

and you will have an irrepressible longing once more to see his face, to hear his voice, and have his arms around you and feel again the warmth and tenderness of his loving embrace. Surely you will need help.

I know of one source only from which that help may be secured. It is from this selfsame Jesus who gave 

His life for others and on the third day took it up again. 

Even with His help you will still weep, but you will not weep in vain. If you will let Him, He will take away all bitterness from your loss. He will touch your broken heart and it will mend, not all at once, perhaps, but gradually and surely. If you will listen to the voice of 

His Spirit and His holy Word, He will convince you that your loved one is not lost, but only separated from you for a time, and that you may confidently look forward to a happy companionship in the not too distant future where there will be no more war, no more cruelty, and no more sad partings from those we love.

It is true that it is necessary that you make preparation for such a glad reunion, but you will know if you listen to Him, what you are to do and you will have the joyful assurance that His promises do not fail. This is the comfort and the hope, my friends, that you and I and all of us so sorely need in the days of trial ahead. 

This is the comfort which the one who knew sorrow better than any other left with His disciples to heal their grieving hearts and the broken hearts of all who should come after them. 

Thank the Lord that comfort is free,-free to everyone who will seek it. Rich or poor, the powerful or humble of the earth,-all need it when sorrow comes and all may receive it, if they will open their hearts to

May I give you one more thought from Easter. A peace will be made some day. It will be negotiated by men. All the world is hoping that it will be just and permanent. The last great war and its aftermath are so recent and so vivid in our memories that we are convinced that something which did not characterize the last arrangement must come into a newly established peace or it will be as futile as before. Some say that the thing most requisite is to render our enemies impotent. Let us not forget that that was done before, for a time at least. Various proposals are made for the establishment of a new world order. What form they will ultimately take no one knows, but this I know, and every follower of the Master knows it, that the peace of Christ will never descend upon the nations unless His Spirit is infused. into the hearts and deliberations of the men who write the peace and into their constituents as well. 

That peace is the peace of brotherhood and good will. It is the peace that grows out of charity, tolerance, kindness, and mutual consideration. It is not founded on force, nor a balance of power among nations, nor hatred, nor vengeance. Love is the keystone in the arch of its structure, righteousness and justice are its foundations. It seems to me that the overshadowing message of this Easter Day is for every follower of the risen Christ to pray for that peace to come to this stricken world, to so live that his prayers may be heard, and to strive with all his strength and power to bring it to pass. God grant that it may come quickly.

CHAPTER XVII FAMILY TIES 

The fact that this is Mother's Day and also the end of National Family Week pretty well dictates the subject of my discussion. The theme is one I have always thought important and upon which I have often spoken, but I confess that I despair of bringing to you anything new about it. So that if I prove to be guilty of repetition, either of my own or others' ideas on the subject, I must ask your pardon.

In the discussion of children you might be interested in a theological concept which I believe has relevancy in its bearing upon the whole subject of family relationship. 

This is the doctrine of antemortal existence referred to in the Bible, (John 6:62, John 16:28, John 17:5, John 

8:58, etc.—and also Numbers 16:22, 27:16 and Jeremiah 

1:5) but made plainer by some passages which have come to us in modern revelation. Here is one: "Now the Lord had shown unto me, Abraham, the intelligences that were organized before the world was, and among all these there were many of the noble and great ones. And God saw these souls that they were good, and he stood in the midst of them and he said: 

These I will make my rulers; for he stood among those that were spirits, and he saw that they were good; and he said unto me: Abraham, thou art one of them; thou wast

chosen before thou wast born." (P. of G. P., Abraham 

3:22, 23.)

If, as this doctrine affirms, the existence of our souls is eternal and mortality is but a phase of never-ending existence curtained off, as someone has suggested, between the veil of forgetfulness which shuts out all recollection of what went on before and the veil of futurity through which we must pass at death, surely such a concept invests our present life with more far-reaching and important consequences than it would otherwise hold. 

Who can say what foreordained potentialities are wrapped up in the little package which comes into your home so like a stranger? What was the spirit life of that child? 

Is he or she one of those "noble ones" with a rich background of prenatal experience selected to come forth in this age of opportunity to do a great work for God and man? Was your home chosen to give entry and nurture to a great soul because it was hoped that in the process of its development you would provide the things essential to its environment?

Please do not confuse this concept of spiritual preexistence and foreordination with the doctrine of predestination. The selection of our spiritual selves to take tabernacles in the flesh at any given time in the world's history does not contemplate interference with or any abrogation of the universal law of free agency. All are at liberty to choose the life they lead and upon the choice they make depends success or failure in fulfilling the high missions to which they may have been preappointed. On the other hand predestination assumes a fatalism beyond mortal control and is repugnant to free agency.

It would be a matter of infinite regret to a parent who in a day to come might be confronted by the Father of all the children of men with a statement like this: "I sent to your home a noble spirit to take on mortality with a great mission to be performed among men. 

I expected you to cherish and love this child which is

I don't know that many of you, my fellow parents, would envision such a judgment as among the possibilities. I readily concede that it may take no such crude form as that in which I have put it, but I deem it profitable to myself, and it may be for you, to measure parental responsibilities by some such conception of their eternal importance.

Coming now to what may be regarded as more practical aspects of family problems, may we consider for a moment situations which confront us today. Figures are available which indicate a pronounced increase in juvenile delinquency. The F. B. I. some time ago issued a statement revealing a very substantial increase in the number of arrests of both boys and girls. The reports of our own law enforcement officers confirm this tendency.

While the figures themselves are probably beyond disputa-

tion, there are sociologists who contend that such delinquencies have not perceptibly increased but that violations have been more diligently sought out and have been given much wider publicity than heretofore. I am not in a position to decide as between these views, but I am certain that whether there is a decrease or an increase there is altogether too much letting down of good moral standards and too many tragic cases bringing sadness to many homes. 99 When misfortunes from moral lapses come to a family it is not much comfort to say, "I told you so,' and in making some suggestions on this subject I do not wish to be put in an unsympathetic position. I am sure the families that have suffered from disgrace are often to be pitied more than those who have suffered losses by death, and I have only the deepest sympathy for them, especially when they would seem to be the victims of an environment so difficult to control. However much we may sympathize, still we cannot withhold the repetition of counsel and principles, the observance of which would have saved thousands from disaster and will still go far to protect from dangers that are imminent.

"And they shall also teach their children to pray, and to walk uprightly before the Lord." (D. & C. 68:25, 26, 28.)

Now I grant that the word of God so put may sound strange in the ears of many of this generation. It is not the language of moderns and the sophisticated, but the meaning is plain so that every one who will may understand. There are three significant things in this scripture. First, the admonition to parents to teach and train their children in the principles of the gospel. 

Second, to prepare them for membership in the Fold of

Now, as to the first, may I ask: Where is a code or set of principles to be found which, in the test of time, has been demonstrated to be so stimulating and effective for the maintenance of high standards of moral and ethical conduct in chastity, integrity, and purity of life as are these Christian doctrines? Has there been discovered a satisfactory substitute for faith in God, prayer, and walking "uprightly before the Lord?" Has any of you personally ever known a man, a woman, or a child whose life was guided by these principles who was not a credit to society and who did not make some contribution, however modest, to the sum total of human happiness?

I am well aware that these principles of conduct are old and that lacking novelty they may be considered passe and outmoded, but if no other reasons existed, would it not seem prudent and wise to stay with the old until something better has been found to take its place? We do know that in times past these Christian standards have served as anchors and safeguards to countless people, old and young alike. If we knew the facts of each case, it would not surprise me to discover that the delinquency of almost every young man and young woman who falls could be traced to the lack of definite and fixed concepts of right living and authoritative sources for such concepts. 

Many young people have confessed to a woeful lack of discernment between good and evil, and many have also deplored the tragic fact that in their education and training the standards and ideals of their youth have been so disturbed and distorted that they find themselves wholly without stability of concept in life's purpose and design. It is true that these things are not always consciously in the minds of youth and many do not know what ails them, but most have a deep-seated feeling that something vital is lacking.

Now, parents, do we have any greater obligation, not only to our own flesh and blood, but to the generation in which we live, than to supply these vital needs? The consistent, sympathetic teaching of gospel truths will do it. 

I think I need not argue much for the second point, -to teach children while they are young. Nearly every one concedes that the early years are the most impressionable age in the life of a child. You will observe from the revelation which I read to you that the age of eight is regarded as the age of accountability in the

I was much comforted in this respect when I read from an educational psychologist some time ago that in his estimation, and perhaps in the estimation of some of his colleagues in the profession, a person learns one-half of all he ever learns in life by the time he reaches the age of eight years. With reference to the third item that the sin is "upon the heads of the parents" for their failure to train their children in the principles of truth, I can make this observation only at this time. The penalty is imposed for an infraction of the obligation to teach. This doctrine does not make the parents insurers of their child's good conduct.

All of us know that unfortunately there are black sheep in the best of families.

If the parents have conscientiously done all they could do, they are exculpated from blame. The point is to do all we can. I ask only that parents examine themselves

to determine the measure of their fulfillment of this divine obligation. 

So, today, my fellow parents, in the emergency which confronts us I know of nothing which holds a brighter prospect for solution of our family problems and the preservation of our youth in virtue and truth than compliance with this word of God which I have so briefly and so inadequately presented to you.

We are at the close of Mother's Day. Even if time permitted it would be almost hopeless for me to attempt to add anything to the tributes which have been paid to our mothers. I am happy to endorse the eloquent and touching expressions of love and devotion which have this day been accorded to her who gave us mortal life.

It has always seemed to me, however, that but one tribute only is adequate and that is the tribute of a good life, a life such as a worthy mother would wish for a son or daughter. There is no praise, no show of affection, nor expression of gratitude comparable to this. To be what a good mother wants you to be is not only the highest devotion to her, it is also the supreme achievement of her life and mission.

I invoke this night upon the mothers of the land the benediction of the Almighty and I entreat our Father in 

Heaven to look in mercy on those good and noble women whose sons have been taken from them,-some never to return in this life. O God, comfort their aching hearts, heal the wounds of separation with faith that is more than hope and knowledge that transcends belief.

CHAPTER XVIII -THE SUPREME CHARITY 

I wish to base these remarks on two recorded words of the Savior: "Judge not." Someone has said that the supreme charity of the world consists in obedience to this simple injunction. Certain it is that thousands suffer from its infraction,-not only those who are its victims, but also those who are guilty of circulating inconsiderate judgment and good causes as well.

There is a proneness to intemperate judgment manifest everywhere. It is seen in the readiness with which people make up their minds on all kinds of questions. 

Relatively few investigate fully before they decide. The difficulty of securing impartial juries to try cases before the courts is an outstanding example of this tendency.

It is reported that in a famous case tried in New York many years ago, more than one thousand veniremen, (that is men summoned to appear for jury servicę,) were examined before twelve only could be secured to give assurance that they had not made up their minds on this notorious case and that they were not so influenced by accounts in the public press that they could impartially hear the evidence and without prejudice decide the issues on their merits. Such a startling condition is but indica-

tive of this altogether too general tendency to prejudge persons and circumstances.

Now this unfortunate practice is productive of many serious results. In the first place, it brings untold unhappiness to those about whom stories and rumors are circulated to the discredit of reputation. Every redblooded man prizes his standing in the community and among his fellowmen. Shakespeare put it thus: "Who steals my purse steals trash; 'tis something, nothing; 'Twas mine, 'tis his, and has been slave to thousands: 

But he that filches from me my good name, Robs me of that which not enriches him, And makes me poor indeed."

At best it is a struggle to build and maintain an unblemished reputation. It is a mean and cowardly thing to stick the knife of slander into the back of one who is earnestly making the effort. Usually one so attacked has no means of defending and protecting himself. Rumors are often accepted at face value and the victim may wait for years and may never get the opportunity to vindicate himself. 

Why is a bit of gossip defamatory of another's character such a delicious morsel to be mouthed about and chewed and rechewed with such relish? I am sure I cannot account for this all too general abuse of human relationships, especially when it is evident that in most cases the gossiper has nothing whatever to gain from the circulation of the tale of defamation. It is true that in many cases the practice is indulged without malicious intent, but this does not greatly mitigate the damage done.

The person who "goes off and leaves his tongue wagging"

167 is scarcely less censurable than the one who commits the offense with malice aforethought.

I need not say more with reference to the bad effects of this regrettable practice. Perhaps I have already said too much. I don't want you to think that everyone is guilty. I know there are exceptions, many of them. I wish there were more.

I think the remedy for this situation is pretty clear. 

All that is required is a different attitude, a different feeling about our fellows, whether they happen to be our associates or not. If everyone could be impressed with the fact that he has a duty to others, which after all is the essence of the Christian doctrine of altruism, that sense of obligation would forbid his saying or repeating those things that might be hurtful to another's good name. 

Then, if in addition to this sense of duty, each could feel a personal regard which, in the broad sense, we designate as brotherhood and love of humanity, he would never have the inclination to indulge this unfortunate practice. 

About the best psychology I know of is set forth in one of our hymns for children. "Nay, speak no ill, a kindly word can never leave a sting behind; 

And oh, to breathe each tale we've heard, is far beneath a noble mind.

Now the next phase of this subject which gives cause for concern is the tendency to indulge in what I call "long-range" criticism of public officials, especially as regards their private life and character. I include within the group not only political officers, but all others who, by reason of their elevation to positions of leadership, assume a responsibility for the direction of movements and causes. I recognize, of course, that the policies of a man in public place and the wisdom of his actions and statements are legitimate subjects for public debate and that proper criticism of such matters is not only allowable, but is to be encouraged in the interests of good government and civic procedure. But is it necessary in such an analysis of issues to bring imputations against a man's character, his intentions, and his honor? I think it is not, and I believe that inestimable and unnecessary damage is done to personal reputations, sensitive feelings,

169 and legitimate family pride by the misconceived and cruel strategy that the way to win an ideological victory is to assassinate the character of an opponent. It is true there may be some cases where the official's character itself is the issue, but the relatively few instances of this kind do not warrant an assailment of character generally by opposing factions.

I think that those who occupy positions of public trust and responsibility in state, church, or businesses are entitled to a measure of sympathy in the discharge of their obligations. It certainly is not easy to please everybody, and the enforcement of the best and soundest policies invariably affects someone adversely. Because a person is affected adversely is no justification for an attack on the personal character of the enforcing or policymaking officer.

So until we can stop calling men "crooks" just because they disagree with us, we have much to repent of. 

When we decide that we are the guardians of the good names of our fellows, as we expect them to guard our own reputations, then, and then only will we truly learn the real meaning of the Christian doctrine of the good neighbor.

Now there is another and concluding item in this subject, which I wish to submit to you. Throughout the land, in the church, and with all organizations there is a persistent cry for unity,-unity of allegiance, unity of purpose, and the fullest cooperation in endeavor. It is not necessary to argue for either the desirability or the necessity of such unity. In the war effort it is essential to victory, and in other lines of endeavor it is requisite to success. The Church, and other kindred causes sus-

tained by the good will and devotion of members, can make no adequate progress without this element of unity and solidarity.

How are we going to get it in the measure in which it is needed? I don't know that I can give a satisfactory answer to that question, but I feel that I can point out one thing that will help, and help greatly. That is to yield obedience to this divine injunction of the Savior, to refrain from intemperate judgment, to impute good rather than evil to the intentions of men, and to investigate and know the facts before deciding. We are much too prone to judge men by the mistakes they make, forgetting that we ourselves are constantly making mistakes. 

We seldom know or consider their intentions and that they themselves suffer most for their mistakes, in both disappointment and consequence. Most people need our sympathy, rather than our censure, in their failures. 

I believe that one of the truest tests of friendship is what one will say or permit said about another behind his back. What a good friend is he, who, when he hears something that casts reflection on the good name of another, has the courage to stand up and say, "I doubt that that is true. I have confidence in my friend and I will require proof to support any statement against him." 

It is a real friend who will take that position, and if all would do it, there would be far fewer heartaches, and fewer pillows wet with sobs of those who suffer from misjudgment, and much more happiness in the world than there is today.

I do not see how a brotherhood founded on good will can come to men, unless they are kind in their estimates of each other. We might give and give and give to relieve

171 suffering and human misery, but unless we have sympathetic understanding of our fellows, and the charity not to judge them harshly, we have little hope of a real fraternity among men. The Lord has said that He "would forgive whom He would forgive, but of you it is required that ye forgive all men." An unforgiving person who harbors resentment and ill feeling against another often suffers more than the victim of his enmity, so that in divine providence there is a self-imposed penalty for uncharitable judgment.

The Savior, on one occasion, said to His disciples, "Except ye are one, ye are not mine." This was perhaps the supreme rebuke to faultfinding and dissension and at the same time the supreme reward for unity and cohesion. 

To be "one" in Christ is the highest attainment of the exalted Christian purpose. If all the world were truly "one" in Him perfection would be reached. Indeed that unity in understanding, action and love is the very epitome of His Kingdom. Of course, it can come only if we do His will and keep His commandments, not the least among which is this holy pronouncement carrying a message of true charity, love and happiness to all men,- "Judge not, that ye be not judged. For with what judgment ye judge, ye shall be judged: and with what measure ye mete it shall be measured to you again." (Matthew 7:1, 2.)

CHAPTER XIX  

I am aware that the subject which I have chosen for these remarks is one upon which there are strong differences of opinion. I feel, however, that we cannot permit to go unchallenged not only some of the statements, but also some of the propaganda which originate from sources within the country which tend to make vengeance and hatred dominating factors in the motivation of our people and our armed forces. The setting of a day or a period to avenge certain infamies or outrages against us, and the loud and persistent cries for retaliation for atrocities of the enemy, and the propagation of the doctrine that it is necessary to be tough to win, and the venting of personal spleen on alien but innocent people are all indicative and consequences of this studied and, I think, regrettable tendency to stir men's hearts to enmity and vengeance.

Too often, when one takes exception to this course of things, he is immediately accused of being unrealistic and "chicken livered," so that many who honestly doubt the wisdom of such policies and in their hearts oppose their teaching are often cowed into silence and submission by the more demonstrative methods of the avengers and retaliators. In fact, I am often led to wonder, without

any adequate means of ascertaining the fact, whether or not those who hammer so vigorously and loudly for vengeance do not represent but a relatively small minority of our citizens in the real expression of their deep-seated sentiments.

When this tendency gives rise to such a statement as I heard recently coming from a student of the social sciences, namely, that some of the training and psychology through which some of our boys are passing would unfit them for participation in any decent society of the future, I think the matter becomes one of public concern. 

Now we all have some conception of what war is and we know that its notorious definition is well merited. 

Lawless enemies, in violation of every standard of right and decency, have thrown themselves at our throats in a desperate effort to throttle us and rob us of the things we hold most dear. Of course, we have no alternative but to repel them and render them impotent for the repetition of any such further assault upon us. 

Not only must we do this for ourselves, but we have altruistic obligations to protect others,-innocent victims of these enemies. Unfortunately we cannot meet these brutal assaults with mere persuasion. We are obliged to use force, all the force at our command,—to crush the enemies of our dearly-bought liberty, our freedom of religion, our homes, and our way of life. We are forced to use the methods of war and to become expert in them. 

It is the only way open to us at the present time. Our forces have to be hardened for the hard job. Their muscles should be firm and tough. Their nerves must be like steel. Their courage must be unflinching and their resolution unbeatable.

All this I grant, but what about the hearts of these men? Is it essential to victory that they abandon all the finer sentiments and permit only the brutish impulses to dominate their lives and actions? In the first place, I doubt that that can be accomplished on anything like a universal scale in the space of a few years. Most of our fighting men have come from good homes,—many with high Christian ideals. They have not been educated to kill and murder as it is alleged the youth in some of the countries have been for the last decade. Hatred and vengeance have had little or no place in their lives. 

While the environment of war may have a pronounced effect upon them, yet they cannot be completely robbed of those finer feelings in which they have been so tenderly nurtured.

In the next place, I believe that while many might succumb to an education for vengeance, such an education will not ultimately help the war effort and contribute to the victory we aim to win. It may be that to get a man mad helps to get him to fight, but I think we have long ago learned that a mad man is a poor fighter. The old saying, "Whom the gods would destroy they first make angry," is not without application today. The modern strategy of war demands clear, calm minds and precise action in its execution.

A brain intoxicated by a flood of blinding madness, as one numbed by the effects of alcoholic drink, does not lend itself readily to the accomplishment of such a methodical pursuit as war now is. 

And then, too, will it not be sad enough for the homes and communities to which these fighting boys will sometime return, that is those of them who do re-

turn, maimed in body, many with wounds from which they will never recover and with shocks to the mind which may forever rob them of a normal life, without inflicting also upon these homes and communities, when the boys come back, an atmosphere charged with vindictiveness, hatred and enmity, antagonistic to every sentiment and concept which has been fostered in the past? We are informed that the creation of this ideology of hate and revenge has been essential for the growth of dictatorship and the militaristic spirit in the enemy countries. Surely we who oppose such autocracy and aggression can ill afford to encourage the cultivation of the inimical and hateful atmosphere out of which grew the things we fight.

If I have rightly interpreted the influence of this campaign for vengeance on our war effort and our chance for victory, how much more pernicious is it in its bearing on our ultimate objective, the establisment of a just and lasting peace among nations. A moment's contemplation will reveal to any thinking person how it will add to the difficulty of composing all the problems of the world if a studied effort shall have been made to inflame hatreds against each other. When the time comes for making the peace, it will probably be deemed prudent to make adequate provision to forestall another general uprising against the peace and dignity of the world, but there will be grave doubts about the lasting efficacy of such provisions, whatever form they may take, unless peoples begin to feel well toward each other and repose confidence in each other.

Looking toward this end, there is one encouraging thing, which I believe we have all observed. It is the

disposition to differentiate between the war lords and their militaristic parties and willing collaborators on the one hand and the decently disposed but helpless citizenry, who have been made the victims of their premeditated scheming and deception, on the other. For one, I hope that however fierce the struggle between nations be, this distinction will never be lost sight of. For I believe that in it lies about the only reasonable prospect for considerate treatment and general good will among peoples following the war.

It is natural to resent the perpetration of crime and offenses against society and human relations and it is lawful to bring offenders to justice. In a Christian civilization, this should be accomplished without the destruction of the very foundations of Christian society which are mutual good will and altruistic service. Vengeance and hatred are antagonistic to the fundamentals. 

The two cannot be propagated together. One will run the other out. ...

There is still another reason why we cannot afford the cultivation of personal enmity even as a war time emergency measure. It is basically wrong. It contravenes the law of God. Here are some of the scriptures: "Avenge not yourselves, .. for it is written, vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord," (Romans 12:19) "Let not the sun go down upon your wrath." (Ephesians 4:26) "He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that ruleth his spirit than he that taketh a city." (Proverbs 16:32) Whatever you may believe about the authority of these laws, I think that most of you will admit that they have been a pretty safe and wise guide for human conduct during many centuries

past, and I think, too, that most of us concede that it is a rather precarious thing to build up vengeance and hatred as the motivating force in a person's life because we are never quite sure that the objects of this hatred may not change. While today animosities may be directed against what seems to be justifiable objections, yet tomorrow enmities so engendered may vent themselves in the accomplishment of far less deserving ends. 

I am convinced that we cannot afford a campaign of vengeance and hatred in this country of tolerance, good will and Christian kindness, no matter what its proponents may claim for it in point of expediency and war time necessity. 

And now, my friends, with your indulgence I would like to give expression to some ideas, which, I think, are constructive in the effort we may put forth to provide our soldier boys real incentive to give their best to the country's cause. It is my opinion, and I have spoken of it in many places, that one of the things our fighting men need most, and many civilians also, is a clear interpretation of the issues at stake in this war. It is not enough for them to do battle merely to resist aggression and suppress a vicious enemy, important as that is. That is an obligation which duty imposes upon us,-a necessity we cannot ignore, but to give zeal and enthusiasm for the cause we support we must understand it and love it. 

One of the speakers addressing the closing session of Stanford University's recently held Conference on the 

Humanities said: "All current plans for a post war world would assume too easily that the major changes needed are in the realm of political and economic organization

alone and that we can produce a new world without producing a new man to govern it." 

He further said: "We are sending our soldiers to war with little better understanding of humanity's essential inheritance than the fascists have and with no better picture of the future than a fatuitous air-conditioned utopia in which human life is run by a series of push buttons." He expressed his conviction "that soldiers with a knowledge of the philosophy which underlies democracy and individual freedom can fight better than those without it."

Just what is this philosophy underlying democracy and freedom? There are many men who would give a long and scholarly definition of that philosophy, but I set it forth in the simple manner in which I perceive it. There are in this philosophy two things which are essential: 

First, the recognition of the majesty of man as the son of God and, second, the divine right of free agency with which man is by nature invested. 

An adequate concept of these verities is all that is necessary to clarify the issues and justify all the sacrifices and inspire all the zeal and resoluteness essential to their preservation in the earth. Our greatest need, in all the research and investigation which men of today are so industriously and patiently making, is for man, as one author has put it, "to discover himself." When he does discover himself, he will find out just what he was told ages ago, that he was created in the image of God, that he is the Father's son, and that the way of life planned for him in the beginning by an all-wise eternal Parent is still the way to complete happiness and satisfaction.

With the firm establishment of these truths in the hearts of men will naturally follow the relationships that will make for mutual understanding and peace. Men of a common father must be brothers, with the natural obligations and affections which that fraternity begets.

It is true they become unnatural brothers when they permit the passions of greed and selfishness to overwhelm them, but when they do, there is nothing more potent than a realization of their noble lineage to draw them together again. There are some who say, and that too with discernible disparagement, that this conception makes a "holy war" out of our present effort. Well, in one sense no war is holy. War is not a holy business, but does it diminish the zeal of the soldiers who fight it or the civilians who sustain them to regard their endeavor as a contribution to sustain holy principles? Does this concept bring less of comfort to those who lose their lives and to others who make unspeakable sacrifices in its prosecution? Did not the Revolutionary founders of our country regard their cause as a holy cause? Were not union and liberty sacred and divinely precious to 

Abraham Lincoln? All I plead for is a recognition of the fact that this great calamity has come upon the world because of the perversion of divine principles and that our only hope lies in men who consciously fight and strive in both war and peace for the restitution of these principles in the hearts and lives of humanity.

The Savior gave to us an exalted principle which men find it very difficult to live by. He said: "Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you and persecute you." (Matthew 5:44) I grant that this seems

well nigh impossible in times of war. It requires a large measure of the divine nature, which characterized Him who suffered more than any other man at the hands of His enemies, to so control one's native passions and feelings. When, however, they can be brought under control men's minds are enlightened, and they are able to distinguish between just punishment and revenge, between the sin and the sinner, and they do not permit themselves to be poisoned with the most deadly virus in all the world,—hate and enmity. Let us never forget that love is ordained as the saving grace for all mankind.

This historic Tabernacle from which I speak is located in an area in Salt Lake City, designated as "Temple

Block." There is near the Tabernacle a stately edifice, constructed of granite rock hewn from quarries in the nearby mountains, with six graceful spires reaching high into heaven, which is the commanding feature of the block and hence gives to it its name. This building is called the Temple by the people who erected it, and the hundreds of thousands of tourists who annually visit "Temple Square" are curious to know why it is never open to the public and why they are not invited to inspect it as they are all other buildings and points of interest on the block. It has occurred to me that some of you may have a similar curiosity and that it might be of some interest for me to tell you a little about the temples of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, which I have the honor to represent.

The Church, during its history of one hundred thirteen years, has built ten temples and has dedicated the sites for two additional ones which were never completed. One of these sites was in Jackson County, Missouri, dedicated August 3, 1831, and one was in

Far West, Missouri, the site being dedicated July 4, 1838, and the corner stone laid April 26, 1839. In both of these places people of the Church were prevented by force from completing the projects they had begun.

All of the other ten temples still stand, with the exception of the one erected at Nauvoo, Illinois.

The temples were completed and dedicated in the following order: The one at Kirtland, Ohio, March 27, 1836 (this building has passed out of control of the 

Church); the Nauvoo Temple, May 1, 1846; the one at 

St. George, Utah, April 6, 1877; the one at Logan, Utah, May 17, 1884; the one at Manti, Utah, May 21, 1888; the Salt Lake Temple, April 6, 1893; the Hawaiian 

Temple at Laie, on the Island of Oahu, November 27, 1919; the one at Cardston, Alberta, Canada, August 26, 1923; the one at Mesa, Arizona, October 23, 1927. The latest one to be erected is at Idaho Falls, Idaho, now nearing completion and will probably be dedicated shortly.

In architecture and external appearance, there is wide variation in these temples, except for some similarity among those at Salt Lake, Logan, and Manti. On the inside of the temples since the days of Nauvoo, while interior finish and embellishments have varied greatly, the arrangement of rooms and facilities has been comparable in all the buildings. This similarity of arrangement has been occasioned by the uniform requirements of the work carried forward in these buildings. The master plan provides for a series of four major rooms of assembly, each being spacious enough to accommodate congregations of from one hundred to four hundred people, the rooms being constructed on ascending floor levels. These principal rooms are underlaid and surrounded with other smaller rooms and facilities in considerable number. In addition thereto, in some of the temples there have been provided on the upper floors large assembly

rooms of sufficient capacity, as in the case of the Salt Lake Temple, to seat several thousand people. 

The materials used in construction have varied with locality and availability, although in all instances stability and endurance have been sought for and achieved. 

The foundations of the Salt Lake Temple, begun ninety years ago, are sixteen feet thick and extend sixteen feet below the surface of the ground. After they were first begun, they were torn out and relaid at great expense and sacrifice to the people because they were not deemed adequate to support the structure for the duration of time envisioned for its use.

Interior finish likewise has been largely adapted to the time and place of construction. The best the people could secure has never been thought too good, although the utility of the buildings for the high purposes which have dictated their construction has always been considered paramount to their embellishment. Careful and artistic workmanship has always been procured, and painstaking labor has ever been a conspicuous characteristic.

The spending of forty years in the construction of the Salt Lake Temple is not alone evidence of the meticulous care used in its building, but also of the enormous sacrifices made by the people to achieve their lofty aspirations which at times seemed well nigh unattainable. 

There is one feature of interior decoration worthy of special mention. In each of the temples now in use artists have been engaged to provide beautiful paintings within the major rooms which I have mentioned to give proper atmosphere to the sacred work there carried forward. In some instances these paintings cover the entire walls of the rooms and have taken long periods

of time in their execution. In other cases they are in the form of panels. The effects achieved are beautiful and inspiring. Statuary and other works of art adorn the interiors, all contributing to a peaceful, sacred influence so essential to the prosecution of a holy work. 

Now having given you this brief and altogether inadequate description of the physical aspects of the temples, I wish to devote a few moments to the concepts which prompted their erection and the purposes for which they are maintained. Of all Christian peoples, I believe the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints is the only one which carries forward what is called "temple work," and the only one to erect and maintain buildings specifically designated as temples for the administration of gospel ordinances as distinguished from other edifices designed chiefly for assembly purposes and the carrying on of church services and ceremonies incident thereto. The founders of our Church all came from Christian homes and the Christian denominations of their day. When the church was established in 1830, they had no idea of temple work. They had probably read the passage from First Corinthians (15:29) in the

Bible, "Else what shall they do which are baptized for the dead, if the dead rise not at all? Why are they then baptized for the dead?" And they may have wondered as to the meaning of these interrogations and the implications arising therefrom, that baptism for the dead was a practice of the Christian people in Paul's time, but however much they may have wondered, they knew nothing about such an ordinance. They had read of temples, of course, in ancient Israel, but it was not until their founder, Joseph Smith, had received a

revelation that they understood that it was an important part of their work to erect temples, or "Houses of the 

Lord," as they were sometimes called.

This direction first came to them in the second year after the organization of the Church, and several years passed before they came to a full understanding of the divine purpose in temple work. In 1836 information came to them, to use the words of the revelation, that "the time has fully come, which was spoken of by the mouth of Malachi-testifying that he (Elijah) should be sent before the great and dreadful day of the Lord come, to turn the hearts of the fathers to the children, and the children to the fathers, lest the whole earth be smitten with a curse." (D. & C. 110:14-15) Then they understood for the first time the meaning of Paul's reference to baptism for the dead, and that it was a part of the mission of the Church to begin that great work in which the living could perform essential ordinances for their dead ancestry, who had died without knowledge of or opportunity to receive these ordinances in their lifetime. They knew then that the work for the dead was necessary to and indicative of the universal justice of the Father of men toward all His children and that none should be left without opportunity, either in this life or the life to come, to accept the principles and ordinances of the everlasting gospel and receive the blessings derived therefrom. It was made clear to them, also, that these sacred ordinances were to be performed in houses specifically prepared and dedicated for such work. 

Later further light came to them and they were informed that there were other ordinances and ceremonies to be performed in the temple for the living, as well

as the dead, and that the highest blessings attainable by men in this life would come to them in these sacred places. These were some of the things which they interpreted to be within the fulness of the gospel of Christ which they were commanded to practice and proclaim to all the world. They deemed this aspect of their work so important and they were so convinced of its rightness and essentiality that henceforth it claimed a very considerable part of their thought, energy, and endeavor. 

Just as soon as the members of the Church were definitely established in the beautiful city of Nauvoo on the banks of the Mississippi River they began the erection of a million dollar temple. Stones for the walls were secured in the vicinity; the timbers had to be procured from long distances with great effort. Great groups of men took time from their much needed vocations to make a living for their families, to devote their skill to temple construction. They prosecuted the work as fast as their circumstances would permit, being ever spurred on by the incentive to prepare a place to receive what they regarded as the supreme blessings of the Almighty.

One of the most heroic episodes in the history of these people is associated with this temple. Troubles came upon them before it was completed. Their prophet and his brother were murdered at Carthage and uncontrolled mobs besieged their city and committed all manner of atrocities against them. At the request of the governor and upon his assurance that they would receive protection, they consented to give up their arms which they had held in a military establishment authorized by the State Legislature. They were defenseless. Hostil-

ities and feeling reached such a point that they decided that it would be necessary for them to abandon their homes and move away from their enemies. In the face of this decision, reached but not published by the leaders of the Church, they proceeded with the work necessary to complete the temple. They hoped almost against hope that some of them at least would be able to secure the blessings which were to come to them in this sacred edifice. God had commanded its erection and they were resolved to fulfill His command. They did. It was dedicated and used for a short period. Then they were expelled from their city in the dead of winter. From the opposite bank of the Mississippi River they saw their beautiful temple desecrated by a band of lawless maraud- It was subsequently destroyed by fire. There is no language to express their feelings.

I don't know that I can make clear to you how much the devoted members of the Church prize their temples and temple work. I have had the privilege of attending the dedicatory services for all the temples completed in the last fifty years. These services are regarded by the people as among the most inspiring of any held in the Church. They usually occupy the major portion of a week, and sometimes, as in the case of the Salt Lake Temple, more than that. The beauty of the building itself incites the wonder and admiration of the people. Sacred music, which is most carefully prepared, and the addresses given are inspiring, and the prayer of dedication by the President of the Church is a most solemn and arresting experience. And more than all these, there comes from these buildings an influence and a spirit which never fails to touch the hearts of those invited to be present.

I once had one experience which convinced me of what I had already known but failed to adequately appreciate, that the dedicatory services of a temple, impressive as they are, are but incidental to other considerations. It was at the beautiful temple at Mesa, Arizona. 

Near the conclusion of the dedication, as I passed out of the temple after one of the meetings, I observed a little family on the front steps. There was a father, mother, and four or five small children. Their clothing and appearance indicated that they were poor, but they looked happy. I stopped to speak with them and I said, “I suppose you greatly enjoyed the dedicatory services." 

The father replied, "Yes, we have, but we are waiting until they are over. We are waiting for the temple to open for regular temple work. We've been poor; we've never been able to afford to go to Salt Lake 

City or some other place where there is a temple, and we're so happy now that my wife and I can go into this holy house and be married in the way we've always wanted to be married. "Of course," said he, "we were married under the laws of the land for time, until death parts us, but that is not the kind of a marriage we want. We want to be married for all eternity, and we want these children of ours to be sealed to us in the holy bond of matrimony so that they will always be ours, so that our home will be preserved to us forever."

This man spoke from his heart. I didn't see the house he lived in, but I saw the home he envisioned. As I beheld his vision, I forgot his poverty and I thought him rich indeed.

His was but a reflection of the concept and spirit

which down through the years has dominated this temple-building people. Millions of dead kindred have been officiated for in these sacred establishments, and many hundreds of thousands of the living have been made supremely happy in the realization of the most exalted blessings the gospel brings to humanity. 

I hope, my friends, that this example of genuine, steadfast devotion to an exalted spiritual ideal will be stimulating in a weary world where faith to believe God's word and do His will is so much needed.

CHAPTER XXII WHERE IS HIS KINGDOM? 

In the early days of moving pictures, before the talking feature was introduced, a great film was prepared and released. It was called the "King of Kings."

Some of you will remember it. It depicted many events in the life and ministry of the Lord Jesus Christ. Most of the scenes were very effective. The whole atmosphere and spirit of the portrayal were such as to induce a reverential regard and a deep sympathy for the Savior of the world and His atoning sacrifice.

I recall distinctly my own enjoyment of this picture. 

I saw it several times and was touched and deeply stirred by many of the episodes so vividly reconstructed by the marvelous facilities of the screen. At the very end of the picture, however, I suffered something of a disappointment. My interpretation of the last message flashed upon the screen may not have been fully warranted, but I could not concur in the implication arising from the final statement which was, "The Kingdom of God is in your heart."

Now I did not feel to disagree with the aspect of this declaration, which places emphasis on personal faith in the Master and love and regard for His life and work and incomparable teaching, nor the assumption that a

personal conviction of the reality of His life and mission is essential to the acceptance of His kingdom. My disappointment arose from the inference in the statement that the kingdom of God has no existence and actuality outside a person's heart.

It is to this latter view that I cannot subscribe, and I am persuaded that an acceptance of this interpretation, which I fear has become very extensive, has not contributed as much to the advancement of the true Christian cause as many of its proponents would like it to do. It is my opinion that this virtual denial of the independent existence of the kingdom of our Father, and the making of His kingdom substantially nothing more than a personal attribute of our thinking and feeling, must result finally in robbing both the kingdom and its King of that authority and potency essential to make them effective in the lives of men and nations. While the essence and force of God's kingdom are in the realm of the spiritual, the kingdom itself is more than a mere personal, spiritual concept.

Since we rely upon the scriptures for our knowledge in these matters, may we turn to them in our discussion. 

In Daniel (7:27, also 18) we are told that "the kingdom and dominion, and the greatness of the kingdom under the whole heaven shall be given to the people of the saints of the Most High, whose kingdom is an everlasting kingdom, and all dominions shall serve and obey him." 

And from Revelation (11:15): "The kingdoms of this world are become the kingdoms of our Lord, and of his 

Christ; and he shall reign for ever and ever." And from 

Psalms (22:28): "For the kingdom is the Lord's; and he is the governor among the nations."

203 Surely no mythical or insubstantial kingdom is here contemplated. Many other scriptures, both ancient and modern, might be cited to show that the kingdom of God is an actual establishment and that the Lord reigns in a very real and tangible sense. His government is one of law and order. While there is no police force set up and maintained to enforce the will of God, yet there are penalties for the infraction of His laws, immutable and more inescapable than those attached to the contravention of any of the laws and regulations of the governments of men.

The constitution of the kingdom is in the written word of God. Its authority is vested in His sovereign will. Citizenship in the kingdom is free and open to every soul under heaven who will subscribe to the just and beneficent regulations prescribed for induction and participation in its affairs. Records are kept by a recorder who makes no mistakes nor misjudgments, and rewards are determined by an invariable, accurate accounting which balances assets against liabilities in terms of both intention and performance.

The government of this kingdom is far more perfect than that which prevails in the institutions of men. 

The only policies and principles enforced are those which have long been tried and never found wanting. Eternal justice is tempered with mercy. A man must pay for his mistakes, but he can correct them and be forgiven.

When he has been forgiven, he can start anew without the handicap of one coming out of an earthly prison. 

His fellow citizens of the kingdom are there to greet him with a helping hand and a cordial reception, to look for the best and not the worst in mortal beings. The

The highest dignity is in the best service and the richest emoluments in the satisfactions which flow from good accomplished.

Some may say that all these attributes, and others which belong to the kingdom, are in the hearts of men. 

This may be true, but it does not obliterate or preclude the independent existence of the kingdom itself. In one sense God Himself is in our hearts. But those who know and understand Him do not deny Him His personality and omnipotence. He is the Author of all principles of righteousness, but He Himself is more than a principle. His kingdom is more than the attributes which flow from Him. 

I feel sure that a universal recognition of the verity of the kingdom of God in the earth, with Christ the risen Lord at the head as master executive, final arbitrator of all controversies, and constant friend to mankind, would do more than all else combined to advance the Christian cause.

Affiliation with the kingdom begets a stability of purpose and a contentment that may be had from no other source. Membership in it provides a home for the wanderer and a refuge for the storm-tossed and weary. 

But the kingdom is no place for the idler. Work and progression are essential in its operation. Knowledge and truth are indispensable to those who serve.

CHAPTER XXIII PERSONAL PHILOSOPHY 

At the conclusion of this series of radio talks I thought I might take the liberty, with your indulgence, to make a few observations consonant with the views I have expressed during the last few months which, for want of a better title, I characterize as "Personal Philosophy." After all, religion and spiritual concepts are essentially personal. While every man is influenced by what others think and the spiritual atmosphere which surrounds him, yet the deep-seated religion of his life is what he thinks, feels, and does. So, perhaps, apologies for personal convictions in the field are superfluous. 

I interpret religion in terms of living. I have never been able to see that an abstract theology which did not have direct bearing on life and conduct was of very great profit. I believe that we really acquire moral and spiritual truth only so fast as we incorporate it into our lives. 

No one secures an adequate concept of worship unless he worships, of prayer without praying, of integrity without honesty, and of love without loving and being loved.

There is no emotion nor emotional experience within the realm of our feeling which is susceptible of definition to one who has never enjoyed the feeling or experience. A persuasive description may lead one to experiment, but

description itself is inadequate to convey the vital realism of spirituality.

It seems to me this is important to bear in mind in matters of religion. It is probably the basic reason for guarantees of freedom in religious worship and it is also the surest way to a real tolerance in the consideration of religious views. When people reach the point where they can say, "I do not understand that man's views; they do not appeal to me, but I have no reason to doubt his sincerity and he may be right," then, and then only, may we hope for the dissipation of dogmatic bigotry which so often precludes open-minded investigation.

It is this kind of investigation of which I speak and which I deem so essential in the world of science in which we live. I have no words to express my tribute to the men of science who, by their untiring, painstaking, intelligent research and investigation, have made our world such a comfortable and interesting place to live in. We are indebted to these men for so many things which have contributed to the happiness of our lives that it would take volumes to detail them. Many of us are old enough to remember when epidemics of diphtheria, typhoid and scarlet fever took scores from our small communities, sometimes whole families of children as in the case of some of my own relations. We have seen many die of maladies now quickly diagnosed and operated with almost negligible fatalities. We have watched the advance in sanitation, the isolation of the bacteria of disease, and the administration of serums whose immunity in prevention and cure is almost miraculous. In our own lifetimes we have seen transpor-

To the men of science and to the skilled workers of the world we owe it all. 

Do we owe it all to them alone? From whom did the concept of free enterprise in a free world come? Who originated the idea of altruistic service in the cause of which these splendid contributions have been made? 

What is it that has fired men with their lofty aspirations and benevolent designs? I think on reflection you will say that there has been an inner moving force in all this. 

I hold to the view that even men of science are responsive to the progressive enlightening spirit that emanates from the Eternal God of Heaven and earth and that in His providence they and their facilities are utilized, unconsciously perhaps, for the unfoldment of the laws and forces of the universe for the benefit of mankind. 

It seems to me more than mere coincidence that the civilizing and cultural forces of the world have emanated in such large degree from those who have had knowledge of the Bible, and the Father and His Son whom it reveals. I am aware that history records the ascendency of pagan nations in the past, but their conquests were limited and effectuated through the force of might. Certainly nothing has been comparable in the world's history to the intellectual dominance of the Christian faith and the doctrine of liberty, equality, and justice which it espouses. In the last analysis our present global conflict is fought over the issue of Christian freedom throughout the earth. This does not mean the war effort is to enforce the Christian religion on people, but it does mean that we are fighting to maintain concepts of lib-

erty and human relations which are derived from the word of God.

I interpret the Christian philosophy to be one of humility, as contradistinguished to the philosophy which the Savior spoke of as "the world," which is that of selfsufficiency. Religious faith can be built only upon humility. There must be confidence and trust in beneficence and power beyond ourselves. The self-sufficient do not feel that confidence and the need for guidance.

Arrogance develops within them and they naturally become inconsiderate of other persons and their desires. 

The dictators who seek to impose their will on the world are not humble. They do not have this philosophy of faith which postulates humility.

I believe the world will never be saved except in the hands of humble people who recognize counsel and wisdom superior to themselves. Abraham Lincoln and

George Washington believed that, as I pointed out to you from their own statements on the occasion of their birthdays.

The truest expression of genuine humility is in prayer. I believe in prayer,—not prayer offered just for effect, but a prayer which is the true expression of one's innermost thoughts and desires. I believe in gratitude, and I know of no more appropriate place for its expression than in a prayer of thanksgiving to the 

Giver of all good. I am sure that prayer brings into a life a composure that helps to meet all vicissitudes and an atmosphere of hope and good will and charity into which people are glad to come. 

I believe in the efficacy of prayer. I am aware that there are those who say, when a prayer seems to have

I believe in home and family. I am sure that the home is a divinely appointed institution, and I view with the utmost alarm the forces within and without it which seem to be making for its disintegration. If it is robbed of its sanctity I doubt that anything can hold it together. 

It seems evident that the state alone cannot do it. When the contracting parties consider themselves free to make the marital status nothing but a matter of personal and social convenience and the state lends its assistance to meet their convenience,-home, family and national life are doomed. This terrible perversion of the only sure foundation in social and national progress, and the lack of adequate parental care for children, offer a greater threat to our future security and happiness than any other problem which confronts us. The instinct for self-preservation will help us win the war, but if traditional family life vanishes, nothing can take its place. 

It is unthinkable that the destiny of America shall be committed to generations without legitimate homes, and yet that prospect may well lie ahead if our people are not aroused to the obligations they owe themselves and their posterity and their country. 

I know of no concept of home so salutary as that accorded to it by true religion. When a man and a woman consider their life partnership is formed not only by the

inclusion of themselves, but of God and the state as well, then stability and purpose come to their compact. They know then that they are engaged in a great enterprise,the greatest in all the world, homebuilding. They realize that the contributions which their home makes to the manhood and womanhood of the world will determine its future progress, and when they have the concept of home as an eternal institution existing not only for this life but for all time to come, then the enterprise they begin with wedding vows becomes great indeed. 

It furnishes the foundation not only for their greatest happiness here, but for their fondest hopes and aspirations in the world to come. They do not permit irritations and annoyances incident to the mortal weaknesses to sever that sacred relationship. They cannot tolerate divorce, except as an escape from an unbearable wrong committed by one against the other, nor can they tolerate the thought of losing one of their children from the eternal family circle through sin, if it is possible for their care and training to avert such a catastrophe. 

How we need such a concept of home all over the world today to safeguard the children and protect them from the evil influences these times throw about them. 

I think if more of our homes today had such a spiritual setting there would be far fewer mothers letting their children run the streets day and night and far fewer juvenile tragedies reported in the press. I question very seriously the wisdom of taking the mothers of children who have not reached the years of discretion out of the home to make munitions of war when they ought to be making good citizens for the republic. I believe in revealed religion. There are things in the scriptures which I do not understand and for which

I do not permit myself therefore to be troubled, beyond a natural inquisitiveness to know their meaning, by scriptural passages which are obscure to me, and I have no disposition to discard that which does seem plain because there are parts which I cannot analyze. I agree with the views expressed by Walter Lippmann in a book written by him several years ago, that there is no tenable middle ground between authoritative religion and humanism; between the objective reality of the kingdom of God, "as definite and as real, in spite of its invisibility, as the British Empire," and the humanistic view which makes belief in this kingdom "necessarily seem a grandiose fiction projected by human needs and desires." I accept the kingdom as real, and I find it far easier to account for what I know of the universe and man on the basis of an intelligent creator whose government is one of law and order, than upon any other hypothesis I have ever heard projected. I may be wrong, but I think that wherever there is a disposition to harmonize findings of science with gospel truth it can be done, especially when the differentiation between the field of science and the province

I prize the comfort of religion and the happiness that flows from it. It furnishes a constant standard for appraisal and choice. It relieves every decision of the uncertainties that must be present where criteria of rightness and fitness are lacking. It is a stimulant for the noblest ambition and the highest type of service, and at the same time it provides the rewards that are satisfactory and lasting. 

No life is free from disappointments, sorrow, and bereavement. True religion provides the healing balm more potent than all else to bring hope out of despair, comfort to sorrow, indeed it is the only thing that brings life out of death. It invests everything with meaning. It puts purpose into every phase of life. It makes brothers out of men and exalts humanity from the materialism of earth to the idealism of the spirit. It characterizes every deed with eternal import and enriches life beyond computation.

This, my friends, is a sketch of my personal philosophy of life. I would not impose it upon you if I were not convinced that it is helpful, comforting, and uplifting to everyone who adopts it. I know that these spiritual concepts have not come to all people and I express my deep gratitude that they have come to me. Every interpretative faculty which I possess tells me that God lives, that Jesus Christ is His divine Son, the Author of the gospel which bears His name, and that the Savior has caused His kingdom, His truth, and an authentic delega-

I know that their application in the lives of men and nations will bring peace and goodness to the world.
