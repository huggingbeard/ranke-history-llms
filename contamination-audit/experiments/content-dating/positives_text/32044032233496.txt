F. C. I. Is City Within Itself By J. T. NORRIS, JR. 

WARDEN R. O. CULVER, head man at Summit, is held in admiration and affection by everyone at the institution-personnel and inmates. His is the king-size job of coordinating all departments and seeing that the entire complex machinery is kept welloiled and in good working order.

The Federal Correctional Institution at Summit, located about 22 miles south of the Ashland city limits, is now entering its second decade, having been established in the fall of 1940. 

The institution is primarily designed to house short-term offenders of the non-habitual type. No one is sentenced there direct from the courts for more than five years, although a few prisoners transferred to 

Summit have longer terms.

The single guiding purpose of the institution, toward which all of its activities are directed, is to provide the means whereby an inmate who desires to do so can successfully rehabilitate himself. Thus, the name "Correctional Institution" is not loosely applied, for the only concern is to correct-not to punish. In fact there is no form of punishment at 

Summit, except isolation from outside society. 

Warden R. O. Culver, a man of long experience in the line of rehabilitative work, summed up the program in a nutshell by explaining that the institution attempts to have "the time serve the man," instead of vice versa.

One of the most impressive features of the institution is its hightype personnel-the people who staff it. The civil service employees at the institution are in career work, which they regard as such and pride themselves in a job well done. This accounts in large measure for the unfailing efficiency in every department of the institution. 

The large majority of the custodial officers (guards) are veterans. 

Many hold college degrees, and all meet rigid physical and mental standards. 

The 20 acres comprising the buildings and grounds of the institution are well-landscaped, decorated with shrubbery, and neatly kept. 

The inside of the institution carries out the same appearance, and is a denial of the Hollywood version of a gloomy and forbidding prison. 

The inside cell buildings are well lighted, well ventilated, and equipped with adequate tile shower bath facilities. In the "honor cells," housing men who have earned the privilege of living there by good conduct, men are allowed to decorate their cells in the color scheme of their choosing, and exercise individual taste as to their furnishings. [ 1 ]

Other quarters consist of dormitory-style barracks, spacious and also well equipped with showers and bathroom facilities. 

This type of surroundings is maintained for the prisoners in the knowledge that a clean, neat, and healthful environment plays an important part in any rehabilitative program. 

Of course there is an insistence on scrupulous "housekeeping," and personal cleanliness by the inmates, but diligence is rewarded. 

The most important part of the Summit program is vocational training itself, which is done on a scale which few realize. 

Vocational training is carried on in the institution's large furniture factory, its tobacco factory (which supplies tobacco products to every prison in the federal system), its extensive farm, its laundry, its kitchen, its machine shop, garage, painting and glass shop, and others. The equipment with which the inmate works and the instructors from whom he learns are the best.

The variety of trades which it is possible for a man to learn in the institution can equip him for almost any vocation which he chooses on the "outside."

Accompanying the vocational training is classroom work, by which the inmate may learn in theory what he puts in practice. Elementary schooling in the three "R's" is compulsory in cases in which it is lacking. 

And to further assure that a man has no drawbacks to his rehabilitation, an excellent dental clinic and hospital put him in the best physical condition possible. These too offer opportunity for the inmate to learn a gainful skill. 

The only missing quantity-one which the institution can't supply -is the will to learn. That must come from the individual. A DAY ROOM in an "honor" cell block is pictured above. The room is used for recreation by inmates who have earned the privilege of living in the block, and is decorated by them. [ 2 ]

A REAR VIEW of the large dairy barn on the FCI farm at Summit is pictured above, showing the stalls in which the institution's milk COWS are kept. 

The herd numbers 21 cows, of which 16 are being milked at present. 

One of the most outstanding features of the Federal Correctional Institution at Summit is its large, modern farm which helps to put the institution on a self-sustaining basis. The farm's manager is Joseph Havelka.

The farm, with about 100 acres under cultivation, produces all the milk, eggs and pork used by the institution, besides enough feed for the herds of swine and dairy cattle. The vegetable garden produces 21 different varieties, raising three crops a year in succession planting. 

The herd of 21 dairy cattle, 16 of which are milked, provides one pint of milk per day per man-or a total of about 75 gal. per day. Before each milking the cows are washed carefully, then milked with automatic equipment.

The dairy has its own vats for pasteurizing the milk. It must measure up to a government-approved standard of bacteria count, which is 2,000 per milliliter, for pasteurized milk and 10,000 per milliliter for raw milk. This standard is much more rigid than that set by public health authorities for commercial dairies-a bacteria count of 50,000 per ml. 

The dairy is under the supervision of Joseph George.

All the pork prepared by the institution's kitchen is produced at the farm from a herd which now includes 239 shoats, 13 fattening hogs, 8 sows, and two boars. The piggery area covers 8 acres on top [ 3 ]

Records Kept 

All shoats are ear-marked for identification purposes, and records on each individual pig are kept on file until it is slaughtered. 

Dr. W. A. Wallace, Ashland veterinarian, inspects each hog before slaughtering, and each carcass afterwards. Some of the pork is cured and smoked in the smokehouse which adjoins the slaughterhouse. 

George Hobbs, who is in charge of the piggery, said that 37,000 lbs. of dressed pork have been delivered to the kitchens since last June, indicating that the yearly goal of 50,000 lbs. for the fiscal year will probably be exceeded. 

Each cultivated field is mapped, numbered, and the soil is tested regularly to determine what type and what amount of fertilizer it needs. Field crops include corn, alfalfa, sudan grass, soybeans, clover, pasture and hay crops.

Improvement Underway 

The U. S. Soil Conservation Service has made a survey of the institution property and has mapped out a detailed, 5-year plan for soil conservation. The program will include tiling, terracing, and grass waterways. When it is completed, the long range plan will have taken a considerable step toward increasing the fertility of the land. Practically all the farmland is steep, and some of it was badly eroded when the institution acquired it. 

The same program will be mapped out for any farmer in this area free of charge, and he will be assisted in putting it into practice. Information may be obtained from Charles Ramey, U. S. Soil Conservation agent, at the U. S. Courthouse in Catlettsburg. [ 4 ]

Vocational agriculture program at the FCI. The program, which was inaugurated in 1946, is designed to show the inmate with on-the-job training what he learns in the classroom. 

Inmates Trained

Four years ago a program was inaugurated at Summit which was designed to teach modern farming methods to inmates from rural backgrounds whose farming practices had eroded their lands and reduced them to poverty. 

Officials in the Federal Bureau of Prisons realized that many liquorlaw violators "bootleggers" and "moonshiners"-had been driven to this manner of self-support when they could no longer exist on the meager production of their farms. About one-fourth of the prisoner population at Summit are violators of the liquor laws, and many of these are from impoverished rural backgrounds. 

This program of vocational agriculture training is in operation in only one other federal institution in the nation. It is somewhat in the nature of an experiment-an experiment which in recent years has proved highly successful.

At Summit it is under the direction of B. F. Cottrell, a graduate of the University of Kentucky School of Agriculture, who was the first man in the federal prison system to be put in charge of this type of program.

The program offers classroom training in the fields of dairying, vegetable growing, swine and poultry. The discussions are timed to coincide with the work actually in progress on the institution's farm. 

Thus they have an opportunity to put in practice what is being taught to them.

In the poultry, swine, and dairying courses the instruction covers the topics of selecting, housing, feeding, and caring for the stock, obtaining the best products and marketing them, and keeping adequate records. [ 5 ]

Movie Slides Used 

Since many of his students are illiterate, Mr. Cottrell accompanies his lectures with color slides, which are projected on a screen, illustrating the various topics he is discussing. The projector will also enlarge illustrations from books, and project them. 

Mr. Cottrell has collected approximately 118 films, each bearing several slides. He has obtained the films from universities, companies manufacturing farm machinery and supplies, and other sources. 

Before a prospective student in the vocational agriculture classes is admitted his background is scrutinized and information is obtained from the county agent in his district about his farm, what it needs, and what he needs to know. Or if he is a tenant with a stable residence, the county agent advises the institution as to which subjects would be most beneficial.

After his release on parole, the county agent in his district again cooperates by visiting the man, and making suggestions and recommendations. The U. S. Parole officer also cooperates by urging the parolee to carry out the county agent's advice.

The FHA, the Veterans' Administration and other state and federal agencies have extended their cooperation to see that the parolee has every assistance possible in carrying out the practices which he has been taught, and putting into operation the plan which has been recommended to him.

Mr. Cottrell and federal prison authorities are enthusiastic about the success of the program, and are anxious to continue enlarging and improving it.

Since the classes were instituted in 1946, 238 inmates have participated in the program, of which a large percentage have been helped to make excellent adjustments, and substantially improve their former economic situation. At the present time the farm and dairy labor detail is made up of 43 inmates, of whom 26 are receiving vocational classroom training.

Classroom discussions in the subjects of dairying, swine, and vegetable growing are all geared to a four-month schedule. They are taught while the corresponding work is actually being carried on at the institution's farm.

Each of the classes is taught for three hours each week, except dairying, which is a 4-hour course.

The theory behind the program, Mr. Cottrell explained, is that either labor on the farm or classroom work taken by itself, would accomplish little good. But when a man is told that this new method of farming is better (and why), and then can see with his own eyes that it gets better results, he is likely to be convinced. Still, it takes a lot of convincing to change ingrained, traditional methods that are handed down from generation to generation. [ 6 ]

In assigning a new man to a work detail, and in determining how closely he shall be confined, the committee is guided to a large extent by his background report which has been prepared largely from information gathered during the orientation interviews. The re- port includes (1) Previous criminal record, (2) Report on his adjustment during orientation period and a summary of the orientation officer's impressions, (3) Social history, which gives information about his family, marital history, personal history, and release plans, (4) Medical history, and (5) History of his past employment, education, religion, and preferences in reading and recreation. 

Men who are designated as "close custody" inmates are housed in cell blocks constructed for maximum confinement, and are kept under continuous supervision. "Medium custody" prisoners may be housed either in cell blocks or in one of the institution's four barracks-dormitories. "Minimum custody" men may live either in the dormitories or in the special "honor cell blocks," where they are not under close restriction. Inmates in the honor cells, who are selected on basis of good conduct, are granted the privilege of exercising individual taste as to furnishing the cell, and in furnishing their recreation room, where they may listen to a radio and play cards or dominoes. 

All cell houses and dormitories are equipped with showers, and ample toilet facilities—all of which are kept spotlessly clean. [ 10 ]

A CLASS IN MATHEMATICS is pictured above. Qualified inmates are allowed to teach a variety of subjects under the supervision of trained personnel in the institution's education department. The prison school runs from beginning classes for illiterates, up to and including the eighth grade. Completion of an eleventh grade education is possible. 

Education Stressed 

While in the orientation section, each new inmate takes a Stanford Standardized Achievement Test, which measures his educational level. 

All men testing below the fourth grade are required to attend the institution's school, providing they are of normal intelligence. 

The classroom work is divided into four sections: "D" section for those testing from illiterate to the 2nd grade; "C" section for those testing in the 3rd and 4th grades on the standardized test; "B" section for 5th and 6th; and "A" section for 7th and 8th grades (although instruction in "A" section will enable a man to score as high as 11th grade on the standardized test.)

After a man completes one of the sections he is tested on the Stanford Achievement Test, and if he has advanced enough, is promoted to the next higher section. 

In addition to the classroom work, the institution offers vocational training in practically all skills and trades. Some of the trades in which "on the job" training is accompanied by related classroom work are: cooking, baking, machinist, welding, carpentry, sheet metal, painting, electrician's work, plumbing, laundry work, automobile mechanics, steamfitting, blueprint reading, shop mathematics, and agricultural subjects. 

Also available is training as a laboratory technician, dental technician, male nurse, typing, filing, and clerical work, accounting, photography.

For courses in which the institution's educational department does not have classroom instruction, correspondence courses are arranged for interested inmates. George H. Wray, supervisor of education, stated that [ 11 ]

approximately 40 inmates are now taking correspondence courses from universities and the I. C. S. in subjects which the institution is not equipped to teach.

The F. C. I. library, which numbers 4,000 volumes, has a fine selection of books, and is used a great deal by the prison population. A total of 1,983 volumes were in circulation during January of this year. 

The library provides a Bible and a dictionary for each man to keep in his quarters. The library also subscribes to leading popular magazines, and several newspapers. 

Recreation, one of the items of major importance at any institution, comes in for considerable attention at Summit. C. W. Burkhart, officer in charge of athletics and recreation, is responsible for directing all leisure time activities.

The largest item on the athletic menu is softball which begins in March and continues through the summer months. Last year the institution had 12 intramural softball teams, and two varsity squads. 

Interest runs high in both the intramural and varsity teams. Last year approximately 25 per cent of the institution's population was participating in the sport.

The varsity team has played several games with "outside" teams in this section both at the prison and away. They have collected an impressive record of 37 wins against 5 defeats in these games during the past three years.

Mr. Burkhart, who is also the varsity coach, said that he stresses sportsmanship as well as the competitive will to win. Unsportsmanlike conduct isn't permitted.

The institution has two softball diamonds and a grandstand to carry out its program. Other popular outdoor sports are basketball, handball, touch football, shuffleboard, croquet, and horseshoes.

Ping-pong, dominoes, checkers, and card games take top popularity honors in indoor sports, and tournaments are organized whenever interest warrants it.

Mr. Burkhart explained that he could not organize and carry out his program without the cooperation of the men themselves. "It's their show, and they have to help run it," he said. A protest committee composed of Mr. Burkhart and inmate managers of the intramural softball teams hears all grievances and establishes the penalties for violation of the rules. Over a period of years the committee has helped him in drawing up the set of rules, Burkhart said. 

Moving pictures are shown once a week, and an inmate council helps select the films which will be presented. Last year inmates produced an amateur show, a quiz program, and a musical revue. At present two musical groups are active-a Hillbilly Band, and a swing orchestra. 

At a recent jam-session of the "bebop" band, more than 200 inmates were in attendance.

Other successful activities last year were the organization of a Toastmaster's Club and the forming of a discussion group. 

The inmate's daily schedule provides for leisure time after supper (which begins at 5 p. m.) until 10 p. m. (taps). [ 12 ]

Each institution in the federal prison system has one or more industries, managed by federal employees and using inmate labor. The institutions sell their products to other prisons and to various government agencies. None of the goods are ever placed on the open market. 

This cooperative network of small factories is known as Federal Prison Industries, Inc. The net result of the plan is that federal prisons keep each other supplied with a wide variety of goods (including about everything from canned fruit to heavy machinery), costing little and comparing favorably in quality with the output of private industry. 

Inmates generally regard work in the prison industry as a choice assignment, since a small wage is paid inmate laborers. 

The Federal Correctional Institution at Summit has two factoriesone manufacturing furniture and the other tobacco products. They are under the supervision of Alvin A. Hinze, business manager. Working on his staff are civil service employees, who have qualified both in their trade and as custodial officers.

The factories are both housed in a long, low structure of brick, steel, and concrete with one ground floor and a basement for storage.

E All tobacco used at Summit is purchased on the open market and comes from the Carolinas, Virginia, Kentucky, Maryland, Tennessee and other southern states. It is shipped to the institution in leaf form in large hogsheads.

The pipe tobacco is an all-burley blend. It is blended while still in leaf form, then stemmed. Next it is dipped in a liquid "casing" mixture, which has been cooked in large vats. The mixture consists of honey, molasses, licorice and other flavoring. 

The strips are placed on racks and dried. They are then put through a "cutter," which chops them to the proper fineness, through a "cooler" or large, revolving drum. The final step is the spraying on of a top dressing, made of mapeline, royal flavoring, rum, and other ingredients.

Plug tobacco is also received in leaf form, blended, stemmed, and dipped in a liquid casing mixture. This consists of sugar molasses, syrup, licorice, nulemoline and other flavorings. The tobacco is run through wringers, dried, and subjected to a "top dressing" of apple flavoring, a yeasty substance called St. John's Bread, and other ingredients. It is then lumped into large plugs, aged for about a week in wooden containers, then cut into individual plugs and wrapped in cellophane.

Granulated, or "roll-your-own," is made from bright leaf and burley scrap. It is treated and blended, granulated, and top dressing is applied. After drying it is then packaged in individual sacks.

Government Agencies Buy Furniture 

The F. C. I. furniture factory, under E. J. Halter, superintendent, employs about 85 inmates. Its business is conducted on the same basis as the tobacco factory, except that its products are sold to wider variety of government agencies, in addition to other federal prisons. 

Its main products are bookcases, costumers, desk trays, and fiber furniture (couches, desks, tables, rockers, easy chairs, straight chairs, lounges, and others).

Included among customers for F. C. I. furniture are the Department of Justice, the State Department, the Post Office Department, the Department of Agriculture, the Veterans Administration, various branches of the armed services, and others, in addition to other federal prisons. 

The furniture products made by prison factories throughout the country are listed in a thick catalogue, similar to that issued by commercial mail-order houses. The catalogues are made available to each federal department and agency, giving them a wide variety to choose from and supplying practically all furniture needs. There are two general features of the Federal Correctional Institution at Summit which are most striking to the person who has no knowledge of it:

The first is the variety of things which are done for an inmate in an attempt to restore him to a normal, useful position in society. 

The second is the size and complexity of the institution itself, and the efficiency with which it is run. Ten years ago, starting from scratch, the government constructed an entire city at Summit with all the elements which make up a modern community anywhere. 

Making the time serve the man, instead of vice versa, is not just a trite slogan; it is the guiding principle. Of course the inmate must rehabilitate himself, and no one can do it for him. But able, qualified employees at Summit can supply him with the means to do it, and encourage and assist him when he is sincere in his efforts. 

This sort of treatment is the policy of the Federal Bureau of Prisons. 

The idea of taking revenge on a convict and punishing him is as obsolete as striped suits. This only serves to embitter him and make him a worse enemy of society. [ 18 ]

The enlightened policy of rehabilitation now in effect in federal prisons is not only humanitarian, it is intelligent. As one officer at the institution, a veteran of 15 years in the service, explained it, "Which man would you rather have as a neighbor, the ex-con who has been beaten and mistreated-or the man who has been treated with fairness and taught a new way of life?"

Of course, the inmate population does not dwell in a land of milk and honey. There are regulations which are to be observed, and if a new man thinks differently, he is soon set straight.

This is not accomplished by physical coercion or by terror. No officer, except those in the towers around the institution's fence, is armed. 

Extreme disciplinary problem-cases are placed in an isolated cell where they are utterly alone. These men soon tire of their own company, long for their former privileges, and take a new outlook. 

To enumerate a few of the rehabilitative measures at the institution, we have the following:

Excellent medical and dental care. Both the hospital and dental clinic have the latest equipment, and are staffed by competent men. The role of a man's health in his outlook on life is of great importance. Some men are in such bad physical condition when they enter the institution that one might well say, "No wonder he wound up here." This obstacle to rehabilitation is removed. Dr. F. B. Bralliar is chief medical officer, and Dr. Stanley Raynor is the dentist. 

CUSTODIAL OFFICERS and supervisory personnel at the institution never stop learning about their jobs. They like to think of their training as a continuous thing, and periodically they meet for sessions in the three Rs: "Retrain," "Re-examine," and "Re-think." Articles are stocked here for supplying every part of the institution's varied operations. The total value of goods in the storehouse is somewhere in the neighborhood of $80,000. The parole and welfare department looks closely into his background and examines his personality makeup. By interviews with the inmate, correspondence with the family, former employer, and information from U. S. district attorneys, the FBI and other agencies, the department attempts to gain at least a partial answer to the difficult question, "Why did he break the law?" This information is extremely valuable to prison authorities in understanding each individual case, and deciding what might be done for him. G. V. Richardson is in charge of this department, assisted by Earl Bridges. 

The education department under G. H. Wray determines an inmate's level of education, and if he tests below fourth grade, elementary schooling is mandatory. The institution's classes continue through the eighth grade, teaching practically the same subjects as in regular school systems. No man is promoted to the next higher class until he has passed a standard exam for his present grade. For inmates desiring education at the college level or further training in special subjects, the department is glad to arrange accredited correspondence courses. Also included in this department is the large vocational training program under which an inmate may learn practically any skill or trade. This on-the-job training is carried on in practically every part of the institution under excellent instruction by civilian personnel. 

C. W. Burkhart, recreation officer, is in charge of all leisure time activities. His program includes outdoor and indoor sports, as well as entertainment, plays, discussion groups, etc. The well organized recreation program serves as a channel for excess energy, as well as contributing to a man's physical well-being and teaching him fundamentals of fair play and sportsmanship. [ 20 ]

When a man is about to leave the institution on parole or conditional release, the parole and welfare department performs the all-important function of lining up satisfactory working and living conditions for him to return to. In this task the department relies upon its report of his inclinations, capabilities, and the training he has received at the institution. 

In finding employment for the inmate, the department corresponds with either family, U. S. Probation Officers in the district to which he is to be released, government employment agencies, or former employers. 

A Big Business 

This large, independent community is operated with smooth efficiency by capable men working in a relaxed atmosphere of congeniality -men who take pride in doing their jobs well. It's clockwork smoothness is not due to government regulations saying that it must be run in that way--it is due to men who want to run it that way. 

Don L. Farmer is the institution's chief clerk. He is the man whose headaches result from trying to operate the entire "city" within a limited budget in the face of steadily rising prices. His is also the task of coordinating the farm production with the kitchen needs. In conference with Joseph Havelka, farm manager, and George Stouder, culinary head, it is determined three months in advance what the kitchen will need, how much of this the farm can supply, and how much must be purchased and what it will cost at current prices. These requirements are worked out in complete detail, and Mr. Stouder forecasts to the pound exactly how much of each food item he will serve in the coming three months. 

For example he has already determined that during April, May and 

June he will serve 32,700 pounds of flour, 38,502 pounds of milk, etc.

The cost of feeding a man for one day is only 28 cents, although this does not include farm expenses.

Mr. Farmer also has direct charge of the institution's storehouse which stocks subsistence articles, clothing, hardware supplies, office supplies, maintenance supplies, and farm supplies. A perpetual inventory maintained by his office could reveal exactly what was in the storehouse if it burned down at any moment, and what each item was worth. 

Mr. Farmer and his assistant chief clerk, Ward W. Robinson, maintain stock cards for between 800 and 1,000 different items in the inventory. 

Like any sizable city, the F. C. I. has its "board of health" in the person of W. W. Meng, director of sanitation and safety. 

Mr. Meng makes regular inspections of all departments of the institution, and checks closely to see that high standards of sanitation are maintained. These standards are in practically all cases more rigid than those in comparable situations "on the outside." Examples of this are the kitchen sanitary requirements and bacteria-count in milk. 

Mr. Meng is also responsible for safety in all departments, particularly in industrial operations. Last year the institution compiled a record of only three lost-time accidents, which is rather remarkable considering that this includes a machine shop, auto mechanic shop, two industries, and other heavy machinery work. 

Officers Important 

The custodial officers at the institution have the fundamentally important job of maintaining the custody of the inmates-but they are no less interested in looking after the welfare and helping to train the men they must supervise. [ 21 ]

MRS. JETTIE HAMPTON, pictured above in the Summit control room, has the distinction of being the only woman correctional officer in any federal prison for men in the United States. The control room is the nerve center of the institution. Here records are kept showing the exact location of any inmate at any given minute of the day. All the keys to each door in the institution are kept in the filing cabinet in the background, as well as all "hot" tools (files, saw blades, etc.)

Fred Hammond is in charge of the officer training program at the institution, through which new officers are selected, orientated, and trained. For the first four weeks of an officer's service he serves an apprenticeship and undergoes a basic training course. 

This course includes orientation lectures, on-the-job training, physical proficiency instruction and firearms training. Throughout the course his progress is rated daily by an older officer who is on the job with him and at the end of the period he is required to pass a written examination. 

Before a man is eligible to be a candidate for the course he must pass a strict Civil Service exam, and must have a high school education or equivalent.

Lt. Hammond likes to emphasize to trainees the extreme importance of this basic training course by illustrating that in trades such as carpentry, bricklaying and others where a man deals with material things he must sometimes serve an apprenticeship of as much as four years. However, in the trade of custodial officer-where he is dealing with human beings he has an apprenticeship of only four weeks. 

Also emphasized is the proper relationship with the inmate. Somewhere between the pinnacle of aloofness and the other extreme of fraternization, there is a happy medium-which could be described as something close to friendly interest tempered by firmness. This middle road is the objective.
