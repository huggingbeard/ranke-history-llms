INTRODUCTION 

This brief biographical sketch is the first of a series of Quaker biographies which will be prepared for the use of young people of the junior high school grade. These sketches do not aim at giving complete information but rather at interpreting the lives of the men and women who have made important contributions to Quaker history.

RUFUS M. JONES 

On the campus of Haverford College in southeastern Pennsylvania, is the home of one of the best-known Quakers in the world. Many of the readers of this sketch have seen and heard Rufus M. Jones speak. If you have not yet read A Small Town Boy, in which he tells us of his early boyhood, you will want to do so. 

This very busy man has taken time from the heavy responsibilities which he carries to recall the incidents of his boyhood and write them down for us to read about. The great value of these stories, which picture the life of a country Quaker boy full of energy and interest and loving fun like other boys, consists in seeing how the influence of his childhood helped to make him what he is. Life held for the country boy of the period in which he grew up more work than recreation, but he managed to get enough of the latter to escape being the "dull boy" which the old rhyme says "all work and no play" makes.

Early Life 

He speaks of his home in Maine where he was born in 1863 as a place that "nurtured in the things that matter most for the shaping of life." In it, the influences not only of his father and mother but of his grandmother and an unusual aunt were active forces. He learned to know and love the Old Testament stories and to know God as a constant, real presence. He nevertheless was no little saint, and became the leader of a gang of boys whose thought was not always about what they could do to make other people happy. With his family he always went to meeting, and he writes in his book of boyhood recollections that "I learned to swim and to enjoy silent worship at about the same time." He portrays the strong points of the vigorous personalities in the meeting as well as the oddities who are usually present in every meeting where the democratic method of speaking prevails. 

His school life was especially important to a lad of keen and inquiring mind, and contacts with superior teachers opened a new world to him. He was very anxious to have a better education than the schools near which he lived afforded. He wanted to go to the 

Friends' school at Providence, R. I. (now known as the Moses Brown School), but the family were too poor to meet the expense. As a last hope he wrote and asked for a scholarship. He rather unexpectedly re-

ceived it. Many years afterward he learned that the grant had been made because a member of the committee who knew his mother had said, "I know that boy's mother. She has told me a lot about the boy and 

I want him to have a scholarship." This opportunity he counts as "a great divide" in his life.

His book gives us a glimpse of the joyous and healthgiving pursuits of boys, climbing roofs, diving off bridges, sailing on cakes of ice, as well as swimming, skating and coasting. His own words sum up the influence on his life of the daring boyish adventures which he records: "My group of boys, as we played together and did daring things together, helped me to build secretly and silently the hidden inner self which they very little suspected." Sketches of some of the village folk and the unique New England Town Meeting add further touches to the influences which played upon a man in the making.

School and College 

The three years at Moses Brown were rich in experience. A study of foreign languages, science and mathematics shared with athletics his absorbing interest. In entering into this new experience he for the first time found what railway trains and boats looked like. In after years he had plenty of chance to know how luxurious or how uncomfortable they could be. The

greatest change in him was his interest in and understanding of the meaning of religion and the characters of the Bible.

When he went from school to college, another important dividing line was crossed. He entered the sophomore class at Haverford College. During the three years he was there as a student, he devoured books and lectures as a hungry man does his food, and his appetite was very large. He was especially fond of 

James Russell Lowell's poems and essays. He developed a great interest in history and at one time thought he might become a teacher of history. At another time he thought he would study law; but when an interested friend offered to pay the entire cost of a law course for him, he had to think seriously about whether he wanted to do it. After thinking very earnestly about it, he decided that much as he admired the profession of law, it wasn't the right thing for him to do.

The college boys went to meeting every week, and in this college period he became very sure that the most important thing about religion was neither listening to sermons nor hymns, but what goes on inside of us. 

And apparently a great deal went on inside of him during his student days. In his Trail of Life in College he says that though he learned a great many useful things to fit him for his life work, the most important

thing he learned was how to live with himself. We all know that is sometimes a hard thing to do.

College life has many curious customs connected with it. At Haverford one of them is the selection of a 'spoon man." The choice is made by vote as to who is the best all-round member of the class. They count standing in studies, athletics, taking a helpful part in the life of the college, and personal popularity. Rufus 

M. Jones had the honor not only of being president of his class, but of being presented with the wooden spoon which was the token that he had been elected first man of his class.

His Chosen Profession 

He chose teaching as his life work. In this field his work has always been in Friends' schools: first at 

Oakwood Seminary, New York, then at the Moses

Brown School, followed by four years as principal of Oak Grove Seminary at Vassalboro, Maine. He was a schoolmaster who not only knew the subjects he was teaching but, what was far more important, he knew his pupils and their needs, and the boys soon found out that he did. In 1893 he began his long service as a professor of Philosophy and Psychology at Haverford

College. The hundreds of students who sat in his classes there as the years went by can, and often do, tell the story of how he helped them to see life as it ought to be.

Minister and Lecturer 

Since he was twenty-five years of age, Rufus Jones has been a frequent and inspiring speaker in Friends' meetings for worship and has spoken many times to audiences of other denominations.

As a public lecturer, his words have reached thousands of people. He has a keen sense of humor and when the interest of an audience might lag on account of the seriousness of his message, he brings in a telling anecdote to impress his point and hold the attention of listeners. His fund of stories seems never to be exhausted and he has one to suit every occasion. If you who read this brief biography have never heard him tell a story, ask somebody who has to relate one for you. 

Of course it will lose something in being retold, for the casual way in which he brings in a story cannot be imitated. A typical one is as follows: "A New England farmer built a stone wall three feet high and four feet wide. When asked by a curious observer why he had chosen such strange proportions for his wall, the farmer said, 'Well, if it gets hit by a wind and blown over, it will be higher than it was before." "

The application is easy: if we are knocked over by disappointment we will stand taller than before if our lives rest on a broad enough character base. He never uses this attractive method of securing attention just

as a recreative pastime. The stories always illustrate a point he wishes to drive home.

Perhaps you are interested in knowing what kinds of subjects he speaks about. The answer to such a question is, almost everything that is worth while-education, social work, peace and good will, Quaker history and biography, the peoples of the earth and their customs, the nature of religious experience, the work of the American Friends Service Committee. He is one of our best interpreters of Quakerism as a way of life; for to him all life is religious and there is no division between practicing religious forms and being good members of families and of society. 

Writer 

The editor of a paper has a fine chance to get his ideas across to a great many people every week. Rufus 

M. Jones made good use of such an opportunity when he became editor of The American Friend in 1893. 

He held this position until 1912, when the publication office of the paper was moved to Richmond, Indiana. 

His constant theme was the personal dependence of man on God, and how people can express their religion in the way they live. Some of those who read the paper did not agree with him, but for many it became a stimulating organ of thought.

He has also written many books dealing with a

variety of subjects. His Stories of Hebrew Heroes, with which you may be familiar, is a compilation of Bible stories which he told his own little boy who, still in childhood, slipped away to his final home with God.

St. Paul the Hero is another book young people enjoy. 

Finding the Trail of Life and The Trail of Life in 

College are especially readable for serious-minded young people because they tell the story of his own. early life.

A book that young people who want to be good members of the Society of Friends will wish to read by the time they are out of school or college is Faith and Practice of the Quakers. It was written to answer the questions about Friends which were being asked by persons who saw what we do, but did not understand why we do it. Organizer

He has always liked to organize people. From the time he was a boy and headed a "gang," he showed an ability to get people together for a common purpose. 

This ability was recognized when he was made chairman of the Board of Directors of Bryn Mawr College. 

He was one of the leaders organizing twelve of the Yearly Meetings of the Friends in America into what is known as "The Five Years Meeting." (This does not include either of the two Philadelphia Yearly Meetings.)

The most important group he ever brought together is the well-known American Friends Service Committee. 

When the first world war began, the English Friends, who believed it was wrong to go to war and kill people, wanted to show that they were not slackers but were willing to give their time and strength to hard tasks when by so doing they could bring help to those in need. They accordingly organized the work of sending persons into France to care for the people whose houses had been destroyed in the battle of the Marne. Rufus 

Jones thought out and started plans in 1917 for a similar work under the care of American Quakers. 

While many others helped to set up and carry on the work, he was its leader and has always continued to be a great vital inspiration. This organization, started for relief in war time, has found an important place in the service which the Society of Friends is trying to give in making a better world. We all know about sewing groups and the tons of supplies which are collected to be sent where needed. We ought to learn more about this great means of helpful service which is still (1942) headed by its founder. 

World Conference of Friends 

One of the results of the Service Committee work was the establishment of a good many small Friends' Meetings in many parts of the world. In 1937 a confer-

ence was held at Swarthmore, Pa., with Quaker representatives from all over the world in attendance. Rufus 

M. Jones was chosen to preside over the sessions. We call him the clerk, but the English called him the "clark." He said it was fair for him to say "clerk❞ half of the time and "clark" the other half. The English were there and sweltered in their hot woolen suits; 

Danes, Norwegians, Swiss and Hollanders were dressed according to the custom of their own countries; kilts and caps, and breeches above the knees mingled with our American and English made garments, while Rufus always looked comfortable on the hot platform in a white linen suit, and kept everybody in good humor while questions very important to Quakerism were eagerly talked over. 

Fellowship Council

Another idea which was born in Rufus Jones's brain has taken form in what is called Friends Fellowship Council. This is meant to include not only all the 

Friends who belong to Meetings the world over, but group who are called "the friends of Friends." They are persons who are sympathetic with Quaker principles and ideals but who do not want to give up their own church membership. This group includes people of all races and denominations. It is based on the idea that

a "society of friends" ought to include everybody who is interested in helping us carry out our principles. 

Mission to Germany 

In 1938, after the persecution of the Jews in Germany had reached an acute stage, Friends in America wanted to send some men to Germany, in order to see whether we could help in any way to relieve the persecuted people. Rufus M. Jones was chosen as one of a delegation of three persons. Because Friends had fed

German children after the world war, our representatives were allowed to talk with officials and were kindly received.

World Traveler 

Occasionally a person makes a trip around the world. 

This distance, if one could go in a straight line over land and sea, is about 25,000 miles. Of course no one travels that way, so the number of miles traveled is much greater than that. Rufus M. Jones went around the world not only once but twice, and his route was a very winding one so that he could touch South Africa,

India, Japan and China. He probably does not know, himself, how many miles he covered in all. His first journey to Europe, which he made soon after he graduated from college, was a tremendous event and he has made many since. He never had time to go over on a

sight-seeing trip as many people do, for he had so many real missions which took him to distant parts of the world. He undertook his first long journey, to China in 

1926, at the request of the Young Men's Christian

Association; the second, in 1932, as a member of a commission of laymen (not ministers) to study about missionary work in the Orient; and the third, in 1938, to visit Friends in South Africa, China and Japan. His friends were deeply concerned about his making such an effort, for by this time he was seventy-five years old. 

He felt sure he ought to go and was able to accomplish all he set out to do.

Whether he is at work in China, Japan, Africa, Maine, Philadelphia or California, his purpose is always the same: he is trying to help people find the true values of living religious lives. He wants everybody to know how much happier people are who live close to God and who do their share of trying to make the world better.

Personal Traits 

Someone may be curious to know whether this learned and religious man, who has made so many contacts with the men who sit in high places in church and state and business, can really be approached by common people like us. If you have a chance to meet him, this question will be settled for you; but as most

of our readers may not have this pleasure, they may be assured that his delight is to mingle and talk with all kinds of persons. He once said to the writer, "I never saw anybody yet that I could not learn something from.”

His amazing ability to understand people's needs and to know how to minister to them, is a result of the constant habit of reaching out into the lives not only of the wise and great but also of the unlettered and simple-minded. Richness of life comes through knowing all kinds of people and wishing to help them know God as a present reality. He is what we call "a good mixer,” whose serious purposes are enlivened by a cheerful disposition and a keen sense of humor. Just as a magnet attracts steel, he seems to be able to attract the best from people. While he has a deep respect for good thinking, he cares little about just what any of us believe, but a great deal about how we act. 

He loves fun (though not the same kind perhaps that he enjoyed when he was a boy in Maine); he loves work; he loves people; he loves to help people in any way that he can; he loves to attend Friends' meeting; he must love to write books, for he has written nearly fifty. There is a bit of advice in the Old Testament which says, "Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might." Rufus M. Jones has always done that, and he has found an unusual number of things to do.

His Great Contribution 

Most persons do very well if they make a success of one thing in life. Few have contributed successfully in so many fields. As teacher, preacher, lecturer, writer, diplomat and organizer he has made important and permanent contributions to the lives of the people among whom he has worked. We never think of asking what branch of Friends he belongs to. He is a member of the Yearly Meeting of Friends for New England, but we like to think he belongs to all of us. Because his home is at Haverford he has attended that Meeting and has been closely associated with Philadelphia Yearly Meeting at Arch Street. 

If we try to think what is the most important thing this man of many accomplishments tries to teach us, we may select one thing which we all can understand as the central principle of his different interests. It is that God is a constant presence to help and guide us. 

Everything that is beautiful or true or good shows the presence of God. He is with us in our work and in our play, in the hours that we sleep and the hours when we are awake. He is with us in our joys and in our unhappy moments. He is the urge within us to make our lives worth while. If we can make this message our own, we will become a little like Rufus Jones. Combining the traits of intellectual and spiritual leadership with a passion for human service, he is a foremost leader

of the Friends of this generation. He is modest about the things he has done and is doing, for he is the kind of man that cares more about being useful than about being honored. He seems to have absorbed and expressed in his own life the spirit of Jesus when he said, "He that is greatest among you shall be your servant.”
