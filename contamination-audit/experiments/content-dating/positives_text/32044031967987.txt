Part I INTESTATE SUCCESSION TESTACY AND INTESTACY 

Terminology. When an owner of property dies, he either does or does not leave a valid will. The state or condition arising from a disposition of property by will is called "testacy"; and that arising from lack of such disposition is known as "intestacy." A person is said to die "intestate" if he has not left a will, and "testate" if he has. These terms are likewise applied to the decedent himself, he being called an intestate if he does not leave a will, and a testate or testator if he does; and the terms are applied also to the property left by him. Property disposed of by will is designated as testate property; that not so disposed of, as intestate property.

Partial. A person may die testate as to some of his property and intestate as to the remainder. This might result from intention, from inadvertence, or from a change in conditions after the will was made. If a will mentions specific property only, which was not all the property owned by the testator, his remaining property would not pass under the will, but would be intestate. 

HEIRS AND NEXT OF KIN 

Terminology. Upon the death of a person, his intestate property passes to others by operation of law, realty descending at once to his heirs, and personalty passing to his administrator for ultimate distribution among his next of kin. An Heir (feminine, heiress), then, may be defined as one upon whom the law casts realty immediately on the death of the owner intestate.

INTESTATE SUCCESSION 

Next of Kin, or Distributees, are those among whom the personalty of a person dying intestate is ultimately distributed after the payment of all just claims. A person cannot have heirs, in the technical sense of that word, until his death as not until then can it be definitely known who will inherit. The Latin maxim expressing this rule is: Nemo est haeres viventis.1 The same is true of next of kin.

Descent or hereditary succession is, technically, the devolution of realty on the heirs; and the property so acquired is called the Inheritance. Distribution is the division of the personalty among the next of kin thru administration. The personalty left by a decedent is known as his Estate; and the proportions ultimately received by the next of kin are known as Distributive Shares. Heirs inherit; but next of kin do not inherit in the technical sense of that word. 

It will be noticed, from what has just been said, that there are four distinctions between heirs and next of kin. First, heirs take realty; next of kin take personalty. 

Second, realty "descends" to the heirs; personalty is 'distributed" among the next of kin. Third, realty passes directly to the heirs, and not thru any intermediary; personalty must pass thru the hands of an administrator. Fourth, heirs are entitled to the realty just as soon as the former owner has drawn his last breath; personalty is generally not distributed until some time has elapsed after the administrator has received it.

In many, perhaps in most, cases the heirs and the next of kin of a decedent are the same persons, the distinction existing only where the statute makes a differentiation between those who take realty and those who take personalty, or provides that realty and personalty shall be taken in different proportions.

The expressions, "heirs at law," "legal heirs," "heirs general," "right heirs," and the like, have the same meaning as "heirs." "Heirs" and "children" are not synonymous terms, tho the children of an intestate are usually his heirs, and this, no doubt, has given rise to the popular error that the terms are synonymous. 

Those who take under a will are not heirs unless they take exactly the same property and in the same proportion as they would have taken without a will. 

An "heir apparent" is one who is bound to inherit from another person if the latter should die first and intestate; thus a son is an heir apparent of his father, as he will be an heir if he outlives his father. An "heir presumptive" is one who would inherit from another person if the latter were to die at once intestate, but whose prospects may be taken away by a subsequent birth. Thus a brother of an unmarried inan would be an heir presumptive, but as soon as the man marries and a child is born, there is no prospect of the brother's becoming an heir as long as the child or its descendants live.

It must be borne in mind that an estate for years in land, whatever its duration, is treated as personalty,' hence the interest of a deceased tenant goes to his administrator and not to his heirs, unless some statute otherwise provides. If a landlord, owning the fee, should die intestate, his interest would descend to his heirs, who would take subject to the lease. 

Consanguinity, Affinity, Adoption.-Heirs and next of kin bear some legal relation to the intestate, which may be thru consanguinity, affinity, or adoption. Consanguinity is the relationship subsisting among persons descended from the same stock or common ancestor, popularly known as "blood relations."

Affinity is the connection, in consequence of the marriage relation, between each of the married persons and the blood relations of the other. It is dissolved by the death of either spouse. In the modern use of this word, the relation of affinity exists between husband and wife.

Adoption is a judicial proceeding creating the artificial relation of parent and child between two persons not legally having that relation. In its legal and proper sense, it is of modern origin and is regulated entirely by statute, which prescribes the form therefor and fixes the rights and the obligations of the parties thereafter, including the right of each to participate, after the death of the other, in the estate of the decedent. 

Usually the relation created by adoption is not the same as that existing in the natural relation; and sometimes rights arising from the adoption of a child under the laws of one state are not recognized by other states. 

Under the common law the only method of adopting a child was thru a subterfuge or by means of a fiction. 

Thus, if A wished to adopt the boy X, A would give a bond to B, agreeing to pay B a certain sum to care for 

X, the "son" of A. B would then bring suit against A on the bond, alleging that A had not paid the money as agreed, and A would set up the defense that X was not his son.

The old common law, however, attached great importance to an instrument under seal, and the parties thereto generally were estopped from contradicting it. 

As A in his bond had designated X as his son, it was taken to be true, and a judgment based on this admitted fact became conclusive as to the relationship. 

In popular language, a foster child, that is a child taken merely to be reared by others than its parents, is spoken of as having been adopted; but a child not

11 adopted in the form prescribed by statute is not entitled to any rights in the estate of a deceased foster parent, except such as may arise thru a will or thru some contract giving a foundation for a claim for services, as in the case of any stranger.

Lineal and Collateral Relations. If heirs are descended one from another, as great-grandfather, grandfather, father, the intestate, son, grandson, great-grandson, great-great-grandson, and so on, they are known as Lineal descendants (from the Latin word linea, a line). 

If they are descended from a common ancestor but not one from another, they are called Collateral descendants (from the Latin words con, with, and latus, side). 

Thus, a brother is a collateral relation, the intestate and the brother having the same parent, but neither being descended from the other; likewise an uncle, a cousin, or a nephew would be related collaterally. Lineal relationship is "ascending" or "descending." Ascending relationship is "paternal" (thru the father) or "maternal" (thru the mother), and descending relationship is male or female.

Ancestors. A person from whom property is inherited is known in law as the Ancestor, which is the correlative of "heir." In the popular use of the word, ancestor means progenitor; but in law a progenitor may be the heir of his descendant, in which case the descendant would be the ancestor, as where a father inherits from his deceased child.

Allowing two children to a generation, three generations to a century, and ignoring intermarriages among progenitors and among issue, every person now living had over one million ancestors living seven hundred years ago and will have the same number of descendants living seven centuries hence. Thus, everyone has two parents, four grandparents, and eight great-grandparents.

INTESTATE SUCCESSION 

Doubling the number for each past generation for twenty generations will result in the astonishing number mentioned above. Likewise, if every one has two children, four grandchildren, and so on, a similar result in the descending line will be reached. 

Considering the roving character of many human beings, this seems to prove that our progenitors came from every race, and that some ancestor of everyone now living must have participated in every important event which occurred two thousand or more years ago on any part of the earth's surface, and that our numerous ancestors must have moved in all grades of society from kings to savages, and ranged thru degrees of morality from saints to criminals, and were among the richest, the poorest, the wisest, the most idiotic, the strongest, and the weakest on the globe.

The same will be true of the descending line. On the same basis the living collateral relations of each person, computed from the common ancestor twenty generations back, would number over two hundred and fifty billion, or more than one hundred times the present population of the world, intermarriages accounting for the difference.¹

Statutes of Descent and Distribution: Object.- 

Statutes of descent and distribution, designating those who are to take intestate property and the proportion in which it shall be taken, are the result of an effort by legislatures to divide such property equitably. The object of such statutes is to give the property to those whorn, in the average instance, the intestate would naturally have selected if he had indicated his wishes. 

That such object has been attained in many cases is shown by the fact that many a property owner assigns the equitable division which will be made under the

However, as in the case of all arbitrary, uniform rules, legal or otherwise, there are many instances in which intestate property is actually unfairly distributed under the statute, and probably not as the intestate would have wished. 

Possible Origin.-In modern times we are so accustomed to the method of distribution provided by statute that the average person regards the statutory method as merely affirming the natural right of certain persons to the property left by an intestate. There is, however, no natural right in such cases; for, in the absence of any law or custom on the point, a person at his death would be regarded as having abandoned his property, and it would belong to the first person taking actual possession. Title by occupancy, no doubt, was originally the method of obtaining title to a decedent's property; and the fact that the nearest relations of the decedent were usually with him at his death, and thus had the first and best opportunity of appropriating his property, may have originated a custom which later became law. 

Furthermore, it seemed just that the family should be given preference in this respect, as they may have aided the decedent in his acquisition of the property and were dependent on him for support. Also, the dying person, realizing that he was soon to leave all his earthly possessions, probably made gifts to the members of his family about him, and this likewise may have had something to do with the selection of his family as the beneficiaries under the law.

In any event, if intestate distribution depended entirely on the theory of abandonment, it would result in unseemly squabbles around the bedside of the late departed; those absorbed by their grief might find that

more remote or sordid relatives had been more prompt in the appropriation of property, leaving nothing for those intimately related, and if the death was unexpected, strangers, without any moral right, would, in many cases, appropriate everything. 

Then, too, if the decedent had been a debtor, there would be nothing left with which to pay his obligations. 

It was to conserve assets for discharging liabilities, to do justice, and to prevent injustice, that the state decided to step in and arbitrarily dictate what disposition should be made of a decedent's property. The right to inherit is not a natural right, but one given by statute, because an inheritance tax is not a tax on proerty but is a tax on the right to take property by descent.

Dower and Homestead.-The student must bear in mind that dower rights, homestead rights, and the like are quite independent of the laws of descent, as these rights attached before there were any heirs. The rights of heirs are subordinate not only to such rights but also to existing incumbrances, such as a mortgage. 

Provisions.-The general rule, in the matter of inheritance, is that the descending line is preferred to all others; that is, children, or their descendants, if any, will take all the property to the exclusion of all other relations. If there is no descending line, then the near collateral relations, such as brothers and sisters, and the immediate ascending line, such as parents, are usually given preference over more remote relations. If there is a surviving spouse, such spouse is usually given at least a part of the property, sometimes all if there are no descendants. If there are no descendants, surviving spouse, brothers, sisters, or parents, then persons having the fewest degrees of relationship between them and the intestate become the heirs.

Part II WILLS Historical Development 

Testamentary Disposition a Privilege. It is so customary in modern times for a person to make a disposition of his property to take effect at death, that the right to make a will is regarded as a natural one, the same as the right to dispose of property during life; and it usually is a matter of astonishment to learn that the common law did not recognize wills as we now understand them, and that our ancestors four hundred years ago were greatly restricted in making dispositions of property to take effect at death. 

Absolute property is a mere fiction of law; it denotes the power of the owner over things while he lives, and does not mean the duration of his power. Practically, ownership of any kind necessarily ends with death; and the ability of an owner of property to designate those who shall take it when he has drawn his last breath, is a mere privilege conferred by statute. 

Before the Norman Conquest. Prior to the Norman conquest of England in the year 1066, testamentary disposition of property was recognized to a limited degree; but the development of feudalism¹ shortly thereafter gradually brought this practice to an end so far as realty was concerned. Courts of chancery, however, by means of uses, evaded the feudal inhibition against wills. The method adopted was to have land conveyed by the owner in the usual form with directions that the grantee was to hold the land for the use of the grantor and to such special uses as the grantor might afterwards designate; and the grantor subsequently, 1See Volume 5, American Law and Procedure, page 5, §4. 19

Statute of Uses. The enactment by the English Parliament of the Statute of Uses, 27 Henry VIII, c. 10,2 which became effective in May, 1536, prevented further evasion in this way, because, under the provisions of that statute, a conveyance of land for the use of another was treated as a conveyance to the person entitled to the use, so that when a conveyance reserved a use to the grantor, the land reverted at once to him,³ with the result that the grantee took nothing. 

Statute of Wills. The popular demand for power to dispose of land by will finally caused the passage of the Statute of Wills, 32 Henry VIII, c. 1, which became effective July 20, 1540. This statute was quite limited in its operation, but subsequent statutes from time to time enlarged the power of testamentary disposition. 

Terminology. "Will" is another of those legal terms difficult of definition, altho everyone may know its meaning. There are so many kinds of wills, and so many different rights which can be created by wills, that it is not easy to formulate a definition broad enough to include everything which should be included and narrow enough to exclude what should not be included, yet sufficiently concise to make the definition of any practical use.

A will is not restricted to the disposition of property or to the testator's affairs. A document which does no more than nominate an executor, or appoint a guardian for an infant, may nevertheless be a will. 

About as satisfactory a definition as any, from a technical standpoint, is to say that a Will is the expression, in the manner required by law, and operative for no purpose

DEVISES AND BEQUESTS 

Definitions. A Devise is a testamentary disposition of realty. The realty so given is also called a devise. 

Where the property given by will is realty, the testator frequently is designated as a Devisor; and the person designated by a will to take realty is called a Devisee. 

The proper verb to be used in connection with a testamentary disposition of realty is "devise."

A Bequest is a testamentary disposition of personalty; or, if consisting of money only, it is called a Legacy; and these designations are also given to the property so given. The person designated by a will to take personalty is called a Legatee; and the proper verb to be used in connection with a testamentary disposition of personalty is "bequeath."

Both a devise and a bequest are frequently designated as "gifts," in a very broad sense of that word; the

INTESTATE SUCCESSION 

person designated to take them is called a "donee" or "beneficiary"; and the testator is said to "give" the property. The use of these words, tho convenient, is technically not a correct one. The word "give" is usually combined with the words "devise" and "bequeath," as "I give and devise," or "I give and bequeath"; or, if a disposition of both realty and personalty is made in one clause, "I give, devise, and bequeath.” 

General, Demonstrative, and Specific Gifts.-Devises and bequests are subject to various classifications. As to the subject matter, bequests are general, demonstrative, or specific.

A General bequest is one of quantity, the subject matter not in any way separated or distinguished from other property of the same kind, and not requiring delivery or payment of any specific articles or fund, or from any particular portion of the estate. Thus, a bequest of a sum of money or of a quantity of grain would be general, even tho given for a special purpose. 

The delivery to the legatee of any money or of any grain of the designated kind and amount would be sufficient. 

A Demonstrative bequest is one of a certain amount or quantity, the particular property from which it is to be made and of which it is a part only being designated or "demonstrated." Thus a legacy of "$1,000 out of the debt of $5,000 owing me by A," or a bequest of "100 bushels of corn out of the crop now growing on my farm," would be demonstrative. A demonstrative bequest, in its nature, occupies a position intermediate between a general and a specific one, having some of the characteristics of each.

A Specific bequest is one of particular property designated, identified, and distinguished from all other of the same kind. A bequest of "my black horse named Snowball" would be specific, as there is but one article which can be delivered to satisfy the gift.

23 A bequest of "all my goods and chattels," or of "all the rest and residue of my personal property," is a general one; so is a bequest of all of a certain kind of property, as all of the stocks owned by the testator at the time of his death; so usually is a bequest of stated sums in bonds, stocks, or other securities, without any direction to select them out of the testator's estate; but if the intention of the testator is to bequeath a particular thing, as "my stock," or a particular promissory note owing by another, or a deposit in a specified bank, the bequest is specific.

A bequest of an entire debt would be specific and not demonstrative. A bequest of "my gold watch" would be specific; but a bequest of "a gold watch" would be general. In the first case the testator had in mind one particular watch; in the second case a particular watch is not designated, and if the testator did not leave a gold watch, the executor should buy one for the legatee. or A devise may be general or specific. A general devise is one of lands not defined, as "all my real estate," "all the rest and residue of my real estate," the testator having different estates which will pass under such designations. A general devise will carry reversions and all interests in realty not otherwise disposed of. 

Devises, however, are usually specific, that is, a definite tract is indicated.

Cumulative and Substitutional Gifts.-A gift may be cumulative or substitutional. A Cumulative gift is one given in addition to another. Illustrations of bequests of this character will appear later when the construction of wills is discussed. A Substitutional gift is one given in lieu of another.

Residuary Gifts.-A Residuary devise or bequest is one disposing of all the realty or of all the personalty of the testator not otherwise effectually disposed of by

the will, even tho the testator may think he has disposed of it. The supposition is that the residuary devisee or legatee was preferred by the testator to everyone except particular legatees; and this is true altho the residuary devisee or legatee is given all property not "specifically" given. A residuary bequest is a general one. 

Lapsed Gifts: Definition.-A Lapsed devise or be quest is one originally valid but which, tho never revoked by the testator, fails to vest on account of something occurring, thru some contingency or condition, between the time of the making of the will and the time when the gift under the will would otherwise have vested. 

Causes.-Lapse may result from the nonexistence of the donee, from the happening or not happening of a contingency or the performance or nonperformance of a condition, from refusal of the donee to accept, or from other causes; but by far the most common cause is the death of the donee after the will is made and prior to the death of the testator. In such a case there is no one at the testator's death answering the description of the donee who can take the gift; and, as a will does not confer any rights before the testator's death, no one can take thru the deceased donee. As a living person cannot have heirs in the legal sense of that word, a devise to the "heirs of A" could not take effect if A be living when the will becomes effective, and the devise would, therefore, fail for want of someone answering the description.

A devise is not prevented from lapsing because made to a person "and his heirs." His predecease would prevent his heirs from receiving anything, as the devise was intended wholly for him, and his heirs must inherit thru him or not at all. Besides, such words are terms of limitation, merely defining the quantity of interest intended. Likewise, if a legacy is given to "A and his

WILLS 

25 executors or administrators," A's prior death would cause the legacy to lapse. The death of the donee simultaneously with the testator, as in an accident, would cause a lapse; but there would not be a lapse if the donee should outlive the testator, even tho a fraction of a minute only, in which case the donee's rights under the will would pass to those entitled to his other property. 

Partial. There may be a lapse of a part of a gift, as where a devise or a legacy is made to two or more designated persons, as tenants in common, and some but not all of them die before the testator; but if such donees were to have taken in joint tenancy, the survivors would take all; and where a gift is made to a class, such as "children," and not to individual members thereof, those surviving the testator will take the whole. 

Corporations.-A gift to a corporation which is dissolved after the will is made and before the testator's death, will lapse, as dissolution of a corporation is equivalent to its death.

Unperformed Conditions.-If a gift is made dependent on a precedent condition or contingency which cannot happen, it cannot take effect; as a devise to an unmarried woman "so long as she remains unmarried," and she marries before the testator's death.

Substitution.-There will not be any lapse if there is substitution of another for the first donee named, which may be (1) in the will itself, or (2) by statute. Substitution in the will may be express or implied. Where a will directs that, in case of the death of a legatee, certain of the legatee's next of kin are to be excluded, there is manifestly an implication that those not excluded are to be substituted for the deceased legatee. The mere fact, however, of the testator's expressing an intention that the gift shall not lapse is not sufficient unless he further indicates who is to be substituted for the donee named.

Void Gifts.-A Void devise or bequest is one which was incapable of taking effect at the time the will was made. 

This might result from the nonexistence of the donee named, or because the attempted disposition was invalid for illegality. Thus, a gift to a person who had died before the will was made, or which violated the rule against perpetuities,¹ would be void. A void gift differs from a lapsed one, as the latter is good at the time of the execution of the will but is subsequently defeated in the lifetime of the testator, while a void gift is merely an attempt to make a gift which is invalid from the beginning. 

Unless the will indicates otherwise, lapsed and void bequests pass under the residuary clause if its terms are broad enough to cover them; otherwise they become intestate property. Under the common law a distinction was made between lapsed and void bequests and lapsed and void devises, the latter going to the heir because of the policy of the old common law to concentrate landed interests in a few hands; but statutes in several states now provide that lapsed and void devises shall be governed by the principles applying to lapsed and void bequests.

Powers. Thus, A owns land which, after the death of his sister, B, he wishes to leave to the nephew or niece best qualified to use it wisely; but, as these nephews and nieces are in another jurisdiction, and he has never seen them, he is not able to decide which of them is the best qualified.

ADEMPTION 

Definition. A will may also come to an end, in part or entirely, thru ademption. Ademption is the entire or partial failure of a gift thru some action of the testator not amounting to formal revocation, or because of the subsequent nonexistence of the subject matter as described.

Methods: Nonexistence.-If the bequest is a specific one, ademption will result from destruction of the subject matter, from a disposition of it by the testator, or from a change in its identity. Thus, if a testator has bequeathed "my gold watch," a subsequent destruction of the watch from any cause would prevent the bequest from taking effect, as there is nothing at his death answering the description of the subject matter. The same result would follow if the testator had transferred his watch in his lifetime, by sale, by gift, or otherwise.

CONTEST 

Any interested person, having reason to suppose that a will is invalid because of the lack of observance of any legal requirement, or because the testator lacked capacity or was subject to undue influence, or because of illegality, or for any other reason, has the privilege of contesting it; but a beneficiary who has received and retained property or benefits under a will with full knowledge of all material facts, is estopped from contesting it.

It has been said that only one will in six hundred is successfully contested, tho, no doubt, there have been many uncontested wills which could successfully have been contested but which were not, either thru ignorance of their invalidity or because interested persons did not wish the expense, trouble, and delay incident to litigation. 

CONSTRUCTION 

Definition. A very important branch of the law of wills is that relating to construction. Construction is ascertaining the meaning of the language of a will when affected by the context or by other language in the instrument, or by its relation to external facts, or where it is elliptical. It consists in harmonizing language drawn into question, or resolving doubt into certainty. Construction may relate both to the subjects and to the objects of the will; that is, to the property disposed of and to the persons designated to receive it. Generally, anyone beneficially interested under a will or interested in having any of its provisions declared inoperative, may bring suit for its construction where its meaning or effect is open to doubt.

INTESTATE SUCCESSION Testator's Intention Governs: Extrinsic Evidence.- 

In construing a will, the intention of the testator, if clear, will govern the courts altho he may not have used correct legal terms, unless some rule of law has been violated; but such intention must be found within the will itself, and extrinsic evidence is not admissible to show an intention not contained in the instrument.

While this rule, like most rules, works manifest injustice in certain instances, it would be very unsafe and would work more injustice to allow outside evidence of the testator's intention. Self-interest would hold forth a strong temptation to distort the facts or even to manufacture false testimony.

Thus, it cannot be shown that the testator did not understand the meaning of the terms used; or what the draughtsman's understanding was of the testator's intention, if the language used fairly indicates something else; or that the draughtsman made a clerical error; or that the testator afterwards announced what disposition he had made in his will, as he may have desired to conceal his actual disposition; or to show that the testator had an unlawful purpose.

From this it will be noticed that construction may not result in effectuating the intention of the testator as it existed in his mind and as he may have wished to express it, but construction will determine his intention as he actually did express it, that is, construction will not necessarily determine what he meant but will determine the meaning of the language he used. Thus, the testator's actual intention may be defeated because he used the wrong term, as "heirs" of a living person when he meant children, and the will does not indicate that he did not use the term in its correct sense.

As to some matters, however, it may speak from the time of its date or execution, or from a time after the testator's death. Thus, when the testator bequeaths certain specific personalty, it is evident that he has in mind articles existing at the time he writes, and not articles which he may acquire thereafter, altho coming within the description. 

Many years may elapse between the execution of the will and the death of the testator, during which many changes may occur in his property. Thus, if a testator bequeaths "my diamond ring," it refers to the ring owned by him at that time and not to a diamond ring afterwards acquired by him, altho he has disposed of the former ring; and a bequest of a leasehold interest does not apply to a renewal of such lease, as the latter is something entirely different from the former which he must have had in mind.

Gifts to a Class: Definition.-A Class is a number of persons, supposedly at least two, designated by some general name, and who usually are to be ascertained at a future time, but meanwhile being uncertain as to number, and who are ultimately to take in some definite proportion, the exact amount which each shall receive being dependent upon the number of persons when ascertained. 

Thus, a gift to "daughters who may be unmarried" after the termination of a life estate, is a gift to a class. Altho a class supposedly and usually consists of two or more persons, yet if there is but one person answering the description of the class when the time for ascertainment arrives, that one will take the entire gift.

Ascertainment of Class.-It is not always easy to determine the exact time when the members of a class are to be ascertained; and, if the distribution is postponed to a time after the death of the testator, just what persons constitute the class if there have been births and deaths since the execution of the will. There is, of course, no difficulty if the testator has clearly indicated when and what members of a class shall take, as the children "then living" at the death of his widow. If the direction be not clear, the general rule is that an immediate gift vests in those in existence at the testator's death, and in those only; and heirs of members of the class who have died before the testator do not have any interest, nor do those coming into existence after death of the testator. 

If the distribution is postponed to a time after the death of the testator, the general rule is that the class is to be ascertained at the time of distribution. If, however, the class consists of the heirs or next of kin of the testator, they are to be determined at the death of the testator; if the class consists of the children of the testator or of a designated person, the class will embrace those in existence at

Thus, if a devise or a bequest is made to A for life, and after his death to B's children, and B has children living at the testator's death, and if some are born after the testator's death and prior to A's death, and some after A's death, the children living at the testator's death and those born before A's death will take; those born after A's death will not.

Distribution per Capita.-Unless the will indicates otherwise, members of a class will generally take per capita, that is, individually and not per stirpes or by stocks as a body, as the law favors equality. Thus, if a gift be made to the children of A and the children of B, each child would receive the same share, altho A might have more or fewer children than B.

Cumulative Gifts.-Where a testator, in the same will or in the same codicil, makes a second gift of the same kind or amount, the gifts are not cumulative, and the donee will take the gift but once, as the second gift is presumed to be merely a repetition of the first. Thus, where one paragraph of a will bequeaths $1,000 to A, and a subsequent paragraph bequeaths $1,000 to A, he will take but $1,000.

The donee, however, will take both gifts, if the sums are unequal, or, tho equal, if they are not of the same kind, or if payable at different times, or, if one is absolute and the other contingent, or if a different motive is stated for each, or, in any case, where the testator indicates an intention to have both take effect. If equal amounts are given in different instruments, a codicil being treated as a different instrument in this respect, the gifts are cumulative; but in such a case if a bequest is made, in different instruments, of the same specific article, it is obviously impossible to give the same thing more than once.

Sociate Words.-One rule applied in the construction of contracts and documents is applied also in the construction of wills, which is that the meaning of a word is sometimes determined from the "company it keeps" (noscitur a sociis); that is, the sense in which it is used is to be determined from other words associated with it. 

Under this rule, a word may be held to have the same meaning as the word connected with it (ejusdem generis), or it may be considered as used by way of contrast and to have an opposite meaning; hence, the first point to be determined in such a case is whether the accompanying words are to be regarded as kindred or contrasting.

DRAFTING A WILL 

In General. The average lawyer cannot specialize in writing wills, as he is not called upon often enough for that purpose.

In many instances a lawyer, when consulted in regard to some legal matter, can postpone action by offering some excuse, valid or otherwise, for delay, and meantime he can inform himself as to the law on the point; but sometimes a lawyer is called upon to draw a will where minutes count and postponement is out of the question, hence, he should be familiar with the statutory requirements for a will in his jurisdiction, for without compliance with such statutory formalities the instrument would be a nullity. In most cases, however, there is no occasion for urgency; and if the testator disposes of realty in another state, the statutes of that state should be consulted, as they govern in regard to the formalities of the will so far as such realty is concerned. 

The general rule is that if a will conforms to the requirements of the state in which the testator has his domicile, it will be sufficient to pass personalty located in another state, regardless of the law of the state where the personalty is located. The statutes of the different states in regard to the requirements for making a will are not in conflict, but merely specify different formalities to be observed, so that it is an easy matter to execute a will satisfying the requirements of several states, by being careful to observe all the formalities of all those states. 

Statutes sometimes provide that a will which is valid in the state where it was executed, will be valid and recognized in the state having such a statute, altho not conforming to the general requirements of the law in such latter state.

Some persons know exactly what disposition they desire shall be made of their property at death; others have very limited ideas on the subject, or none at all, and require advice. In the latter case a lawyer, when consulted in regard to making a will, should thoroly familiarize himself with the affairs of his client, and, as far as practicable, with the characters of the members of the client's family, unless already acquainted with them. In any event the lawyer has the responsibility of selecting the language to be used in expressing the wishes of the testator, and should exercise the greatest care in choosing words to effectuate his intention, making sure that the testator understands the meaning of the words used.

Followers of every profession and trade are inclined to overlook the fact that technical terms which they use daily, and with whose meaning they are very familiar, may not convey the same meaning to others. In a deed or other contract between living persons (inter vivos) the parties, in many cases of ambiguity, can explain their intentions; or, in event of mistake, can have reformation in equity,¹ or, by a subsequent instrument or by litigation, may be able to correct some former unwise action. 

This, however, cannot occur in the case of an ambiguous or imprudent will. As a will does not take effect until the death of the testator, the language used therein does not come from the living. "But from a dead man in his grave, Who cannot change his mind." If the intention of the testator is expressed, the wording of a will may be most informal. Corporate and Individual Trustees. Whether, in event a trust is to be created, an individual or a corporation should be selected as trustee, must depend on circumstances. As in many situations in life there are advantages and disadvantages under each plan. The advantages of a corporate trustee are that a corporation does not die; it usually possesses financial responsibility, and its officers, from experience, possess the knowledge and ability required in such matters. The chief disadvantage of a corporate trustee is that it frequently is the owner of, or a dealer in, securities or other property, and there is a temptation to invest trust funds in securities and property for which the corporation has been unable to find a purchaser. Naturally, such investments are likely to be unsafe, sometimes becoming of little value, and, when the trust terminates, are not readily sold.

Number of Trustees.-If it is decided not to select a corporation as trustee, it is better, in most cases, to have but one individual as trustee. Where there are cotrustees, usually one of them ultimately takes the entire management, the other or others becoming mere figureheads; or, if all take an active interest in the trust, they may not feel the degree of responsibility they should, each being inclined to think the others will perform the

INTESTATE SUCCESSION 

necessary duties, and that the blame for any negligence can be thrown upon them. 

Where there is but one individual named as trustee, there should be a provision in regard to a successor in the event of a vacancy in the office. 

Alterations. It is inadvisable to have any alterations, such as erasures or insertions, in a will. If the document, as first drawn, does not correctly express the wishes of the testator, it is much better to rewrite it, if time allows.

Whenever alterations are made, they should be in the same handwriting as the rest of the will or in the handwriting of the testator himself, and the attention of witnesses, when signing, should be directed to them.

It is a good plan to refer to them in the attestation clause. 

Execution. It is advisable for the testator to sign or place his initials on the margin of each sheet, and also the word "Witnesses," followed by the signatures or initials of all the witnesses. This enables the witnesses to identify each sheet, and prevents substitution.

Attestation. Statutes usually require attestation by two witnesses, but good business requires at least three; and, tho it may not be required by statute, it is likewise advisable to add the address of each witness after his signature in order to locate him when needed, as the testimony of such witnesses is not required until after the testator no longer is available to give any information in regard to them.

While witnesses should know that the document they are attesting is a will, it is generally unnecessary that they should know its contents or any part thereof, except its date and the signature of the testator; but they should read the attestation clause before signing it.

1. Property Rights.-It is important that you understand that we are dealing with legal rights in this discussion rather than with physical objects. The physical

INTESTATE SUCCESSION 

objects to which the rights attach make such a strong impression upon the mind that it is difficult to avoid thinking of them rather than of the rights. In fact, it is customary in popular language and even in legal discussion to speak of the thing, the physical object, when, in reality, what we have in mind is not the thing itself, but rights relating to its use, possession, and control. Calling attention to the fact that we are dealing with rights and not with things is sufficient for our present purposes. If you keep this in mind, it may help you to understand some things that would not otherwise be clear.

The classification of property rights given in §6 sug gests the terms Real Property, Personal Property, and 

Choses in Action, tho choses in action are usually classed as personal property. These terms are mentioned here to assist you in keeping in mind the classification given in the text.

It would be well, in this connection, to turn back to Volume 4, pages 1 to 6, and read what is said there as to the nature of property rights. 

2. Survival of Causes of Action.-The text discusses at some length the survival of the benefit of claims. "Claims" is used here in the sense of causes of action. 

The use of the word "benefit" indicates that the discussion is limited to causes of action in favor of the deceased, but the same general principles apply where the claims are against him.

When it is said that a certain claim or cause of action survives, it is meant that the personal representatives, i.e., executors or administrators, may sue upon it and recover. When it does not survive, no action can be maintained upon it by anyone. It is a maxim of the common law that a personal right of action dies with the person- Actio personalis moritur cum persona. This was con-

WILLS 

71 sidered to apply primarily to tort actions or actions ex delicto, but to some extent it applies to contract actions and causes of action also, for many contracts are so personal in their nature that they cannot be carried out after the death of one of the parties. 

The language of the decisions constantly emphasizes the form of action. If you read the cases it is hard to avoid the conclusion that contract actions (actions ex contractu), and causes of action that may be sued upon in a contract form of action, survive, while tort actions (actions ex delicto), and causes of actions that must be sued upon in tort, do not survive. But the real distinction is not between contract and tort, it is rather between property and person. Causes of action affecting property rights usually survive; causes of action affecting the person, generally do not. The explanation of the language used by the courts is probably this: Nearly everything in law was expressed in terms of procedure; causes of action sounding in tort, to use a familiar legal expression, usually affected the person or personal rights, rather than property or property rights, while contract actions were more likely to affect property. (a) Aside from statute, the general rule still is that claims arising out of torts do not survive and cannot be sued upon by the administrator. This is clearly the rule where the injury is to the person, as in the case of assault and battery, false imprisonment, injury by negligence, seduction, libel, and slander. When the injury is to property and not to the person, except where the estate of the wrongdoer has been enriched, the cause of action does not survive. But when the estate of the wrongdoer has been enriched, the cause of action survives. It is said that wherever, under the principles presented under the head of quasi-contracts, it is possible to waive the tort and sue in a contract form of action, the cause of action will survive. This does not mean that the suit

INTESTATE SUCCESSION 

must be in any particular form. It means rather that if there is unjust enrichment the cause of action will survive, for as the older lawyers expressed it, there is in substance a contract implied in law, the case is founded upon a contract right, in reality a property right. Claims for damages to land as shown in §9, tho affecting property, do not survive. The wrongdoer is not enriched. (b) Aside from statute, claims based upon contracts survive, and after the death of the owner may be inforced, or the contract performed, by his administrator or executor. This is the general rule. As in the case of torts, there are exceptions. Many of these have been indicated in the text. A contract for the personal services of the deceased is discharged by the impossibility of performance. There is no question of survival. The same, of course, is true where defendant has contracted to render services that require the presence of the deceased. A few causes of action which do not survive are: 

1. For breach of promise to marry. 

2. For breach of contract to carry safely, as where deceased was injured by the negligence of a railroad company while a passenger. 

3. For failure of a telegraph company to deliver a message within a reasonable time and the claim is for mental anguish resulting from the delay. 

4. For negligence or malpractice on the part of a physician or surgeon.

It will be observed that the damage or injury is in each case purely personal to the deceased and that, with the exception of the first, they may be looked upon as being based upon tort as easily as upon contract.

Curtesy has been abolished in about three-fourths of the states, dower in more than half. Dower.-Dower is defined in §23. At common law it is the widow's right to use one-third of her husband's lands for life if she survives him. To entitle her to dower, three facts must appear: (a) A lawful marriage, (b) the husband must have been seized-that is, possessed of-an

77 estate of inheritance in land, and (c) the death of the husband before the wife. Note that dower attaches to land, not to money or personal property, and that it attaches only to an estate of inheritance in land. An estate of inheritance is one that may be inherited by the children of the owner. The widow does not have dower in land in which the husband has a life estate only, nor where his interest is a leasehold even when the lease is for ninety-nine years, for these are not estates of inheritance. 

The husband cannot cut off his wife's dower right in his lands either by deed or by will. He may, however, make a provision in the will in lieu of dower and force the widow to elect whether she will take under the will or insist on having dower assigned. If he conveys the land by deed or mortgage and the wife does not join in the deed, the grantee will take the land subject to the wife's dower right. The usual method of barring the wife's dower right is to have her join in the execution of the deed. Dower is cut off by an absolute divorce. 

There are three stages of dower that should be noted: 

1. Inchoate Dower.-Inchoate dower is the wife's dower right in her husband's lands while he is yet alive. 

It is called inchoate because it is incomplete. It is not an estate or interest in land, but a mere possibility that she may have an estate in the future. It is, however, an incumbrance on the land, so that in case of conveyance to a third party it must be released to pass clear title. 

2. Dower Consummate.-Upon the death of the husband before the wife, her dower right becomes complete. 

It is then said to be consummate. It is the right of the widow to have one-third of her husband's lands assigned to her as dower.

3. Assigned Dower.-As soon as the dower is assigned, the widow has an estate in the land. She has a life estate, and may sue for possession and maintain actions for trespass or other infringements of her rights in the land just as any other owner of a life estate may. The only limitation on her rights with respect to the dower lands is that she must not commit waste. For instance, she has no right to destroy the dwelling house or to cut all the timber off the land. She does have the right to cut timber for firewood, fences, and repairs on the premises. But she would have no right to cut timber off the dower lands, to be used in building fences or repairing houses on other lands owned by her.

Since the widow has only a life estate, it terminates at her death, and there is nothing that can be inherited from her by her heirs. The lands set apart as dower, upon the death of the widow, revert to the heirs of her husband.

GIFTS "MORTIS CAUSA" 

For the purposes of this lesson there are two kinds of gifts: gifts inter vivos and gifts mortis causa. Most gifts are inter vivos. They are irrevocable. They may be made at any time without reference to the condition of the giver's health or his fear of approaching death. Such gifts bear no resemblance to testamentary dispositions and are mentioned here merely for comparison with gifts mortis causa. As gifts mortis causa are revocable during the life of the giver and under some circumstances may be reclaimed by the administrator, it is important that we know the distinguishing marks. 

1. A gift mortis causa can be made only by one who is in fear of approaching death. A gift made at any other time is a gift inter vivos and is irrevocable unless

INTESTATE SUCCESSION 

made expressly upon condition. A person who fears death can make a gift inter vivos, but the presumption is that a gift made by a person who apprehends death, either from a disease from which he is suffering, or from some peril of which he knows, is a gift mortis causa. 

Evidence may be presented, however, to show that an absolute gift was intended, and if this can be established the gift will be held to be inter vivos and irrevocable. 

2. The gift must be conditional. If it is not conditional, then it is an ordinary gift inter vivos as shown above. The law implies the condition from the circumstance that the gift is made in apprehension of death. 

The presumption may, however, be rebutted by competent evidence. There is no objection to the donor's stating the condition expressly. The condition is that the gift shall become absolute in case the donor dies as a result of the peril or before recovery from the disease which causes his fear of death, but that if he does not die, he may reclaim the thing given.

It is a peculiar characteristic of a gift mortis causa that like a will, it may be revoked any time before the death of the donor. And that if the donor recovers from his disease the property automatically reverts to him. 

The rights of creditors cannot be impaired by means of a gift mortis causa. If the donor does not leave enough other property to pay his debts, his administrator may reclaim the gift or enough of it to satisfy the claims of creditors. The law says that a man “must be just before generous."

PRETERMITTED CHILDREN 

Many careful draftsmen make it a point to leave a small sum of money, as, for instance, one dollar to each one of the testator's children or other near relatives to whom no substantial gift is made, merely for the purpose of showing clearly that no one has been unintentionally omitted. Probably, as a result of this extra precaution to remove all cause of contest, there has grown up a popular belief that if the testator wills nothing to one or more of his children, or, if he has no children, to those who would be the natural recipients of his bounty, the will is invalid. While this probably had no foundation in law when it first made its appearance, there is perhaps some statutory ground for it at the present time. California, for instance, has a clause in her statutes providing that when any testator fails to provide for any of his children or the issue of any deceased child, unless it appears that the failure was intentional, such child or issue shall have the same share in the estate of the testator as if he had died intestate.

A number of states, among them, Arkansas, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Montana, Nebraska, and

New Mexico, have similar statutes. Children who are omitted from their parents' wills are often spoken of as "pretermitted children," and the rule established by statute in many states on this point is stated thus, "afterborn children, pretermitted children, and the issue of pretermitted children take as if there had been no will." 

It is not necessary under these statutes to make any gift to those whom the testator wishes to disinherit. He may mention them for the purpose of disinheriting them expressly. Usually, if it is made clear in the will that there is no oversight and that the testator intended to leave nothing to certain of his children, the law will give effect to the will.

CONTRACTS TO MAKE WILLS 

Essentials. There is no public policy which prohibits contracts to make wills. If a man owns property, he may dispose of it during his lifetime by gift or by sale, and so long as he does nothing to defraud creditors, he is the judge as to what consideration he should receive for it. Agreeing for a consideration to dispose of property by will in a particular way may be looked upon as a species of sale, or as a contract to sell. It is even more favorable to the seller than a sale, for it leaves him the full enjoyment of the property during his life, and at the same time gives him the enjoyment of the consideration or price which he receives.

Contracts to make wills or to provide for another in a will are governed by the same rules of law as apply to other contracts. The essential elements of such a contract are the same. Briefly they are: competent parties, mutual agreement, lawful subject matter, consideration, and legal form. By competent parties we mean parties competent to make a contract. Agreement is usually arrived at by offer and acceptance. The evidence of agreement in this class of cases must be very clear. One party to the agreement is always absent when the case comes up, and to prevent fraud the courts must insist upon having very convincing proof. For this reason a written memorandum of such agreements should always be obtained. 

As already indicated, the promise to make a will in one's favor is lawful subject matter on one side. The consideration for which this promise is given must also be lawful. An illegal or an immoral consideration will not support the contract. Thus, a contract to leave property to another in consideration of his refraining from prosecuting the testator for a crime, or a contract by a man to will property to a woman in consideration for her living with him as his mistress, would not be enforcible, because the consideration or subject matter is not lawful.

Legal form has reference to such matters as seals and the statute of frauds. Any formal requirements that the law of the particular jurisdiction may make must be complied with. 

2. The Statute of Frauds.-Contracts to make wills are said not to be within the statute of frauds, unless they are to devise real property. They are not within the clause requiring contracts, not to be performed within a year, to be in writing because they may be performed within one year. A will takes effect at the death of the testator and the time of death is uncertain; it may occur within one year. A contract to devise real property, so far as the statute of frauds is concerned, seems to be equivalent to a contract to convey and, to be enforcible, must be in writing and signed according to the provisions of Section 4, Statute of Frauds. 

Contracts to bequeath personal property would seem to be equivalent to contracts to sell personal property, and if the amount involved is sufficiently large, Section 

17 of the statute of frauds should apply. There are not many cases discussing this point, probably because actions on such contracts are seldom brought until after the death of the person who was to make the will, and by that time, usually, there has been part payment, which is sufficient to satisfy the statute. Wholly executory contracts to will personal property, made orally, are not enforcible unless the amount involved is small, because of the statute of frauds.

Contracts to make wills take a variety of forms. Perhaps the most common form is the contract to make a will in return for services rendered or to be rendered. In fact there are no limitations or requirements other than those imposed by the principles of the law of contracts that the consideration must be a legal detriment to the promisee.

X. Y. Z. Railroad Company, and cash and securities equivalent to $50,000.

At the present time he is not indebted to anyone. 

His family consists of a wife, two sons, and a daughter. 

To his wife, Sarah L. Osborne, he wishes to leave a life estate in Kensington Farm, the use of the household goods for life, and $5,000 in cash, in lieu of dower. To his son, James M., who is of age, he wishes to leave nothing. 

The watch and the library are to go to the second son, Thomas R., who is a minor. Whatever is left of the household goods at the death of the wife is to go to the daughter, Myra. He wishes to leave $3,000 to Samuel G. Osborne, his favorite nephew, and $2,000 to Thomas L. Johnson, a lifelong friend. Kensington Farm at the death of his wife is to go to Thomas.

117 Mr. Osborne has not fully decided how to dispose of his other property, but wishes it arranged so that in case of his death, Myra and Thomas will be properly educated and supported from the income of the estate until they reach the age of twenty-five, and that at that age each shall have one-half of the principal. 

His wife, Sarah L. Osborne, is to be executrix, and he wishes it so arranged that she shall not be required to give bond. In case of her death or inability to serve, Thomas L. Johnson is to be executor. 

Prepare the will and have it properly executed. 

35. Four years after the execution of the will of William H. Osborne he comes to you, stating that he wishes to change his will. Since the will was drawn, Thomas L. 

Johnson has died, and he wishes the $2,000 that he was to receive to go to his son, James M., with whom he has since become reconciled. He also wishes James M. to share equally with the other two children in the residue of the estate. Prepare the necessary papers to accomplish these results and have them properly executed. 

36. Ten years after William H. Osborne executed his first will, his wife died, and a short time afterward he lost the bulk of his estate. In the meantime, James M. and Myra have become self-supporting, but Thomas R. has developed into a spendthrift. Osborne's estate now amounts to about $5,000. He wishes to make a new will, leaving all his property to Thomas, but so arranging it that Thomas cannot spend in any one year more than the income for that year. Frederick R. Williams is to be executor of the new will. 

Prepare a will for Osborne that will accomplish this purpose as nearly as possible.
