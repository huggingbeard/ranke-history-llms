Preface 

Guidance is a part, and an important part, of every phase of the child's education; it is not a separate something that can be readily and completely assigned to one teacher or period. 

Each teacher has definite responsibilities in it through all of his contacts with his pupils. 

And increasingly, teachers are recognizing and accepting these responsibilities. 

In any school there are many significant educational interests, activities, phases, and emphases that lie outside of the regular curriculum. Probably some of these will, in time, be included in it; perhaps some of them never will be. Further, there are, in any pupil's life, legitimate, deserving, and profitable interests and activities that are not now provided for. 

Here again, probably some of these will ultimately find their way into the school. With these, as with curricular and extracurricular material, there must come a provision for adequate and competent guidance.

Guidance is now provided in several different ways: through regularly scheduled classes; through home room, club, group, and other organized settings; through supervised study halls and library work; through individual teacher-pupil contacts; and in other ways, as well as through various combinations of these. 

The material of The Junior Citizen Series is designed to be immediately helpful in all of these settings. Practically all of it is equally applicable to both the upper grades and the junior high school.

Group-guidance work is coming to be recognized more and more as the ideal center for co-ordinating all the environmental influences which surround individual children. School classes and activities, street and playground contacts, home influences, neighborhood and civic enterprises, all serve as stimuli to which the pupil must make some sort of adjustment. 

Guidance teachers and sponsors are in a peculiarly fortunate position to take advantage of the situation. The basic problem is to make whatever time has been set aside as profitable as possible.

A wealth of literature has appeared in recent years containing all manner of suggestions as to the best use of the home-room period. The general tenor of these suggestions seems to be along the following lines: Only a minimum amount of time should be spent on checking absences, making announcements, and other administrative details. The group may spend the time in free discussion, in written lessons, or in a combination of both. In each of these procedures prepared material is desirable. It helps to direct the work into useful, effective channels. However, the use of prepared material should not be allowed to become formalized. The work should be interesting in itself, not dull or laborious. Emphasis should not be placed on troubles or misdemeanors or on a rule-of-thumb sort of virtue that does not appeal to boys and girls, but rather on positive suggestions for building a plan for the good life. 

The Junior Citizen books are designed to supply material for what may constitute the core of a character education program. The aim of such a program is to help to induct the pupils into accepted folkways and mores; and, when there are discrepancies between the accepted standards of different groups, to help the pupils to choose in such a way that they will develop into integrated and well-adjusted personalities.

That the books may serve their purpose the more advantageously, the following suggestions are made: 

1. It is the pupil's idea or viewpoint with which the work should start and upon which the lesson or discussion should be built. Hence pupil offerings, though sometimes meager and faulty, should be accepted and encouraged. 

2. Plan ahead and know what is coming, so that questions will be anticipated and preparation made for materials (library books, pictures, and so on), interviews with teachers or administrative officers, trips, or committee organization which may be called for in the lessons. 

3. Before the pupils begin to write answers to the questions in any lesson, be sure that each member of the class has a good idea of the purpose of the lesson. Add such illustrations and explanations as may seem helpful.

4. Consider the written responses of the pupils as a basis for possible individual conversations (which they will in many cases seek) with a view to helping them in their personal adjustments. 

5. Above all, do not allow the work to become mechanical. The material will go a long way toward teaching itself; but if the techniques of skillful teaching and the magic of personal interest and enthusiasm are present, the periods during which these materials are used can be made stimulating, helpful, and vitalizing experiences. 

The books may, in general, be used at whatever grade level they seem most appropriate. 

Getting Acquainted with Your School is especially designed for groups new to the school, and Looking Toward a Vocation for pupils who are expected to be making plans for the selection of their courses for senior high school. 

Meeting Difficulties should be used last, as it is definitely more advanced.

To the Pupil 

A vocation is an occupation, a job at which you work. 

You are not ready to go out and get a job just yet, of course, or even to begin the regular courses that train you for one; but there are many things that you need to know about different kinds of work even this far ahead of time. The lessons in this book try to give you information that will be of value to you both now and later. 

You need to know what different kinds of work there are so that you can choose wisely. You need to discover what sort of things you are interested in and fitted to do. How can you find out about different jobs? What courses should you take to fit yourself for a particular kind of work? How valuable is schooling in helping a person to get a job? How does one apply for a job?

All these matters are considered here. It is hoped that, through the written work and discussions, you will discover the answers to many of your vocational questions. 1 1 10

Lesson 2. When You Were Younger 

If you were to ask your doctor whether he planned, as a small boy, to be · a doctor, he would probably laugh and say that as a child he had had no such idea, but perhaps wanted to be a policeman or a circus performer, or something else very different. And if you were to ask your groceryman whether he had planned to be a groceryman, he might tell you that as a child he wanted to be a streetcar motorman, or a doctor, or perhaps a cowboy.

The ideas that boys and girls have about the work they would like to do usually change as they grow older. They seldom follow the occupation that they had planned to follow when they were. small children. Probably you, too, when you were younger, had many ideas about the work you wanted to do when you grew up. Perhaps they were very different from what you now think that you want to do, and they may even seem funny to you. Or perhaps what you planned to do when you were a child may still be interesting to you.

Lesson 3. Classes of Occupations 

Every ten years a census is taken in the United States in which information is obtained about the people living in this country. Among other things, a record is made of the type of work that each person does. This is called his occupation or vocation. There are, of course, hundreds of different occupations. In the United 

States Census, however, these are divided into ten classes or groups. These groups of occupations are listed below. An explanation follows each one, and also a list of workers typical of that group. Read and discuss this information with your classmates.

Agriculture: 

Raising crops on farms and plantations, or in truck gardens and orchards; raising animals useful in producing food and clothing. Farmers, truck gardeners, ranchers.

Forestry and Fishing: 

Providing raw materials, such as fish for food, and lumber for the making of houses, furniture, and paper. Foresters, lumbermen, fishermen. Mining: 1 Obtaining raw materials from the earth, such as coal, iron, copper, stone, and oil. Miners, quarrymen, oil operators.

Manufacturing and Mechanical Operations: 

Transforming any raw materials into finished products: cotton into cloth, metal into machines, wood into paper. Weavers, dyers, machinists.

Transportation and Communication: 

Carrying raw materials to the manufacturing plant, or carrying manufactured products to places where they can be used; transporting people and products from one place to another by trains, trucks, vans, busses, steamships, and airplanes; operating means of communication, such as telephone, telegraph, radio. Locomotive engineers, truck drivers, radio operators. 

Trade: 

Exchanging raw materials or products for others or for money. Buying and selling. Shopkeepers, salesmen, merchants.

Public Service : 

Working in an official capacity for the state or national government whether elected or appointed. Senators, policemen, judges. 

Professional Service: 

Working in fields requiring special education and training. Doctors, lawyers, teachers, engineers.

Domestic and Personal Service: 

Caring for the personal needs of people. Barbers, cooks, waitresses.

Clerical Occupations: 

Keeping of accounts; work that has to do with correspondence, or keeping records. Clerks, secretaries, stenographers. 15

Lesson 5. Useful Vocations 

In choosing a vocation, it is a good idea to think about the various sides of the question. For example, you might want to consider: (1) how valuable a certain type of work is to the people of the community; (2) how much you are interested in the kind of work you would be doing; (3) whether you have the kind of ability or training needed, and (4) what openings there are, if any, in that kind of work. 

These points will be considered in the lessons in this book, as well as some of the ways to apply for a particular job. 

People engage in many different kinds of work to make a living. It is easy to make the mistake of thinking that some kinds of work are more useful and therefore more important than others.

Lesson 10. Suggested Questions for an Interview 

When you are planning to interview someone who might be able to give you information about the work you hope to do after you have finished school, there are several points that must be considered. 

First, you must keep in mind that this person, whether he is a personal friend or merely an acquaintance, is doing you a favor by giving you his time and the information for which you ask. Therefore, be sure that you let him set the time and place for your interview. Ask him by letter or telephone when it would be convenient for him to talk to you. 

Second, try to take as little of his time as you can. Because you want to get as much information as possible in this short time, it would be well to plan the questions you wish to ask before you go. The list below includes some of the most important facts that you will want to learn. Others may, of course, be added to this list. 

1. How much education is needed for this work? 

2. How much special training is needed? 

3. Is there an opportunity to get more education and training while on the job? 

4. What kind of things do you do on the job? 

5. What kind of job do you start on in order to work up to the job you wish? 

6. What chances are there for advancement? 

7. How many hours a day do you work? 

8. Is it a job that lasts all year, or are there periods during which work is slack? 

9. Are conditions generally healthful in this work? 

10. What does the job pay? 

You may add other questions to this list. Write any that you wish to ask. 33

Lesson 19. A Letter of Application 

A letter of application is for the purpose of obtaining an interview with an employer and should, therefore, be so worded as to make him interested in seeing you. Your letter advertises you. You are trying to interest someone in your services. For this reason you will want to write your letter with great care. The letter stands for you.

Below are listed some of the most important points to remember when you are writing a letter of application. 

1. Before writing your letter, make a list of the things that you feel it is important to have an employer know about you. When this is completed, write your letter with this list before you. It should include facts about such matters as the following: a. The position or the kind of work you are interested in. b. Your age. c. Your qualifications for work of this type, such as the amount of education and special training you have had. d. Any previous experience you have had in work of this type, naming the firm or place of employment. e. The names and addresses of persons to whom the new employer may refer in case he should be interested in finding out more about you. 

These are called "references." 

2. Writing a letter about yourself so that it gives all the necessary information in such a way as to arouse the employer's interest in you is not an easy task. 

Some other things to watch are the following: a. Write your letter in a businesslike manner, briefly, and to the point. b. Never use slang. c. In telling the experiences that you have had, write simply and directly. d. Do not brag about what you have done or are able to do. 

3. Some of the items listed in this part may appear foolish to you. They may seem to have nothing to do with your ability to hold a job. However, many a person has missed getting the job he wanted because he was careless about these things. a. Always write your letter on plain white paper. b. Write on only one side of the paper. c. Either use pen and ink or typewrite your letter. d. Never send a letter with a blot, or with a word crossed out or erased. e. Spell all words correctly. f. See that your sentences are correct grammatically. g. Punctuate your letter correctly. 55

Lesson 21. Applying for a Job-Appearance 

Ruth was applying for a position which had been advertised in the paper. 

She had answered many questions satisfactorily. Her school references being good, and her appearance generally pleasing, she was accepted. Ruth was delighted. Her employer asked her to write her name and address on a card. Ruth slipped off her glove, took the pencil that was handed to her, and wrote her name carefully-but because her hands and fingernails were dirty, she lost the job.

This is a true story, though it may seem an extreme example because the results of carelessness do not often come so suddenly. However, people are rejected because of such seemingly small matters. 

Whether or not you get the position you are applying for depends to a large extent upon your appearance and your manners. No matter how well you can do the work and no matter how good your references are, if your appearance or manners are not pleasing, an employer is quite likely to feel that you are not the person for the job. Sometimes it is only a little thing that a person is careless about, but it may cost him a job.
