PREFACE 

In the present economic organization, with large corporate structures tending to overshadow smaller individual businesses, it is inevitable that legislative efforts be made to balance the benefits of the large company against the initiative of the mall, The public interest in fair and equitable competition in trade, the interest of the consumer who is trained through clever advertising to buy merchandise by brand, the historical tendency toward trustbusting, all merit consideration in a work on Trade Regulation. 

At intervals, the Federal Trade Commission, as vatchdog of this public interest, is in the headlines in its continuous efforts to curb business practices which are violative of statutés banning monopolies, restraint of trade, and illegal price maintenance. No longer can big business resort to its abuses of the nineteenth century to choke the national economy; but an unprecedented chance for initiative in all business is offered to American private enterprise. 

The various statutes have been correlated so that the student will be able to follow the developing trend of the regulation of trusts and monopolies.

TRADE REGULATIONS SCOPE AND HISTORICAL BACKGROUND CHAPTER I 1. ECONOMIC BASIS OF MODERN TRADE REGULATION. 

The need for trade regulation and restrictions on unfair competition presupposes the existence of free competition. The body of common law in this field could not therefore arise until the period which saw the end of the guild systom, monopolistic grants and restrictions on the area of mercantilism. "Unfair competitionn" and its attendant problems arises only in a laissez-faire ecosy where monopolies are regarded as a derogation from the right of any member of the community to exercise any trade or business he pleases in such manner as he thinks best in his own interests.

American history followed a pattern somewhat similar to the English. The colonial crafts never attained the importance or thorough organization of the English guilds, but a similar outlook on the question of regulation of trade was to some degree prevalent. The English Crown Monopolies had their counterparts in grants of exclusive privileges in the colonies, and further restrictions on colonial manufacture took the form of restrictive legislation enforced by England to protect her own trade interests. 

2. MONOPOLY 

Originally, the word monopoly meant a license granted by the King of England giving the exclusive right to buy, sell, manufacture or use any article. 

The practice of granting monopolies solely to encourage new invention and manufacture was perverted by the English kings who commenced to use this power to reward court favorites by granting monopolies in fields other than new invention. 

These abuses culminated in the famous case of Darcy v. Allen, 77 Eng. Rep. 

1260 (K.B. 1602) where the court held void as against public policy a grant by the Crown of the exclusive right to make playing cards within the realm. The court there said: "There are three inseparable incidents to every monopoly: 

1. That the price will be raised. 2. After the monopoly grant, the commodity is not so good as it was before. 3. It tends to the impoverishment of divers artificers and other's who before by their labour had maintained themselves and their families, who now will of necessity be constrained to live in idleness and beggary. 11 There followed the Statute of Monopolies, of 1623, voiding the prior or subsequent grant of monopolies, but containing an exception allowing the grant of 14-year patents for new inventions. This forms the basis of our modern law of patents.

In America, the term monopoly has been generally associated with schemes for the control of the market by concerted action. It is this type of monopoly which is subject to federal regulation under the Sherman and Clayton Acts.

As the court said in Granada Lumber Co. v. Mississippi, 217 U.S. 433, 440, "An act harmless when done by one may become a public wrong when done by many acting in concert." This principle is the basis of laws regulating monopolistic combines, exclusive dealing arrangements, and trade boycotts. ST 

5. UNFAIR COMPETITION 

Under this general heading are included the numerous ways in which one person may pass off his goods as those of another or otherwise appropriate the customers and general good will of his commercial rivals. It includes the infringement of technical trade-marks, the imitation of trade names or the appearance and packaging of an article, the appropriation of the intangible assets of a business such as trade secrets, inducing breach of contract between the rival and his employees, suppliers or customers, commercial bribery, disparagement, misleading advertising, physical molestation, and, in general, any interferenco with the good will of a business. Resale price maintenance and price discrim ination might also be included in this broad category. 

However, apart from the foregoing, the right to engage in a business of one's own clioosing, the right to compete by fair means, whatever the motive, the right to choose one's own customers, to control prices in businesses not "affected with the public interest" and, in general, the right of freedom of contract are upheld by the common law. See, for example, Katz v. Kapper, 7 Cal.

App. (2d) 1 (1935) where the right to drive a competitior out of business by lawful means was upheld, even though a malicious motive was admitted. 

While legislation restricting the manner of doing business exists on all levels from the municipality to the federal government, legislation attempting to prevent newcomers from establishing themselves in business of their own choosing is regarded as unconstitutional under the fourteenth amendment. For example, an Oklahoma statute which limited person engaging in the ice business to those who could obtain certificates of convenience and necessity from a license bureau which had the authority to refuse a certificate on the ground that the facilities already licensed were sufficient to supply the community, was declared unconstitutional at the instance of the plaintiff who had been denied a license on this ground. New State Ice. Co. v. Liebman, 285 U.S. 262. 6. 

LEGISLATION AND ADMINISTRATIVE REGULATION. 

At common law, the courts could, at the instance of an individual, redress the wrongs done him by another, within the limitations noted in Section 5. The courts could not, however, redress wrongs done to the public at large, Such practices as misleading advertising where no specific competitor was injured tat the public was deceived, exclusive dealing arrangements and tying contracts, railroad discrimination and many similar practices lay beyond the power of the courts under common law doctrines. CHAPTER II 

TRADE MARKS AND TRADE NAMES 7. GOOD WILL--THE FUNCTION OF THE TRADE MARK. 

The trade-mark and the trade name are often the most effective device for the development and maintenance of "good will" in the business of the manufacturer or distributor of a product since they enable the purchaser to distinguish between the products of various manufacturers and distributors and to call for the products by name. They enable the manufacturer or distributor to advertise his products and thus to utilize the market established by trade-mark reputation in the launching of a new product. Further, the trade-mark serves as a guarantee to the purchaser that goods bearing the same trade-mark will be of the same character or quality.

In protecting the owner of a trade-mark against infringement or the owner of a trade name against unfair competition, the courts are, in effect, protecting the property right of the owner in the good will of his business, his reputation and advertising investment, by preventing a competitor from passing off his goods as those of the owner of the name or mark. 

8. TECHNICAL TRADE MARK AND TRADE NAME DISTINGUISHED 

"The term 'trade-mark' includes any word, name, symbol or device or any combination thereof adopted and used by a manufacturer or merchant to identify his goods and distinguish them from those manufactured or sold by another. 

Lanham Act (Public Law 489, effective July 5, 1947) Section 45. 

11 A trade name includes terms which serve the same purpose but which do not meet the requirements of the technical trade-mark (Section 9) and so cannot be exclusively appropriated.. For the most part, cases on technical trade-mark infringement and those on unfair competition in the use of trade names follow the same pattern, for the essence of the wrong in both groups of cases is the same: 

The passing-off of the goods of one manufacturer or distributor as those of another, or in any other way, the impairment of the reputation and good will of the owner of the mark or name.

THE ACQUISITION OF A PROPERTY RIGHT--USER. 

The property right in a trade mark, a right appurtenant to the business in which it is used, rests upon common law and not upon statute. Trade Mark Caser, 

Regin- 

A trade mark is not a government grant like a patent or copyright. tration of a trade mark, which provided for by statute, is simply a recognition by the government of the owner's right to use the mark in commerce, and serves as notice to subsequent users. (See sections 15 and 16).

Rights in a mark are acquired not by invention or adoption of the mark but only by actual use on, or in connection with the sale of, a product. United 

Drug Co. v. Rectanus Co., 248 U.S. 90. 

The first user (not the first registrant) acquires rights in the mark, as long as the use is not merely spasmodic and inconsiderable. Worden Cannaliato, 285 F. 988.

See sections 16 and 18 on effect of registration under Lanham Act and on territorial limitations of trade-mark right. 

11. AFFIXATION. 

Under common law and under the Act of 1905 no rights could be acquired in the trade-mark unless it was affixed to the goods or wrapper. Use of the mark merely in advertising displays, newpaper articles or pamphlets accompanying the product was regarded as insufficient to establish a trade-mark right. Gray v. 

Armand Co., 24 F. (2a) 878.

Under the Lanham Act, (Sec. 45) use on pamphlets or advertising displays associated with the goods is sufficient, but mere use in newspaper or billboard advertising is not. However rights in service marks (defined in Section 23) may be acquired also in this way. 12. 

SECONDARY MEANING--FIRM NAMES AND PERSONAL NAMES 

Secondary meaning is a matter of association in the public mind. Thus "camel" is an animal, but in a secondary sense it has become associated in the public mind with cigarettes.

Where a personal or family name is used as a trade name it may develop secondary meaning in the public mind. Thus "Ford" means an automobile to the public today. Once a personal name trade name has developed this secondary meaning, it can be protected against unfair competition just as a trade-mark in protected against infringement, and under the Lanham Act it can be registered as a technical trade-mark if the use has enjoyed substantially exclusive use of the mark for five years prior to registration. Under the Act of 1905, personal names could be registered as trademarks only if displayed in a particular format, or, under the 10-year clause, if they had been used exclusively by the registrant from 1895 to 1905 (Sec. 5.) Even after a personal-nare trade-name has acquired secondary meaning, the user cannot prevent someone of that name from using the name in his business (unless he does so fraudulently), but he can restrict the latter's use to avoid confusion and misleading of the public and the consequent misappropriation of his good will.

13. SECONDARY MEANING--GEOGRAPHIC AND DESCRIPTIVE TERMS.

14. PACKAGING AND GENERAL APPEARANCE OF GOODS. 

The functional elements of an article are those which are necessary to permit successful practical operation of the article or to reduce the cost of manufacture. Such elements may be copied by a competitor. 

Non-functional elements may also be copied as long as they have not become associated in the public mind with the product of the first user so that, like a trade-mark, they are an indication of source. In order to be protected, the dress or packaging of the goods must be so distinctive as to have taken over some of the functions of the trade-mark of the first user. Crescent Tool Co. v. Kilborn & Bishop Co., 247 F. 299.

The shape of a bottle (if it is unique and non-functional) may be protected.

16. EFFECT OF REGISTRATION. 

Registration, under a Federal Act gives the Federal courts jurisdiction of suits for infringement, even in the absence of diversity of citizenship. Following the decision in Erle v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64, in which the Supreme 

Court held that there is no federal common law except in the realm of federal question, great confusion salsted on the qeustion whether in the case of registered trade-marks and attenant unfair competition litigants were bound by the law of the state in which the action was brough, even though the case was heard in a Federal court. The Lanham Act (Sec. 21) apparently resolves this question by making federal decisions and rules applicable to all registrants under that Act. Registration thus affords the very valuable benefit of uniformity in the application of the law. 

Under the Act of 1905, (Sec. 16) registration was not regarded as notice of any kind, At best, if afforded prima facie evidence of the ownership of the mark, but this was rebuttable by a showing of innocent use by another, even if the use commenced after the registration.

Registration under the Lanham Act (Sec. 22) is constructive notice to all users, both interstate and intrastate, and has an effect similar to that of recording a deed of property. A prior user of the mark can come in within five years to request cancellation of the mark upon showing that he was the prior user or that the registration was fraudulent, or to request concurrent registration.

After a mark has remained on the Register for five years and has been in continouse use for five consecutive years since the date of registration, it becomes "incontestable" except upon a showing of fraud in registration, fraudulent use or abondonment. A prior user of the mark (before its registration) can no longer have the registration cancelled by he may be permitted concurrent registration for a limited territory.

Page Thus under the Lanham Act, (Sec. 15) business expansion can be protected, since although the owner of a mark may use it in only two states he can gain an "incontestable" right to its use in all states by virtue of registration, assuming, of course, that there is no prior user. 

17. METHODS OF INFRINGMENT. 

Infringement occurs when someone other than the owner of a mark affixes the mark (or a confusingly similar mark) to his goods, or uses it in advertising or sells the goods of one manufacturer as those of another. Trade names, nonfunctional, distinctive dress or packaging will also be protected against misappropriation.

Infringement of trade-marks and unfair competition in the use of trade names can occur in a variety of ways: (1) By a competitor who affixes the mark or name as a trade-mark on his own goods, or uses a confusingly similar name or mark (See Section 18 on what is confusing similarity). If a descriptive word has acquired secondary meaning as a trade name, the owner can prevent competitors from using the word as a trade-mark, (See the Dyanshine case in Section 13), but he cannot prevent their using the word descriptively. Thus the owner of the trade-mark "Mufflet" for mufflers cannot prevent a competitor from calling his mufflers "Mufflers" though the names are confusingly similar. Hygienic Fleeced Underwear Co. v. 

Way, 137 F. 592. (2) By use of the mark in advertising competing goods. Earlier cases, e.g. N. Y. Mackintosh Co. v. Flam, 198 F. 571, held that there was no trade mark infringement (though there might be unfair competition in a competitor's use of the owner's mark as long as he did not affix it to his goods but used it solely on advertising and stationery.) At present, however, affixation by a comp etitor is not necessary to afford a right to the owner to enjoin the use of the mark. In Jantzen Kintting Mills v. Spokane Knitting Mills, 44 F. (2d) 656, the defendant had used the "diving girl" trade mark only on direct mail advertising and counter displays. The use was enjoined as an infringement. The Lanham Act expressly provides that such use of the mark is infringement. (3) By using the mark on the owner's own goods or on goods from the same source without, the owner's permission. For example, where the trade mark is that of a distributor, another distributor may not use the mark on goods purchased from the same manufacturer. A. Bourjois & Co. v. Katzel, 260 U.S. 689.

Similarly, where a distributor repackages goods bought from a manufacturer, he may not use the manufacturer's trade-mark without his permission, Coca Cola Co. v. Butler & Sons, 229 F. 244; Bayer Co. v. Sumner Printing Co. 7 F. Supp. 740, but it may indicate on the label that the product is that of the manufacturer, packaged by the distributor. Prestonettes, Inc. v. Coty, 264 U.S. 359. This is also true of ingredients.

A similar rule applies where one manufactures replacement parts to fit a trade-marked article. He may indicate on the label that the parts will fit the trade-marked article, but he cannot use the trade-mark as a trade-mark on the article. Thus if the Wilson Co. makes wheels for a Ford car, it may advertise "Ford Car Wheels manufactured by Wilson Co." but may not advertise "Ford Car 

Wheels". Ford Motore Co. v. Wilson, 223 F. 808.

22. SCOPE OF REMEDY--DAMAGES. 

The remedy both in infringement cases and in cases of unfair qompetition is the injunction. In the discretion of the court, a preliminary injunction may be granted to preserve the subject matter under controversy as it exists at the time of bringing suit. This remedy is used cautiously since it in effect grants the relief ultimately prayed for. It should be granted only in cases where the damage to the plaintiff, if it continues, cannot be a lequately measured. The relative injury to both parties will be considered. If the defendnat is guilty of fraudulent conduct, a preliminary injunction is more likely 

Where the court denies preliminary injunction it may protect the plaintiff by ordering defendant to post a bond and to keep account of sales in order that damage to the plaintiff pending suit can be measured. 

The final injunction can be absolute or partial--ordering defendant to refrain entirely from using his mark, or to use it only in certain ways (see Dyanshine and Emory cases supra #13, 18) It may be a mandatory injunction requiring defendant to add to its labels certain qualifying phrases indicating source (See Ford Car Wheel Case, #17 (3)). 

Where plaintiff can prove loss of sales due to defendant's infringement or unfair competition the burden of proof shifts to defendant to show what part of his profits were due to his use of plaintiff's mark; otherwise plaintiff is extitled to all his profits. This doctrine was first established in a patent case, Westinghouse Co. v. Wagner, 225 U.S. 604, and was applied in a Trade mark case in the "American Girl" case, Hamilton Brown Shoe Co. v. Wolf Bros, 240 U.S. 251.

The theory of awarding defendant's profits to plaintiff is that of unjust enrichment, and will depend largely on the good faith of the defendant. 

Where, the defendant uses plaintiff's mark on goods of an entirely different character, plaintiff may not be able to show loss of sales at all and so will not be entitled to an accounting. "Fashion Park" case--men's suits v. men's hats. Rosenberg v. Elliot, 7 F. (2) 962.

CHAPTER III 

MISAPPROPRIATION OF COMPETITOR'S TRADE VALUES TRADE SECRETS. 

Trade secrets are of two types: The first includes ideas, plans and processes of all kinds which, although original and secret, may be unpatentable; for example, the ingredients of a product may be known, but the manner of mixing them may be a valuable secret. The second type consists of facts about the internal methods of running a business, such as sources of supply, customers' lists, plans for expansion and advertising campaigns and the like. This latter group has no independent value as original ideas; its value to the owner consists in the fact that the plans are not known to competitors. 

25. PROPERTY RIGHT--PREREQUISISTES OF PROTECTION. 

The courts recognize a property right in trade secrets. In order to obtain protection of this right the owner must show that the secret is really secret, that he intended it to remain secret, and that he has not published it. 

26. WEAT IS A SECRET? 

Facts or idean which are common knowledge, generally or in the trade, cannot be protected as the trade secrets of an individual. An original idea must be novel in order to be protected as a secret. Lueddecke v. Chevrolet Motor 

Co., 70 F. (2d) 345. An idee "leponding for its realization upon the concurring minds of any individuals, each of them unbound by contract and free to act as he chose***can scarcely be called property". Haskins v. Ryan, 71 N.J.Eq. 575.

Thus in that case an idea for the purchase of a number of independent companies to form the United Lead Co., originated by plaintiff and communicated by him to defendant and carried out by the latter was held not entitled to protection, and the plaintiff could thus claim no share of defendant's profits. 

The internal facts of a business, to qualify as trade secrets, must be such as are not known to others who pursue a similar business. Taylor Iron and Steel Co. v. Nichols, 70 N.J.Eq. 541.

Secrets, fairly discovered, may be appropriated. Chemical analysis of a product to discover its ingredients is permissible. Clothing designs, unless patented as design patents, remain secret only until goods manufactured according to the designs are sold to the public. Cheney Bros. v. Doris Silk, 35 F. (2a) 

279. (Certiorari denied, 281 U.S. 728.)

However if a secret is in fact discovered by unfair means, the fact that it could have been discovered by fair means does not give the discoverer the right to use the secret he obtained unfairly. Thus where defendant might have obtained the secret of the manufacture of plaintiff's pumps by buying one and scrupulously copying it, but where he did in fact learn the secret by purchasing the plaintiff's own patterns from a repairman hired by plaintiff,' he was enjoined from using the patterns. Tabor v. Hoffman, 118 N.Y. 30.

28. PROTECTION AGAINST DISCLOSURE BY EMPLOYEES AND PARTNERS. 

Where a contract of employment includes a covenant by the employee not to disclose trade secrets, the covenant is enforceable and is not regarded as a contract in restraint of trade. Such agreements are however, limited to the disclosure of bona fide trade secrets. A contract by which an employee agrees never to divulge at any time "any information of any nature now known to him or hereafter acquired by him during the term of his agreement, relating to the process of steel making" was held to compel the employee either to work for this employer or to remain forever idle at his trade. The contract was therefore not enforced. Taylor Iron & Steel Co. v. Nichols, 70 N.J. Eq. 541. 

In all cases involving use of trade secrets by former employees, there is present the conflict between the right of the employer to keep secret his plans and business methods, etc. and the right of employee to use what knowledge he - has gained in his trade or profession, while working for his employer. An employee, even though he begins work as an unskilled worker, has a right to use all the knowledge and sill he gains in his trade, and this includes ceneral knowledge of sources of supply, methods of obtaining business and the like. 

The more important an employee's job is, the more difficult it is to distinguish between general knowledge which he may use and special secret knowledge which he may not.

While it is quite clear that an employee may not steal a list of customers and sell it to a competitor or use it himself when he established a competing business, opinion is divided on the question of whether he may solicit trade from former customers when he establishes a competing business if he appropriates no list but simply remembers the names and addresses of the customers. In Colonial Laundries v. 37. SUMMARY OF FEDERAL LEGISLATION. 

The first landmark of federal control in this field was the Federal Food and Drugs Act of 1906, which prohibited the adulteration or misbranding of food and drugs. It provided for seizure of prohibited goods and additional criminal penalties. Jurisdiction overmisbranding was confined to false statements on labels. (Enforcement lay in the Food and Drug Administration of the Department of Agriculture.) The 1912 amendment to this act added a prohibition against false statements of the curative or therapeutic effect of drugs. 

The Food, Drug and Cosmetics Act of 1938 (the Copeland Act) extended the 

Administration's jurisdiction to include cosmetics, contained more stringent labeling requirements and severer criminal penalties. Under this act, "labels" include accompanying pamphlets, but the Administration still la cks jurisdictio over advertising material unconnected with the packaging of the article. 

In 1940, the Administration was transferred from the Department of Agricul ture to the Federal Security Agency.

Under the Federal Trade Commission Act of 1914, Sec. 5, the Federal Trade 

Commission was empowered "to prevent person***from using unfair methods of competition in commerce" wherever it can show "public interest". Under this act, the FTC undertook to prevent false and misleading advertising. Its jurisdiction to prevent false and misleading statements on labels and packaging of goods overlaps that of the Food and Drug Administration. The FTC's powers are enforced through Cease and Desist orders and violations of such orders carry criminal penalties.

The Wheeler-Lea Amendment of 1938 broadened the power of the FTC by giving it jurisdiction over "unfair or deceptive acts, or practices in commerce affecting the public interest". "Competition". is no longer a jurisdictional element.

The cease-and-desist orders of the Federal Trade Commission may be appealed to the Circuit Court of Appeals. Before the 1938 amendment, the jurisdiction of the Circuit Court was regarded as original to the extent that in affirming the order of the Commission, it issued its own injunction. Under the 1938 amendment, the jurisdiction is appellate, so that the Court affirms the order of the Commission and it remains the Commission's order. Orders of the Commission, if not appealed from, become final when the time to appeal has expired. 

Section 43 of the Lanham Act provides for a right of action by any person who believes that he will be damaged by the use of false descriptions in advertising, labeling, etc. The FTC retains concurrent jurisdiction. This section of the Lanham Act also reaffirms the common law right of suit for geographical misrepresentations of origin. (See 36).

CHAPTER V DISPARAGEMENT OF COMPETITORS PRODUCTS 44. THE LAW OF LIBEL AND SLANDER. 

Protection against the kind of unfair competition occasioned by the publication of disparaging statements concerning the quality of a manufacturer's or dealer's goods or financial standing or method of doing business has lagged. behind protection in other fields of unfair competition because the courts have, until quite recently, regarded themselves as bound to apply the traditional common law rules applicable to cases of libel and slander. 

Under the common law rules regarding libel and slander, protection against disparagement is difficult if not impossible of achievement.

In the first place, libel is actionable per se (that is, without proof of special damage) only if the words used disparage the plaintiff's reputation socially or in business. To say of a manufacturer that he is a crook is actionable per se because it damages his personal reputation. To say, however, that the raincoats which he manufactures are not waterproof is a statement only about the quality of his goods and not of his character, and although the resultant loss of business may be greater, no action will lie unless the manufacturer is able to prove special damage. In proving special damage, the plaintiff must show that specific customers, ready and willing to purchase from plaintiff, were deterred from so doing as a direct result of defendant's disparaging statements. 

In modern business where the manufacturer deals with the customer only indirectly through jobbers, retailers and salesmen, and where the customer remains for the most part anonymous, this type of proof is in most cases impossible to adduce Thus the plaintiffs in the following cases found no remedy in the courts: 

Defendant, a dealer in baby foods, sold plaintiff's baby food, attaching a circular stating that a competing food was more nutritious and recommending its purchase. White v. Mellin, L.R. 1895, A.C. 154.

THE INJUNCTION AS REMEDY. 

In addition to the virtual impossibility of proving special damage, the rules of libel and slander have impeded justice in this field of unfair competition in another and perhaps more serious fashion. Traditionally, libel cases have required trial by jury. They have therefore been actions at law for damages and not actions in equity for injunction. Even if a manufacturer can succeed in proving the loss of specific sales as a result of defendant's defamatory statements, he will succeed only in obtaining damaes in the amount of the loss. of sales. He cannot restrain the defendant from continued publication of the defamation, either during the pendency of the action or upon its termination. 

This not only results in a multiplicity of suits but also in a hopelessly inadequate remedy to the modern businessman. Under this state of the law, it might easily be profitable for a competitor to continue disparagement which can reach a wide segment of the public and be prepared to pay the relatively inconsequential damages which his rival will be able to prove. 

In England, the common law rule against equity jurisdiction in libel cases was altered. by statute in 1873, but the American courts followed the English common law rule.. The leading case denying the fight of injunction in a libel action is that of Kidd v. lorry, 28 F. 773 (CCA Pa. 1886).

50. TYPES OF PREDATORY PRACTICES. 

One of the duties of the Federal Trade Commission is to watch carefully that the basis of our industrial system, the preservation of free competition, is observed, but also that the competition shall not be allowed to have such a free hand that it will cause injury to, or destruction of, any business through unfair means. If a business fails to flourish because it cannot stand the heat of competition, then it deserves to fail. But if certain unfair or predatory trade practices have taken place to contribute to the failure, then the businessman who has seen his business undermined may be entitled to an injunction or to damages, if definitely ascertainable, for the business loss incurred. 

Among the practices which may be considered illegal, there may be included the following: 

1. Procuring the business or trade secrers of a competitor by espionage, or by bribing the employees of the competitor. 

2. Inducing the employees of a competitor to violate their contracts of employment. 

3. Paying excessive prices for supplies to corner the market. 

4. Interference with the product of a competitor. 

5. Boycott. 

6. Commercial briberty. 

7. Diversion of business or customers by other illegal means.

A common business practice of manufacturers has been that of attempting to require retail dealers tohandle the products of the particular manufacturer alone, not carrying lines of goods produced by competitors. Thus, agents of one publication who sold competing publications might be subject to a lawful refusal of the publishers to continue to supply them. Pictorial Review Co. v. Curtis Publishing. CHAPTER VIII MONOPOLY THE SIERMAN ANTI-TRUST ACT 62. MONOPOLY: DEFINITION 

Monopoly has been defined as the sole power of dealing in an article or performing a specified service, generally or in one particular locality, A legally authorized monopoly is an exclusive right, license or privilege, usually for a limited period of time. 

63. HISTORY OF MONOPOLIS. 

Monopolies were originally prerogatives of the monarch; they date back to the edicts of Emperor Zeno of Constantinope. The guilds exercised monopolies in various crafts, so much so that they invited attack by Louis XVI of France, In England, whenever the sovereign became embarrassed for funds because of the frequent wars and extravagances of the court, it was usually possible to se cure mony from some wealthy individual by granting a monopoly or letters patent for many activities, such as leather manufacture, salt, playing cards, etc. 

With the exploration of new parts of the world, monopoly of foreign trade was granted to such companies as the East India Company and the Hudson Bay Company. 

So many activities fell into the field of monopoly that the public had to pay excessive prices and the workman could not exercise individual initiative, but had to go to work of rhte monopolistic companies. Finally, in the famous "Case of the Monopolies", Darcy v. Allen, 11 Coke 84, Justice Coke declared that a grant from the crown was incompatible withe English Constitution, and hence void at common law. Subsequently the Staute of Monopolies was enacted, outlawing prior monopoly grants and regulating future grant's. 

64. COMMON LAW 

It was not a criminal offense at common law to enter into a contract in restraint of trade. Such contract was not enforceable, however, By enactment of the Sherman Act, (Sec. 1) the making of such contract was deemed a misdemeanor, punishable by as much as five thousnad dollars fine and imprisonment for a year, when the contract had a direct effect on interstate commerce. 

When any combination or agreement might in its operation have a tendency to restrain trade, create a monopoly, control production or supply of a commodity, or curtail the opportunity to obtain such supply, or reduce competition, the agreement at common law could not be enforced in law or equity. The usual test is, " does an injury to the public occur. But competition is necessary to trade, and is deemed to be in the public welfare. Thus a bad result to the public is not an essential element if the combination tends to lessen competition or create a monopoly. Morgan v. Gove, 206 Cal. 627; Jackson v. Sullivan, 276 Ky. 666.

Once exception to the above rules may be pointed out. A monopoly has always been permitted when it will aid the government in the execution of a public project. "If there be any sort of business which from its peculiar character can be restrained to no extent whatever without prejudice to the public interest, then the courts would be compelled to hold void any contract imposing any restraint, however partial, on this peculiar business, provided, of course, it be shown clearly that the peculiar business thus attempted to be restrained

65. COMMON LAW PRACTICES 

There are several practices now outlawed by statute which were indictable at common law, since they sought to control the supply of and demand for commodities to the disadvantage of the public. 

Forestalling consisted of the buying of victuals while on the way to the market, intending to hold them off the market and to create an artificial scarcity.

Regrating consisted of buying up victuals, especially corn, in the market, with resale at another place within four miles, thus creating a scarcity in the central market place by setting up subsidiary markets. 

Engrossing consisted of any other buying of victuals, whether still growing in the fields or not, in large quantities, thus exerting a control on the market to the public detriment.

The present-day equivalent of these practices which tended to control supply, are the corners and trusts which bar a free market. A corner of the cotton market, violative of the Sherman Act, (Sec. 3) is illustrated in United 

States v. Patten, 226 U.S. 525. 

66. PURPOSE AND APPLICATION OF SHERMAN ACT 

The Sherman Anti-Trust Act was enacted in 1890 for the purpose of the preservation of freedom to trade, and applies to transactions having a direct effect on Interstate or foreign commerce. When the effect on interstate commerce is merely indirect, an agreement tending to restrain trade will be upheld. 

United States v. Fur Dressers' Assn., 5 Fed. (20) 872.

A manfuacturer may still exercise independent discretion as to the parties with whom he will deal, and he may announce, in advance, circumstances under which he will refuse to sell. United States v. Colgate & Co., 250 U.S. 300.

Section 2 makes the penalties of the Sherman Act applicable to all persons "who shall monopolize, or attempt to monopolize, or combine or conspire with any other person or persons, to monopolize" interstate or foreign commerce, and prescribes the penalties of a misdemeanor for such acts. "Commerce includes "traffic, intercourse, trade, navigation, communication, the transit of persons, and the transmission of messages by telegraph, indeed, every species of commercial intercourse among the several states. Adair v. United States, 208 U.S. 161. 

67. CONTRACTS AND COMBINATIONS IN RESTRAINT OF TRADE. 

Sections 1 and 3 of the Sherman Act make illegal contracts, conspiracy, or combination in restraint of trade, whether in the form of a trust or otherwise. 

A conspiracy, of course must consist of two or more persons, the act of one 

Conspirator being the act of all.

Page Certain practices are not forbidden: A partnership may be formed, if no monopoly is created, nor price control exercised, Corporations may merge, or buy stock in other corporations, as consolidation, unlike combination, does not contravene public policy.

In a prosecution, it is proper practice to charge monopoly and combination in' separate counts.

Size alone does not constitute a monopoly, although it may tend to have that effect. The fact that prices are actually lowered through a combination. is not a good defense; intent of the combination is immaterial. United States v. Swift & Co., 196 U.S. 375.

The application of the Sherman Act, like that of the Clayton Act, is somewhat modified in relation to common carriers. The Interstate Commerce Commission may permit combination, division of traffic, and pooling of earnings, involving a combination among companies normally competitive, whereby a uniform rate is set and maintained, the profits being shared on a percentage basis. 

(See Chapter II, infra). 

It has been held that a contract is not in restraint of trade which forbids the divulging of a secret process on the sale of a business. Tode v. Gross, 127 N.Y. 480,

It is to be noted that while Section 1 of the Sherman Act applies to commerce between states or with foreign nations, Section 3 extends the penalties of the Act to contracts involving trade with territories and with the District of Columbia, and between territories and the District of Columbia and foreign nations. 

68. WHEN COMBINATIONS NOT ILLEGAL. 

All combinations may not be considered illegal, if they do not amount to a monopoly. The consolidation of companies is not per se unlawful. American 

Press Assn. v. United States, 245 Fed. 91. A combination having power which is much greater than that wielded by any single competitor, but which is less powerful than all competitors combines, may not constitute a monopoly. United 

States v. United States Steel Corp., 251 U.S. 417. 

"Undoubtedly there may be combinations so destructive of the right of the people to buy and to sell and to pursue their business freely that they must be declared to be void upon the ground of public policy. In such cases the injury to the public is the controlling consideration. But it does not follow that every combination in trade, even though such combination may have the effect to diminish the number of competitors in business, is therefore illegal. Such a rule would produce greater public injury than that which it would seek to cure. 

It would be impracticable. It would forbid partnerships and sales by those engaged in a common business. It would but off consolidations to secure the advantages of United.Capital and economy of administration. It would prevent all restrictions and exclusive privileges, and hamper the familiar conduct of commerce in many ways. CHAPTER IX THE CLAYTON ACT 71. LEGISLATIVE HISTORY 

In the twenty-four years of its operation, various loopholes had appeared in the protection against trusts and monopolies afforded by the Sherman Anti-Trust Act. Therefore it became necessary to enact legislation to supplement the exist ing laws against unlawful restraints of trade and monopolies. Consequently, in 

1914 the Clayton Act was enacted. Later at the same session, the Federal Trade 

Commission Act was passed, but in this act it was specified (Sec. 11) that its operation would entail no interference with the enforeement of the existing antitrust acts, the Sherman Act and the Clayton Act. 

72. DIFFERENCE BETWEEN OPERATION OF ACTS 

The Sherman Act was intended to deal primarily with restraints of trade, combinations, trusts and monopolies whose practices had already become established so that restraints of trade had become already consummated. The Sherman Act. was too broad in its intention, too general in its, purpose. The Clayton Act, on the other hand, was aimed at being specific in regard to its prohibition of certain methods of restraint of trade which might still be at most, incipient, in that instead of having already accomplished a restraint of trade or monopoly, such practices could be held to be likely to produce a tendency to restrain trade or create a monopoly. By the enforcement of the Clayton Act, monopolies could be nipped in the bud by cease and desist orders, without the event of a long-established monopoly or unfair trade practice being overturned. The business man could know more specifically just what would be construed as an unfair trade practice, as tending to restrain trade or create a monopoly, and could thus with lesser risk of later prosecution, plan his business operations to stay within the limits of the law. Due to the prospective rather than retrospective view taken by the Clayton Act, the business man who happened in some way illegally to overstep the bounds, stifle competition or indulge in restraint of trade, would soon be made aware of his error, and would be able to amend his practices before they should continue for a considerable time, with the penalties becoming increasingly severe. 1 

73. OPERATION OF ACT 

The Clayton Act (Sec. 2) bars discrimination between purchasers in regard to the price of commodities which are marketed. through the channels of interstate or foreign commerce, wherever the effect of such discrimination would be to lessen, injure or destroy competition, or tend to create a monopoly. Further prescribed practices include the making of payments to customers for services furnished by them in marketing commodities, if such arrangement for payment is not available to all customers on proportionately equal terms; that is, the granting of discount, advertising charge or other charge not available to the competitors of the customer. Even when it does not tend to cause a monopoly, no commission or brokerage except for services rendered, may be offered. Federal Trade Commission v. Raymond Bros. -Clark Co., 263 U.S. 565.) However, discounts may be offered for purchase in quantity, if such discounts are available to all customers. emphasis is placed on the item of costs; hence the manufacturer may offer a better price, when he is not busy, if all customers can expect the same consideration. The seller may classify customers, if the classification has some The

Page realistic basis. The commodity offered to different customers, if of the same grade and quality, comes within the operation of the Robinson-Patman Act, Sec. 

Great Atlantic & Pacific Co. v. Cream of Wheat Co., 224 Fed. 566. 

74. BURDEN OF PROOF 

The "rule of reason" in the application of anti-trust acts, as to whether any practice would reasonably be expected to produce a monoply or lessen competition, does not apply to price-fixing agreements. Such agreements per se constitute restraints of trade which are deemed unreasonable. 

Therefore discrimination in fixing prices makes out à prima facie case of illegally lessening competition, creating a monopoly, and restraining trade; the burden of disproving such allegations, or of pleading justification therefor or an exception to the statutory ban is on the defendant charged with price-discrimination. However, the defendant may justifiably prove that the lower price was set in good faith, in order to meet an equally low price of a competitor, or some special service rendered by a competitor. Chamber of Commerce of 

Minneapolis v. Federal Trade Commission, 13 Fed. (2d) 673. 

75. REMEDIES 

Several remedies are provided by the Clayton Act for infractions. The Federal Trade Commission has the power to issue orders to cease and desist from illegal price discrimination; (Sec. 11) if such cease and desist orders go unheeded, the Federal Trade Commission is empowered to bring action in the District Court for an injunction to enforce its orders. Or an individual who has suffered actual damage through some illegal practice or price-fixing discrimination is enabled to bring an action in the District Court either for simple damages under the common law, treble damages as provided by the Act itself, or for an injunction to halt future illegal discrimination (Sec. 4). Such actions may have venue in the district in which the defendant resides, or in which the defendant may be found; but subpoenas for witnesses may run into any other district. While the appointment of no special master is permissible for the purpose of computation of damages, depositions on such matters are allowed. 

76. APPLICATION TO COMMON CARRIERS 

The provisions of the Sherman and Clayton Acts are somewhat modified insofar as they relate to transportation and common carriers. It has been held that such carriers may, with the permission of the Interstate Commerce Commission, combine or consolidate, divide traffic by agreement, or pool their earnings. The use by railroads of union terminatls in certain cities is tolerated, provided that no monopoly is created whereby any railroad is totally excluded from terminal facilities in the city. United States v. Terminal R. Association, 224 U.S. 383.

But certain practices have not been allowed. When one person attempted to purchase the controlling stock in two competing rail lines a trust which would tend to lessen competition and thus constitute a violation was formed. United 

States v. Union Pacific Ry. Co., 226 U.S. 61, 470. On like reasoning, a holding company for stocks of competing railroads was the target for a desist order. 

Northern Securities Co. v. United States, 193 U.S. 197. 

When railroads attempted to fix rates in interstate commerce, by establishing. rates for all freight, with notice of change of rates to the chairman of the railroad association required, and any modification of rates had to be approved

Page unanimously by eighteen railroads, it was held that intent to restrain trade was immaterial, since this practice resulted in a restraint which was prohibited, whether reasonable or not. "Does the agreement restrain trade or commerce in any way so as to be a violation of the Act? We have no doubt that it does. The agreement on its face recites that it is entered into 'for the purpose of mutual protection by establishing and maintaining reasonable rates, rules and regulations on all freight traffic, both through and local. To that end the association is formed and a body created which is to adopt rates, which, when agreed to, are to be the governing rates for all companies, and a violation of which subjects the defaulting company to the payment of a penalty, and although the parties have a right to withdraw from the agreement on giving thirty days' notice of a desire to do so, yet while in force and assuming it to be lived up to, there can be no doubt that its direct, immediate and necessary effect is to put a restraint upon trade or commerce as described in the act. For these reasons the suit of the government can be maintained without proof of the allegation that the agreement was entered into for the purpose of restraining trade or commerce or for maintaining rates above what was reasonable. The necessary effect of the agreement is to restrain trade or commerce, no matter what the intent was on the part of those who signed it. 11

80. APPLICATION TO INSURANCE 

Following the old doctrine of Paul v. Virginia, 75 U.S. 168, the business of insurance had never been deemed to be interstate commerce; since the Anti-Trust 

Acts applied only to interstate commerce, insurance companies could not be regulated by the federal government. 

But in 1946 this whole concept was overturned, in the case of United States v. Southeastern Underwriters Association, 322 U.S. 533. In this case, insurance companies had grouped together to fix premiums and agents' commissions, indulging in boycotts and intimidation of non-member insurers. It was held that the insurance business, formerly considered intrastate was sufficiently related to the stream of interstate commerce to be so considered. 

The upset to the established business practices of the insurance companies, formerly considered safe from anti-trust prosecution, is practically incalculable. 

The old premise that there is no obligation in an insurance policy to perform any act in interstate commerce, since the contract provides only for the payment of money in case of a loss, is simply swept away. The benefit to the insuring publice from this decision may be considerable. 

Other late cases waver between the new and the old doctrine. In this regard, see Robertson v. Peo. of California, 328 U.S. 440; Prudential Insurance Co. v.

Benjamin, 328 U.S. 408; State of New Jersey v. Prudential Insurance Co., 64 N.E. 

(2a) 150.. 

81. EXEMPTIONS UNDER THE ROBINSON-PATMAN ACT 

As previously mentioned in this chapter, the Robinson-Patman Act applies to price discrimination which tends to lessen competition or create a monoply, and is directed against any person who grants or knowingly receives the benefit of discrimination, or customers of either of them. The commodities must move in interstate commerce, and be sold for resale within the United States. Whether discrimination has occurred is to be tested on the basis of whether the goods are of like grade and quality. 

However, two important exemptions are contained in the Robinson-Patman Act (Sec. 1 (a), one based on quantity differentials and the other based on price changes in response to changed conditions. "Provided, however, that the Federal Trade Commission may, after due investigation and hearing to all interested parties, fix and establish quantity limits, and revise the same as it finds necessary, as to particular commodities or classes of commodities, where it finds that available purchasers in greater quantities are so few as to render differentials on account thereof unjustly discriminatory or promovite of monopoly in any line of commerce; and the foregoing shall then not be construed to, permit differentials based on differences in quantities greater than those so fixed and established." 

This provision is necessary to prevent a single large retailer from getting a great competitive advantage through cornering the market due to a quantity purchase at a considerably lower price. "Nothing herein contained shall prevent price changes from time to time where in response to changing conditions affecting the market for or the marketability of the goods concerned, such as but not limited to actual or imminent

Page deterioration of perishable goods, obsolescence of seasonal goods, distress sales under court process, or sales in good faith in discontinuance of business in the goods concerned." 

This provision merely provides a legal way to carry out good business practice, in being able to move old or deteriorating stock.
