DETERMINATION OF HEROINE AND QUININE SPECTROPHOTOMETRICALLY 

by 

Maynard J. Pro, William P. Butler, Alex P. Mathers

Alcohol and Tobacco Tax Division, Laboratory Internal Revenue Service 

Washington, D. C.

The increasing need of forensic chemists for a rapid, accurate quantitative method to determine mixtures of heroine and quinine prompted this investigation. This study has as its purpose the development of a method for the simultaneous determination of these alkaloids, employing two-component spectrophotometric analysis. Its application to routine drug seizures containing a variety of diluents has been given particular consideration.

Since qualitative (1) identification is required by the narcotic law (2), the results obtained by this procedure are intended as secondary supporting quantitative evidence. The present volumetric methods (1) have sufficient accuracy but are tedious and time consuming. Colorimetric (3), (4), (5), chromatographic (6), (7), electrolytic (8), infrared (9), (10), ion exchange (11), fluorescent (12), (13), polarographic (14), and potentiometric (15) techniques have been published for the identification and determination of many alkaloids covered by the narcotic laws. However, most of these methods were devised for specific mixtures and require more manipulation than the ultraviolet absorption procedure. Since the chromatographic, ion exchange and other adsorption techniques offer means of removing interferences, it may be advisable in certain complex mixtures to employ them in conjunction with the ultraviolet spectrophotometric approach. 861264

Clark utilized these spectral characteristics to determine this alkaloid in the presence of codeine. Extension of this work to mixtures of heroine and quinine in alkaline solution has not been previously reported. 

Experimental 

Absorption data for heroine, quinine and a mixture of these alkaloids were recorded in 0.IN sodium hydroxide. These curves, presented in Figure 1, show that at the point of maximum absorption of hydrolyzed heroine, 297.5 mu, equal molar concentrations of quinine and hydrolyzed heroine have almost the same absorption intensity while at the quinine maximum, 330 mu, hydrolyzed heroine has no absorption. 

The extinction coefficient of diacetyl morphine (heroine) in 0.IN sodium hydroxide is greater than in O.IN hydrochloric acid, and inspection of the absorbance curves prepared by Rotandaro (18) verifies this observation.

Both alkaloids obey Beer's law in the concentration ranges shown in Figure 2.

A critical phase of this study concerns interferences from the absorptions due to other alkaloids, synthetics, stimulants and inert substances at the morphine and quinine maxima. Elvidge (23) noted the similarity among alkaloid absorption curves and recommended care in their application to unknown mixtures. Carol (16) reported that many alkaloids and synthetics have no absorption at the quinine maximum, 347.5 mu, in 0.IN hydrochloric acid. Grant (17) made a general statement that the cinchona alkaloids could be determined by multicomponent analysis provided interferences were absent. Since morphine has no absorption at the quinine maxima, 330 mu, in 0.IN sodium hydroxide, it is believed that the spectral absorption of many other alkaloids at this wavelength is negligible. Although the absorption of morphine is somewhat removed from that of many diluents, detailed studies concerning specific concentrations of interferences were considered necessary. 

The data presented in Table 1 represent the absorbances of these diluents at 297.5 and 330 mu's after preparation according to this method. Those interfering substances insoluble in anhydrous methanol or sodium hydroxide-methanol solutions were removed by filtration or

6centrifugation and the clear supernatent liquid was carried through the procedure.

Milk sugar (lactose), mannitol and starch (corn and soluble) were found in a greater portion of the heroine-quinine seizures. These diluents were insoluble in anhydrous methanol. However, lactose and mannitol were soluble in sodium hydroxide-methanol solution, but gave no absorbance at the maxima of either alkaloid. Preliminary treatment of the sample with anhydrous methanol was preferred because these and other inert substances were removed leaving clear solutions from which to measure absorbances. 

Calcium and sodium sucaryl, sodium chloride, magnesium sulfate and sodium thiosulfate precipitated in anhydrous methanol, and were effectively removed by the filtration technique used in this method. 

Methadon, narceine and narcotine in concentrations of 100 ppm. were soluble in methanol but precipitated in sodium-hydroxide-methanol solution. Filtration of these alkaloid suspensions was difficult due to their flocculent character, so removal was accomplished by centrifugation. A detailed study of the occlusion and co-precipitation of morphine and quinine with the above was not undertaken. prepared by Dyer (22) in solutions pH12 show narceine and narcotine absorb at the morphine maximum while only narcotine absorbs at that of quinine. Spectrograms

Benzocaine, brucine and novacaine (procaine) in concentrations of 100 ppm. had considerable absorption at the heroine maximum and none at that of quinine.

Cocaine concentrations up to 200 ppm. and codeine up to 75 ppm. offered no interference at the morphine and quinine maxima.. The spectra of codeine and morphine in this same solution was also reported by Rotandaro (18) and show 50 ppm. codeine had no absorption at the morphine maximum.

Absorptions of thebaine and papaverine in the same alkaline solution were noted by Rotandaro (18). Also, Dyer (22) reported the spectrogram of papaverine in solution pH12. Their curves show papaverine ab sorbs at the heroine and quinine maxima while thebaine interferes only with that of heroine. The spectrum of intracaine (base) in the same solution was reported by Rotandaro (18) and no interference is offered at either wavelength. Thebaine and papaverine are significant because of their solubility and spectral interference in the solvents used in this procedure. 

Analytical data

As shown in Table 2, weight ratios of heroine and quinine in mixtures were determined spectrophotometrically and volumetrically. 

Samples of these alkaloids were prepared containing 25, 50 and 75 percent by weight of lactose, starch and mannitol to simulate a majority of the seizures. In the volumetric procedure the diluent was separated from the alkaloids by micro extraction with chloroform solvent. The extract containing the heroine and quinine was evaporated to dryness, O.IN sodium hydroxide added and heroine was hydrolyzed to morphine. 

Quinine was separated from the alkaline solution by benzene extraction. 

The aqueous phase was made slightly acid, and then just ammoniacal. 

Morphine was removed by chloroform-isopropanol continuous extraction,

and the extract evaporated to dryness and then dissolved in methanol. 

The benzene solution was also evaporated to dryness and the quinine redissolved in methanol. Both alkaloids were titrated in dilute methanol solutions. 

Those samples representing actual seizures were analyzed by both methods and the results presented in Table 3. 

Discussion 

Certain advantages obtained by determining these alkaloids in 0.IN sodium hydroxide do not preclude other solvents. Absorption curves prepared in 0.IN hydrochloric acid show that the quinine absorption is much greater than that of heroine at the quinine maximum, 250.5 mu, while at its 318 and 347.5 mu maxima even large concentrations of heroine do not absorb. At 285 mu, the heroine maxima, equal weight concentrations of quinine absorb appreciably. However, the two alkaloids can be determined simultaneously in this acid medium. 

The separation of diluents insoluble in anhydrous methanol is advantages because samples obtained from routine seizures may contain many inert substances. This step in the procedure is not absolutely essential when the diluents and cleanliness of the sample has been established, but is considered desirable because the possibility of absorption from these sources is reduced. Although lactose and mannitol do not absorb at either maxima, their removal was preferred as a general precautionary measure.

The coefficient of Standard deviation for the spectrophotometric and volumetric methods is 1.5 P.P.M. and shows both procedures have almost the same accuracy. The spectrophotometric technique is preferred because it has the advantage of speed and ease of manipulation.
