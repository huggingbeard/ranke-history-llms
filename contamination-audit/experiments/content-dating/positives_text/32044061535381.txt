I. TAX CONVENTION WITH JAPAN A. PURPOSE 

The purpose of this convention, which was signed on March 8, 1971, is to replace the existing income tax convention with Japan dated April 16, 1954, as modified and supplemented by protocols of May 7, 1960, and August 14, 1962. According to the Administration, it follows in general the pattern of income tax conventions now in force between the United States and a number of other countries and reflects changes in American and Japanese tax laws and recent developments in treaty policy of both countries. To the extent that policy and technical considerations permit, the convention follows the model draft convention published in 1963 by the Organization for 

Economic Cooperation and Development which the United States and Japan joined in 1961 and 1964, respectively. In addition, it is similar to income tax convention recently concluded by the United 

States with France, Finland and Belgium.

B. PROVISIONS OF CONVENTION 

The Tax Convention with Japan contains a preamble and 29 articles. These are summarized in the Under Secretary of State's 65-119-71-1

letter of submittal which is incorporated in the President's message transmitting the convention to the Senate. According to the Department of State, the following changes from the existing convention represent substantive improvements: 

1. The force of attraction rule would be eliminated. Under the new convention the reductions in the rate of withholding tax on investment income would be applicable as long as the investment income is not effectively connected with a permanent establishment which the taxpayer has in the taxing country. Under the existing convention the mere existence of a permanent establishment makes the reductions inapplicable. 

2. Interest income can be taxed at 10 per cent under both the existing and new conventions, and both conventions provide an exemption for interest received by the Bank of Japan and the Export-Import Bank of Japan and the Federal Reserve Banks of the United States and the Export-Import Bank of the United States. The new convention extends the exemption from Japanese tax to interest received by residents of the United States on debt obligations guaranteed or insured or indirectly financed by those United States banks and reciprocally extends the exemption from United States tax to interest received by residents of Japan on debt obligations guaranteed or insured or indirectly financed by those Japanese banks or insured by the Government of Japan. 

3. The definition of direct investments for purposes of the 10 per cent withholding rate on dividends would be changed to reduce the stock ownership requirement from more than 50 per cent of the stock to at least 10 per cent of the voting stock. 

4. Rental income from tangible personal property not connected with a permanent establishment of the recipient would be exempt under the new convention, whereas the existing convention applies the 10 per cent royalty rate to rental income. The exemption would not, however, apply to rent from leases of ships or aircraft by a person not engaged in the operation of ships or aircraft in international traffic. 

In addition, at the time the convention was signed, there were two exchanges of notes relating to understandings with regard to certain provisions of the agreement. The Department of State's explanation of these two exchanges (reprinted on pp. 25-28 of the President's message) is set forth below:

Ig "One of the related exchanges of notes deals with exemption of United States residents deriving international shipping or aircraft income from Japanese enterprise tax (or tax substantially similar to the enterprise tax) if state, county, or local governments in the United States do not impose an income tax (or a tax substantially similar to the Japanese enterprise tax) on the operation by a Japanese resident of ships or aircraft in international traffic." "The other exchange of notes relates to the application of the exemption for international shipping and aircraft income to income from containers used in international shipping and to income from a lease of a ship or aircraft by a persor engaged in international traffic to another person engaged in international traffic. This exchange also recognizes that interest income is derived from a debt obligation indirectly financed by the Export-Import Bank of Japan where that bank provides funds in the form of export, import, or investment credit to a resident of Japan for the purpose of making a loan to a U.S. résident."

C. DATE OF ENTRY INTO FORCE 

Pursuant to the provisions of Article 28, the convention will enter into force 30 days after the date on which instruments of ratification are exchanged and its provisions will have effect for taxable years on or after January 1 of the year following which the treaty comes into force. The convention may be terminated by either party five years after it enters into force by giving notice of termination at least six months before the end of any calendar year. 

II. PROTOCOL TO FRENCH TAX CONVENTION A. PURPOSE AND BACKGROUND 

The purpose of this protocol, which was signed on October 12, 1970, is to extend to certain United States residents the benefits of a tax credit available under French law to residents of France who receive a dividend from a French corporation. In effect, this would require France to give American portfolio investors in French corporations a credit equal to one-half of the French corporate income tax paid with respect to profits out of which dividends are distributed. 

According to the Administration, under French law, when a shareholder residing in France receives a dividend from a French corporation, he is considered to have paid half the profits tax charged to the corporation. The French corporate tax is nominally 50 per cent, but it is viewed as though the corporation paid a tax of 25 per cent and withheld the other 25 per cent on behalf of the government from the dividends distributed to the shareholder. The shareholder includes the latter portion in his income and then claims it as a credit against his personal tax liability, receiving a refund in appropriate cases. 

As explained in the State Department's letter of submittal, this type of credit was introduced for residents of France in 1965. During the negotiation of the 1967 treaty with France, the United States proposed the inclusion in the agreement of a similar credit for American investors, largely on the ground that the French tax on U.S. shareholders should not be greater than on French shareholders. The 

French would not accept the proposal at that time but agreed to reopen discussions on the subject in the future if the credit were to be granted to nonresidents of any other country. France has since undertaken negotiations on this matter with certain other countries and for this reason agreed to the pending protocol.

B. PROVISIONS 

The protocol contains one substantive provision which adds a new paragraph (6) to Article 9 (relating to the taxation of dividends) of the existing 1967 tax convention with France. Pursuant to the terms of the protocol, the investors who would be entitled to the French tax credit are (a) United States resident individuals; (b) United States corporations owning less than 10 percent of the shares of the paying French corporation; and (c) United States regulated investment companies of which 80 per cent or more of the shares are owned by United States residents and citizens. 1209927

It should be noted that related corporations (where the recipient owns 10 per cent or more of the shares of the paying corporation) will not be entitled to the credit with respect to dividends between them. 

This is confirmed in an exchange of diplomatic notes which took place at the time the protocol was signed. The notes are included in the 

President's message transmitting the protocol to the Senate. 

C. DATE OF ENTRY INTO FORCE 

Under the terms of Article 2, the protocol will enter into force one month after the instruments of ratification are exchanged and its provisions will have effect for dividends declared on or after January 1, 1970. The protocol will remain in force as long as the 1967 convention continues in force.

APPENDIX STATEMENT OF EDWIN S. COHEN, ASSISTANT SECRETARY OF TREASURY 

Mr. Chairman and members of the committee, I am pleased to be here today to discuss a protocol to modify our income tax convention with France and a new income tax convention with Japan to replace the one that is now in effect. I propose to discuss first the protocol amending the French income tax treaty since that is the briefer document. 

I. PROTOCOL TO U.S.-FRANCE INCOME TAX TREATY 

By way of background, I should point out that France revised its tax system several years ago to provide substantial integration between the corporation income tax and the personal income tax by granting shareholders a credit, called the avoir fiscal, against their personal income tax for one half the corporation income tax allocable to the dividends they receive from a French corporation. This development in the French tax system has also been receiving intensive consideration in other countries, notably in the United Kingdom and Germany. 

Under the French tax system, a corporation pays a tax of 50 percent on its profits, and when dividends are distributed to shareholders, they are considered to have paid one half of the corporate tax. Thus, if a French corporation earns $200 profit, it pays $100 in income tax, and if the remaining $100 is distributed as a dividend, the shareholders are considered to have received $150, of which $50 represents taxes already paid on their behalf. In other words, the shareholder is required to "gross-up" the dividend by reporting $150 in dividends in his personal income tax return and after computing his personal tax liability he claims a credit for the $50 of corporate tax allocable to the cash dividend he received. If the credit exceeds his personal tax liability, the shareholder receives a refund, and if his liability exceeds the credit, he pays the excess.

This system of tax credits does not apply to foreign shareholders in a French corporation. When a French corporation declares a dividend and $100 is payable to a U.S. shareholder, the French do not take into account the $50 of tax paid at the corporate level which is allocable to the $100 dividend and, indeed, levy a 15 percent withholding tax on the dividend, so that the U.S. shareholder receives $85 in cash. (The statutory withholding rate is 25 percent, but the existing income tax treaty with France reduces the rate, reciprocally, to 15 percent.) As a consequence, a U.S. shareholder in a French corporation is considerably worse off after taxes than a French shareholder in the same corporation. This is illustrated in the attached table, prepared on the assumption that both the U.S. and French shareholder are subject to a (5)

personal tax rate of 40 percent, which shows that of $100 in dividends payable to a shareholder the French investor is left with $90 while the 

U.S. investor is left with $60.

The Protocol would correct this result by providing that France will grant American investors in a French corporation the same avoir fiscal that French investors receive. The result is shown in the second part of the attached table. With respect to the $100 dividend payment, there will be withheld a 15 percent tax (or $15), resulting in his receiving a net amount of $85. But in addition, under the new protocol, as in the case of the French shareholder, the American shareholder will be considered as having paid one half the French corporate tax allocable to a dividend or $50 in our illustration; this will be refunded to him by the French government less a withholding tax of 15 percent, giving him a net additional receipt of $42.50. 

On his United States tax return the investor would include as income $150, and assuming a 40 percent U.S. tax bracket we would first calculate a U.S. tax of $60. Then he would claim credit for $22.50 of French tax withheld ($15 plus $7.50), and pay a net tax to the U.S. of $37.50. In the end after paying French and United 

States taxes aggregating $60, he would have left $90, just as in the case of a French shareholder who is subject to the same personal tax rate.

II. INCOME TAX TREATY WITH JAPAN 

Turning now to the proposed new treaty with Japan, I shall discus only the more significant features, since I shall submit for the record a detailed technical memorandum which explains, article by article, all of the provisions of the new treaty. Background The proposed changes from the existing treaty with Japan reflect in large part the changes in concept and approach to tax treaty relations which have evolved over the last decade. The 1963 OECD Draft 

Double Taxation Convention provided a strong impetus to the countries of the world to review established treaty concepts. In addition, the Foreign Investors Tax Act of 1966 affected our approach to certain treaty provisions. Similar changes have been evolving in Japanese treaty policy. 

The treaty now pending was largely completed by 1969, but an important provision, conferring power on the competent authorities of the two countries to give effect to administrative agreements to avoid double taxation where refunds of tax are to be made after the usual statute of limitations has otherwise expired, could not be effectuated in Japan without statutory authorization. As a result, signature of the agreement was delayed until this year. 

Abandonment of "force of attraction"

A major change in the new treaty with Japan is the elimination of the so-called "force of attraction" principle. This change conforms the treaty to a provision that has been in our law since the enactment of the Foreign Investors Tax Act of 1966 and conforms it also to other recent treaties.

Under the existing treaty with Japan a firm of one country that had a permanent establishment in the other was taxed by the other country on any income derived there as though it were the business income of the permanent establishment. The result was that investment income, which would ordinarily be taxed at a limited withholding rate of 10 percent or 15 percent depending on the type of income, was taxed at the full corporate rate.

A limited exception to the "force of attraction" principle was introduced in a 1962 protocol to the existing treaty with Japan to deal specifically with installation, construction, or assembly projects. The new treaty goes further in this direction by adopting the so-called "effectively connected" concept that is now in our Federal income tax law. Thus investment income such as dividends, interest and royalties will be treated as industrial and commercial profits only when they are effectively connected with a permanent establishment. In all other

cases the reduced rates of withholding tax will apply to such investment income. 

Residence 

In some circumstances an individual may be deemed a resident of both Contracting States under the existing treaty, with the result that he may not be eligible for the benefits of the treaty. Under the new treaty, a series of tests is adopted to establish exclusive residence in one country or the other. A similar concept has been introduced in several of our other recent treaties. The power of the United States to impose tax on the basis of citizenship is not affected by this provision. 

Shipping and aircraft income

Proposed changes in the existing reciprocal exemption provision dealing with shipping and aircraft income exemplify the need to modernize a treaty as a result of changes in the nature of international business activity. One development has been the increasingly common practice of an airline in one country to lease aircraft to an airline in the other country. A second development has been the widespread use of containers in surface transportation. 

Under the new treaty income derived by carriers from leasing and from the use of containers is treated as income from operation of ships or aircraft. This is spelled out in an exchange of notes accompanying the treaty.

A second exchange of notes accompanying the treaty grants exemption from the Japanese prefectural tax for international transport profits of a U.S. firm, if state and local governments in the United 

States provide reciprocal exemption to Japanese enterprises. At the time the new treaty was being negotiated, California and Hawaii imposed such taxes. We discussed this matter with the authorities of both states. They were extremely cooperative, and I am pleased to report that both states have now enacted legislation granting exemption from state income taxes to foreign shipping and aircraft companies under appropriate circumstances. Thus under the treaty American firms will hereafter be exempt from Japanese prefectural tax on their profits from international transportation. 

Reduced rates on investment income expanded Under the existing treaty, the withholding tax on royalties, dividends from direct investment and interest is limited to a maximum rate of 10 percent. Portfolio dividends are subject to a maximum witholding tax of 15 percent. These rates are not altered by the new treaty. However, the stock ownership requirement for determining direct investment is reduced from more than 50 percent to 10 percent or more.

The present treaty exempts from withholding tax the interest paid by the central banks and Export-Import banks of the two countries. 

This is broadened under the new treaty to include interest paid on debt obligations that are guaranteed, insured or indirectly financed by these institutions or by the Government concerned. 

Income which a resident of one country receives from the rental of tangible personal property situated in the other country is treated as royalty income under the existing treaty subject to tax on the gross amount at a rate of 10 percent. Under the new treaty, however, such

income is generally treated as industrial and commercial profits and will be exempt from tax in the country of source unless it is effectively connected with a permanent establishment situated there. 

Capital gains 

The existing treaty has no provision relating specifically to capital gains and each state applies its internal law. The new treaty confers exclusive taxing rights over capital gains to the state of residence, except in the case of real property and in other cases in which the seller has been present for 183 days or more in the country where the gains were realized. This provision is similar to that of other recent treaties. 

Income from personal services

The new treaty contains minor drafting changes in the provisions of the existing treaty exempting from tax a resident of one country who visits the other country for a limited period of time and earns income from the performance of personal services in the country he is visiting. This provision often presents difficulties in treaty negotiations in the case of actors, entertainers and others who may earn large sums of money in a very short period of time. Our treaty with 

Japan has dealt with this problem, first by limiting the amount of tax-exempt income in such cases in any year to $3,000 and, second, by denying it to a person who works for a corporation that provides his services to third parties, if he has a 25 percent or more stock interest in that corporation and if the corporation derives 50 percent or more of its income in the country imposing tax from the furnishing of that individual's services and others with the same ownership interest. These provisions are retained in the new treaty. In addition, the income tax exemption that would normally be accorded to a corporation, if it has no permanent establishment in the country where services are rendered, is denied under the new treaty to the corporation itself if the individual whose services it supplies is not entitled to the exemption.

Nondiscrimination 

A new element in the treaty is a comprehensive nondiscrimination article. The provision prohibits a state from discriminatory taxation of any kind by any level of government against individuals, permanent establishments, or corporations resident within its borders based upon the nationality of an individual or of the owners of a corporation. This provision is similar to one incorporated in other recent treaties. "Competent authority" powers

As in other treaties, the competent authorities of the two countries. are given broad authority to carry out the objectives of the treaty, particularly in connection with the allocation of income and expenditures when transactions occur between related enterprises. The principal change from the existing treaty is that if the competent authorities of the two countries, in order to prevent double taxation, reach an agreement that requires a refund of tax to an enterprise, they would be empowered to make the refund of tax even though the normal statute of limitations would otherwise bar such a refund. 

This authority is also to be found in several other recent treaties.

PROPOSED INCOME TAX CONVENTION BETWEEN THE UNITED STATES AND JAPAN 

(Memorandum prepared by the staff of the Joint Committee on 

Internal Revenue Taxation) 

The existing income tax convention between the United States and Japan was signed on April 16, 1954. This convention was modified by protocols of May 7, 1960, and August 14, 1962. The convention and protocols will be replaced by the proposed convention. 

Revision of the existing Japanese income tax treaty is desirable for a number of reasons. Over the years various changes have been made in the internal tax laws of the two countries, especially by the United States in the Foreign Investors Tax Act of 1966 which significantly changed our tax laws relating to the treatment of foreign persons. 

There also has been a trend in recent years toward modernizing and standardizing international tax relationships. This is seen in the model income tax convention of the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) and in the revision in recent years of other 

U.S. income tax treaties.

The proposed convention with Japan, in general, follows the approach of other U.S. income tax treaties. The most significant change made by the proposed convention is the adoption of the "effectively connected concept" in place of the so-called "force of attraction

doctrine." Accordingly, a resident of one country who derives investment income from the other country will be entitled to the reduced rates of, or exemptions from, tax provided by the proposed convention even though he has a permanent establishment in the source country as long as the income is not effectively connected with the permanent establishment. This change follows the approach embodied in the 

Foreign Investors Tax Act of 1966 and other recent U.S. tax treaties. 

The other more important features of the proposed convention are the following: 

(1) Under the existing convention, an exemption from tax is provided for interest received by the Bank of Japan, the Export-Import Bank of Japan, the Federal Reserve Banks of the United States, and the Export-Import Bank of the United States. The proposed convention extends this exemption to include interest received on obligations which are guaranteed, insured, or indirectly financed by the United States banks, and to those which are guaranteed or indirectly financed by the Japanese banks, or insured by the Government of 

Japan. 

(2) The existing convention provides a 10-percent withholding rate on intercorporate dividends when the recipient corporation owns by itself (or in connection with other corporations) 50 percent of the payor corporation and also owns by itself at least 10 percent of the payor corporation. The proposed convention eliminates the 50-percent ownership requirement. (3) The proposed convention exempts from tax rental income from taxable personal property not connected with a permanent establishment of the lessor. Under the existing convention the 10-percent royalty rate is applied to this income. (4) Specific provision is made for the two countries to mutually agree on common meanings for undefined terms which are used in the proposed convention. This provision, which will help insure the availability of the benefits provided by the proposed convention, is contained in only the most recent U.S. income tax treaties. (5) The reciprocal exemption for income from shipping and air transportation is extended to gains arising on the sale of the ships or aircraft. In addition, it is made clear that rents derived by an operator of ships or aircraft in international traffic from leasing ships or aircraft is subject to this exemption. (6) An exemption from source country tax is provided for capital gains (other than on real property) which are not effectively connected with a source country permanent establishment. In addition, nonbusiness capital gains of an individual who does not maintain a fixed base in the source country and who is not present there for more than 183 days during the year will be exempt from tax by that country. (7) The limitation on the foreign tax credit provided under the proposed convention is not formulated in terms of a specific type of limitation. Our other income tax treaties normally impose a per-country limitation on the foreign tax credit. Thus, a U.S. taxpayer claiming the foreign tax credit under the proposed convention will be subject to the limitation applicable to that year under the Internal Revenue Code (at present the per-country limitation or the overall limitation). (8) Business profits which Japan may tax under the proposed convention are treated as from sources within Japan, even though under the Internal Revenue Code source rules the income might be treated

as from sources outside Japan. This will help insure that a U.S. taxpayer who incurs Japanese taxes under the terms of the proposed convention will be entitled to a foreign tax credit for those taxes where the per-country limitation on the credit is utilized. (9) The exchange of information provisions of the proposed convention are made applicable in the case of all Federal taxes, rather than just the income tax.

A detailed explanation of the proposed convertion on an article-byarticle basis is presented below. 

Article 1. Taxes covered 

The proposed convention applies to the U.S. Federal income tax. 

In the case of Japan, it applies to the income tax and the corporation tax.

The proposed convention also contains a provision generally found in U.S. income tax treaties to the effect that it will apply to substantially similar taxes which either country may subsequently impose.

Additionally, it is provided that for purposes of the nondiscrimination rules of the proposed convention (Article 7), the convention applies to all taxes imposed by the United States or Japan or political subdivisions or local governments thereof. For purposes of the exchange of information provisions (Article 26), the proposed convention applies to all taxes imposed at the national level by the United 

States and Japan. 

Article 2. General definitions 

The standard definitions found in most of our income tax treaties are contained in the proposed convention. It also is provided that a term not specifically defined by the convention generally will have the meaning which it has under the tax laws being applied. 

Article 3. Fiscal domicile 

The benefits of the proposed convention generally are available only to residents of the two countries. The proposed convention defines "resident of Japan" and "resident of the United States," and in addition provides a set of rules to determine residence for purposes of the convention in the case of an individual with dual residence. This provision of the proposed convention is based on the fiscal domicile article of the OECD model convention and is similar to the provisions found in the French, Finnish, and Belgian treaties.

Under the proposed convention, an individual whom both countries. consider to be a resident according to their general rules for determining residence will be deemed for all purposes of the convention to be a resident of the country in which he has his permanent home, his center of vital interests, his habitual abode, or his citizenship. If the residence of an individual cannot be determined by these tests applied in the order stated, the competent authorities of the countries will settle the question by mutual agreement. 

Article 4. General rules and other provisions 

The proposed convention contains the basic general rules of taxation which are found in most of our other tax treaties. It provides that a resident of one country may be taxed by the other country only on income from sources within that other country (which includes under

the source rules of the proposed convention, any business profits attributable to a permanent establishment of the resident in that other country). The proposed convention also provides that it is not to be interpreted as denying tax benefits available presently or in the future under the tax laws of the two countries or under any other agreement between the countries.

Additionally, the usual savings clause is contained in the proposed convention. Under this clause, it is provided that with certain exceptions the proposed convention is not to affect the taxation by a country of its residents (and in the case of the United States of its citizens). 

The principal exceptions involve the benefits provided under the foreign tax credit, the nondiscrimination, and the governmental salaries provisions.

The proposed convention provides, as does the existing convention, that a resident of Japan who is not a resident of the United States will be allowed personal exemptions for his spouse and dependent children if they are living with him in the United States at any time during the taxable year. The amount of these additional exemptions which may be claimed, however, is limited to the proportion thereof which the taxpayer's U.S. income that is effectively connected with a U.S. business bears to his total income from all sources. Under the 

Internal Revenue Code, a nonresident alien generally is not allowed these additional personal exemptions.

Under Japanese tax law, a person who is a resident of Japan for not more than 5 years and who does not intend to reside permanently in Japan is exempt from Japanese tax on his foreign (i.e., non-Japanese) source income, except when it is remitted to (or received by) him in Japan. To prevent the proposed convention from resulting in double benefits, it is provided that U.S. source income of a Japanese resident which is exempt from Japanese tax because it is not remitted to Japan is not to be entitled to the reduced rates of, or exemptions from, U.S. tax provided by the proposed convention. 

Article 5. Relief from double taxation 

As with the existing treaty, the proposed convention provides that the United States and Japan will each allow its residents (and citizens in the case of the United States) a foreign tax credit for the appropriate amount of taxes paid to the other country. The credit allowed by each country under this provision is subject to the law of each country applicable to the year in question.

The proposed convention also makes a credit available in situations where a corporation of one country receives a dividend from a corporation of the other country in which it has at least a 10-percent ownership interest. In this case, a credit will be allowed for the corporate tax paid to the other country by the distributing corporation on the earnings out of which the dividend is paid. 

Since these provisions do not contain a specific formulation of the limitation on the credit allowed (i.e., the per country limitation which is usually found in other U.S. income tax treaties), a foreign tax credit claimed by a U.S. taxpayer under these provisions of the proposed convention will be subject to the applicable limitation provided by the 

Internal Revenue Code. 

The proposed convention follows in substance the recent treaties with Belgium, Finland, and Trinidad and Tobago by providing that

income which is taxed by Japan pursuant to the proposed convention will be treated for purposes of the foreign tax credit as income from sources within Japan. Thus, a foreign tax credit will be available with respect to this income, even though under the Internal Revenue Code the income might have its source elsewhere in which case a foreign tax credit would not be available under the per country limitation, or at all if the source of the income was the United States. 

Article 6, Source of income 

One of the ways by which income tax treaties can eliminate double taxation is to provide rules for determining the source of income. The source of income is important in view of the general rule in treaties that one country may tax residents and corporations of the other country only on income from sources within the taxing country, and also in view of the fact that the limitation on the foreign tax credit provision is based on the source of income.. 

As with the existing treaty with Japan, the proposed convention contains basic source rules, which conform in general to the source rules contained in the Internal Revenue Code, to be used for purposes of the proposed convention.

Additionally, the proposed convention follows the existing treaties with Finland, Belgium, and Trinidad and Tobago by including a rule for determining the source of bsuiness profits. Business profits attributable to a permanent establishment will be considered as from sources within the country in which the permanent establishment is located. For purposes of this rules, business profits include passive income which is treated as business profits because it is effectively connected with the premanent establishment. This rule is found in our more recent income tax treaties.

Article 7. Nondiscrimination 

The proposed convention contains the more comprehensive nondiscrimination provisions which have been embodied in other recent U.S. income tax treaties. It provides that one country cannot discriminate by imposing more burdensome taxes on its residents who are citizens of the other country, or on permanent establishments of residents of the other country, than it imposes on its comparable taxpayers. The proposed convention also extends the nondiscrimination provision to corporations of one country which are owned by residents of the other country. 

Article 8. Business profits 

Under the existing convention, business profits of a resident of one country are not taxable in the other country unless the resident has a permanent establishment in the other country. 

The proposed convention modifies this rule to bring it into accord with similar provisions found in other recent U.S. income tax treaties. 

Under the proposed convention, business profits of a resident of one country are taxable in the other country only to the extent they are attributable to a permanent establishment which the resident has in the other country. In computing the taxable business profits, the proposed convention allows the deduction of all expenses, wherever incurred, which are reasonably connected with the business profits. 

It is further provided that business profits will not be attributed to a permanent establishment merely by reason of the purchase of mer-

chandise by the permanent establishment, or by the resident of which it is a permanent establishment, for the account of that resident. 

The proposed convention sets forth examples of types of income which are considered business profits and in so doing follows the approach of our other recent treaties and the Internal Revenue Code by including within business profits investment income arising from a right or property which is effectively connected with the permanent establishment. The types of passive income included within business profits are income derived from real property and natural resources, dividends, interest, royalties and capital gains.

A significant departure from the existing treaty is the inclusion within the definition of business profits of rental income from tangible personal property (other than ships and aircraft). The effect of this is to exempt these types of rentals from tax in the source country unless they are attributable to a permanent establishment of the lessor in that country. Under the existing treaty, rental income from personal property is generally treated as a royalty and thus is subject to a 10-percent tax.

Article 9. Definition of permanent establishment 

The existing convention contains a somewhat limited definition of "permanent establishment." The permanent establishment concept is one of the basic devices used in income tax treaties to avoid double taxation. Generally, a resident of one country is not taxable on its business profits by the other country unless those profits are attributable to a permanent establishment of the resident in the other country. 

In addition, the permanent establishment concept is used to determine whether the reduced rates of, or exemptions from, tax provided by the convention for dividends, interest, and royalties are applicable. 

The proposed convention contains an expanded definition of permanent establishment which follows the pattern of the OECD model convention and other recent U.S. income tax treaties. In general, a fixed place of business through which a resident of one country engages in industrial or commercial activities in the other country is considered a permanent establishment. This includes a branch, an office, a factory, a workshop, a warehouse, and any building site, or construction or installation project which lasts for more than 24 months. (The existing convention treats a construction project as a permanent establishment if it lasts for more than 12, rather than 24, months.)

This general rule is modified to provide that a fixed place of business which is used for any or all of a number of specified activities will not constitute a permanent establishment. These activities include the use of facilities for storing, displaying, or delivering merchandise belonging to the resident, the maintenance of a stock of goods belonging to the resident for purposes of processing by another person, and the purchase of goods or collection of information for the resident. 

The proposed convention also provides that a resident of one country will be deemed to have a permanent establishment in the other country if it maintains an agent in the other country who has, and habitually exercises, a general contracting authority (other than for the purchase of merchandise) in that other country. 

The proposed convention contains the usual provision that the agency rule will not apply if the agent is a broker, general commission agent, or other agent of independent status acting in the ordinary course of its business.

Article 10. Shipping and air transport 

The existing convention, like most other U.S. tax treaties, provides that income derived by a resident of one country from the operation of ships or aircraft registered in that country will be exempt from tax by the other country.

The proposed convention generally continues this rule and, in addition, broadens the exemption in two respects. First, in the case of a resident of Japan, the exemption is extended to income derived from ships or aircraft leased by the resident, even though they are not registered in Japan. Second, the proposed convention extends the exemption to gains derived by a resident of one country from the sale, exchange, or other disposition of ships or aircraft in cases where the income from the operation of the ships or aircraft is exempt from tax by the other country under the general rule. However, this exemption is only available in respect of ships or aircraft registered in the country of residence. The only other U.S. income tax treaty extending the exemption to gains arising from the disposition of ships or aircraft is the recent Belgian treaty.

In two exchanges of notes, accompanying the proposed convention, the countries agreed to certain understandings regarding shipping and aircraft income. First, it was agreed that so long as no State or local income tax is imposed on income derived by residents of Japan from the operation of ships or aircraft, the Japanese enterprise tax (which essentially is a local tax that is collected by the Japanese government) will not apply to income of U.S. residents from this type of activity. 

Second, the exchanges of notes indicates that income derived from the use or lease of containers in connection with the operation of ships or aircraft in international traffic by a person engaged in the operation of ships or aircraft will be treated as shipping income entitled to the benefits of this provision. Rules are delineated to determine when containers are to be considered used in international traffic. Finally, the notes indicate that income derived by a lessor engaged in the operation of ships or aircraft in international traffic from the lease of a ship or aircraft to a lessee also engaged in these operations will be treated as income which is subject to this provision of the proposed convention.

Article 11. Associated enterprises 

The existing convention and most other U.S. tax conventions contain a provision similar to section 482 of the Internal Revenue Code which allows the allocation of income in the case of transactions between related persons, if an allocation is necessary to reflect the conditions and arrangements which would have been made between unrelated persons. The proposed convention includes a provision of this nature.

Article 12. Dividends 

Under the existing convention, the rate of withholding tax in the source country on dividends derived by a resident of the other country is limited to 15 percent if the recipient does not have a permanent establishment in the source country. The withholding tax is limited to 10 percent in the case of intercorporate dividends if (1) the recipient corporation by itself (or in association with not more than three other corporations of the same country) owns more than 50 percent of the

payor corporation, and each corporation owns at least 10 percent of the payor corporation, (2) not more than 25 percent of the income of the payor corporation is derived from interest and dividends other than those received from subsidiary corporations, and (3) the recipient corporation does not have a permanent establishment in the source country.¹

In general, the principal change made by the proposed convention in the treatment of dividends is the adoption of the effectively connected concept which is embodied in the Foreign Investors Tax Act of 1966, the OECD model convention, and other recent tax treaties. 

Accordingly, the reduced rates of tax on dividends will apply unless the recipient has a permanent establishment in the source country and the dividends are effectively connected with the permanent establishment.

In the absence of a convention, the applicable tax rate on dividends would be 30 percent in the United States, and 20 percent in the case of Japan. 

The proposed convention also modifies the existing rule regarding the availability of the reduced tax rate in the case of intercorporate dividends. The proposed convention eliminates the 50-percent ownership requirement of the existing treaty and, thus, the recipient corporation will qualify for the reduced rate if it has a 10 percent or greater ownership interest in the payor corporation.

Article 13. Interest 

The existing convention limits the rate of withholding tax in the source country on interest derived by a resident of the other country to 10 percent, if the recipient does not have a permanent establishment in the source country.

Additionally, the existing convention exempts interest received by the Bank of Japan and the Export-Import Bank of Japan from U.S. tax. Similarly, interest received by the Federal Reserve Banks of the 

United States and the Export-Import Bank of Washington are exempt from tax by Japan.

The principal change made by the proposed convention in the treatment of interest is the adoption of the effectively connected concept. Thus, the generally applicable reduced rate of tax on interest will apply unless the recipient has a permanent establishment in the source country and the interest is effectively connected with the permanent establishment. This treatment of interest generally conforms to that provided by the OECD model convention and other 

U.S. tax conventions. 

The proposed convention also extends (subject to the effectively connected rule) the total exemption from the source country tax presently accorded interest received by the Bank of Japan, the Export-Import Bank of Japan, the Federal Reserve Banks and the Export-Import Bank of the United States, to interest paid on obligations guaranteed, insured, or indirectly financed by such banks or obligations insured by the Government of Japan. A similar exception to the permanent establishment rule is also contained in those provisions of the existing convention dealing with interest and royalties.

In the absence of a convention, the U.S. tax rate on interest paid to a nonresident would be 30 percent and the Japanese tax rate would be 20 percent.

The proposed convention contains a limitation on the application of the interest article which is found in the OECD model convention and other recent U.S. tax treaties in situations where the payer and the recipient are related, to the amount of interest which would have been agreed upon by the payer and the recipient if they were not related.

Article 14. Royalties 

The existing convention limits the rate of withholding tax in the source country on industrial and artistic royalties received by a resident of the other country to 10 percent if the recipient does not have a permanent establishment in the source country. 

The principal change made by the proposed convention in the treatment of royalties is the adoption of the effectively connected concept. In other words, the reduced rate of tax on royalties will apply unless the recipient has a permanent establishment in the source country and the royalties are effectively connected with the establishment. This treatment of royalties is similar to that provided in the OECD model convention. 

In the absence of a convention, royalties derived from the United States by a nonresident would be subject to a 30 percent tax. Royalties derived from Japan by a non-resident would be subject to a 20 percent tax.

As in the case of the interest provision, the royalty provision of the proposed convention does not apply to that part of a royalty paid to a related person which is considered excessive. 

Article 15. Income from real property 

The existing convention generally provides that real property income of a resident of one country from property located in the other country (not including interest derived from obligations secured by the property) will, at the election of the resident, be taxed by the other country on a net basis as it such resident had a permanent establishment in the other country.

The proposed convention contains the rule usually found in our income tax treaties which provides that real property income, including natural resources royalties and gains from the sale or exchange of the property or right giving rise to the royalties (but not including interest on obligations secured by the real property or royalty interest), may be taxed by the country in which the property or natural resource is located. The proposed convention also eliminates the provision of the existing convention dealing with the net basis election. This provision is no longer necessary since both the United States and Japan allow income from real property to be taxed on a net basis. 

Article 16. Capital gains 

The existing convention has no general provision regarding the treatment of capital gains.

The proposed convention generally provides that capital gains derived by a resident of one country will be exempt from tax by the other country unless the recipient of the gain has a permanent establishment in the other country and the property giving rise to the gain is effectively connected with the permanent establishment. In the case of an individual resident of one country, gains derived from the other country will be exempt from tax from that country unless the individual either maintains a fixed base in the other country for more than 183 days during the year and the property giving rise to the gain is effectively connected with such fixed base, or the individual is present in the other country for more than 183 days during the taxable year. 

These exemptions from tax for capital gains do not apply with respect to gains derived from the sale or exchange of real property (or the sale or exchange, of royalty interests when the amount realized on the sale or exchange is contingent on productivity, use or disposition of the interest). The treatment of capital gains contained in the proposed convention is similar to the treatment provided in other recent U.S. tax conventions.

In the absence of a convention, the United States imposes a 30 percent tax on capital gains derived from the United States by nonresident alien individuals who are present in this country for 183 days or more during the taxable year.

Articles 17 and 18. Independent and dependent personal services 

Under the existing convention, an individual who is a resident of one country is exempt from tax in the other country on income from personal services performed in the other country if (1) the individual is not present in the other country for more than 180 days during the year and the services are performed for a resident of his country of residence or (2) the individual is not present in the other country for more than 90 days during the year and the income does not exceed $3,000.

This exemption does not apply to the compensation of employees of a corporation (or other entities) who have at least a 25 percent ownership interest in the corporation (or other entity) if 50 percent or more of the income of the corporation (or entity) from the source country is derived from furnishing the labor or personal services of employee-owners of this type. 

The proposed convention in general adopts the treatment of income from independent and dependent personal services provided in the OECD model convention. This treatment also generally accords with that provided in other recent U.S. tax conventions. 

Under the proposed convention, income from the performance of independent activities in one country (the source country) by a resident of the other country generally will not be taxable in the source country unless the individual either (1) is present in the source country for more than 183 days during the year, or (2) maintains a fixed base in the source country for more than 183 days during the year and the income is attributable to the fixed base. In the case of public entertainers (such as actors, athletes, etc.) the proposed convention in effect continues the rule of the existing convention. In other words, income which a public entertainer derives from the source country will be taxable by that country if he is either present in that country for more than 90 days or if his income from that country for the year exceeds $3,000.

In the case of income from services performed as an employee in one country (the source country) by a resident of the other country, the proposed convention provides that the income will not be taxable in the source country if three requirements are met: (1) the individual is present in the source country for not more than 183 days during the year, (2) the individual is an employee of a resident of, or a permanent establishment in, his country of residence, and (3) the remuneration is not borne by a permanent establishment of the employer in the source country.

However, this exemption is limited by a provision similar to that contained in the existing convention. That is, remuneration is not exempt from source country tax if it is paid to an individual who is a substantial owner of the employer (i.e., who has at least a 25 percent ownership interest in the employer) and if 50 percent of more or the income of the employer from the source country is derived from the performance of services by substantial owners of the employer The proposed convention also excepts from the exemption provision a director's fee paid to a resident of one country as a member of the board of directors of a corporation of the source country in cases where the fee is not deductible in that country by the corporation paying it, but rather is treated as a distribution of profits. In this respect, the laws of both the United States and Japan disallow the deduction of unreasonable compensation of directors.

Article 19. Teaching and research 

The existing convention provides that a teacher who is a resident of one country will be exempt from tax in the other country on income from teaching in the host country if he is present in that country for a period not exceeding two years pursuant to an agreement between the countries or between educational institutions of the two countries, or pursuant to an invitation of the host country or an educational institution of the host country. 

The proposed convention follows the approach of our other recent income tax treaties and extends the exemption to research activities, as well as teaching, if the research is undertaken in the public interest and not primarily for the benefit of private persons. It also provides that an individual who is exempt from tax under Article 20 of the proposed convention (dealing with students and trainees) need not be a resident of the country other than the host country at the time he receives the invitation contemplated by this provision in order to be entitled to the exemption provided. Accordingly, a resident of one country (or a student or trainee who was a resident of one country and who received the benefits of the student or trainee provision of the proposed convention) will be exempt from tax in the other country on income from teaching or research for a period of two years, if he is present in the host country at the invitation of the Government or an accredited educational institution for the purpose of teaching or engaging in research (for the public interest) at an accredited educational institution.

Article 20. Students and trainees 

The existing convention provides three basic exemptions for students and trainees: (1) a full exemption for all remittances from abroad received by a resident of one country who is temporarily present in the

other country solely as a student at an educational institution; (2) a full exemption for a grant, allowance, or award received from a charitable organization of one country by a resident of that country who is temporarily present in the other country; and (3) an exemption for up to $6,000 of compensation paid by an enterprise of one country to a resident of that country who is temporarily present in the other country for not more than one year to acquire technical, professional, or business experience from a person in the other country. 

The proposed convention generally follows the pattern of the existing treaty. Certain changes are made, however, to conform the provision to those embodied in other recent U.S. tax treaties. Under the proposed convention, residents of one country who become students in the other country will be completely exempt from tax in the host country on gifts from abroad used for maintenance or study and on any grant, allowance, or award received from a governmental or charitable organization. In addition, a limited exemption is provided for personal service income derived from sources within the country in which the individual is studying. This exempts from tax in the host country $2,000 per year of personal service income (such as income from a parttime job). The exemptions provided by this provision (the complete as well as the limited one) and the visiting teachers exemption may not be utilized for a period longer than 5 years from the time of the individual's arrival in the host country. 

In addition to the exemptions regarding students the proposed convention follows the approach of other U.S. income tax treaties and provides a limited exemption for personal service income of residents of one country who are employees of a resident of that country and who are temporarily present in the other country to study at an educational institution or to acquire technical, professional or business experience. This exemption is available for a period of 12 months and is limited to $5,000. The proposed convention also provides a $10,000 exemption for income from personal services performed in connection with training, research, or study by a resident of one country who is temporarily present in the other country for a period not exceeding one year as a participant in a Government-sponsored exchange program.

Article 21. Governmental functions 

The existing convention provides that citizens (or nationals) of one country (who have not been admitted for permanent residence in the other country) are exempt from tax in the other country on compensation, including pensions and annuities, paid by the country of citizenship with respect to services rendered as an employee of that country in the discharge of governmental functions. 

The proposed convention continues this rule and extends it to compensation paid by a political subdivision or local governmental authority.

Article 22. Rules applicable to personal income articles 

The proposed convention provides that reimbursed travel expenses are exempt from tax under the personal income articles without regard to maximum amount of the exemptions. The proposed convention also provides that only the benefits of the most favorable personal income article may be claimed by a taxpayer for a year if more than one of those articles is applicable in that year.

Article 23. Private pensions and annuities 

The existing convention does not contain a provision dealing with the treatment of private pensions and annuities. 

The proposed convention follows the pattern of other U.S. income tax treaties and provides that private pensions and annuities derived by a resident of one country from the other country are exempt from tax in the other country. This rule is also made applicable to social security payments. This treatment of social security payments differs from that found in other recent U.S. tax treaties which generally provide that social security payments are subject to tax by the payor country and not by the country of residence of the recipient. 

Article 24. Diplomatic and consular officials 

The proposed convention continues the rule contained in the existing convention that its provisions are not to affect the fiscal privileges of diplomatic and consular officials under the general rules of international law or the provisions of special agreements. 

Articles 25, 26, and 27. Administrative provisions 

The existing convention contains various administrative provisions. 

Under the proposed convention, these provisions are modernized and expanded generally along the lines of the provisions contained in other U.S. tax treaties. In general, the proposed convention provides- (1) For consultation and negotiation between the two countries to resolve differences arising in the application of the proposed convention and also to resolve claims by taxpayers that they are being subjected to taxation contrary to the terms of proposed convention; (2) For the exchange between the countries of information pertinent to carrying out the provisions of the proposed convention or preventing fraud or fiscal evasion with respect to taxes covered by the proposed convention, and (3) That each country is to assist the other in collecting taxes imposed by the other country to the extent necessary to insure that the benefits provided by the proposed convention are enjoyed only by persons entitled to those benefits.

The proposed convention specifically includes the definition of terms as an item on which the countries are to consult and attempt to reach mutual agreement. Although most of our other income tax treaties contain mutual agreement procedures, generally, they do not have a specific agreement procedure relating to definitional matters. 

Similar provisions, however, were included in recent treaties with 

Belgium, Finland, and Trinidad and Tobago.

Unlike other U.S. income tax treaties, the exchange of information provisions of the proposed convention apply to all Federal or national taxes, rather than just the income tax. 

Article 28. Entry into force 

The proposed convention will enter into force on the 30th day after the exchange of the instruments of ratification. It will become effective for taxable years beginning on or after January 1 of the year following the year in which it enters into force (or in the case of the United States withholding taxes with respect to any obligation to pay such taxes on or after that date).

When the proposed convention becomes effective the existing income tax convention and protocols between the United States and

Japan will terminate (the convention of April 16, 1954 and the protocols of May 7, 1960, and August 14, 1962).

Article 29. Termination 

The proposed convention will continue in force indefinitely but either country may terminate it at any time after 5 years from its entry into force by giving notice through diplomatic cahnnels. 

PROPOSED PROTOCOL TO THE 1967 INCOME TAX CONVENTION BETWEEN THE UNITED STATES AND FRANCE 

(Memorandum prepared by the staff of the Joint Committee on 

Internal Revenue Taxation) 

The proposed protocol to the 1967 income tax convention between the United States and France modifies the treatment provided under the convention for the taxation of dividends paid by a French corporation to a U.S. shareholder who is a portfolio investor in the corporation. 

The purpose of the revision is in general to extend to a U.S. portfolio investor in a French corporation the more favorable French tax treatment of dividends which is presently applicable in the case of a French investor and, thus, to remove the present disparity in the French taxation of portfolio dividends paid to a French investor and dividends paid to a U.S. investor.

As a part of a 1965 revision of its tax law, France changed the taxation of dividends paid to French shareholders. Generally, it was provided that a French resident who received a dividend from a French corporation would be treated as having paid one-half the corporate tax paid by the payor corporation on the earnings out of which the dividend was paid. In other words, that amount of tax would in effect be considered as a withholding tax imposed on the shareholder and, accordingly, the shareholder would be allowed a credit ("avoir fiscal") against its tax liability on the dividends. In determining its income for French tax purposes, the shareholder includes the amount of the credit in income as part of the dividend (i.e., grosses up the dividend by the amount of the credit), and then may claim the credit against his tax liability on the grossed-up amount of the dividend. At the present time, the amount of the credit is equal to 50 percent of the dividend.

At the time the 1967 income tax convention between the United States and France was entered into, France was not prepared to extend this credit in the case of dividends paid to nonresidents of France. France did agree, however, that if the credit were granted to nonresidents of any other country, it would reconsider this matter in the case of the United States. After France undertook negotiations with other countries on this matter, agreement was reached by the United States and France on the proposed protocol which in general makes the dividend credit available to U.S. residents who receive dividends from portfolio investments in French corporations (including persons who make their investments through U.S. mutual funds).

Under the proposed protocol, an eligible U.S. shareholder who receives a dividend from a French corporation will receive the credit. against his French tax liability on the dividend, unless the dividend is effectively connected with a French permanent establishment of the

shareholder. Under the 1967 convention, the rate of French withholding tax on the dividend in this type of case is limited to 15 percent. 

As in the case of dividends paid to French shareholders, the U.S. shareholder will gross up the amount of the dividend by the amount of the credit. The 15-percent withholding tax will then be applied to the grossed-up amount of the dividend, but the shareholder may claim the credit against the withholding tax. In many cases, the availability of this credit will result in a refund of French tax to the U.S. shareholder.

The availability of the credit under the proposed protocol is limited to portfolio investments and is not available in the case of dividends from French corporations received by a related corporation. Thus, it is provided that the credit will be available only if the U.S. shareholder is (1) a U.S. resident individual; (2) a U.S. corporation with less than a 10-percent ownership interest in the French corporation paying the dividend; or (3) a U.S. regulated investment company (i.e. a mutual fund) with less than a 10-percent ownership interest in the payor French corporation, but only if less than 20 percent of its shares are owned by persons who are neither citizens nor residents of the United States.

The following example illustrates the present taxation of portfolio dividends received by a U.S. resident from a French corporation and the changes made by the proposed protocol. Presently, a U.S. resident receiving a $1,000 dividend from a French corporation would be subject to a 15-percent French withholding tax, or $150. The $1,000 dividend would be subject to U.S. taxation and the shareholder would, within the applicable limitations, be entitled to a foreign tax credit against its U.S. tax liability on the dividend for the French withholding tax.

Under the proposed protocol, if a U.S. shareholder receiving a $1,000 dividend from a French corporation is eligible for the dividend credit ("avoir fiscal"), he will be considered to have paid one-half of the French corporate taxes paid on the $2,000 of profits from_which the $1,000 dividend was distributed. Assuming the present French corporate tax rate of 50 percent, the U.S. shareholder would thus be deemed to have paid $500 of the French corporate tax and will receive the dividend credit for that amount. In determining the U.S. shareholder's income for French tax purposes, the actual dividend received will be grossed up by the amount of the dividend credit. Thus, the dividend subject to the 15-percent French withholding tax would be $1,500, resulting in a withholding tax of $225. Since, however, the 

U.S. shareholder will be allowed a $500 dividend credit against this tax, he will be entitled to a $275 refund from France. The shareholder's 

U.S. tax liability on the dividend received from the French corporation will be computed with reference to the amount of the dividend, and then, within the applicable limitation, the U.S. shareholder will be entitled to a foreign tax credit against that liability for the amount of the 15-percent French withholding tax imposed on the dividend. 

The protocol will enter into force one month after the exchange of the instruments of ratification, and will apply to dividends declared on or after January 1, 1970. The protocol will remain in force as long as the 1967 income tax convention between the United States and 

France remains in force.
