THE INDEPENDENT: 

His Position In and Responsibility To the Oil Industry By RALPH T. ZOOK* THIS ASSOCIATION was formed sixteen years ago under conditions which threatened the very existence of the domestic oil industry. The same story was being told then that is being told in many quarters today, namely that we were running out of oil and that to conserve our domestic petroleum resources we should depend on the importation of large amounts of cheap, foreign oil. Had that policy prevailed then, I firmly believe that instead of my returning from a recent trip which included a part of the territory of defeated Germany, at the very least the German military would be in possession of the eastern coast and the Japs the western coast of this country. 

The safety of this nation demands that insofar as possible we be self-supporting in our supply of petroleum and products. Certainly, it is not necessary to go back far in our memory to the days when the Mediterranean was closed to Allied shipping, denying access to the large oil reserves of the Middle East; when the Caribbean area was the happy hunting ground for U-boats with some of their refineries being shelled; and when oil from the Gulf Coast was shut off from the 

Atlantic Seaboard necessitating the establishment of an entirely different method of transportation to make it available for our civilian economy and war use. At that crucial time it was oil from the United 

States that first checked the Axis Nations and then turned the tide 604066

I am sure no one will deny that oil offered by Texas and Louisiana influences the entire crude oil market of the United States regardless of where produced. Similarly, crude oil offered in international trade, regardless of its source, affects the market for oil from the Gulf Coast. 

If too much oil is offered in international trade, it will first mean a fight for the world markets. The next step will be pressure to import large amounts into the United States. Either one of these can be disastrous to our industry. An orderly offering of oil in international trade can prevent either one or both of them from coming about. That is the reason I have been so interested in an international oil agreement. 

Middle East Oil Reserves and Production 

IN 

IRAN, Iraq, Saudia Arabia, Kuwait, and

Bahrein will be found the greatest known potential oil reserves of the world. Mr. E. DeGolyer, Chief of the Middle East Mission for the 

Petroleum Reserves Corporation, reported upon his return from an investigation of that area that the actual proved reserves amounted to 16,000,000,000 barrels and the indicated reserves between 25,000,000,- 000 and 27,000,000,000 barrels. Mr. James Terry Duce in his recent testimony before the O'Mahoney Committee gave the reserves in this area as 26,000,000,000 barrels. These reserves were discovered by drilling less than 150 wildcat wells. To develop the oil reserves of the 

United States has required perhaps 50,000 wildcat wells.

Production in the Middle East during 1938, the last prewar year, was 332,572 barrels. Planned expansion of production and refining capacity will bring production to an estimated 600,000 barrels by the end of 1945. This is an increase of approximately 265,000 barrels daily over the year 1938.

While the Arabian Pipe Line is pretty well "dead" as a governmental project, it is far from that as a privately owned and operated line. The planned capacity is given as 250,000 to 300,000 barrels daily. Iraq 

Petroleum Company is planning a second twelve-inch pipe line to parallel the present twelve-inch line to Haifa on the eastern shore of

PAGE FOUR 

the Mediterranean, which will make possible a delivery of an additional 115,000 barrels daily from Iraq. These two new lines will add 350,000 to 400,000 barrels daily production to the 600,000 barrels planned for 1945. Physical limitations will prevent the completion of these lines for an estimated two years. However, at that time, if used to capacity, they will bring the total production from that area close to 1,000,000 barrels daily.

On our recent visit to London we were the luncheon guests of the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company. After luncheon we had the opportunity of looking over a number of maps showing the oil fields of Iran. One field has produced 100,000,000 barrels from 30 wells. There are still remaining 100,000,000 barrels which can be produced from these same wells. This is at the rate of almost 7,000.000 barrels per well. Another field is currently producing 40,000 barrels daily from three wells. The oil flows to a high point on the lease where it is treated and under the same well pressure flows to the refinery about 120 miles away. The entire field operation is carried on with five men. 

South American and Caribbean Area

THE PROVED OIL RESERVES of this area are given as approximately 7,000,000,000 barrels. They are small compared to the possible reserves of the Middle East but the large production developed in this area for war use is dynamite unless it is handled constructively. It can cause great harm to the domestic and world oil industry.

Imports during August, mainly from South America, are estimated at 378,000 barrels daily, of which there is no criticism for we were still under war demand. Current figures are not available, but the August rate compares with an average of 148,800 barrels daily for 1938. It is now time to insist that imports be reduced concurrently with the reduction of our domestic production. 

During 1938, the last prewar year, the oil production from South America and the Caribbean area totaled 720,000 barrels daily. It is expected to reach 1,165,000 barrels at the end of 1945, an increase of 

445,000 barrels daily over 1938.

With these developments in the Middle East and South America, there will be available from these two areas at the end of 1945 a total of 1,765,000 barrels daily. Within two years, from plans now under way, this production can be increased to 2,150,000 barrels daily.

Foreign Markets WHERE WILL THIS OIL FIND MARKET? The total demand for petroleum production in Europe and Africa during 1938 amounted to approximately 1,000,000 barrels daily. Two hundred and thirty five thousand (235,000) barrels were supplied from within their own boundaries, leaving 765,000 barrels to be supplied from outside sources. Of this amount 235,000 barrels were imported from the United States and 340,000 barrels from South America and the Caribbean area. The remainder was supplied, with the exception of a few thousand barrels, from the Middle East. 

I do not know the future of synthetic production in Europe. It can well be that the policy of the Allied Governments will prevent the re-building of these plants. Seeing part of the devastation brought about by the recent war and the impoverished condition of those countries visited on our recent trip to the Continent, I am not optimistic over a quick return to the prewar 1938 demand for civilian consumption. 

Military requirements may well take up that slack, but, assuming that the demand equals that of 1938, there will be much more oil available than is needed during the immediate postwar years. If that oil which has been exported from the United States is added to the amount available from the Middle East and South America, there will be conservatively 1,000,000 barrels a day in excess of that required to supply the immediate postwar demand in Europe and 

Africa. That is more than twice the amount needed.

If that amount of oil is thrown on the market, it can bring about a world oil war. This would adversely affect each one of us who depends on the domestic oil industry for his livelihood. It is to prevent such a situation that we have fought for a sound Anglo-American Oil Agreement.

Anglo-American Oil Agreement THE HE INDEPENDENTS took the lead in op posing the first Anglo-American Oil Agreement. It was not that they did not desire an agreement on oil between Great Britain and the United States. They objected to that Agreement because it could have brought about complete control of our domestic oil industry In speaking before the Interstate Oil Compact Commission at Oklah ha City on October 7, 1944, I clearly outlined my position in connection with

PAGE SIX 

that Agreement, which position was later approved by the Board of Directors of this Association. I further said, "I feel it is important to the independent producer that an effort be made to develop some other vehicle, to replace the present Agreement, which will be helpful in bringing about an orderly offering of oil in international trade." In my opinion, the Agreement just executed by the United States and Great Britain is that vehicle. It has my wholehearted approval. 

When the previous Agreement was withdrawn from the United States Senate by the President, the Petroleum Administrator asked the Petroleum Industry War Council to give him in specific language the wording of an Agreement which would remove the industry's objections. It has been my privilege to serve as a member of the National 

Oil Policy Committee which was charged with the responsibility of meeting the Administrator's request.

The re-writing of this Agreement consumed many months. It was my main source of concern and study over a long period of time. It was given serious thought by the other members and a number of the committee submitted completely new drafts. Each one of these recommendations was discussed paragraph by paragraph in full Committee meetings which lasted for days. The result was a carefully considered, accurately worded document that met with my whole-hearted approval. 

My only fear was that after it left the Petroleum Industry War Council there was danger that some Governmental agency or those who negotiated the Agreement would change its intent or purpose. 

Fortunately, this did not occur and the Agreement just completed with England is in substance the same as the draft submitted by the 

National Oil Policy Committee to the Petroleum Industry War Council and in turn to the Petroleum Administrator for War. It is an excellent example of proper Government-Industry cooperation. The State Department, the Department of Interior, and the oil industry cooperated in bringing about what, I believe, is a complete understanding. The appointment of six industry advisers to go to London for consultation during the period of negotiations with the English was constructive. 

We were fully consulted during every step of the deliberations and our suggestions given full consideration.

This treaty was developed in the most cordial atmosphere. I confess that there was some fear in my mind that our trip was poorly timed because of the political change in the English Government. I was greatly pleased when I saw the personality of Mr. Shinwell, Minister of Fuel and Power, who headed the British delegation. I believe that he and Secretary Ickes had an instantaneously favorable reaction to

PAGE SEVEN 

one another. They are somewhat the same type. Both are frank and speak without reservations.

At one of our informal meetings, someone who was talking to Mr. 

Shinwell asked me to explain to him the position occupied by the independent oil industry in the United States. It took place after the execution of the Agreement. I told him about the independents and also expressed my satisfaction at reaching an understanding. I said that because of our different political beliefs, I had feared we might have some difficulty in doing so. He surprised me when he said, "I feel that the role of Government is to promote efficiency in industry." That did not sound to me like nationalization of all industry. He continued by saying, "Now you may wonder why in the light of that statement that I am going to nationalize the coal industry. It is because the coal industry has gotten so bad that private industry is no longer able to make it efficient." He continued, "I believe there is as much danger in bureaucracy in government as there is in monopoly in industry." 

This statement was so foreign to what I had expected that the next day I repeated to him what I had understood him to say the day before. He said, "That is exactly right." I asked for permission to repeat to you this conversation, and he complied without reservation, in fact, with enthusiasm.

This Agreement removes my objections to the previous one. In the development of the draft submitted by the National Oil Policy committee, I said on numerous occasions, "Let's get it down to oil country language, language that I can understand. If that is done, then there will be no cause for misunderstanding." In my opinion everything that is intended to be covered is there in words that cannot be misunderstood. I believe it is difficult to read anything into it which was not intended.

There are several Articles and paragraphs in the Agreement which I would like to read to you. I think they will give you ample assurance that our domestic indusry is fully protected from any regulation or control, either expressed or implied. The first is Article VII which reads as follows: 

"The signatory governments agree: 

(a) That the general purpose of this Agreement is to facilitate the orderly development of the international petroleum trade, and that no provision in this Agreement, with the exception of Article II, is to be construed as applying to the operation of the domestic petroleum industry within the country of either government

PAGE EIGHT 

(b) That nothing in this Agreement shall be construed as impairing or modifying any law or regulation, or the right to enact any law or regulation, relating to the importation of petroleum into the country of either government." 

What is the exception in Article II? Article II reads: 

"In furtherance of the purposes of this Agreement, the signatory. 

Governments will so direct their efforts: (a) That all valid concession contracts and lawfully acquired rights shall be respected, and that there shall be no interference directly or indirectly with such contracts or rights; (b) That with regard to the acquisition of exploration and development rights the principle of equal opportunity shall be respected; (c) That the exploration for and development of petroleum resources, the construction and operation of refineries and other facilities, and the distribution of petroleum, shall not be hampered by restrictions inconsistent with the purposes of this agreement." 

To me, this means that the governments will so direct their efforts that each shall have the right of free operations in the other country on the basis of that country's citizenship. 

The commission of six members to be established under the Agreement is advisory only. It is well to read its duties and responsibilities. 

They are specified in Article IV as follows: 

"(a) To study the problems of the international petroleum trade caused by the dislocations resulting from war; (b) To study past and current trends in the international petroleum trade; (c) To study the effects of changing technology upon the intertional petroleum trade; (d) To prepare periodic estimates of world demands for petroleum and the supplies available for meeting the demands, and to report as to means by which such demands and supplies may be correlated so as to further the efficient and orderly conduct of the international petroleum trade; (e) To make such additional reports as may be appropriate for achieving the purposes of this agreement and for the broader

general understanding of the problems of the international petroleum trade."

But again, to be doubly sure that the Commission is advisory only, Article VI, paragraph b, states as follows: 

"(b) That no provision in this Agreement shall be construed to require either government to act upon any report or proposal made by the Commission, or to require the nationals of either government to comply with any report or proposal made by the commission, whether or not the report or proposal is approved by that government."

Now if you would still like to have some further assurance, Article 

VIII states in part: 

"This Agreement... shall continue in force until three months after notice of termination has been given by either Government or until it is superseded by the International Petroleum Agreement contemplated in Article III." 

The Agreement provides for industry consultation. Article IV, para graph 2, states in part: "...To enable the Commission to maintain close contact with the operations of the petroleum industry, the signatory governments will facilitate full and adequate consultation with their nationals engaged in the petroleum industry." 

The intent of this paragraph is plainly stated. I believe that regardless of the membership of the Commission, an advisory group from the oil industry would be helpful. To be most beneficial, it should be appointed concurrently with the naming of the Commission. I am sure we will all agree that on that group there should be ample represensation of those who are substantially interested in foreign fields. I believe, too, there should be representation by those interested solely in domestic production.

I assume this Agreement will take the same course as the previous one and be presented for concurrence by the United States Senate. The 

Senate Foreign Relations Committee will at that time have the opportunity and responsibility of a full inquiry into all of the circumstances and understandings pertinent thereto.

It may be necessary that Congress enact special legislation to permit those companies dealing in international trade to comply with the recommendations of the Commission. If so, I hope that Congress will see fit to enact such legislation.

Some may ask whether this Agreement will perform the service for which it is intended. I think it will. My answer is based on the results from the time-tested principle of talking things out instead of fighting them out. When we disagree with one another, we generally have to talk it out in the end regardless of what legal steps we take. If we sit across the table from one another and thoroughly discuss our different viewpoints in an atmosphere free of compulsion it is ten to one we will reach a solution that is mutually fair and agreeable. That is the basis on which this Agreement was written and the atmosphere in which it was negotiated and concluded. The fact that the complete Agreement was reached in less than one week is an indication of the sincere desire by both parties to reach an understanding and speaks well for its future. 

I hope that after study and deliberation the membership of the Independent Petroleum Association of America will at some time during these sessions express their sentiments concerning this Agreement. There are other members of the advisory group present who are as well or better informed than I am, and who, like myself, will be glad to enter into a complete discussion of any of its phases. 

Price of Oil 

I DERIVE great satisfaction in realizing that my term of office expires at the same time that the Petroleum Industry War Council completes its successful war activities and the Anglo- American negotiations have ended satisfactorily. There is, however, one important item of unfinished business that was turned over to me by our previous President. It was the great regret of Frank Buttram that he was not able to see an adequate price for oil during his presidency. 

I have that same regret.

To maintain a strong domestic industry we must have both an adequate market and a fair price for our oil. One is worthless without the other. We can have a good market and a poor price and be no better off than if we have a good price and a poor market. The two go hand in hand. This can be no better illustrated than our experience during the past four years of price control. There has been a market, but the price has been inadequate.

During the war crude oil production was materially increased. 

Twenty-six (26) larger companies increased their production more than the average total increase throughout the United States. Their cost per barrel was reduced. The thousands of other producers had

PAGE ELEVEN 

increased costs. With decreased demand, the larger companies will have increased costs, provided the cut-back in production takes place where the increases were made, which is as it should be insofar as possible.

The need for an increase in the price of crude oil will then no longer be a theory to some and a fact to others. With the decreased allowables and increased finding costs, for the first time in four years, we shall all find ourselves in the same boat, whether large or small. This in itself should serve to bring about the sound business policies in production, refining and marketing of oil that will correct our present unsatisfactory price situation.

The early peace-time demand for domestic oil and products may be as much as 750,000 to 1,000,000 barrels daily lower than the peak demand during the period immediately preceding the end of the Japanese war. During this critical adjustment period, the actions of the 

State Regulatory Bodies will have a most important bearing on the oil industry. If they act courageously and fearlessly to bring production in line with consumptive demand and prevent waste through holding production to the point where unneeded oil is not placed in aboveground storage, they will perform a great service to the oil industry and the nation.

This alone will not be sufficient. Industry must do its part. The present situation calls for leadership of the strongest character on the part of the major oil companies now that crude oil is available in quantities in excess of the amount needed to supply a sound market demand. If they nominate the amount of oil they need to supply sound consumptive demand rather than the amount they would like to sell, they can go far toward bringing about a proper price structure. 

We all want to get away from the regulation and, I believe, most of the operators would like to get away from price control as quickly as possible. The latter situation, however, can be either good or bad news for many. The Office of Price Administration is not expected to relinquish price control until it is sure that prices will not be expected to advance. At the ending of price control, or perhaps before, the stripper well subsidy will cease to exist and unless there is an equivalent price increase it will mean the death knell for many independent producers.

The independent producer has maintained for years that the proper method of arriving at a crude oil price is to base it on the cost of exploration, development and production, including replacement cost plus a reasonable profit. That principle should be accepted instead of

PAGE TWELVE 

paying the producer what remains from the sales price of products after marketing, refining and transportation costs have been deducted. 

The fight for an adequate price for crude oil continues and will continue until a just solution has been found. Not until then will this effort be laid aside. It must be obtained, not only to save many independents from extinction, but to preserve and strengthen the industry for the sake of national security.

The independent is no hot house flower that requires artificially favorable conditions to survive. He is a stout, sturdy fellow with plenty of initiative and courage who is quite able to take care of himself in the robust competition that prevails in his line of business. He must have, though, in order to survive and prosper, a fair and reasonable price for his oil. That cannot by any stretch of the imagination, be considered as special favor or protection. 

Oil Industry Forum THE MEETING of the Petroleum Industry 

War Council scheduled for the coming week will probably be the last meeting of that group. For the great reason that the war is over, we are all glad this day is here. However, it does mean the breaking up of the 

War Council and Industry Committee meetings which have brought about a better understanding of the problems that relate to the petroleum industry. It means, too, the loss of that personal relationship which brings mutual confidence and respect for one another's opinions. 

I hope, before the Petroleum Industry War Council disbands, that either the Chairman will appoint a committee or that some of the members on their own volition will form a group to explore all of the possibilities that show any likelihood of continuing and expanding the advantages which have come through the personal acquaintanceship and cooperation of the various branches of the industry during the war period. Not only must we hold what has been accomplished but we must find some means to broaden the activities to include a greater part of the oil industry.

I have given this much thought and have talked to many about it. 

It does not seem to me that another oil organization would be the answer. I doubt if it is possible to bring under one head all of the various interests in the industry, for each has its particular problems.

PAGE THIRTEEN 

Nevertheless, there is common ground for many of them. It is not sound to believe that one segment of the industry can long prosper at the expense of another; moreover, it is a fallacy to proceed on the theory that what is good for the independents is bad for the larger companies or what is good for them is necessarily bad for the independents.

Most of our difficulties come through misunderstanding. Most of them, as in our personal lives, can be solved through a firm desire to find the answer which, although it may be a compromise, is the best for all concerned. It is when we refuse to explore the possibilities of solution that we fail.

Our Interstate Oil Compact has been most helpful. It is a forum for discussion of the problems that, up to the present time, have related particularly to the producing branch of the industry. It is an agency approved by a special Act of Congress. Is it possible that this agency had not reached its full possibilities and that its activities could be broadened to become the clearing house for expression on other problems?

With the Interstate Oil Compact as the forum for domestic problems and the Advisory Group of the Anglo-American Oil Agreement as the forum for discussion of matters pertaining to oil in international trade, there would be two agencies approved by the Government that could be used as clearing houses for the various subjects which would properly come before them.

If the Interstate Oil Compact is not the proper solution, then let us give serious thought to developing one that will be. Perhaps it may be an Oil Industry Forum similar to the Forum of the Air where controversial questions are debated but no conclusions reached. It may take special legislation. Even that could be worth the effort. We have supported other legislation that we felt would be helpful. We did not scare away from the Interstate Oil Compact because it needed approval by our Federal Government to bring it into being and by our State Governments to make it effective. 

I do not know the answer. I am making these suggestions to stimulate thinking rather than to propose a plan of procedure. I do believe that if we set our minds to it there is a good likelihood that we will find the solution. If we do not do so, I believe we have missed a golden opportunity for greater unity and better understanding in the oil industry.

PAGE FOURTEEN The Independent's Place THIS ASSOCIATION has grown from a small organization formed under the necessity of protecting the domestic oil industry from the importation of large quantities of cheap, foreign oil and has come to represent the independent oil producer in all phases of legislative and industry matters. Each year it has grown in stature. Its position has not been obtained nor can it be maintained by negative actions. Position carries with it responsibility. When we ask for a seat at a council table, we must assume the responsibility that goes with that position. I believe that we have fulfilled that obligation and that the independent has been enhanced in dignity and respect, publicly and in the industry generally. 

Any listing of the activities of the Association during my two terms in office and prior thereto cannot reflect the effort employed in their achievement. I do believe it is appropriate to make brief mention of some of those which absorbed much of our time and thought during the period of the war.

When the Arabian Pipe Line was proposed, the independent took the lead in opposing it as a Government-owned project. It was not that we objected to an Arabian Pipe Line; we objected to Government ownership and Government participation in the oil industry. At that time, we recommended a Foreign Oil Policy for the United States which was adopted unanimously by the Petroleum Industry War Council and is a basic part of the recommendations of that group. 

The issue of a proper price for crude oil occupied much of our time and thinking. We did not make rash statements unsupported by facts. 

We have on numerous occasions submitted data which, to any fairminded individual or body, would have been sufficient to justify the raising of crude ceilings to offset increased finding, development and operating costs.

In all matters of taxation we have been out in front to protect the oil producers from unjust and unfair proposals. We were forced to conduct another defense of the depletion allowance and the intangible expense opposition. The attack was made by the President and the 

Treasury Department. Committees of both branches of Congress, after extensive hearings, turned back the attempt which would have severely crippled the producing industry. We were particularly aggressive in the correction of the condition brought about by the F. H. E. tax case decision through the passage of Concurrent Resolution 50.

PAGE FIFTEEN 

We took the lead in opposition to the creation of the War Petroleum Corporation with $500,000,000.00 capital. This measure proposed to give authority to Government to engage directly in the industry and in this manner determine the conduct of the industry. Our opposition was firm, fair and positive. This move was abandoned. 

We took the lead in preventing the use of the Petroleum Reserves Corporation, a Government organization, whose Charter authorized it to engage in all phases of the foreign petroleum business including production, refining, marketing and transportation. It is now dormant. 

This Association, as early as consistent with censorship restrictions, called attention to the ill-advised Canol Project, later condemned by Senate Committee. This project was carried through with stubborn insistence even after it had been proved a failure as an oil supply for Alaskan defense. It is a lesson about Government in industry dearly bought but unlikely to be soon forgotten.

When we entered World War II, the producer of petroleum was faced with a greatly increased demand for crude petroleum. To meet this demand required full employment of every available producing property at rates of production that were often in excess of proper production rates and harmful to his properties. He was required to reckon with governmental attitude that was based upon serious misunderstanding of the problem. The Government did not recognize the importance of supplies of essential material, the necessity for manpower, nor the inadequacy of the price the producer received for his product.

In spite of this tragic misunderstanding of producing problems by the governmental agencies responsible, the producer has continued with his job. He stayed with his wells; he nursed the ailing wells and stimulated the stronger to greater production. He responded to the call for conferences on industry problems. He worked on committees although this required much time and entailed great expense. He complained some at the treatment his problems had at the hands of those in authority, but he never lessened his efforts. He finished the job. 

During the first six months of 1945, the independents were responsible for the drilling of approximately 75% of the wildcat wells within the boundaries of the United States. They produced nearly 50% of the oil, and it is estimated they own about 40% of the oil reserves of the nation. There are approximately 20,000 oil producers in the 

United States and all of them, excepting twenty to thirty, are generally classed as independents. They play a most important part in the finding, development and production of crude oil and in influencing course of the industry.

A Program I HAV HAVE on a number of occasions, both in appearance before various groups in the oil industry and in writing, outlined what I believe to be a sound program for the oil industry. I think it will bear repeating. It is in accordance with expressions of leaders of the oil industry and the traditional policy of the Independent 

Petroleum Association of America. It is as follows: 

1. The production of crude oil in the United States should be on sound conservation principles. 

2. It must be in amounts which will not cause waste through excessive aboveground storage. 

3. The domestic industry should always have the first opportunity to supply the petroleum needs of the United States at fair prices. 

4. If imports are necessary to supplement the domestic supply, they should be only in amounts required to furnish adequate Government and civilian requirements; they should not be in quantities that will be harmful to the domestic oil industry. 

5. The price of crude oil within the United States should be such as to: (a) Encourage the exploration for and development of new flush production; (b) Preserve wells of small production; and (c) Encourage secondary recovery operations. 

6. The development of substitute fuels should not be neglected. 

Our problem here and the purpose of this meeting is to analyze and vitalize this program. To simply state it is of no value. Its establishment, however, would provide the oil industry of the United States with the encouragement and confidence necessary to find new reserves, to increase producing capacity, and to make the nation self-sufficient in both time of war and time of peace. 

Governmental controls, which have been enforced during the period of the war emergency, are rapidly being relinquished. This freedom for which we have clamored so long can be helpful or harmful, and I think we can well consider a paragraph from the editorial by Keith 

Fanshier in the August 17 issue of The Chicago Journal of Commerce wherein he says, "Freedom can mean many things, the extremes of which may be far apart. There is freedom to wreck, as well as freedom to construct; freedom to run a amok, as well as freedom to build and conserve." 

Let us use that feedom to "build and conserve.”
