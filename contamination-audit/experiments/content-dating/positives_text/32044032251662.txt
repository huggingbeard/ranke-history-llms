PROCEDURE IN THE SUPREME JUDICIAL COURT * 

The Supreme Judicial Court The Supreme Judicial Court consists of one Chief Justice and six associate justices (c. 211, sec. 1). 

The Supreme Judicial Court has general superintendence of all courts of inferior jurisdiction to correct and prevent errors and abuses therein if no other remedy is expressly provided; and it may issue writs of error, certiorari, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and all other writs and processes to such courts and to corporations and individuals which may be necessary to the furtherance of justice and to the regular execution of the laws (c. 211, sec. 3). 

The Superior Court now has original jurisdiction, concurrently with the Supreme Judicial Court, of many of these matters (see St. 1939, c. 257, printed at p. 11, infra).

Single Justice 

As a single-justice court (the six associate justices sitting in rotation) the Supreme Judicial Court acts as a court of equity (having original jurisdiction concurrent with the Superior Court under c. 

214, secs. 1-3, and St. 1939, c. 257, and exclusive or concurrent in many other equitable matters) or as a court of law, in such matters as are provided by statute. 

Although the statute (c. 211, sec. 17) authorizes jury sittings to be held by a single justice, no such sittings have been held for many years. *All references to statutes are to the Tercentenary Edition of the General Laws (1932) and all references to rules are to the Rules for the Regu lation of Practice before the Full Court (1926, as amended 1939), unless otherwise stated. 562806 1

A single justice sits to hear matters within his jurisdiction at Boston every

Tuesday and Friday excepting from the fourth Tuesday of June to the first Tuesday of October, during which period he sits every Wednesday but hears no case on the merits unless for special cause shown (see 

Amendment to Order of Business in Suffolk 

County, June 26, 1934). At such sittings he hears and determines suits in equity (except during the summer period) and motions therein arising in any county (c. 

214, sec. 37), but not during a sitting of another single justice in such county except for special cause shown (see Order of Business in Suffolk County, S.J.C. Rules, p. 55).

He may transfer equity cases and interlocutory proceedings therein from one county to another, to prevent delay, or for other necessary reason (c. 214, sec. 18; note to Sup. Ct. Rule 67).

A single justice sits at Springfield on the first Mondays of February, June, August and December, to hear such matters in equity as may be heard and determined at chambers, which arise in Berkshire, Franklin, Hampshire and Hampden (c. 214, sec. 38).

Opinions of the Justices The justices of the Supreme Judicial 

Court may be required to render opinions to the House or Senate or to the Governor and Council, upon important questions of law, and upon solemn occasions 

(Massachusetts Constitution, Part II, c. III, art. 2).

The Full Court 

The Full Court acts solely as an appellate tribunal for the hearing of questions of law (excepting in those rare instances when 2

it acts under c. 211, sec. 4, on the removal of certain officers-see, for example, Atty. 

Gen. v. Tufts, 239 Mass. 458, Atty. Gen. v. 

Pelletier, 240 Mass. 264, and Atty. Gen. v. 

O'Brien, 280 Mass. 300).

The Full Court is the Supreme Judicial 

Court in banc, and four justices constitute a quorum to decide all matters to be heard by it (c. 211, secs. 2, 20), but five members usually sit, always including the Chief Justice unless he is incapacitated or disqualified, in which case his duties are performed by the senior justice present and qualified to act (c. 213, sec. 1).

No justice can sit on the hearing of any proceeding in the nature of a review of any judgment, decree, order, or ruling made by him; but this does not apply where it is necessary to secure a quorum and, in case the other justices of the Court are equally divided in opinion, the justice whose decision in that event otherwise will stand may be called in to sit in review of such decision (Rule 10; see also Rule 4, infra, p. 7).

The Full Court is the only court in Massachusetts which can pass finally upon errors of law (see c. 211, sec. 5).

Questions of law may be taken to the Full Court from a single justice of the Supreme Judicial Court, from the Superior Court, the Probate Courts, the Land Court, the Appellate Divisions of the District Courts or from the Appellate Tax Board (by way of the county S.J.C.).

Scope of Review by the Full Court 

Entry does not, except as otherwise provided by law, transfer the whole case to the Full Court, but only the question or questions to be determined (c. 231, secs. 135, 109; c. 278, sec. 33; c. 214, secs. 19, 26; Kel jikian v. Star Brewing Co., 1939 A.S. 645, 

653-654, 303 Mass. 53). 3

In an action at law, the Full Court considers and decides only questions of law. 

Findings of fact must be accepted as true if warranted upon any view of so much of the evidence as appears in the record (Elec. 

Welding Co. v. Prince, 200 Mass. 386, 392; 

Atlantic Maritime Co. v. Gloucester, 228 

Mass. 519, 522; Norton v. Musterole Co., 235 Mass. 587, 589; O'Brien v. Board of 

Election Comrs., 257 Mass. 332, 338; Ryder v. Lexington, 1939 A.S. 939, 945, 303 Mass. 281).

In a criminal case, entry does not transfer the case, but only the question to be determined (c. 278, sec. 33, as am. by St. 1933, c. 265); except in a capital case, in which entry transfers the whole case for consideration of the law and the evidence (St. 1939, c. 341, amending c. 278, sec. 33E).

A bill of exceptions in equity, as in law, presents only questions of law (Kennedy v. 

Welch, 196 Mass. 592, 594; Eaton v. Eaton, 233 Mass. 351, 369; Porter v. Porter, 236 

Mass. 422, 425; Flint v. Codman, 247 Mass. 

463, 468).

An appeal from a final decree in equity opens all questions of fact, discretion and law, so far as they are presented by the record (Trade Mutual Liab. Ins. Co. v. 

Peters, 291 Mass. 79, 83; Merchants Mutual 

Casualty Co. v. Leone, 298 Mass. 96, 100; 

Smith v. Stratton, 302 Mass. 17, 19; see 

Wyness v. Crowley, 292 Mass. 459).

In equity appeals the Full Court does not reverse findings of fact made upon conflicting oral testimony unless convinced that they are plainly wrong (Trade Mutual Liab. 

Ins. Co. v. Peters, 291 Mass. 79, 84; Grocer v. Montifore Cemetery Assn., 1940 A.S. 

1451, 1452, 1453). 

Appeals from the Probate Court under c. 215, secs. 9-12, are considered in the same

manner as appeals in equity (Old Colony 

Trust Co. v. Yonge, 302 Mass. 49; School

Committee of Winchendon v. Selectmen of 

Winchendon, 300 Mass. 266).

Appeals under the Workmen's Compen sation Act are treated as equitable rather than legal in nature, procedure and final disposition (Gould's Case, 215 Mass. 480, 483; Liberty Mutual Ins. Co., Petr., 298 

Mass. 75).

Sittings of the Full Court 

Sittings of the Full Court for hearing cases on oral argument are customarily held as follows (c. 211, sec. 13; Rule 12):

At PITTSFIELD (for Berkshire) on the third Tuesday of September;

At GREENFIELD (for Franklin and Hamp shire) on the Wednesday after the third 

Tuesday of September in every odd year;

At NORTHAMPTON (for Franklin and 

Hampshire) on the Wednesday after the third Tuesday of September in every even year;

At SPRINGFIELD (for Hampden) on the 

Thursday after the third Tuesday of September;

At WORCESTER (for Worcester) on the first Monday after the third Tuesday of September;

At TAUNTON (for Bristol, Dukes County and Nantucket) on the fourth Monday of 

October (c. 211, sec. 19, as am. by St. 1938, c. 115, sec. 1);

If no case has been set down for oral argument at least two weeks before the day determined for any of the above sittings, the sitting may be omitted, and if only one case has been set down for oral argument, it may be transferred to any other of said sittings which may be most accessible or convenient for the parties, or to a sitting for the Commonwealth if the parties so agree (c. 211, sec. 13; Rule 12).

At BOSTON (for Suffolk, Middlesex, Nor folk, Essex, Plymouth and Barnstable in civil matters and for any county in criminal matters) beginning on the first Mondays of October, November, December, January, February, March and April and on the first or second Monday of May (see c. 211, sec. 12).

The October sitting at Boston lasts but one week (five days). The other sittings at

Boston are not longer than two weeks each (shorter at any sitting at which the list is exhausted). Cases that are not reached at any sitting are carried over and put upon the list for the next sitting. 

The Court does not sit on Saturdays. 

The Court does not sit on the Tuesday after the first Monday of November in even-numbered years (state election day). 

The clerk's office is open on that day, however.

Consultations 

Ordinarily a consultation is held at Boston in the second week of September and in the last week of each month to and including June.

Any cases that are submitted on briefs without oral argument after adjournment of any sitting are generally taken by the Court for consideration at the next consultation. These may be cases arising in any county (c. 211, sec. 14; Rule 3).

Whenever the justices before whom a law question has been heard so desire, others of the justices may be called in to take part in the decision, upon a perusal of the record and briefs, without formal re-argument; unless one of the parties makes objection at the argument (Rule 4; see also Rule 10, supra, p. 3; Wall v. Old Colony Tr. Co., 177 Mass. 275, 277, 278). 

Petitions for Admission to the Bar

Petitions for admission to the bar may be filed in the Supreme Judicial Court or in the Superior Court. Admission in either permits practice in all the courts of the 

Commonwealth (c. 221, sec. 39).

Clerks of the Supreme Judicial Court The Supreme Judicial Court for the 

Commonwealth has its own clerk in the 

Court House at Boston (c. 221, secs. 1, 2, 15). 

There is a clerk for the Supreme Judicial

Court for the county of Suffolk (single justice) in the Court House at Boston (c. 221, secs. 3, 15).

In all of the counties except Suffolk the clerk of the courts is clerk of the Supreme

Judicial Court, of the Superior Court and of the county commissioners (c. 221, sec. 3). 

Transfer of Cases 

The Full Court sitting in any county, or for the Commonwealth, has jurisdiction of all questions of law and of all cases and matters at law or in equity, civil or criminal, which arise in any county other than that for which it is sitting, and which might properly be heard and determined by it sitting for such county; and, upon the application of a party, the Full Court may order any such questions of law, or case or matter, to be entered and determined by the Full Court sitting in any county, or for the 

Commonwealth (c. 211, sec. 14). 

Application for such transfer must be made before the Full Court. Technically, the transfer is made by the Full Court, but in practice the Chief Justice may permit it and afterward ask consent of the other justices. It is usual to inquire whether the other side has any objection. 

Customarily entry is made in the county where the trial was had and the case receives a number on the Full Court docket for that county; it is then transferred to 

Boston, where it receives a new number. It

is immaterial whether the application to the Full Court for transfer to Boston is made before or after entry (Com. v. Robertson, 162 Mass. 90, 95).

Questions of law arising in criminal cases in any county and questions of law arising in civil cases in the counties of Suffolk, Middlesex, Norfolk, Essex, Plymouth and Barnstable are ordinarily entered and determined in the Supreme Judicial Court for the Commonwealth at Boston (c. 211, sec. 12).

By consent of the parties filed in the case, such questions arising in civil cases in other counties, and such questions for which no other provision is made, may also be entered and determined at Boston (c. 211, sec. 12).

If the justice before whom an action or proceeding is tried in the Supreme Judicial Court or the Superior Court in counties other than Suffolk, Middlesex, Essex, Norfolk, Plymouth or Barnstable considers that a question of law which arises for the determination of the Full Court is immaterial, frivolous, or intended merely for delay, or that the interests of the parties or of the public require a more speedy determination thereof than can be reached in the sitting established for the county in which the action or proceeding is tried, he so certifies and orders the question of law to be entered and heard at the sitting of the Court for the Commonwealth; and all the other proceedings are the same as if the question had arisen in one of the abovenamed counties (c. 211, sec. 16).

It should be noted that the above section applies to cases from the Supreme Judicial Court (single justice) or from the Superior Court and not to cases from any other court. 

A case so transferred to Boston is entered

immediately on the docket. Consent of counsel is not required.

Unless special cause is shown, cases transferred under c. 211, sec. 16, are not heard in oral argument until all cases ready for hearing, which originated in the counties for which the Court for the Commonwealth sits, have been heard (Rule 7). 

Cases may be submitted on briefs (without oral argument) at any time, without regard to the county in which they are pending and without regard to the place where the Full Court is sitting (Rule 3). 

If the Full Court is not sitting in the county where the case is pending, when it is submitted without oral argument, the briefs and the copies of the record are transmitted by the clerk of the court where the case is pending to the clerk of the Supreme Judicial Court for the Commonwealth (Rule 3). 

Hearings in Certain Criminal Cases 

The exceptions alleged at the trial of a criminal case in any county, not subject to the provisions of secs. 33A to 33G, inclusive, of c. 278 in accordance with said provisions or not made subject thereto by order of a justice of the Superior Court in accordance with sec. 31 of said chapter, may be entered and determined either at the law sitting of the Supreme Judicial Court held for the county in which they arise, or, upon the order of the justice presiding at the trial, at the sitting of the Court for the Commonwealth including an adjourned sitting (c. 

211, sec. 15). 

An appeal with assignment of errors, under c. 278, secs. 33A to 33G (applicable to felony cases), is entered at the next sitting of the Full Court after the assignment of errors is filed, either in the Supreme 10

Judicial Court for the county in which the case is pending or in the Supreme Judicial

Court for the Commonwealth, whichever first has a sitting, including an adjourned sitting in the case of the Court for the Commonwealth (c. 278, sec. 33E).

A justice of the Supreme Judicial Court has power to grant further time where the defendant neglects to file an assignment of errors within the time specified (c. 278, secs. 

33D, 33F).

No order extending the time for filing an assignment of errors under c. 278, sec. 33D, can be made by consent or except after notice and hearing, at which the defendant need not be present in person; nor unless motion therefor be filed before the expiration of the period for filing granted by the justice of the Superior Court (Full Court 

Rule 9).

Extension of the Concurrent Jurisdiction of the Superior Court 

By c. 257, acts of 1939, the Superior 

Court now has original jurisdiction, concurrently with the Supreme Judicial Court, of all proceedings relating to habeas corpus, certiorari, quo warranto and informations in the nature of a quo warranto, mandamus (except a writ of mandamus to a court or a judicial officer), and also of all matters relating to the dissolution of corporations, and of all cases and matters of equity of which the Supreme Judicial Court has had original jurisdiction under c. 214, sec. 2, or otherwise, other than cases arising under the statutes relating to insolvency of which general superintendence and jurisdiction are given to it by those statutes, or arising under c. 25, sec. 5 (relating to the Department of Public Utilities), or under c. 58A, 11

sec. (relating to the Appellate Tax 

Board), or under c. 167 (relating to banks and banking), or under acts of 1934, c. 376, sec. 12, paragraph (F), as amended by acts of 1937, c. 428, sec. 5 (relating to the Milk

Control Board). 

Questions of law arising in any proceedings of which jurisdiction is vested in the Superior Court by the foregoing may be reserved and reported to the Supreme Judicial Court for the consideration of the Full Court in the manner provided in c.. 

231, sec. 111, if such proceedings are at law and in the manner provided in c. 214, secs. 

30, 31, if in equity; and c. 214, sec. 32 (governing the transfer of causes in equity between the Supreme Judicial, Superior and Probate Courts), now applies to proceedings within the concurrent jurisdiction provided for above. 12
