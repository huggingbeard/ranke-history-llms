FOREWORD 

It is everywhere apparent that the unnecessary waste of human life is the most terrific fact in man's history. The carelessness and indifference of men to their own life interests and welfare are the cardinal sins that mark the highway along which the foolish and simple-minded pass on and are punished. 

This waste is the most direful of all depressions because it is continuous and involves the whole human family. There is, therefore, no more serious problem confronting nations, society, and the individual for solution than how man can positively and consistently order and direct his life so as to save it and arrive at the greatest good. This is the problem of every new age and of every new deal; of all religion, philosophy, and science. These

There are ideal principles of conduct which sanctify life and add the divine spark to animal man. we should seek to know and understand. Too often, however, there is failure to consider these fundamental principles; neglect or refusal to seek knowledge, direction, and wisdom until, with the passing of the years, we find it difficult to make even a beginning. A few there are who see no aim or meaning in life. It is a 

Big Bad Wolf seeking sacrificial victims. They declare it vanity and not worth the candle, but generally they are anxious to keep the candle burning.

Little by little, however, man is becoming more and more aware of himself and of the divinity within him;

FOREWORD 

that he can give meaning to life if he will; also that he can miss life and lose his soul here and now. We cannot be everything any more than we can have everything, but we can increase our knowledge of life and consider the ways to get the most out of it. Human development is the hope of civilization, the function of spiritual religion and of all human instrumentalities. 

Life is ours, we cannot avoid its obligations and responsibilities, and it is worth living and saving. 

Therefore, we must consider it in all its aspects and relations if we hope for a successful conclusion. There are many adverse agencies, many disturbing factors, which constantly threaten, but there are also many encouraging and inspiring allies to give help and inspiration. These we endeavor to point out and make intelligible and appealing to the mind and heart of the reader; to emphasize the fundamentals and let every reader make his own deductions and application. 

We have endeavored to keep man in mind as a human being with a human mind and body, human impulses and desires, having human interests, tasks, ambitions, difficulties, problems, frailities, and limitations. 

But to attain any depth, power, unity, beauty, or satisfaction of life this human must become conscious of the spiritual gifts within him, seek to know their nature, understand the laws of their cultivation and growth, and observe and practice these in his daily life. 

The principles of action, therefore, in which there are safety, security, hope, vitality, and healing are the subjects stressed, with due attention to the things which consume and destroy. 

Whoever or whatever we are; whatever our birth, positions, abilities, capacities, problems, environment, or opportunities we all need to perceive the good in

This is the purpose of this book.

Getting the Most Out of Life CHAPTER I  

"We never think of the main business of life till a vain repentance reminds us of it at the wrong end." -L'Estrange.

The man who is rich in life is the fortunate person of great possessions, for he has laid up treasures that cannot be taken away from him. Nevertheless, in spite of the teaching of history and experience, what small value generally is placed upon life itself. How very little serious personal attention is given to know, understand, nourish, cultivate, strengthen, enrich, protect and preserve our human lives to make them seem worth while. In various ways the whole human family is seeking satisfactions of one kind or another; but is it not tragic that so many have so little appreciation of life, and come so far short of living at all? What can be done about it?

Experience teaches that there is much that the individual can do and ought to do and much that he had better not do if he has any desire to discover life, and these things are well worth thinking about. There is a beauty in life and there is a beauty of life, and one should aim at both.

Because of its difficulties, adversities, and mysteries, 13

many seem more afraid of life than of death. Life is the Great Dread, the Great Unknown, which had better be let alone and not examined too closely. So there is little desire to think about it, look it in the face, estimate its value, or as far as possible divine its purpose. There is not even willingness to trace the growth and results of life in the experience of others. 

This fear to consider life accounts for much of human failure. We need a proper and intelligent appreciation of our own nature and powers, but we also need to evaluate life itself and the things of life in order not to spend our strength and energy upon unworthy and disappointing illusions.

If there were less vain speculation, less morbid introspection, less striving for the vanity of self-righteousness, less nourishing of self-conceit in day-dreaming and a more sincere desire to appreciate and understand life itself and our relation to it, we would display more intelligence and avoid many hidden dangers.

LIFE MAN'S GREAT POSSESSION 

Courageous man has always considered life his most precious possession. Whatever other possessions may seem good and desirable, life itself is the summum bonum-the supreme good. This is why we speak of our war dead as having made "the supreme sacrifice."

Other losses may be regained, but not the loss of life. Man's acts may generally contradict his belief in the importance of life, still he clings to it with great tenacity and in the face of great difficulties, and persistently pursues his way.

Think of how mankind has persisted and held on to

life through the ages. Nothing, however adverse, has been able to break man's love, faith, hope, courage, his energy, or ambition. He has survived ice periods, famines and plagues without number, cruel barbaric invasions, and even more cruel massacres and persecutions by religious fanatics and ungodly civil authorities; he has endured innumerable industrial revolutions with their hardships and privations; suffered the horrors of slavery and all kinds of economic and social injustice. Countless wars have left him despoiled and apparently ruined. Still he goes on. Why? Because nothing can break his hold upon life. There is a divine urge within him to fight, to continue, to know, and to conquer.

Life is a divine Principle in the mind of man; it is the will to power, the volition to overcome and triumph over difficulties, over defeat and over self. Life is something to be fathomed, understood, controlled, and enjoyed. Life is good, but the only way to know it good and to keep it good is to use it sanely and intelligently.

When and where life originated is not so very important to know in our struggle to survive. No one knows precisely what life is, where or when it began, but we all know that it is, that it is ours to have and to use, and that law governs it. We are not obliged "to figure it all out"-it can't be done-we must take it like men or leave it like cowards. It is good and interesting enough to take on its own terms. We should, therefore, endeavor to gain some broad and adequate idea of life itself, of our relationship to it, and how to make the most of it if we would build any reality into our human existence and daily work.

LIFE HAS SPIRITUAL PURPOSE 

Through all the ages the true life of man is seen not simply as a physical animal urge to exist, although of course that is always present, but as desire and effort of the wisest and best of the race to obtain lasting life results and to express the spirit within them in creative spiritual forms and ideas of excellence and beauty, and in works of usefulness and beneficence. 

This great fact is expressed by the poet John Keats when he wrote, "I would sooner fail than not to be among the greatest." All history bears testimony to human accomplishment as the work of the spiritual mind, which has given us what civilization we have and has greatly enriched all life. So while, as in the case of Keats, the physical life may be weak and unsteady, the spiritual creation goes on in the life of mankind. Great or small, gifted or otherwise, every individual is a part of universal spiritual life and he should seek to make his part worthy of the whole. 

The coral islands of the Pacific are the result of the faithful labor of countless millions of little creatures, no one of which, we are told, ever built so much as a hundredth part of a cubic inch, but each worked faithfully and added its small contribution to the splendid result. The discoveries of the scientist not only add new facts and principles to knowledge, but give new impetus and spiritual direction to the work and thought of mankind.

Often has it been true that the significance of a man's life work has not become manifest until long years after his death and has then borne immortal fruit. The Augustinian Monk Gregor Johann Mendel devoted many years of his life to the problem of

heredity to discover its principles. His experiments were original and revolutionary, but it was not until many years after his death that his work was recognized as applying to the whole world of living things. 

Mendel still lives and will continue to live. It is worth remembering that while the physical man dies, his spiritual work, whatever its nature or in whatever form expressed, goes on in reproducing power. Beyond the material and in the material the spiritual mind of man is ever working out the problems, desires, and conceptions of his inmost soul. Life encourages thought, thought leads to imagination, examination, and criticism, and these lead to initiative and creative power. The spirit of life is immortal. Life, therefore, becomes reasonable and desirable because it is so infinitely important and holds in its lap such splendid possibilities within the reach of all. 

THE DESIRE FOR LIFE 

Life has ever been the theme universal and a challenge to mankind. The prayer of the psalmist— "Show me the path of life"-has been the unvarying petition of thoughtful men and women of the whole human family. The cry of the patriot-"Give me liberty or give me death"-is the voice of the soul of man saying that without liberty life is unendurable.

All such expressions (and they are numberless) are indications of man's universal sense of the importance. and reality of life and of his own soul. Just in proportion as we realize the significance of life itself do we become interested in and aware of the nature and reality of our own existence-not simply as machines,

but as human beings with spiritual power to live and to give meaning to life.

PROGRESS AND LIFE 

As we study the history, literature, philosophy, and science of the world we see that the struggle, work, and aspiration of the thinking men of our race have been for life and for more life here and now. Not always, it is true, has the pursuit been in the highest or best form, but in whatever form there has been the desire for an increasing life-material, mental, and spiritual. The mind of the multitude has been slow to move but it has advanced and is advancing. We can never have civilization until man's whole life is controlled by and subject to the spirit of life. Great progress has been made in many directions, material comforts were never so abundant or widespread, and the world has become in many ways a more desirable place to live in than formerly. But all the knowledge, science, and inventions of man have not yet made the face of humanity to shine with heavenly wisdom or given men great understanding of their own high life interests. However, underlying all the diverse and varied "plans" to increase human welfare and happiness there is an increasing realization that the individual is capable of and entitled to a "more abundant life" than he now has and that human society must be reorganized to make it possible for men not only to get a living but to live. As Professor Einstein recently said of science: "It is paradoxical, but apparently true, that the very instruments of science, instead of being devoted

to help the common man to a greater understanding, in reality are doing just the opposite, and only succeeding in befuddling things."

What is true of science is true of knowledge in general. In our present haphazard world this may be to some extent unavoidable, but man must learn to properly value and use his knowledge to promote human values-in other words, he must become intelligent regarding his own life interests, and use the discoveries. of science to advance those interests. To do this he must obtain a higher and better appreciation of life and a truer knowledge of the purpose of the instruments of life. The tragedy of life is the defeat of progress.

In our economic bungling, devilish oppressions, lack of insight and humanity, inefficiency, lawlessness, injustice, foolish and shackling laws of tyranny and bigotry, hymns and prayers of hate and hypocrisy, supreme and shameless selfishness and robbery of the poor, racial and religious pride and complacency, debauched ethics and crucified ideals, cruel and senseless wars, the human spirit is depressed with fear, the mind is confused, and doubt takes the place of faith. It is here that we need the knowledge of the basic issues and appreciation of the fundamentals of life, the clearsighted view of the simplicity of living, and see that in spite of all the evil that man can do—and he can do plenty-the laws, principles, and ways of life are good and desirable, that we can discover them and obey them in independency and freedom and live as human beings should live. The battle of the present is not for dogmas, forms, theories, or institutions, but for life, truth, justice, righteousness, honesty, decency, and

manhood; not for shadows but for realities. Man is more truly estimating the worth of life and his own. spirit, and demanding that all interests and institutions, inventions and discoveries, laws and governments of whatever kind serve the purposes of his existence in truth and reality. 

LIFE FAILURES 

Life failures are by no means confined to the poor and ignorant, but are among all classes and conditions of men. LaRochefoucauld once said, "Few know how to grow old." Human history shows that still fewer know how to grow wise. Now, it is not enough to say, "Get wisdom," we need to know what wisdom is, where it lives, how to get it, and how excellent it is. "Wisdom is that which makes men judge what are the best ends, and what the best means to obtain them, and gives a man advantage of counsel and direction," said Sir William Temple. Wisdom is the power to keep a man from making a fool of himself. 

The maxim of the Great Teacher-"A man's life consists not in the abundance of the things he possesses" has not yet found lodgment or realization in the thought and life of the world. Schopenhauer, despite his pessimism, confirmed the truth of Jesus' declaration when he wrote: "Men are a thousand times more intent on becoming rich than on acquiring culture, though it is quite certain that what a man is contributes more to his happiness than what he has." 

Culture is an inclusive word, but much more than "refinement of the mind" is involved in making a man what he ought to be.

SOME BIG QUESTIONS 

What then is life? Has it any meaning, aim, or purpose for the individual, and, if so, what? Is the science of life an enigma beyond the interpretation of the average intelligence? Why is life not more generally productive of good? Is it a merciless machine over which the individual has little or no control? 

Has it any significance apart from material interests? 

What relation does it have to work? Does it demand submission to law and to what laws? Does it involve personal responsibility? Is the individual important, and why and how? Why is it that so much of human life is meaningless, futile, unsatisfactory, fruitless? If life does not consist in the possession of "things," then in what does it consist?

These are big but not meaningless questions, and the answers (as far as they can be answered) are of supreme importance to every soul who desires to become intelligent, useful, efficient, self-reliant, and happy.

They are also serious and practical questions and must be approached with an open and understanding mind and reverent and teachable spirit if we expect to decipher the life needs of our own souls. 

FAILURE TO REALIZE 

Scores of men tell us that they have obtained little satisfaction from life, and we only need to look around us to realize that this is only too true. They realize, too late, that they have lived to no purpose and the years have been a great waste. They say, "If I had my life to live over again I would live it differently."

This simply voices their disappointment and sense of

defeat. As has been truly said, if a man's life were different it would not be the same life as well as not the same kind of a life. We are what we are, and that, to some extent at any rate, of our own choice. 

The fact is, few ever try to get any intelligent understanding of life, what it means to them or what they can get out of it. They never ask themselves why they exist at all, what results their course of life will bring, what duty they owe to themselves and others, where they are going or in what direction. They do not concern themselves about the nature of the social order in which they find themselves, or their relation to it. 

They stagger along from youth to old age without any guiding star or definite purpose-wanderers of the night simply trying to "muddle through." Professor 

Walter Pitkin, of Columbia University, after extensive researches, states that only about one in a hundred has any definite life ambition. 

If a man should undertake the building of a skyscraper without thorough knowledge and understanding of engineering problems, the nature and value of building materials, the value of time and labor, the ultimate usefulness of the completed structure, you would say he was mentally unbalanced. What then shall we say of one who expects to build himself into a life of character and usefulness but who never considers life-its nature, problems, relations, requirements, duties, and the instruments and laws of success. 

Is it a reasonable way to build?

No wonder, therefore, that, comparatively, so few find life good, fruitful, joyous, and satisfactory. They have refused or neglected to enter in and faithfully use this greatest of possessions, and too late wake up and behold the picture of a fruitless, withered fig tree.

We teach the nature and value of mind, body, education, social relations, thrift, and the moral virtues, but give little attention to teaching the youth the nature and value of life itself. We should begin at the beginning.

Intelligence and skill are factors, but not the only factors. There must be a right attitude toward life, high ambition, a proper sense of the importance of man himself to back up whatever intelligence and skill we have. Therefore, we need character-training as well as training of the intellect, for intellect without character is deceptive and destructive.

In the following chapters we discuss and emphasize some fundamental factors necessary and possible to a satisfactory human life. The ideas discussed are not new-the needs of man's nature and life do not change overnight, but remain the same yesterday, today, and forever. There is a golden thread of fundamental law, acknowledged and accepted by all right thinking persons, running through all life. This fundamental law is for all men and cannot be ignored without disaster. Only those who discover it and observe it need hope for fullness of life.

CHAPTER II THE RIGHT TO LIFE 

"Trust thyself; every heart vibrates to that iron string." "We but half express ourselves, and are ashamed of that divine idea which each represents." "My life is not an apology, but a life. It is for itself and not for a spectacle.' "I cannot consent to pay for a privilege where I have intrinsic right."-Emerson. 

The doctrine that man has certain inalienable rights inherent in his own nature antecedent to every government and which all states and governments are bound to respect and secure, has been generally acknowledged, but damnably trampled upon by all governments, ancient and modern. 

The right to life gives (or should give) to man all those fundamental rights necessary to pursue, preserve, and maintain his own independent existence; rights which minister to his personal welfare, liberty, progress, comfort, and happiness, but do not interfere with or destroy the rights of others. These are socalled natural rights and must not be confounded with civil rights which men enter into and enjoy as members of society. Among these natural rights, as stated by The Declaration are "life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness," without which possessions life becomes meaningless slavery. 24

AN INDIVIDUAL DUTY 

Now the right of the individual to life and its benefits implies and imposes a duty to live worthy of life, and to add his contribution, large or small, to the common good. We show our recognition of the right to live by faithful living. 

One of the chief drawbacks to the average man's success is his personal disbelief in the potential possibilities of his own mind. Without a conscious belief in himself he never has the courage to even make a start. There is no moral or legal obligation for anyone to attempt the impossible, however desirable it may seem, but that is no excuse for thinking our natural powers are not capable and worthy of great and fine development.

Despite innumerable encouraging examples of men rising from the ranks, we are too apt to think all men competent and capable but ourselves, refuse to sincerely enter life's race and put our energies and powers to the test. Belief in one's self is the first step to success; lack of faith here is the supreme disbelief.

There are three things which every man wishing to achieve must believe thoroughly: (1) That there are real attainments worth striving for; (2) That they are, to some extent at least, within his possibility of achievement; (3) That personal equipment is necessary to win. Unless these are fixed principles of his soul the right to life will have no meaning. 

NOT GENIUS BUT DESIRE 

There is a multitude of men; mighty few great geniuses, but probably enough. These are the wonder-

men of all other men-great immortal spirits of human life and history. They are comparatively few, but without them human life would be poor and barren. The world needs them. They live that the race should more truly live. Great men clear our minds of egotism. They are good for the spirit, the encouragement to endeavor. They show us possibilities. Their modesty is reassuring. Their superiority is inciting. 

Their failures, limitations, mistakes, selfishness, lack of wisdom, and inferiority are also cheering. They belong to common humanity. They are more generously endowed and higher in attainment than their fellows, but not essentially different. 

A careful study of even this small group reveals very clearly that something more than the "gift of genius" is required to get the most out of life. The possession of genius or special talent is no guarantee that the man is not both ignorant and foolish outside the province of mentality. The great genius is often "strangely queer" and manifests his common brotherhood by his irrational, unreasonable, and unhappy way of life. This is not because of his genius, but because he is deficient in mental and moral balance and in the wisdom of life. Apart from his genius he is an ordinary human being with human faults and weaknesses, and needs adjustment. Absence of steadfast and righteous principles is as ruinous to the great man as to anyone else, and to attempt to imitate the great is weakness and folly. Don't think for a moment that the right to life is a right to make a fool of yourself. 

A recent writer declares that the Greeks were "just people." That's all that humanity is or ever has been anywhere-the great and the small belonging to the family of man and all needing instruction and disci-

Too often the common man is given to worshiping genius or talent as something different from anything existing in himself, while as a matter of fact every man has talent if he would only take time to develop it according to his position, tastes, sympathy, capacity, and opportunity. The missing link is desire. Men lack efficiency, character, and personality because they lack development. It is easier to worship than to work. If the common man would seek the development of his own powers he would find far more satisfaction in giving self-expression, even in a small way, to the talent he has, than by losing himself in adoration of the more especially gifted. He may not be a genius but he can be a man. The fact is genius or talent is not so much a "gift" as a discovery. The individual becomes aware of himself-his inner, hidden powers and earnestly seeks to express what is within him. The man of genius should be an encouragement to every soul of man, for he does that very thing.

Omitting the immortals, a wide and candid look at the general life of mankind is not very impressive or inspiring. An encouraging number rise superior, but they are far less numerous than they ought to be or might be. The way is narrow, but it is never closed to those with desire. The terrific fact is that in a world of splendid opportunity and attraction multitudes are content with a lazy, wasteful, vapid, ignorant, and senseless existence; prefer the association and habits of the common; are satisfied with a machine-like existence and do not wish to be disturbed. As Cicero long ago declared, "What perversity is this in man-

99 kind that when fruits are to be found they prefer to live on acorns.' Puffed up with their own narrow conceits they hate instruction and mock at the superiority of their fellows. The right to life is theirs, but they acknowledge no duty or obligation to fit themselves to live or add to the worth of the social life around them. No man, common or otherwise, finds life worth living unless he makes it so. 

To give any meaning to life we must first find it, possess it, pursue and use it. Every right implies a duty. 

THE DETERMINATION TO LIVE 

Desire is good, but it must be backed up and energized by determination and will. Wistful wishing is the insanity of fools. This simply means that we are not willing to play the game. We are defaulters and cowards. We expect success, power, fullness of life without giving any measure of mind or heart to their accomplishment, thinking to defeat the laws of life and get something for nothing. Without individual energy and self-reliance born of determination, will, and insight which produce courage, the right to life means nothing at all. We belong to the weak, the negligent, the inefficient, and remain all our lives victims of childish dreams, never reaching independence or the ability to rule or care for ourselves or to become efficient.

NO NEED FOR DISCOURAGEMENT 

The slow growth of civilized ideas and the national and social sluggishness to perceive and serve the general welfare need not discourage any individual in the

The intolerance of race, religion, wealth and power has wrought great havoc through the ages and is still tremendously active in many ways, but it is not quite so violent, savage, vindictive, or widely expressed in its manifestations as formerly and has become highly offensive to the moral sense of mankind. We must remember, however, that the human animal is a descendant of wolfish ancestors, and while the present system is wofully defective, it cannot be destroyed over night and replaced by any other promising immediate economic security and justice. This is not the best of all possible worlds, but whatever it is every person must do his best in it for himself and for others, and live his life in all good conscience up to the measure of his light, knowledge, and ability. 

The great encouragement is that thousands of men and women, amid adverse and difficult situations such as beset the general life, have successfully fought the good fight of faith, greeted "the unseen with a cheer," and made all life glorious by the victories of their courage and determination.

Professor Einstein's tribute to Mme. Curie at a testimonial meeting in 1935 is as true as it is significant. What is true of this eminent woman must become true in every life that desires to live. Life is a climb

Anything -if we are going in the right direction. that helps us mount the ladder is good; anything that hinders is bad. As the Scottish Bruce received inspiration and courage from the redoubtable spider, so as we perceive men and women starting at scratch and achieving poise, personality, serenity of life, and the power of accomplishment, we see the star of faith and hope shining for every one of us. 

Do not procrastinate your life-live today.

Before we are properly in the way to get the most out of life it is essential to get rid of some foolish popular notions that are deadly in their restraining influence and self-suppression. Such ideas as that the cards are stacked against us and there is no use trying; that results are not dependent upon our personal attitude to life but lie outside of ourselves; that good fruit can be gathered from an unnourished life; that we can successfully outwit life by ignoring its laws and demands; that we can get without giving; that success is the outcome of fortunate accident or lucky circumstance, et cetera. 31

ACHIEVEMENT NOT A MATTER OF LUCK 

All history and experience show that real human achievement-whether in material things or in things of more importance-depends largely upon "the will to conquer" and the ability to support the efforts of the will. If wishes were horses everybody (including beggars) would ride, but life is not like that. This is a world of hard fact, and one of the chief lessons that mortals need to learn is that success in life, especially in its higher aspects, is not the result of accident, magic, or special miracle. 

True, what we call happy accident, good luck, or bad luck, as you will, has sometimes seemed to play a vast part in human affairs and in individual life. Many great events and the careers of many great men furnish instances where so-called luck has seemed to "make mock of calculation." However, no great character or accomplishment is, by any stretch of the imagination, the pure result of chance or luck. To think so is to fool ourselves, and too many people like to be fooled, desire to shift their responsibilities, and to cry "luck" for their own failures and for the success of others. The lucky man is generally in a position where luck can strike him and has prepared himself to take advantage of the "lucky" opportunity.

Ordinary mortals seeking real accomplishment may well leave the discussion of luck to visionaries, speculators, gypsies, magicians, and fortune-tellers. It is interesting, but not very important, and deserves little consideration in the thought of serious people. Luck seldom occurs to reward the ignorant, the thoughtless, or the followers of Micawber's philosophy, and when it does the result is generally temporary and of little

LIFE, LUCK AND LABOR 

33 account. As Pasteur declared, "Luck comes only to the mind that is prepared." Real success flows not from any utopian optimism, but from the adoption and application of right habits and methods, choosing correct standards, laying substantial foundations, faithful and patient work, and acquiring the mental insight to evaluate those things of life that concern our progress and well-being. Dreams of success sometimes come true, but seldom if ever unless the dreamer is alive and awake, and not simply a dreaming fool. Notwithstanding the fact that experience has shown the God of Luck to be a very unsafe, false, and deceiving guide to believe in, trust or follow, thousands still worship at his altar and sacrifice life to this fetish-the spirit of incarnate delusion.

Thousands of unfortunate men and women in America today realize what following the philosophy of luck can do to them. For many years they turned the lip of scorn at thrift and the devoted independent spirit and are now reaping the bitterness of want and dependence. Luck is a grinning, mocking devil of deception. Cast him out.

EFFORT AND ENERGY NECESSARY 

No man is in the way of good who puts such a low value upon life as to think that human achievement in any form or degree is the result of accident. Life holds in its lap infinite possibilities for every one of us, but it is only the seeker that finds out what they are. It is not what luck or accident do for us, but what we do for ourselves that counts. Neither life nor the government owes us a living-high or low. 

We must work for it. We had no choice about coming

into life, but being here we must either become producers or parasites. Society and human organization should and must afford every individual the opportunity to live well and adequately, but that is another story. We owe life something. God has created us, but he has never obligated himself to provide for us in order that we may live the easy life of indolence; ignore His laws in personal ignorance or obstinancy and expect help from above. Praying to get something for nothing is as immoral as it is foolish. "Give us this day our daily bread" does not imply divine provision in answer to our prayers without human effort. 

If "any will not work neither should he eat." Work and sustenance are the divine order as well as the order of common sense. It is equally true that "man does not live by bread alone"-he does not come into possession of qualities that satisfy the longings of his heart or the higher aspirations of his being by chance or accident, but by his own sincere endeavor intelligently directed. The truth that there is no royal road to desirable attainment without industry and patient and devoted striving seems difficult for men to understand. They prefer to trust to luck and become the "lambs," the "suckers," and the "easy marks" of a laughing world.

In other words, from Adam to Einstein experience teaches that the high results of life must be personally achieved. This has always been true and will always be true. Every individual must resolve his latent power into purposeful energy. Mere existence without effort is a poor thing, a sad and shameful thing, and we see its victims on every hand. Without earnest purpose and intelligent intent any real achievement in the art of living is impossible. "To overcome diffi-

LIFE, LUCK AND LABOR 

35 culties is to experience the full delight of existence," wrote Schopenhauer. To know how to live is the supreme_purpose of human beings, say the philosophers. True enough; but the steps to success must be carefully scrutinized and thoroughly learned and are not realized by accident or chance, but by sincere desire, plus clear-sighted understanding and persistent purpose. 

Said Theodore Roosevelt: "I never won anything without hard labor and the exercise of my best judgment and careful planning, working long in advance. 

The average man who is successful is not a genius. He is a man who has merely the ordinary qualities that he shares with his fellows, but who has developed those ordinary qualities to more than an ordinary degree." "Those who labor in the earth are the chosen people of God," declared Thomas Jefferson, and he stated an eternal truth.

TRUSTING LUCK A DELUSION 

In a life-long association with men and women of great attainment in many fields of endeavor I have taken the opportunity to ask many of them, “What in life has brought you great personal satisfaction?"

While the replies varied greatly, they were always interesting and suggestive. A Catholic clergyman said: "The love of my people"; a Protestant minister: "The culture of my mind"; a scientist: "Adding a little to the knowledge of life"; a business man: "A useful life and affording others the opportunity to live"; the president of a woman's college: "My own character, such as it is"; a teacher: "That I have met the crises.

of life and have not been discouraged." Not luck but personal achievement.

Whether we are favored or not by birth, education, social position, or encouraging circumstance, human experience emphasizes the fact that life's victories come not to shirkers but to workers; not to lucktrusters but to men and women of personal devotion, supreme purpose, and faithful endeavor. 

Disraeli once said, "You can have anything you want if you only want it badly enough." Of course, he did not mean that literally-that one could become a Shakespeare by a supreme effort. He was indicating a general principle-which his own life verified that the attainment of any worthy object within the possibility of a person's ability can be realized only when all the powers of body, mind, and soul are converged and determined upon it. There is an inspiration in the conscious belief in one's own powers and abilities which nothing on earth can take the place of. It is this belief which casts out fear and stimulates courage. 

There is joy and satisfaction in the rendering of useful and fruitful service, in the pursuit of high ideals, the achievement of character, getting and retaining the respect of men, identification with life in a broad and sincere sympathy, but these are not attained by accident. There is little satisfaction to be derived from luck even when it strikes us. 

That some very intelligent men do not win "success" proves nothing against intelligence; that some men achieve material success without the exercise of much intelligence or personal industry proves nothing in favor of ignorance, laziness, or luck. The world is as it is and it is easy to pick out apparent contradictions. 

The dollar yardstick of measurement is too often the

No matter what our sphere, work, or hopes, to cherish the delusion, as many do, that the golden fruit of life can be gathered by trusting to the God of Luck is to kill the spirit's ambition and run counter to all the experience of life. It is to die at the source, to remain all our lives children of expectation, never able to enter the realm of intelligent realization. Saint 

Paul's instruction to the Ephesians, "Walk not as fools but as wise," is an excellent precept to keep in remembrance by all men, for cherishing the notion of Luck is the snare of the devil.

CHAPTER IV MAN AND HIS PROBLEMS 

Problems of life there are aplenty-big ones and little ones; some we inherit, many others we create ourselves, while others are made for us by politicians, lawyers, reformers, theologians, philosophers, promoters, and bandits; some are easy of solution and some prove mighty "knotty." But whatever they are, or whence they arrive, we are obliged to think about them, for thought is necessary to understanding and preliminary to action.

MAN HIMSELF THE PROBLEM No 

If "the proper study of mankind is man" it is because man himself is a universe presenting problems which must be studied and solved in the interest and for the salvation of the whole life of mankind. man lives to himself, he is a member of an existing social order and because of this his individual life and problems are significant and important, not only to himself but to society. Supplying the physical needs of man has always been the white man's burden. As the Hebrew writer long ago declared: "All the labor of man is for his mouth, and yet his appetite is not filled." It is not a problem to be despised, but to be solved. To raise the standard of living, to furnish opportunity for employment, to create better ideals of 38

MAN AND HIS PROBLEMS 

39 social justice, to provide ways and means for a more reasonable human life are great problems of the state, government, and society, but even here man is the center and crux of the whole business. However, in this book we are not directly concerned with general problems, but with the problem of the individual life -how can it be lived, developed, and maintained in freedom, strength, and power; what are the principles, sources, methods, laws, which must be learned and followed? We are men and women of the present, and must live our lives in the world as it is and not as it ought to be or may be.

HOPEFUL SIGNS 

Underlying great national, governmental and social movements in the world today is human betterment. 

The Communist, Fascist, Hitlerite, Socialist, Democrat, all profess this is their aim, no matter what their methods. But human betterment never has been and never can be achieved by crushing the individual or making him a cog in a great governmental or economic machine. Sooner or later he will rebel and crush the machine, for the vital problems of his life are individual, and without freedom of life he cannot live.

Men are thinking, will continue to think about life and its problems, and time is on the side of the thinkers. 

Whatever helpful things governments may do, the individual still remains an individual and must work out his own salvation and be permitted to do so. 

AN OLD PROBLEM 

The direction of the individual life to desirable ends is a problem as old as humanity. It has been the uni-

versal theme of philosophers, scientists, educators, poets, statesmen, prophets, and religious teachers since the world began, and is today the widest and deepest subject of human concern. But no one can solve the problem for another. It is an individual obligation and duty which cannot be shifted. To gain possession of one's own soul is the business of life as well as its problem. If a man will seek first the kingdom of manhood, the kingdom of heaven will be found near at hand and other things will generally be obtained, for seeking this kingdom need not interfere with sane and reasonable material progress. 

Now let us summarize briefly what is involved in this old individual problem of life. We answer:

Recognition of the reality, value, and importance of our present human life. The hope of a future life is locked up in the present.

Securing an intelligent idea of what life offers, the real things that conduce to our stability, peace, satisfaction, and happiness. All things are not equally important and many things are unimportant. 

Some understanding of the laws governing success, and our personal relation to life and the ways of life. 

Cognizance of our personal responsibility for results.

Living for some purpose that justifies our existence on the earth and finding out what that purpose is. 

Development of individual character to secure a higher mode of reality and personal independence. 

Building on a foundation of sincerity, truth, morality, and reality.

Obtaining freedom from prepossessions, superstitious notions, bigoted and unworthy prejudices that

MAN AND HIS PROBLEMS 

41 enslave, and securing real liberty and breadth of mind in thought and action.

The capacity for enthusiasm and devotion that comes from a conscious belief that life is worth living, influencing one to practice and not to shirk. 

Doing right without regard to consequences.

A sense of duty, honor, loyalty, justice that gives more fear of shame than of death.

Training mind and conscience to evaluate life and the things of life-to know the true from the false, the real from the superficial, the good from the bad, the high from the low, the fine from the coarse, the spiritual from the material. 

To become alive to the world we live in its nature, its history, philosophy, work, and problems-and as far as in us lies to learn to appreciate its wealth and beauty.

A philosophy of common sense teaching us to know ourselves the powers of our minds and bodies and their relationships.

It is manifest, therefore, that we do not have to think very hard to realize that life is a real problem -one that has to be solved individually as we move along, and which the longest human life can never completely decipher. There are many difficulties, many contrary winds to be breasted, many obstacles to overcome, many defeats. But these are what give flavor to life and make it interesting and worth while. 

FITNESS TO Live 

Every normal person has, to some degree, a desire to succeed, even though he has little ambition or intention. No one wishes to be or intends to be a failure.

Ideas of what constitutes success may sometimes be very crude, lamentably narrow and conceited, very material and self-centered, and wrongly based. Nevertheless, as far back as we have any trace of mankind. we observe some desire to know and to advance, to improve his condition relatively and absolutely. The principles and laws governing the upward climb are very simply and readily understood and apply to all men without reference to birth, rank, station, or primary advantages. We all begin at scratch, although to get a start some have to scratch harder than others. 

Many declare this is all very well but they say, "Life for the average man is best expressed by 'Verily man at his best state is altogether vanity.' Man is the victim of his own existence. He is perplexed and bewildered, not only about life but about living. The common man has little time, even though he had the desire, to think much about life or how best to use it. 

Nor has he the mentality that might be developed to determine for himself what is or is not important in life. Life is not simply a problem, it is a riddle, an enigma, that no wise man has ever been able to solve for himself or for others, so why should ordinary mortals bother their little minds about anything except making a living and providing for the rainy day." 

This hopeless philosophy has been and is very prevalent among men, and has robbed multitudes of a true prospective of life and destroyed any urge to seek more than the necessities for a mere physical existence. 

It ignores man's real business in life to fit himself to live as an intelligent moral and spiritual being, and degrades all work to a selfish and meaningless slavery. 

Whatever one's situation or calling it is his human duty to carefully consider his life and work it out to

MAN AND HIS PROBLEMS 

43 the greatest possible success and not worry about the plans of Almighty God, who is able to take care of his own plans whatever they are. Were there no difficulties to be encountered man would lack incentive to live or to establish himself as a personality. Difficulties are a challenge to the life-principle within him. 

The solving of difficulties reveals needs as well as capabilities and neither are discovered until one grapples with life in an attempt to learn and to grow. As we climb and conquer we grow, and as we grow victories become not only easier but more pronounced. 

This is the law of Nature and of life. 

The road to glory and to security lies not along the primrose path. The easy way, however alluring, never leads to a productive effort or serviceable benefit. It is the way to defeat and decline-in economics, industry, government, morals and religion. You may kick against Nature's law as "hard," but you will obey it or be ground to powder. Hardness and difficulty are the yardsticks that measure man's character and integrity.

LIFE HAS REWARDS AS WELL AS PROBLEMS 

Life has problems, but it also has rewards. If there were no problems there would be no rewards. There is pleasure and happiness in the doing and the joy of compensation in accomplishment. That is what so many seem to lose sight of. We must sow and fight in order to reap. Unfortunately many see reward in one direction only-the material. All problems are material, all success is measured in terms of dollars and cents. Work is just something for bodily existence or to relieve our "restlessness.' This is the curse

of true living for it blinds us to the real issues of life. 

There are other rewards, however, more real, precious and enduring, which we will discuss in later chapters. 

But whether the problems or the rewards are material, mental, or spiritual we cannot expect fruit without labor, something for nothing. It is permissible for the poet to give "to airy nothing a habitation and a name," but we are not poets nor can we live on poetry. 

The solution of problems of any kind demands energy, strength, courage, perseverance, insight, calmness, and direction, and rewards are the benedictions of success. 

Life is no idle fancy, no fantastic ideal beyond achievement, even in the present topsy-turvey age, but the supreme concern of every individual soul who desires success in its richest and best form and substance. 

To live or not to live that is the real question for every one of us. However, life's problems can be solved only if we have eyes to see, ears to hear, and heart to understand, and to say with Browning, "Life has meaning to find its meaning is my meat and drink.”

When all men see the present life as affording the opportunity to make the most of our natural abilities, an arena to develop every high interest of the common life, an incentive to individual effort, a plan founded in infinite wisdom to bring to fruition all that is best, noblest, and richest in life, thus placing work, growth, peace, and happiness on a firm foundation, then will existence itself become a joy and holy desire, the problems take on new meaning, and the rewards be perceived as worthy of human effort. This will be civilization.

CHAPTER V  

If there is anything self-evident in life it is the fact that growth is the mother of civilization. We grow into life and life grows into us. Nothing was ever made complete or perfect-neither man nor his world -somewhere, sometime they began and through long ages have continued by a process of evolution and growth. Every person is the sum total of his growth and experience. We came into the world with certain life equipment designed for growth. How that equipment is used-whether it is neglected and abused, or cared for and expanded-determines our chances of success or failure as human beings. Some may be "better born," with greater physical and mental capacity, but in any case the life has to be directed into right channels of behavior if it is to attain health, and this means growth.

MAN BECOMES AS HE GROWS 

History is the record of man's unfolding, the story of his growth and development as a human being, advancing from a lower to a higher state; his work and achievements in the various fields of human endeavor. 

All advance he makes in any direction declares the presence, necessity, and vital power of this distinguishing characteristic-growth. Life moves in all direc- 45

tions, ever enlarging, has increasing interests and relationships, and to keep in the running man must grow in all spheres of his life. 

Man becomes man-outwardly and inwardly-because he grows. Everything about him and in himhis strength and effectiveness of body, his knowledge, understanding, intelligence, and skill, his affections and spiritual power-is the result of growth. If his body grows in strength and power he has a mighty effective and serviceable instrument of life. If he grows mentally he becomes acquainted with himself, with the nature and laws of his life, with the principles underlying his welfare and happiness, and with life itself. 

If he does not grow in mind and body then he becomes an impoverished soul without hope and without recourse; strength is lacking, there is confusion and darkness, the vision is clouded, life is paralysis and stagnation. No matter how bright and full the light may shine, without growth darkness is never able to comprehend it. 

Life is the great adventure, the noble experiment, the golden opportunity, the supreme promise, the desirable attainment. How often, however, do we find it a sorry thing, hopelessly snarled and entangled, starved, and impoverished? Individually and collectively. How terribly ugly, irrational, and utterly foolish? What's the trouble? How does it get that way? Why are men content to have life so? Why do they not see how absurd and unprofitable is the irrational life? Why do they not learn from past experience? The trouble is not chargeable to life itself, but to man's neglect and rejection of the law of growth.

He thus misses that expansion of mind and soul which would enable him to see and cherish the realities of

life, and so he blindly determines and continues to pursue the way of his own lustful desires, let the cost fall where it may—and the cost is defeat of life! 

PHYSICAL AND MENTAL GROWTH 

We are told that there are living things and dead things with fundamental differences in properties and abilities. Living things are called organisms, deriving their substances from the realm of the lifeless. Man has a physical organism and replenishes the needs of his body by taking food and water. 

We well know that the physical life is importantvery important-and should be and must be studied, nourished, cared for. The body is a living machine and must be kept going by supplying food for its energy.

It is not the part of wisdom or good sense to decry the physical. There are delights and satisfactions and substantial results that come from and belong to a healthy and fit physical organism, and cannot be secured in any other way. What we forget is that man is more than body. He has other and important vital possessions, the growth and expansion of which are supremely important to his life as a human being, and neglect of which is not only unfortunate but criminal and stupid. There is a world of the body and a world of the mind and a world of the spirit, and we must live in all three to realize the joy of living.

Physical growth is primary and essential and failure here in the early stages of man's life often accounts for his inability to gain power to live. This we will discuss further in the chapter on The Temple of Life. 

We should remember, that man has a body but he is

a mind, and that our hope of salvation as human beings depends upon growth of mind, not growth of body. 

Within the physical organism there is a large mass of nerve tissue known as the "brain"-a great central station of the nervous system; a regulator and director of complex activities.

As a going machine man is differentiated from other living organisms by the quality and extent of his mentality. Even though he neglects his brain and shows it little respect by refusal to feed or exercise it, that excellent being "the average man"—would be highly insulted if called brainless. However little he uses his brain, he knows that in his skull is encased a wondrous mechanism, and that upon its proper training and use his hope of life depends. He rejoices in the fact that he belongs to the Creator's highest product of things that "are very good." This gives him a feeling of importance and superiority and he is inclined to think that on the whole God did an excellent job in his particular case. But the acknowledgment of the Creator's work is about as far as he is willing to go. He honors the Lord with his mouth, but gives small attention to the "works" of the Lord within him.

Life at best is not easy. It has its ugly, disheartening, monotonous, slavish, and wearisome side. It is full of routine, drudgery, hard work, problems, difficulties, and depressions. Perhaps unnecessarily full, but that's the way it is. It is unorganized and unrelated. Not alone for the common man, but for all men. This life, to some degree, we all have to face whether we like it or not. It is fact—not poetry. It demands a strong body and a clear mind, the result of constant growth and conscious development. FAILURE TO GROW 

Whatever the reason, failure to grow is the curse and tragedy of mankind. It is the dragon that slays the spirit of hope and progress. The results of this failure are so self-evident that it seems amazing that such a multitude of men and women should choose to exist without living; see no necessity for an adequate growth requisite for accomplishment; seek no development of spirit to fill life and work with meaning and purpose. Weak, narrow, prejudiced, indifferent, unenlightened, unthinking, undisciplined, unconcerned, and unashamed, they continue their foolish waywretched spectacles of the irrational life! 

They have neglected their opportunities, and in their blindness and ignorance they blame God, man, circumstances, environment, and the social order, and whine and lament that they get nowhere and find life unsatisfactory! These are the undeveloped men and women who have resisted the law of growth and failed to discover themselves and life. 

No doubt individual cases of failure are the result of untoward circumstances and conditions, and which call for pity and charity, rather than censure or criticism. However, this is beside the point. Most men are not in this list. We are all too apt to look for an excuse to account for failure. A little honest thinking would show us that the way of life is the way of growth. To be "well-born" is very desirable, but unless the well-born are also "well-grown" it were just as well if they had never been born at all. 

In these days the means and opportunities for the development of every quality and faculty of mind and body are everywhere and abundant so that anyone with

desire, determination, and purpose can secure "abundant growth," without which his prospects of securing a "more abundant life" are exceedingly small. 

A healthy mind in a sound body is a good slogan, but both results are the result of growth. A dead mind in a "developed" body must make angels weep.

Think of it. Every solicitation for the body and letting the mind go unnourished and expecting to win happiness and victory in life! Millions try to do it, but it never has been successfully done, and cannot be done. Make no mistake about it: when we cease to grow mentally we are already dead, even though there is physical perfection.

Man's failure to grow and advance is generally due to four things-(1) His desire is not strong enough to cause him to give serious and proper attention; (2) he adopts ways and means that are unsuitable or inadequate; (3) habits and ideas so bind him that he is unable or unwilling to alter or change his manner of life; (4) he unwisely attempts things for which he has neither capacity nor training. Growth is in line with. an evolutionary life process which pushes us upward and onward through hardness and difficulty to accomplishment. When we become the victims of softness and degeneration we are discarded and cast out as unfit.

GROWTH POSSIBLE AND NECESSARY 

Growth is possible because we have the equipment to think and to learn; the power to discern and appreciate to some degree the finer things of being and to profit by our own work and experience and that of others. If, however, we do not give our minds a

chance to grow, what then? There is inevitable mental paralysis; power or capacity is lacking and existence itself becomes dry and lifeless-a very humdrum affair. We are lost souls here and now. Music, art, poetry, literature, science, religion, the beauty, variety, and splendor of the things of our common life, have only a very general and indefinite meaning and we find ourselves without interest to know or capacity to understand.

Now, as we all know, growth-physical, mental, or any other kind-is a continuing process. It is not entirely dependent upon a suitable supply of food, but also on proper environment and favorable conditions. 

All organisms need light, heat, air, activity, exercise, and room to grow, as well as food. Growth must be continuous and constant or we deteriorate. When the mind stops growing interest ceases; initiative and ambition fade; any new advance at any age becomes impossible. It is the end of life. The reason why the mature years of so many celebrated men have been marked by unusual activity and splendid output is because their mental powers grew with their years and they saw no reason to quit and die while they were able to think and work.

There are great and fine possibilities in life—just as many now as ever. 

The great discoverers, inventors, scientists, philanthropists, pioneers who have endowed the life of mankind with new power and interest all had the mental growth to see hidden possibilities, and this gave them the faith and courage to continue and to overcome all difficulties. There is no wisdom in living in a world of illusion and falsehood; deceiving ourselves with silly expectations. Whether we realize it or not this

is a world of progress, of hard fact and reality, and there is absolutely no hope for life, in any real sense, unless we grow. Dead things do not grow-only living things.

CHAPTER VI  

"A sound mind in a sound body is a short but full description of a happy state in this world; he that hath these two has little more to wish for, and he that wants either of them will be but little better off for anything else."-Locke.

Man has always been a great builder of temples and shrines to gods known and unknown-tribal, national, religious, et cetera. Upon these temples he has lavished his substance, his artistry, his time and his thought. However, the temple in which he himself lives, moves and has his being is very often wofully neglected, uncared for, allowed to deteriorate and go to ruin, while at the same time he is moving heaven and earth to achieve material success and to establish for himself a position among men. 

The human body is one possession man has been aware of from his earliest existence. Wherever he began, his first mission was to earn a living by the sweat of his brow. He probably did some thinking, but it was, for the most part, how to obtain food for existence. "He watched and drew, and killed and ate."

This still consumes much of his time and thinking. 

The body has been man's constant companion; it is his to observe, handle, and examine, to care for, to struggle with in sickness and disease, to wonder at its secret sources of power, and to minister to in many 54

ways, often not wisely but too well. Man is aware of his body, but seldom asks himself, is it as good, efficient and serviceable a body as it ought to be or might be? And, if not, why not? He has been frightfully indifferent about its powers and its needs. Thousands of parents take great interest and pride in their children's "education," but show a woful lack of sense and judgment in looking after their physical growth and nourishment.

CURE-ALLS 

Human life has always been characterized by great invalidism, not only of mind but of body, resulting in great waste of life because of the inability of men to do their best or to rise superior to the common and the ordinary.

During the time of man's ignorance and lack of real knowledge of himself many strange, crude, and superstitious beliefs and notions concerning the body—its nature, ills, and healing-grew with his growth, inspired by exploiters, pious medicine men, and all kinds of quacks and charlatans. These cure-alls still continue to vex mankind like so many internal devils. The story of the experiments and cruelties upon the body to cure the ills of mind and soul would make an interesting and colorful book showing how Good Intention has long performed in the hospital of Wrong Direction.

However, modern medical science has done much to correct these old ideas and give man a more sane and rational view and appreciation of the nature and worth of his body. Books have multiplied, written by men competent to speak, presenting the facts of life

so clearly and lucidly that any intelligent layman may read, learn, and practice.

But in this crazy world men who will spend money freely to fill their stomachs with death-dealing foods. and drinks will balk about giving up a few dollars for a book or for a medical examination that might mean worlds to their health and happiness. It has been computed by the National Recreation of Chicago that in an average year the average American spends $4.00 for candy, $4.50 for soft drinks, $5.10 for cigarettes, $6.20 for cigars, $5.15 for pipe and chewing tobacco. 

But he balks at giving up a dollar to maintain his health, and at 50 the newspapers announce he died of a "heart attack!" 

This chapter is not intended to tell "what ails you" or suggest cures, but to emphasize in the most positive. way that the man who is too busy, too mean, or too indifferent to look after the needs of his own body is a fool in the widest sense of that word. Life can be impeded as well as aided by its instruments, and if impeded, then it will "crack down" on you sooner or later.

To obtain life's rewards means not only skill to avoid its dangerous currents, but ability and strength to seize its possible opportunities as they present themselves. To do so we must make the most of ourselves in every direction and by every possible means. 

A healthy body, in which life resides in fullness, is unquestionably a mighty fine aid. It is perhaps true that more attention has been given to the study of the body than to the mind, but too little attention is given to either. If "the living mind and the living body are inseparable and together make up the individual,"

then it is man's imperative duty to know himself and care for himself in both respects. 

Thus today we are beginning to understand that the body is not only something to be fed, but a machine to be understood; not something to be disdained or condemned, but a temple in which resides life forces; not something inferior and temporary to be neglected, subdued, or flayed for the good of the soul, but Nature's splendid gift, possessing divine functions and purposes.

Man needs power, energy, and ambition to live worthily. Power of body as well as of mind. We all know this, but we do not realize how important it is to maintain the source of supply in good working order. To a large degree all of these factors depend upon the proper care and harmonious working of the various parts of our human bodies. Many a man who has a marvelous knowledge of science, literature, art, religion, etc., is supremely ignorant of the functions and needs of his own body.

The body is not one thing but many things, all related, and if one or more are undeveloped, overdeveloped, or out of working order the human organism will not function effectively. If there is a screw loose somewhere and the machine does not run smoothly there is loss of power and energy and loss of life. 

The man is not himself. The unbalanced individual, whether in body or mind or both, is sick and needs treatment-adjustment to himself and to life. Society seeks to protect itself by holding men legally and morally responsible for their acts, and generally we cannot plead freedom from this responsibility unless the sickness is of such a nature as to effectually cloud the vision as to the reality of our doings. However,

the degree of responsibility, if any, often depends upon physical and mental capability, and this depends upon health of body and mind. The more power to rightly perform and the more energy to resist, the greater the assurance that the man will act like a reasonable and responsible human being.

GOOD HEALTH NECESSARY 

Good health is necessary for effective performance. 

Whatever the defects or however caused-whether inherited or caused by misbehavior-the person in physical ill health is likely to have a distorted view of life and finds it harder and constantly more difficult to successfully face unusual situations and problems or to carry on effectively. The physically weak are at a serious disadvantage in the struggle of life. Every man to be at his best needs adjustment, not so much to his environment as to himself, for it is not the environment that is the trouble but the man. 

A host of people fret and worry about their souls, minds, morals, and future, when the primary trouble lies with their digestive system, heart, lungs, or nervous system. A good digestive system and strong muscular development are mighty important, for without a strong, healthy body the individual is handicapped and lacks the power, activity, and persistance necessary to accomplishment. 

Theodore Roosevelt was a sickly child. Charles J.

Bonaparte, attorney-general in the Roosevelt cabinet, says of his youth: "Before going to college and while there, as well as afterward, he gave much time to athletic exercises of various kinds, and although handicapped by physical deficiencies, became by sheer force

of will and indomitable perseverance, a good rider and marksman, while by frequently living in the open air and persistence in strenuous exercise, he not only threw off the maladies of his childhood, but became a tireless walker and rider and developed a magnificant physique, able to respond for many years to the exhausting calls he made upon it." * 

Had Roosevelt not overcome his bodily weakness, he might still have made a name and a place for himself, but it is certain he would have come far short of any such career as marked his active life. His is only one case of thousands.

The character of our every-day actions and the quality of our lives are mightily determined by the state of our physical health. Physical condition also has much to do with the phobias, illusions, hallucinations, delusions, melancholies, and depressions which affect, afflict, and destroy. The strange thing is that although this is disclosed to our very eyes every day we live, we move along careless and indifferent. 

It is not only unfortunate but truly pathetic that such a large number of excellent people feel no need of a strong, cooperative, active, harmoniously working physical machine. They fail utterly to realize that to make a real fight and to successfully meet life emergencies which often come suddenly like thieves in the night-they need not one but many strong qualities of mind and heart-will, courage, self-reliance, hope, cheerfulness, patience, persistence. What has health of body got to do with these children of the mind?

THE DISTINGUISHING MIND 

The mind may not be "everything," but without an energized, active, adjusted, disciplined, healthy, clear seeing and understanding mind what is man that anyone should be mindful of him? We behave like intelligent human beings or act like fools generally not because "we are made that way," but because of acquired mental sight, health, vigor, and control, or the lack of these in our daily lives. We belong either to the quick or the dead, and our status is determined by the alertness, development, and power of the thinking machine in the control of life. 62

To the man with the growing, thinking, observing mind the life of human beings-his own included-is full of interest and instruction. To be true to truth as we see it is a fundamental duty; it is, however, also fundamental that we strive for a mental growth that will enable us to perceive what is truth and what is right and duty in order to harmonize our thought and properly order our actions.

NOT LACK OF ABILITY, BUT LACK OF Desire 

Generally, as has already been said, men do not lack mental ability. This does not imply that all men are "created equal" or that the average human is an undeveloped genius, or has any genius. He already has more knowledge than he uses and far more than he can use because he has not acquired the ability to interpret the data of life. It has been established times without number that the man of average ability can accomplish wonders if he will only give his brain a chance by using it. Our complaints of the dullness and the weariness of life simply mean that in a world of splendid possibilities and wonderful opportunities we have failed to secure mental adjustment and the necessary mental development to win the crown to which ambition beckons; have failed to use our brains to make life productive or fill it with the joy of living. 

Is it not so?

It goes without saying that the great majority can never hope to become scholars of distinction, intellec-

THE INIMICAL SPIRIT

It is, however, widespread and deadly enough to call for a new Era of Enlightenment and a new course in education, for its ungracious and malevolent character curses him who has it and embitters those who have to endure it.

CHAPTER X CHARACTER AND PERSONALITY 

"The sum of individual character makes national character."-E. C. Mann. 

The moral character of an individual is revealed by all his acts. While it is doubtless true that the things which we primarily weigh and evaluate as of moral worth are actions, the actions of an individual do not form a haphazard aggregate of unrelated deeds. We do what we are, and what we are morally is not the result of any one set of habits or tendencies, but the product of the whole. There is a certain continuity in individual action, and this continuity is due to the fact that behind the shifting play of emotions that is to be found in the mental life of everyone, there is a background of permanent emotional associations and emotional processes, and this stable background of the moral life is the character. Character, therefore, is not an accident, but a growth; it is crystallized life, the moral fiber of human beings who live in conformity with and obedience to the higher laws of life, practicing what they are and what they preach in all good conscience toward God and men. As a man thinks and acts so he becomes, and this is character-the chief attainment of the whole of man's earthly life. Truth is the expression of character which makes individuality significant. 89

Character is often designated as worthy or unworthy, high or low, good or bad. This fitness results in character-disciplined self, mastered self-the knowing mind redeeming the life by the use of the powers of the intellect. If this does not endure nothing endures. It is this realization of character which not only gives faith in immortality, but leads to a fine conception of the present life, and gives faith, courage, and moral fearlessness to live as "seeing the invisible."

CHAPTER XI THE OPEN MIND 

We are constantly exhorted and admonished to have an "open mind," "wide view," "broad vision," by which is meant that we should preserve a sane and tolerant attitude toward life, man, and advancing knowledge and not lock our minds in stubborn, bigoted antagonism for fear something will happen to our cherished notions.

OPEN MIND NEEDED 

This attitude of mind is undoubtedly an achievement of great importance and value. Mental nearsightedness is a sore affliction because it results in a narrow and circumscribed view which is disastrous, encouraging conceit, prejudice, bigotry, intolerance, social antagonisms, and a host of other evils, all of which impede human growth in fellowship and understanding, bind and contract the individual mentally, morally, and spiritually. The person of the open mind is not governed by grudges, spites, or selfish motives, but is distinguished by a character of good-will, which is one of the noblest traits and as broad as humanity. 

The true scientist always maintains an open mind. 

He is not content with what he knows or thinks he knows, but seeks to learn new facts and is ever ready to revise his opinions and theories in the light of new 97

knowledge. He is convinced that gradual change has been characteristic of all life and he searches to find out the processes and methods-not to prove himself right or someone else wrong, but to arrive at possible truth. If he did not have an open mind he would not be a scientist.

So if ordinary mortals are to obtain any appreciation of the work of the scientist or of others who are working upon life's problems-discovering the wonders and realities of the world above, beneath, about and in us there must be the open mind to see, to learn and to keep on learning with the same purpose and devotion that mark the spirit of the searchers after truth.

We live in a world where life is constantly expanding and growing more complex. It is being driven at an incredible speed, forever being reorganized and given new formations. Its simplicity has departed and its difficulties increased. Change is everywhere. Science, philosophy, religion, education, art, literature, government, business, and the social order are all different than formerly. Not a little different but widely different. The purpose and aim may remain the same, but the form and methods change, and these may be good or bad. A new world of thought, work, and action has been created, which demands clear thinking on the part of the individual if he hopes to understand and to steer an even course.

In such an imperfect, changing, growing order, there is tremendous need for education, open-mindedness, broad vision, tolerant spirit, keen perception, wide sympathies, acquaintance with and understanding of the great principles of life if we would gain an intelligent view of life in general, maintain our balance,

CHAPTER XII PERSONAL INDEPENDENCE 

The independence of the individual to live his own life, to think and to work in freedom of action, with a due sense of obligation to the rights of others and the laws governing human society, is a fundamental human ideal, necessary to the advance of civilization, the preservation of liberty, the growth of the human mind, and the welfare of all men and nations. It is not too much to say that it has been the basis of man's creative work and of all worth while advance movements throughout the entire history of man's existence. 

It has distinguished every form and avenue of life. 

Without this passion for independence civilization would still be in a state of barbarism, man still the slave of despots, and human life destroyed. Eliminate or suppress the independent spirit and the creative and initiative soul of man would die within him. As John 

Stuart Mill has well said, the worth of a state in the long run is the worth of the individuals comprising it, "and a state that dwarfs its men in order that they may be more docile instruments in its hands, even for beneficial purposes, will find that with small men no great thing can be accomplished." Personal liberty depends upon personal independence, and the true basis for a nation's freedom lies in the independence in the soul of its citizens. 103

WORTH FIGHTING FOR 

Independence of life, therefore, is important to nations and society, essential to every individual who desires a broader and fuller life, and should be sought, secured and maintained. It is worth a fight to the finish for it is vital to all effective living. It is the high road to freedom of mind and spirit, to expression of character and personality. It is hard to achieve and maintain, and therefore cannot be said to be among the popular virtues. Like all good achievements it requires the thinking and seeing mind and the brave and faithful heart.

Absolute independence in human life is a groundless fancy. For many things-for our support and supply -we are dependent upon others. Absolute independence or freedom we can never have any more than we can have perfect character or perfect beauty. Personal independence in the higher modes of being, however, is generally possible where there is will and desire, and means much for our peace, welfare, and happiness in the business of living. In this, like all our other ideals, we will not reach perfection, for the attainment of character in whatever sphere or form is a growth as we go along, but where there is no vision. that leads to effort we perish.

Independence means that the individual must learn to stand in his own shoes; be able in the ordinary and personally important things of life to be self-reliant and not all his life be under the dominating control and subjugation of persons, systems or of hereditary beliefs. Thomas Aquinas, Joan of Arc, Franklin, Emerson, Paine, Ingersoll, Washington, Jefferson, Lincoln, Livingstone, Stanley, Edith Cavell, Jane Addams, and scores of others of like spirit are remembered because of their independence, nonconformity, and opposition to the life and thought, the political, scientific, social, and religious ideas and conditions of their own world and time. Their independence was very different in form and purpose, but it was always the voice of liberty. We may not belong to the company of the independent great, but we can all be followers of the spirit of greatness, but never unless we cultivate the spirit. of independence within ourselves.

A PERSONAL THING 

To have enough independence to depend upon ourselves, pursue our own way, and mind our own business is great wisdom. Jesus taught Peter a great lesson-one that we all need to learn. Peter came to his 

Master and asked, "What shall this man (John) do?" 

Jesus replied, "What is that to thee? Follow thou me." Attend to your own individual life and workconcentrate on yourself-and do not fret yourself unduly about what the other man is to do. Every man has enough of a job to take care of his own life without managing the life or future of other people, and it requires great independence and courage of soul to do it. Breadth and depth of personal life, as well as distinction of performance, depend upon the measure of our independence in thought and action. 

True independence does not mean that we undervalue or take no thought of the life or work of others, or assume a superficial and hypocritical attitude-an

PERSONAL INDEPENDENCE 

107 aristocratic complacency-toward other people or their ideas or toward social life. The really independent man does not do that. He is too broad in his mind, too free in his conscious knowledge, too sincere in his living, too charitable in his spirit to place himself in false positions or relations. His life and thought are far removed from envy, malice, smallness, and uncharitableness.

Independence is not a spirit of antagonism, aloofness, or self-importance, but the expression of conscious dignity, sincere purpose, and sound mind, preserving always a positive, but sane and reasonable, attitude toward all men and all things. Without malice or uncharitable feelings, it nevertheless demands the right to be, to do and to think, and to voice its faith and belief to the fullest extent. "A man's shortcomings," said Goethe, "are taken from his epoch; his virtues and his greatness belong to himself;" and Spinoza said, "The foundation of virtue is no other than the effort to maintain one's being; a man's happiness consists in the power of so doing." Seneca said, "If you would subject all things to yourself, subject yourself to reason." This is philosophic wisdom. There is no virtue or greatness in any action that does not spring from personal independence, personal understanding, personal convictionfrom the personal inner soul of man-his independent self. There is strength in independence, but it is the strength of freedom, of sincerity, goodness, rightness, intelligence, and knowledge, all working together for good. Nobility of birth, or any other nobility, has no claim to respect unless it manifests itself in the nobility of the courage of independent life and serviceable action.

The real pathfinders of life and progress have been earth's great independent spirits, and today the whole worth is full of their glory. Many whose opportunities in life were great have sacrificed their intellectual and moral independence because of fear, unholy ambition, lust, or love of mammon, and passed under the cloud and into oblivion.

Men of the independent spirit whose memory we hold dear have not always been wholly right or even reasonable, but they were true to the highest light within them, and for the most part that light was not darkness but the divine light of intelligence shining in a dark world. Those who have sold their independence for any reason whatever have always reaped the whirlwind of sorrow and the reprobation of mankind.

Here history is constantly repeating itself. 

AN EXAMPLE OF THE INDEPENDENT SPIRIT 

In 1862 President Lincoln was besieged by radical

Republicans and anti-slavery men to take a more decided stand against slavery. They were sincere but mistaken. Horace Greeley, in a severe letter of criticism, wrote him that he was "sorely disappointed.' 

Lincoln's reply is well worth quoting for it shows the fine courage of his independent spirit: "If there be those who would not save the Union unless they could at the same time save slavery, I do not agree with them. If there be those who would not save the Union unless they could at the same time destroy slavery, I do not agree with them. My paramount object in this struggle is to save the Union, and is not either to save or to destroy slavery. If I could save the Union without freeing any slave, I

PERSONAL INDEPENDENCE 

109 would do it, and if I could save it by freeing all the slaves, I would do it; and if I could save it by freeing some and leaving others alone, I would also do that. 

What I do about slaves and the colored race I do because I believe it helps to save the Union; and what 

I forbear I forbear because I do not believe it would help to save the Union. I shall do less whenever I shall believe what I am doing hurts the cause, and I shall do more whenever I shall believe doing more will help the cause. I shall try to correct errors when shown to be errors, and I shall adopt new views so fast as they shall be shown to be true views. Such is my purpose according to my view of official duty. 

I intend no modification of my oft-repeated personal wish that all men everywhere might be free." 

In this succinct form Lincoln left no doubt as to his own policy and political attitude and his opponents could make the most of it. 

AN EXAMPLE OF FAILURE 

Pontius Pilate is a remarkable instance of failure. 

Pilate had a fine chance to show moral independence in personal greatness and courage of action. The accused Jesus was before him. Pilate knew he was innocent, he declared he could find no fault in him, he wished to release him, he despised his accusers, he tried to evade responsibility and put Jesus' death up to the Jews, he sent him to Antipas, he acknowledged his power to release him or to give him up to death, but the cry went up, "If thou let this man go thou art not Caesar's friend!"

Pilate had been brave enough in flouting the Roman standards in the face of the people of the Holy City, in slaughtering Galileans, in sending his troops against

the peaceful Samaritans, in rifling the sacred treasury of the Temple, but now, when the freedom of this innocent, defenseless Jew meant charges to Caesar and the possible loss of his position, he had not the moral independence to act according to right, justice, and personal conviction. Pilate passed into oblivion, but Jesus passed into immortality.

INDEPENDENCE A UNIVERSAL NEED 

We are sometimes inclined to think that in our ordinary lives there is no great demand for independence. 

We have something of an aversion to independent people-intellectual, moral, or otherwise. They smack of smugness, superiority, a holier-than-thou atmosphere. We like people who agree with us, whose "convictions," if they have any, are held in the background, who are such "good mixers" that our ways of life will not be challenged or disturbed, who will conform to our notions or the standards of the time in order to be agreeable or sociable; people who go with the crowd, and agree with everybody and everything-colorless people. Independence is well enough, very necessary perhaps for those in responsible positions, or who are leaders in thought and action, but for ordinary folk the less independence the easier it is to get along in life. 

How we deceive ourselves! No one really likes a coward and lack of independence is cowardice.

The man who lacks independence lacks personality -he is a spineless creature, without positive character, and generally devoid of great influence-dead while he lives. The morally courageous independent man is the salt of the earth, he has the mark of self-respect and of self-supremacy. If this is needed anywhere it is

111 needed in ordinary life, for independence is not a destroyer but a redeemer of life. Parents should teach their children to be independent, self-reliant; encourage their originality, develop their individuality and not make them victims of suppression. 

Real independence lies in the seeing mind, in the moral sense, in the spiritual soul expressing itself in life—it is the man himself, alive to the dignity and reality of his own personality, enlightened with the knowledge of the meaning of life, with a deep appreciation that both his acts and attitude must honestly reflect his sincere convictions.

Now, it is characteristic of modern life that it is so unsettled, restless, unsatisfied, superficial, material, self-centered. With all our getting we lack stability. 

Many have swung away from the old moorings, of old principles and ideas, and have not been able to steady their lives or find themselves. They have become drifters without captain, conviction, balance, or destination. This has encouraged a false freedom and independence and led to much ignorant boasting. 

We fuss about small matters, make much ado about things of no great importance. We are long on scandal and intemperate living and short on virtue and morality. We sell our souls for gain. We are little people, without the courage of moral independence. 

When a people live on the surface, become superficial and shallow, have no high ideals, call evil good and good evil, live for the body and deny the soul, lose sight of the nature of truth and justice, become timeservers and self-seekers, then come intellectual, moral, and spiritual ruin and depression. This has always been true, is true today, and will always be true, in individual and in national life. After the fall of

Greece and the decline of the Roman power the world went into great darkness for a long time and the life of men suffered eclipse. But the light never went out entirely. Here and there men of independent and courageous spirit, mental and spiritual power, kept the fires of knowledge burning. Men went back to the thought and ideals of Greece, light dawned, independence awoke, and the Renaissance began. This is human history-liberty and independence are always being born anew, and every attempt to crush them only deepens man's sense of their reality and importance. 

Sometime mankind will learn what life is, what morality is, what independence is, what goodness is, what happiness is, what justice and righteousness are, and when they do then will begin the renaissance of the spirit and we will see how good is life. 

A MARK OF THE SPIRITUAL SOUL 

Moral independence is a mark of the spiritual soul -whether Christian or non-Christian, Jew or Gentile, rich or poor, learned or unlearned. Among all the careers of men the life of the Man of Nazareth stands out in the splendid significance of its moral and intellectual independence. His divine mission and goodness are marvelous and unique. His splendid independence is also glorious and has brought him close to the heart of humanity. His cleansing of the Temple of its commercial desecrators, his meeting with the woman of Samaria, his attitude toward the despised harlot, his denunciation of the hypocritical Scribes and Pharisees, his teaching of the universal love and goodness of God and the brotherhood of all men, his identification of his life with the poor, the unfortunate,

Independence of life may not be the easy way, but it is divine. No man has the marks of success who is without it, and no life can be fruitful in which it is absent. It is a real achievement of the thinking soul desiring life.

CHAPTER XIII  

"I am Education. I bear the torch that enlightens the world, fires the imagination of man, feeds the flame of genius. I give wings to dreams and might to hand and brain."-Better Schools League. "The philosopher, the saint or the hero, the wise, the good or the great man very often lie hid and concealed in a plebian, which a proper education might have disinterred and brought to light."-Addison.

A LIFE PROCESS 

Education, in its true and full sense, means very much to every individual who desires life. It is a life method of instruction, training, development, and attainment. There is no such thing as a "complete or finished education" any more than there is a complete and finished human being. Knowledge is always relative, partial and incomplete. No one knows everything, or can know the details of everything, and he should not try. As we grow we learn, as we learn we perceive, reflect, understand, and become intelligent, and as we become intelligent we get wisdom to live. 

No school can educate a man, his education depends upon himself in school or out. Whatever contributes to the systematic and harmonious enlargement of man's natural powers by way of instruction, training, discipline, adjustment, observation, example, or experi- 114

ence is education; whatever helps to draw out, build up or complete the physical, mental, moral, or spiritual resources so as to enable the individual to work and to live is education.

Man's whole life, therefore, is a process of education. It extends from the cradle to the grave, and whatever we are or become is the result of educative forces to which we have been subject and which have moulded and developed our lives. When the infant "begins to notice" then, or even before, his education starts, and as body, mind, and soul develop he makes more and more contacts and his education goes on through his whole life. The educated man never gets beyond the period of learning.

In the biblical conception of man's life-story the history of his education began with two adult persons in a garden paradise. They were taught some things by the Serpent-Devil and other things by God, and later were turned adrift to be taught many more things from life itself. Their fall was a swift and mighty descent, but it had its compensations. They lost a lovely paradise and an easy life, but gained the knowledge of good and evil, secured to themselves the privilege of going on "their own," obtaining an independent living by the sweat of their brows, and getting an "education" in a new and unknown world. They were not destroyed, but given the opportunity to become. 

Their descendants, all beginning at scratch, have continued in the good old way of life with varying success and with singular persistence until the present day. In this twentieth century the whole world is still looking to discover the tree of life and how safely to eat the fruit thereof without loss of prestige or favor. And, as in the paradise garden, man is often guilty of eating

the wrong fruit and incurring a bitter result, and that often without any compensation. He needs education. 

EDUCATION BEGAN WITH PRIMITIVE MAN 

Whether the biblical account is fact or poetic imagery we may leave to theologians; it is evident that education began with primitive man in his efforts to think, to know, to live, to protect himself from enemies, to obtain food and shelter, to subdue the earth, to realize the meaning of his own existence and to add value to it as life expanded. Thus education has gone on through all the ages in a multitude of ways and systems, organizations, methods, schools, sciences, philosophies, and religions, all supposedly seeking the national and social welfare and the development of the individual in knowledge, efficiency, and character.

In whatever way we get education, whether in a school or outside the regular channels, it is to be observed that education of any real kind is linked with life, and the less remote it is from life the more valuable and serviceable it is. And this is what we wish to see, how education of mind, heart and soul serves the common life-what is its value. 

EDUCATION AN AID TO A WIDER LIFE 

In modern times great stress has been placed upon physical and manual training, and education regarded largely as an instrument or means of making a living —making a material success of life. Now, this is one function of education and its importance is not to be denied. In the physical world education is a prime

factor and an increasing essential in realizing life's ambitions. The trouble is this is often so emphasized that one would think it was the chief, if not the whole, purpose of education. Anything else for the average man is misapplied effort-trying to make a silk purse out of a sow's ear.

However, education has a much larger province than to make successful workers or brain-trust prodigies. The aim of education is to develop man's whole nature to fit him to live as well as to work; to make a man and not simply an expert user of tools. 

Man has emotional life as well as intellectual life; it is not the purpose of education to make intellectual sharpshooters, but men of feeling, sagacity, sensitiveness, gentleness, imagination, insight, desire and determination, all of which give form, warmth and worth to pure intellect and understanding, and control to the intelligence.

If life is more than material success (and who denies it?) then we need more than material eyes to see and know what it is, and it is the purpose of education to widen life by enlarging our native capacities to know, to see, to appreciate, to understand, and to perform. 

Let us then consider specifically how education helps. 

ENLIGHTENMENT 

In the first place, the purpose of education is to give enlightenment. The unenlightened mind lives in a world of darkness. We begin life without any knowledge of ourselves, our abilities, our duties and obligations, our surroundings, the world in which we live, our relations to it or to one another. We need enlightenment as to all these things to aid our progress

and to keep us from stumbling. It is only the man who sees who is able to foresee-and this applies to all life. Education is a method, system, acquirement designed to assist us to see, apprehend, and apply. It is not simply acquiring enough knowledge to care and provide for the needs of the physical life, important as that is, but training and development that give mental insight to discover life principles and vital values, not as gods to consecrate and worship as the ultimate attainments of reason, but as perspectives and ideals of our present life to live to and by. 

Thus education includes and involves the culture of the heart and the spiritual soul as well as the purely manual and intellectual powers, and demands adjustment, self-training, and discipline, bringing enlightenment and direction to the whole personality. It enables one to perceive the allurements which belong to the body and the allurements which belong to the spirit, and to see these so clearly and sanely as to be able to comprehend, to some degree at least, their nature, to estimate their relative worth and value, and to determine our relation and actions accordingly. It is only in this way that we are able to gain control of ourselves and receive conscious desire to follow after the things that make for our growth, welfare, and character. "If you will consider what are called the virtues in mankind you will find that in all cases their growth is assisted by education and cultivation." (Xenophon.) To have no sufficient mental vision of what is good or evil, high or low, desirable or undesirable, important or unimportant, true or false, honest or dishonest, fact or theory, or what will be the future effect of action, is to be blind to life's high interests and invite disaster. It is the practical purpose of edu-

cation to help us see life and the facts of life as they really are, enabling us to gain wisdom to live. Every 

Age of Enlightenment in human history, despite its blunders and vagaries, has been a period of education leading to greater vision and insight, changing man's thought and securing some definite permanent results. 

SEEING WITH THE MIND 

It has been popularly said of Einstein that he "sees with his mind," which is no very great discovery. The artist, the musician, the poet, the historian, the scientist, the philosopher, the novelist, the editor, the discoverer and inventor, the engineer, the mechanical and industrial worker all see with the mind, and the sum total of what they see ministers to human enlightenment. Blind Milton saw with his mind and we have "Paradise Lost." Michelangelo, standing before a huge block of marble, asked his companion, "What do you see?" The man replied, "I see a fine piece of marble." Michelangelo replied, "I see an angel." He saw with his mind. Helen Keller sees with her mind. 

This is not simply intellectual apprehension, but spiritual insight-seeing beyond the purely material or physical. But seeing with the mind is not confined to the elect few, for every person sees with his mind if he sees at all. Just in proportion as the healthy mind is developed to see the breadth, depth, extent, and significance of life and to perceive and understand the real facts concerning ourselves and the world in which we live, our relation to God and humanity, are we stirred to live in a way calculated to make us men and women of reality, reasonableness and substance. All we have of civilization we owe to the enlightened mind.

Knowledge is not enough. Knowledge must be shot through with enlightenment to save it from narrowness and conceit. The scholarly man is often as intolerant as the religious fanatic and as dangeroushe needs enlightenment of mind and spirit to modulate his narrow conceptions and give him a freer and wider. outlook. Often it is true as Franklin declared, "A learned blockhead is a greater blockhead than an ignorant one." Life is something more significant than knowledge. To live is more fundamental than to know.

The crimes, cruelties, and barbarisms of human history were not performed by men without knowledge, but by those of the unenlightened spirit who planned and practiced their malicious deviltry for the preservation of themselves, their beliefs, and their divine institutions. Their "honesty" or "conscientiousness" scarcely lessened their essential hypocrisy or mitigated the sufferings of countless thousands. It has required centuries of education to make men see the immorality of inhumanity, and other centuries will lapse before there will be a society among men not built on selfishness, injustice, fear, blind faith, racial superiority, but ruled by an informed and enlightened civilized intelligence the result of advancing education. 

We well know that bigotry, bias, narrowness, conceit, pride, vanity, duplicity, stupidity, hatred, fear, jealousy, selfishness, covetousness, superstition and all that brood of evil are characteristics of folly—children of the darkened, unenlightened mind. These all bind and enslave, and no one ever got or ever will get much satisfaction out of his life who is subject to mental and spiritual bondage. To become free and superior to destructive ideas we need the enlightenment of mind

and spirit which a broad and true education provides. 

Abraham Lincoln in his log cabin, studying by the light of a pine-knot fire, said, "I will prepare myself, and when I am needed I will be ready." His opportunities were small, but he made the most of them to educate himself. He had the thinking, seeing mind which revealed to him the necessity for preparation. 

For many years his education went on, and when his time came his mind was as clear as his purpose was firm. His intellectual mastery, moral grandeur and humanity are today the glory of his country. As 

Cardinal Newman truly said, "Education is the preparation for knowledge." It is also preparation to live. 

Education is necessary to mental perception, and clear thinking is preparatory to clear vision and rational living. Until the mind is quickened and enlightened to think and to judge we cannot hope to make our way firmly or to steer our course successfully. Therefore, education that enables us to think straight, to see clearly and to act reasonably is fundamental. The mind has eyes but they must be trained and educated to see, understand, and truly evaluate the things of life. No soul ever seeks the kingdom of God or the kingdom of life (which in the best sense is the same thing) until he sees the desirability of possession. 

Unless we have mind-sight our wisdom or skill in using the things of life will be very small. All efficient men are those with their mental eyes wide open; all pioneers in human progress have been men and women of the enlightened spirit. It is enlightenment that gives wings to courage, and freedom to honest thought.

Our abilities may be limited, our knowledge not wide, our formal education faulty, our experience

limited, but if with open minds, courageous hearts, and souls awake we seek enlightenment of the free spirit we will find life good and our advance in knowledge a sustaining power.

UNDERSTANDING 

The old proverb says, "Understanding is a wellspring of life." It surely is, and blessed is the man who has such an inexhaustible fountain. As we all know life is very complex. The only people who find life easy are the dead ones. There are principles and powers, duties and obligations, loyalties and devotions, emotions and passions, which must be understood, obeyed, observed, and controlled if we are to be lifted to some higher platform.

The world's lack of understanding has always been and still is very great and very destructive, leading to countless blunders and wrongs. If a man must pray it should not be for bread, but for understanding—which is a much greater need. Understanding comes only through the seeing mind-the result of experience, mental and spiritual growth. Knowledge which is information derived from books or other sources is good, but without understanding it is something of a vain possession. In general our failures are due not so much that we lack knowledge as that we are devoid of experience and understanding. Knowledge is something to be used, not information to be stored away. 

Unless we use it there is no advance and life remains a fog. "Of ten things that annoy us, nine would not be able to do so if we understood them thoroughly in their causes, and therefore know their necessity and true nature." (Schopenhauer.) 99

Reflection, therefore, is a necessary element in education for it is reflection that keeps the mind clear, opens the eyes of the understanding, and gives meaning to the experience. It is reflection that lights the candle of understanding in the soul that can never be put out. If many men knew less and reflected more upon what they do know, they would be far more intelligent and possess far greater understanding, and be better fitted to live. It is the seeing, reflecting, understanding mind that lays hold of the truth of the facts of life and knowledge and is able to interpret their meaning and significance. As Jane Porter has well said, "Reflection is the alchemy that changes knowledge into wisdom." We have life but we do not understand what it means; we have knowledge but do not understand how to use it or make it serviceable; we have instincts and emotions but do not know how to control or direct them; we have desires and ambitions but fail to realize them because we lack the intelligence that gives mastery-the product of the educated, understanding mind.

The wider and more substantial our knowledge, when we seek to understand its nature and what it means to our life development and security, the greater its contribution to make existence harmonious and intelligent. When nourished by the understanding and experience of the educated mind, knowledge becomes the foundation of true culture and self-development. 

It is probably true that the higher we advance in knowledge and wisdom the greater the sensibility and capacity to suffer, but it is also true that our advance introduces us into new realms of helpful understanding, and also gives us knowledge, discipline, and con-

trol to discount the suffering and deprive it of the power to harm or annoy.

To apprehend the outward form only, to see the shadow and be unconscious of the reality, to observe the appearance and not to divine the essence, to see only the temporal and ignore the eternal, to have no apprehension of ourselves or our place in the scheme of things, denotes the superficial mind without the penetrating power of understanding. 

APPRECIATION 

Another benefit of a broad and liberal education is that it enables one to obtain a true and proper appreciation of life and the things that are essential and fundamental-things that add breadth, depth, and richness to our every-day living. Life is very deeptoo deep for us to understand all at once. But it is the appreciative soul that discovers values that fill life with meaning and relief as the days go on. The person who has been trained to see and appreciate splendor in beauty, harmony in music, meaning in poetry, greatness in nobility, divinity in heroism, glory in accomplishment, good in everything, has an education that will keep him from despair and discouragement. 

Appreciation is the bread of life.

All things are not equally important, either in themselves or to us as individuals, to know or to have. In ignorance and blindness thousands of men pursue the unimportant or the less important, seek the outward and the perishable, strive for the comparatively worthless and unsatisfying. They worry and fret about matters that in no essential way are important, that mean nothing to comfort or enjoyment or yield any

permanent fruit. Surrounded on every hand by the evidence of moral and spiritual values-beautiful and realizable; encompassed by myriad examples of supreme excellence-shining with light and glory in the life and work of noble human characters, the man who lacks the power of appreciation or sense of recognition is to be classed among the most unfortunate of men. 

He lacks that education which lifts him above the commonness of life into new fields of wonder and beauty. 

To live we must not only seek knowledge to free ourselves from vain fears and illusions, foolish conceits and practices, ignorant superstitions, but also obtain an adequate extension of mind and heart to enable us to appreciate the splendor of a moral life, the worth of man himself, the marvelous beauty of the works of God in nature, and the significance of the productions of the mind of man. Such appreciation fills the life with health, color, and delight. It leads to the development of body and mind, makes us more humane and just, gives desire to the heart for better things and moves us toward more worthy purposes. 

Real education should and does give one capacity for enjoyment-what to do with and how to make the most of leisure time—and appreciation of time itself.

Attending dinners, dances, football games, races or the opera may serve as relaxations if they do not become dissipations. But continual change, confusion, and excitement have very temporary satisfactions. There are times of solitude and quiet-and these increase as life goes on-when every soul has to fall back upon itself and draw whatever sustaining power it can from within. Here education which has led to true appreciation of enduring values is always found a faithful and valuable servant. The only man who can live with

himself and enjoy it is the man of educated mind, heart, and soul.

Effective education, therefore, is as broad as the whole nature of man. It does not mean the making of intellectual gymnasts or walking encyclopedias of facts. It means training the mind to think, to see, to understand, and appreciate; directing the emotions, inculcating right ideas and eliminating bad ones, teaching moral values, stimulating the imagination, forming character, creating love for the good and the beautiful, fitting man to work and to live, so that whatever our sphere in life we gain something of an adequate knowledge, understanding, and appreciation of that which is highest and best and most desirable, and obtain desire and power to carry on.

Life is real and important. We must see it exactly as it is, but we must see it as it might be-its powers and possibilities—and we must see it as it ought to be and assume the obligation to make it so. This demands education-that intellectual apprehension, comprehension, and discernment so necessary to good sense and rational living, and the power to adequately appreciate the values and realities of life. "The wise man lays up knowledge" says the proverb, not as something to minister to his vanity or selfconceit, but as a source from which vision, understanding, and appreciation can draw wealth to minister to his life.

From all the history of human experience we fashion our dreams and ideals in the hope of living accomplishment; we live in a world that is constantly moving on in ever increasing life and with which we must keep contact to advance. We need acquaintance with the

finer accomplishments of man's spirit to lift and encourage our own. We have moral and spiritual duties and obligations and must discover and know what they are. Surely, then, man needs and should seek education of mind and soul that leads to insight, understanding, and appreciation and reveals the living realities of life. He who possesses an educated mind possesses a kingdom.

Life is good, knowledge is good, happiness is desirable, character is fundamental and the pursuit of all is man's everlasting duty and obligation, but unless the pursuit is directed by the educated mind, giving sight to the blind, understanding to the simple, and appreciation to the spirit, little fruit will be gathered at journey's end. "He that refuseth instruction despiseth his own soul."

CHAPTER XIV  

"Life is too short for mean anxieties.”—Charles Kingsley.

In this chapter we consider "enemies" and in the chapter following "allies," with all of which we are more or less acquainted. We have heard about them, read about them, been lectured concerning them, but unfortunately we fail to seriously consider how important a part they play in human life and destiny. 

All forms of life have their own special enemies. 

The farmer and the fruit grower, as we well know, have their troubles in fighting crop enemies. Animals, birds, fish all have unfriendly and preying neighbors. 

Human life also has its enemies-physical, mental, moral, spiritual-strong, powerful, resisting, deceptive human traits and characteristics which if allowed to run riot lead to tragedy and self-destruction. The world is a stage in which is going on the drama of man-the struggle of the ages. This is a battle for individual liberty and freedom in the fullest and deepest sense between the man who is and the man who wishes to become. It is the struggle for manhood, for selfhood, for personality. We may not be able and it may not be necessary to entirely destroy the foes that war against our well-being, but we must become superior.

The enemies of the physical life are many and vari- 128

ous. Disease of the body plays a large role in human affairs and often prevents attainment of life's ambitions. Much is being done to aid men to overcome these destructive physical agents. The foes with which we are here particularly concerned, are more difficult to overcome. Generally when a man is sick physically he knows it, asks aid, and is willing to be helped; but when the trouble is mental, moral, or spiritual he often does not know it, refuses to believe it, and does not feel the need of deliverance. It is now generally recognized that many persons are "carriers" of infectious diseases-typhoid fever, scarlet fever, diphtheria, tuberculosis, etc. These carriers are not aware of having the disease germs. In the moral world we often carry within us the germs of defeat without being aware of their dangerous character, not alone to ourselves but to others.

All history and experience teaches that any man who sets out to be true to his ideals, just, free, independent, or well balanced-to become a person and act the part -will always find that he has a fight on his hands which demands strong effort, heroic courage, and great persistency of purpose. To achieve victory it is necessary that he clearly discern the things that fetter and warp his life, and obtain as full and accurate a knowledge as possible of their nature, their operations, and their destructive power. It is then up to him to fight the good fight of courage, faith, and perseverance.

It is sometimes as difficult to love our friends as to love our enemies. If we cannot love either of them we can ignore both without great loss. But the enemies of life which we here deal with should neither be loved nor ignored. They should be recognized, controlled, and suppressed. It is well, however, to remember that

the inborn stock of human passions are a fundamental part of man's nature and like the poor are "always with us," but that is no good reason why we should allow them to enslave and imprison us and make life miserable. To excuse ourselves and refuse to fight is as unwise as it is cowardly. 

The most unbalanced estimate a man ever makes is an estimate of himself-scarcely ever accurate because of a disinclination to take stock of himself and acknowledge the facts of his personal situation. Unless one is willing to carefully consider his life, candidly look all powers, forces, and tendencies in the face, and recognize and admit their reality as well as their terrific influence, he will not get far in bringing his life to a successful issue. Therefore, to become even partially aware of these adversaries of our common life is a necessary, vital, and profitable study. They are not lovely to contemplate, but that is no good reason why we should not look them over and get a line on their meaning and nature.

SELF-CONCEIT 

There is probably no enemy that has a larger part in making man look and act foolish and ridiculous than the arrogant and supercilious egotism of self-conceit. 

It is a deceptive and treacherous appeal to vanity and stupid opinions and desires which blinds the vision and blasts the moral sense. It leads to egotism and arrogance.

We well know that right ideas are very important in any sphere of life. The pride of self-respect is an excellent thing but the pride of self-esteem is a vulgarity. Confucius said, "The superior man sees things

according to the principle of justice, but the inferior man sees them according to the selfish interest they bring." Just different views of life. Self-conceit is blind and is inimical to truth, justice, fellowship, or good-will. It engenders false views of life, position, duty, accomplishment, friendly relations. It is deception's trump card of appeal to which the great and the small fall victims. It is without justification. 

The desire for admiration, praise, possessions, power, fame, or success is often centered in the underlying wish to magnify our self-importance, and we observe how "just a little" of any of these things does "puff us up" with the pride and vanity of self-conceit. 

We select the most fashionable church, join the most exclusive clubs, endeavor to become acquainted with people of wealth or distinction, build big houses and fill them with costly furnishings, books and paintings, try to break into high society, acquire and exhibit family escutcheons, trace our family descent, choose exclusive schools for our children-with what intention? None of these things are wrong in themselves, but if we are simply seeking outside means to establish our self-importance and minister to our self-conceit we are not building our life interests on a foundation of reality and are riding to a fall.

Self-conceit places us in wrong relations to ourselves and to every other true relation in life. It engenders a superiority complex which breeds a critical and suspicious spirit, darkens the mind, and lives upon its own emptiness and shallow pride. Self-love never renders to God, Caesar, country, neighbor or friend the rightful things to which they are entitled, because it is essentially hypocritical and false.

ENVY The envy of ill will in the heart is poison in the life -the greatest possible obstacle to personal peace and happiness. It is an enemy of life because its world is blind, hypocritical, vindictive, malicious, unreasoning, and there is scarcely any limit to the objects and situations which may be made to minister to this unhappy spirit. Envy begins to show itself very early in life, and unless checked, grows with our growth, eats the bread of hate and drinks the wine of bitterness. Its malevolence is characteristic of all races, nations, classes, all sorts and conditions of men and women, because everywhere humanity is the same and everywhere exist conditions to excite and encourage this evil passion which is always the sign of a narrow and ungenerous nature.

For anyone to possess what we have not got and which perhaps we see no hope of obtaining-whether it be physical, mental, social, or material possessionsis something that the envious spirit feels is not as it ought to be, something of which it has been unjustly deprived. It is the spirit of selfish desire and ill will, and often seeks satisfaction for its wounded emotions by inflicting pain, loss or misery upon others, even though the action does not bring any personal benefit, and does bring suffering to innocent parties. Envy is a mean, spiteful spirit, to which all humans are more or less subject, and of which all broad-minded people are mentally ashamed. Envy lives in a house of unhappiness inhabited by seven constantly working devils -egoism, jealousy, hatred, morbid desire, vanity, petty meanness, and vindictiveness.

Life is altogether too short and too valuable to be

vitiated by the spirit of envy, for as the wise man of the Bible declares: "A sound heart is the life of the flesh but envy the rottenness of the bones."

VANITY 

One of the most prevalent, persistent, and ignoble human vices is vanity. There is probably no enemy that leads more men and women into making fools of themselves than vanity. It is not only vexation of spirit, but is in conflict with common sense. Vanity is the mantle that inferiority seeks to throw over its lack of reality. It is the "snob" act of tragedy. It is trying to give appearance to a virtue we do not possess, an endeavor to show superiority where there is bankruptcy of life. It is pretension without merit. It is the deceit of pretense and not the pride of accomplishment. It is the "show off" of shallow minds and feeble spirits to attain results which minister to pride, conceit, arrogance, and egotism. It is imitation, eccentricity, bravado, weakness. Vanity puts self in the foreground and knows nothing of good will. It sticks the poisoned dagger of jealousy and hatred into friendship, affection, and modesty. It is unfriendly, unneighborly, and unsocial. It is a monster with a thousand eyes, each one with an evil slant.

There is no more disgusting vice than vanity. Unconscious of its own foolish face, and desirous of vaunting its own self-centered excellence, it loses no opportunity to intrude and exhibit its obnoxious presence into every form and function of human life. It is the spirit that judges everything and knows nothing. 

It is not a rare bird, but a thoroughly offensive one. 

The parade of physical, intellectual, moral, or spiritual

vanity shows a lack of intelligence and a mind of narrow compass.

The germs of all enemies of life are in the mind and heart, and the only way to overcome them is in a change of mind. We are all foolish enough by nature and should avoid making ourselves ridiculous by empty affectations. Vanity feeds upon itself, has an enormous and malignant growth, and unless curbed ends in empty conceit and egotism.

JEALOUSY 

Another extremely active enemy of human life is jealousy. There is probably nothing that more effectually clouds the judgment, double-crosses peace of mind, incites paroxysms of malice, malignity, spite and revenge, and that produces a larger crop of hatreds and enmities, wild emotions, and unreasoning outbursts of human fury than this mean, petty, suspicious spirit. Jealousy arises in the mind and fills the heart with suspicion and doubt. It magnifies molehills into mountains, feeds upon the imaginary, and eventually and inevitably leads to suffering and defeat. Like pride and envy it is unreasonable and ridiculous, selfcentered and pernicious, but even more deadly. It is the insanity of life.

The pride of self-conceit, although sometimes humorous and sometimes pathetic, is an attempt to assume a virtue we do not possess. This obsession and its vain results are largely limited to the individual. 

Not so with jealousy. It is not subject to reason, and its suspicion, envy, and ill will seek an object-many times an innocent one-upon which to vent its fury and its spite. It is largely sexual, but the morbid desires.

of the jealous are often inflamed by observing the possessions or attainments of others. It is and has always been the curse of nations, races, families, and individuals. There is neither justice, honor, nor virtue in jealousy, although the jealous person usually claims justification. Its spirit feeds on revenge, and the whole experience of mankind proves that the attainment of any high degree of peace of mind or heart, or contentment in life's relationships, is impossible while jealousy lives in the mind and heart. 

There are no more blind or unfortunate mortals than the slaves to jealous feelings, and their name is legion. Jealousy is a devouring noxious growth that feeds upon unrealities and seeks to justify itself by the shallow appeals to self-defense, self-injury, self-justification, and self-interest. Jealousy fosters dislikes, fears, passions, moral indifference, and these all inflame the mind so as to make right judgment, clear perception, honest appreciation, unselfish action, respect for the rights and welfare of others impossible of realization. The jealous person is never aware of any mote in his own eye but finds an immense number everywhere else.

COVETOUSNESS 

The third enemy that deserves execration is covetousness. The human desire to possess good and proper things is altogether worthy. The possessions of another may awaken a desire in our hearts that is without any feeling of ill will toward the possessor or even of jealousy or complaint. But covetousness-the greedy, avaricious, envious spirit-is that unlawful, inordinate, extreme desire to possess things we have no

right to, have no need of except to gratify pride, selfishness, or possession-an unhealthy state of mind and heart that inflames the passions, warps the judgment, and often leads to criminal acts. 

Like the enemies already mentioned, covetousness is absurd and foolish, as indeed are all acts that go under the name of "sin." Covetousness has no justification in sense or reason and its great prevalence in our own day shows how slow we are to learn from experience and to attain a life of wisdom. 

In the very nature of the case the covetous man is one of the most unhappy men in the world. His desires are always ahead of his attainment or even the possibility of attainment, and so his unhappiness is continuous and a burning fire within his bones. What do we need to be happy? Not much in the way of "things." With all the acquisitions of modern life is the average man happier than men of former generations? He is more comfortable, but on the whole is he happier? It is doubtful.

To be happy we need the underlying and controlling spirit of happiness, and this never exists in the covetous heart. Happiness is not dependent upon great material possessions. Children of the poor are generally happy. The poor little rich boy is often very unhappy. 

Riches, station, or bank accounts are not happiness, but the covetous man thinks so.

The splendid work of the mind of man should promote his happiness and would do so if he would act like an intelligent and reasonable human being and not fill his mind with enemies of unhappiness and take to his heart false gods of his own foolish creation.

Covetousness is an inimical, hostile, antagonistic attitude of mind. The covetous man had an unfriendly

and hateful disposition. He not only envies his neighbor's possessions, but feels himself annoyed, distressed, offended, robbed. Covetousness breeds the spirit of sullen discontent within men. It builds a spite fence around the soul, and the heart is consumed with ungracious thoughts and unnatural desires. 

The lustful covetousness of Israel's King David brought death to a brave man and suffering and rebuke to himself. If the lustful, beastly, and unrighteous actions of men chargeable to this covetous spirit could be written, the record would probably eclipse in magnitude of evil any other that could be made. The "covetous eyes" of nations and of men are directed by the very devil of unholy and unlawful desire—a cancerous spirit which becomes a running sore in the heart of humanity.

If Moses did not receive the Ten Commandments direct from Jehovah, he deserves tremendous credit for having concretely summarized some of the chief and most fatal obsessions of mankind. Some moderns think these commandments out of date, but the one "Thou shalt not covet" will never be obsolete—it will look man in the face until the millenium arrives. The covetous spirit is silly, stupid, asinine-unworthy of the thinking mind. Avoid it as you would a pestilence if you value your happiness, for it is fatal to peace of mind.

FEAR AND WORRY 

Through all the centuries of human existence Fear has been the paralysis of life, causing doubts and worries which have cast their enervating spell over innumerable lives and made man weak when he should

be strong, timid when he should be courageous, vacillating when he should be decisive, and hesitant when he should go forward.

Fear has ever clouded man's judgment and often made him a traitor to truth and progress-a tyrant of injustice, hypocrisy, deceit, and cruelty. Fear made 

Adam a hypocrite in blaming the woman; fear inspired

King Herod the Great to slay the children; hatred inspired by fear asked for the head of John the Baptist.

The Pharisees hated Jesus Christ because they feared him he was a disturber and a revolutionist. The 

Church feared the effect of Galileo's statements and silenced him, and for the same reason burned John Huss at the stake; Calvin feared Servetus and hounded him to his death; men the world over feared witchcraft and burned the witches; the Puritans feared heresy and banished Anne Hutchinson as "a heathen, a publican, and a leper." Fear has been the devil's right bower in making trouble.

Fear of gods and devils, of death and the future, has been a constant source of worry. Fear of new ideas and beliefs, of change in government, institutions, and methods, of the advance of scientific knowledge have all been disturbing factors to many people and have filled life with anxiety. Fear freezes ambition, weakens character, produces a sense of inferiority and encourages hypocrisy.

As human beings we are all more or less subject to this terror, which breeds infirmity and inertia. Fear is not necessarily "sin," but can become very harmful and destructive.

In the early days of man's existence, when he had little knowledge of himself or his world, and was without instruments of defense against the hostile forces

of nature and life, it was inevitable and pardonable that he should suffer many fears, doubts and worries; imagine vain things and attribute his troubles to vengeful gods and evil spirits. Life and the future became full of great dreads and menaces. These delusive beliefs were universal among mankind, and became instilled so early into man's very nature that he has never been able to free himself entirely from their influence. It is a great mistake for parents and teachers to subject children to fear, for this emotion, once taking control of the mind, becomes a life possession of weakness and despair, robbing life of confidence and tranquility.

Human life being what it is has its ups and downs, its sunshine and shadows, its trials and disappointments. These come to every individual more or less and bring fear, anxiety, and worry, often unduly magnified. A man loses his money, his position, or has suffered defeat, and fear, doubt, and worry take possession and he decides "to end it all." It is not so much the loss or the defeat as the creeping fear and anxiety concerning the future which break the spirit and lead to despair.

Opie Read, an American author, at the celebration of his 81st birthday, declared, "Life is largely a series of worries." It undoubtedly is to a host of people. 

But why pick on life? To a large degree we fret and worry about nothing at all, fail to come to any intelligent understanding of life, fill our minds with silly and superstitious fears and doubts, and then cry, "Isn't life awful!" It is the common everyday unreasonable phobies-in which there is constant apprehension or anticipation of evil, active and painful forebodings, foolish imaginings-which cause perplexity of mind,

anxiety of heart, depression of spirit to countless thousands.

The foregoing are enemies which everyone recognizes, and which all should seek to overcome. They are the result of inheritance, superstition, false teaching, ignorance, narrow and distorted views of life, lack of knowledge of the mind and physical constitution. 

That these unhappy and absurd foes of life should in this twentieth century have a strong hold upon multitudes of people shows what simple-minded children we still are and how devoid of intelligence and understanding. Instead of cultivating values which would make us superior to these enemies of a perverted imagination, we make pets of them, feed them with the milk of kindness and the bread of encouragement and make life hard and barren.

Self-conceit, envy, vanity, jealousy, covetousness, fear and worry-these are all interlocking enemiesundesirable offspring of an unhealthy imaginationchief links in the chain of lustful desire from which spring manifold other evils which blast life and destroy peace and happiness. No man ever did or ever will know the high satisfactions of life until he becomes master of himself, and he never secures mastery until he breaks with the foes of his inner life that consume and destroy.

In the light of experience, the best ways to deal with these enemies are:

Enlarging the faculties of the mind and heart to see life broadly and sympathetically, and so obtain a calm, reasonable, inspiring, and sincere view of life and its ways.

Building a healthy reality into our lives that radiates faith and hope.

Gaining an understanding of true values, giving life and thought new direction thus saving us from vain illusions.

Seeking the independence of contentment that lives in a world of good-will.

Personal identification with real purposes and useful work.

Association with men and women of broad mind, high spirit and devotion to give help and encouragement.

Cultivating a fraternal spirit toward all men and a reasonable spirit toward all life. 

Striving for the moral character that sustains and the independence that lifts above the trivial and the common.

All of which are as possible as they are sensible and indispensable. Are we personalities of the free and independent spirit or are we slaves imprisoned? Who is free? "The wise man who can govern himself." (Horace.)

CHAPTER XV  

If there are enemies of life which have to be watched, controlled, mastered, and cast out, there are also allies of life of exceeding great importance. These the wise man seeks to know, to cultivate, and to develop during the course of his life. These allies minister to a universal need and are as available as they are important.

In very early times, says H. G. Wells, "Everywhere mankind was seeking where knowledge and mastery and magic power might reside." So grew magicians, priests, sacrifices, and gods all through the centuries, and so still flourish many vain illusions. In Greece, says Gilbert Murray, "When the Oldest Men in the tribe could not tell you the right thing to do you went to the blessed dead. All Oracles were at the tombs of Heroes. They told you what was 'Themis,' what was the right thing to do, or as religious people would now put it, what was the will of God." 

Seeking vain outside allies of instruction, help, and direction has been a favorite pastime of man, keeping step with his belief in devils, witches, and evil spirits seeking his destruction. He has given scant attention to know and develop the living forces and powers within to bring his spirit into harmony with the spirit of life. In this chapter we are interested only in discussing allies within the human soul, and they are many and real. 142

It is perhaps true that we pay more attention to enemies than to friends, for they seem more abundant, their influence and work more evident. It is said that there are three times as many good germs as bad ones. 

Our powerful and helpful allies outnumber the enemies, but are too often unrecognized and neglected. 

It is certain that if we were more intent upon cultivating the deep and helpful moral and spiritual forces of the inner life and becoming acquainted with the principles of living we would not be troubled so much by the hateful and disrupting things that vex and destroy our peace and happiness.

There is a lot of trouble in this world, both within and without ourselves, which is senseless and unnecessary, and could be largely prevented if we only used a little good sense. We waste our possibilities for happiness. We make life difficult, somber, and fearful without any reasonable justification. We become victims of our own shortsighted stupidity. The man who cares for his body to keep it in health is not so subject to the diseases which imperil an enfeebled constitution. 

The man who keeps his mind, heart, and spirit in close alliance with the good and the healthful is without question better prepared to live and better able to resist the evils which threaten his mental, moral, and spiritual life.

It is generally true that when we ignore or forsake the higher we become victims of the lower. Life seems evil when we are subject to its miserable enemies, but it seems good when we are fortified with allies which make us strong in character and lift us to a higher plane of thinking and living. How glorious are geniuses like Edgar Allan Poe, John Keats, and others, when in moments of inspiration they pour forth the

splendid poetry and philosophy of their fertile minds. 

How infinitely sad and distressing the record of all such unfortunate lives. Whatever the explanation, the fact remains. Something was lacking-something is always lacking.

THE GOOD CONSCIENCE 

Human experience shows that one of our most helpful allies is a Good Conscience. This is just another term for the inner conscious knowledge we have of our own moral selves, resulting from a clean, honest, and upright life. This is definition enough. 

This inner register which, despite the Behaviourists, we are all aware of and which we carry all through life, when kept clear and void of offense, sensitive, properly attuned and nurtured, is a powerful aid in keeping the ship of life on an even keel and sometimes from complete shipwreck.

Much of the perverted life of the world can be traced to a perverse, deadened conscience. Men tamper and keep on tampering with their sacred selves and reap whirlwinds of disaster. Read the biographies of great life failures in all ages and nations and see how true this is. It is true of all men, high or low. 

No one can load his conscience with evil thoughts or deeds without suffering mental, moral, and spiritual depression and demoralization. The knowledge of our condition may not always be acutely manifest to ourselves or cause us great present anxiety. But whether we know it, realize it, feel it keenly or not the fact remains. Men of perverse, dissolute, and corrupt life often seem well content with themselves and their ways. King David was quite serene until he was

visited by the accusing prophet, but prophets are not generally so well received. Moral condition does not depend upon personal recognition of it, but upon the nature and quality of our lives. Conscience is not a theory but a fact.

The great moral principles constitute a natural law and afford a standard of action, although the application to human behavior may vary from age to age. 

Stealing, deception, lying may be and often are blinked at and sanctioned by public opinion and even by law, but neither law nor public opinion change or can change the fundamental principle. It is one thing to recognize new attitudes and relationships, but it is something else to run counter to "eternal verities."

Deep in the human conscience is a feeling of moral right and that human action should have some other basis than desire or expediency. The greatest conflict a man ever has is not with external forces but with himself. Covetousness, greed, ingratitude, hypocrisy, selfishness can never be made right, good, or attractive.

The universal knowing conscience is seen from the fact that men who do wrong invariably wish to cover it up, to hide it, to keep it in the dark. A guilty conscience does make men cowards because they know themselves. It is not the innocent error, blunder, or hasty action, but the deliberate betrayal of ourselves, the wilful wrongdoing, that bites and stings and burns.

This inner ally of conscience lives ever with the man of right purpose and intention, constantly performing its duty of encouragement and helpfulness toward good, as well as serving as a positive restraint and safeguard against evil.

Conscience in the wrong doer, who is not lost to all moral feeling, is an accuser that fills the soul with fear

and dread, regret and shame when brought face to face with reality. Our evil deeds not only live after us, as Shakespeare says, but what is worse, they live in us and with us. Conscience, which should be a sustaining ally, becomes life's anathema. There is no worry in life so deadly as that which comes from the knowledge of wrong doing. Wrong acts, whatever their nature, consciously and deliberately performed, are foul spots in a man's character and mean loss of self-respect, independence, and peace. No individual can be two different men and avoid an inner conflict unless he is mentally and morally outcast. 

However, in the man seeking to know the right and faithfully to do his duty, careful for his personal honor, desirous under all circumstances to preserve integrity of mind and peace of soul, the good conscience always proves a mighty ally of salvation. 

Moral rules of life there are and they are as old as man, and every normal human being is to some degree aware of them. Without these moral precepts life would be intolerable. These rules were not made by conscience, but when we violate one of them the quickened conscience cries out against it and we reject the warnings of the inner voice at our peril. Schiller said, "The game of life looks cheerful when one carries a treasure safe in his heart," and no man ever carried a greater treasure to brighten life than a good conscience.

How many are the useless regrets and repentances that might have been avoided had the conscience been kept free and active? What does it profit to gain wealth, power, fame, high station at the expense of integrity of life and forever carry within us a consuming fire? There are some questions to which there

is only one answer and are not subject to argument. 

The wise deep-seeing man said, "Wisdom is the principal thing: get wisdom," and if there is any greater wisdom than a clean moral life no philosopher has ever been able to discover what it is. Figure it out any way you please, the big trouble with the world today, as always, is the bacillus of mental and moral disease which perverts the conscience and destroys the life of men and nations.

The unconsenting conscience that never voluntarily yields to the low, the wrong, the impure, to the easy way, the compromising attitude, the temporizing spirit may be rare, but whoever has it has a good ally of the first magnitude. This is human common sense, backed by every fact of life and experience. We may not be able to understand Einstein and his "Relativity," but certainly every one of us has gumption enough to understand this. With all our learning we should cultivate the art of securing a seeing and discerning mind, which healthy instrument would lead us into the secret of the moral splendor of a right life. 

FRIENDSHIP 

Another valuable ally is Friendship. Here is a wide sphere of life, the power of which is rarely discovered, understood, or appreciated as it might be and ought to be. There are friendships of the mind, heart, and spirit, vital, strengthening, enduring, which we should not overlook or neglect, for they are among our most precious life-possessions. Emerson declared, "A friend may well be reckoned the masterpiece of Nature." 

There is nothing that more truly sweetens and cements life than a rich personal friendship. During Mr. Lin-

coln's period of melancholy how fine and sustaining was the faithfulness of his friends.

The man who forms strong personal friendships of the right sort, not merely as supports to lean upon or acquirements to selfishly use in emergencies, or to enlarge his self-esteem, but as stabilizing, stimulating, inspiring, and uplifting human attachments and fellowships, is in possession of deep and enduring moral forces which constantly minister to his development and well-being. As Robert Hall has well put it: "He who has made the acquaintance of a judicious and sympathizing friend may be said to have doubled his mental resources."

The study of the personal friendships of great men and women is not only interesting but illuminating, for however great, and apparently self-sufficient, the association, communion, friendship, and fellowship of like spirits generally played a part, and sometimes an exceedingly important part, in their life and work. 

Charles Kingsley, when asked about the great influences in his life, said: "I had a friend." Friendship has given impetus and direction to many a noble life. 

Too often we cultivate a cold, belligerent, contentious, superficial, hostile spirit, an attitude that is inimical to deep friendships, hinders our growth, makes us cynical, separates us from the common bond of human fellowship, and robs us of wise counsel and the helpfulness of kindred or superior minds. This undoubtedly was a prime fault in the character of Woodrow Wilson. His failure was due to his aloofness. He had little use for friends or advisers and quarreled with most of them. His was a great spirit -a fighting, dramatic spirit-but the success of his whole program suffered from the isolation he chose

for himself, and played a large part in the fiasco of his European adventure. His ideals were more important to him than his friendships, but he need not have sacrificed the one for the other. He made the issues so personal that to many Europeans he seemed to be talking like the Saviour of the world. This isolation may seem heroic, but as a general thing it shows poor judgment and lack of wisdom. Theodore 

Roosevelt was just as imperious and dogmatic as Wilson, but he had a broader life experience, more human sympathy, and withal the happy faculty of making and keeping friendships among all classes of people. When we live in an unfriendly atmosphere, shut ourselves up in our own self-sufficiency, retire to devouring solitude, reject friendly advances, we are not alive to our own human need. Is it any wonder that life then becomes drab, uninteresting, unsatisfactory, disastrous, and that its evening becomes solitary? 

Fine human friendships are generally possible and they are always desirable, but to acquire them we must have the spirit of friendship. Standing alone may be the "grand style" but for common mortals it is a foolish philosophy. No man should live to himself alone. We need contact with men of knowledge, vision, sanity, imagination, and goodness to inspire our own souls, strengthen and renew our faith, stimulate our hopes, and increase our love of life. It is the friendly spirit that gets beyond itself and finds fine contacts with other friendly spirits, and this adds zest and interest to life. The hostile man is neither lovable nor happy. Adopting a superficial independence, living apart from people and from life is a sure way to dry up the streams of mental and spiritual life. The man who lives to himself in an atmosphere of unfriend-

liness may not be a fool, but he certainly is not wise. to his own life interests.

IMAGINATION 

The development of the Imagination will also prove a fine ally of life. No one is without imagination. 

Some possess it in greater measure than others, but this is true of all mental or spiritual possessions. But however small, it is possible and profitable to develop it sufficiently to greatly enrich life and add to the joy of living. But it must be developed in the right direction. When Love, Faith, Hope, Imagination are allowed to "run wild," spending themselves on evil or unworthy objects, then they become trump cards of the devil of destruction. Let us emphasize the fact that what has always been needed and is now needed is intelligent insight and control of life to lift us out of aimless, senseless dreaming. Imagination we need, but we must use the faculties of reason and will to give it practical effect and make it an agent of a life of reason.

Our interest in detective and wonder stories, the lives of eminent men and women, the great events of human history, the wonders of science, discovery and invention is largely due to their appeal to the imagi nation. How many histories and books of biography have died as soon as they were born. No one ever read them or desired to read them. They were a dreary chronicle of events. The writers never lived in the times or the scenes they describe. No imagination, so the work is without vivacity, interest, or power. It is so with life-without imagination we

have minds, but no wings. Imagination is the "air of the mind."

Poetry has been called the "expression of the soul." 

It is the insight of the poet into life and beauty, into the deep things of the spirit and into the marvelous things of the common life, transformed by the imagination into an immortal flame. More and more the wise turn to poetry for the interpretation of life. But to interpret its meaning, understand its beauty, and learn to love and appreciate poetry the reader's imagination must become alive and awake. We may not be able to see the picture whole or to appreciate it fully, but whatever awareness we have of its truth and beauty is the result of the development of imagination in our individual selves. This we must cultivate, for imagination leads to appreciation, and appreciation leads to knowledge, and knowledge leads to love. 

Imagination lifts love from a sensuous, selfish emotion to a spiritual thing of beauty.

We need to know the facts of life. Facts in themselves are wonderful things with tremendous significance, but we need to use imagination to visualize them. We read that there are some thirty million stars in the Galactic system, that light travels 186,271 miles per second, that the light-gathering power of a great 200-inch reflector is 640,000 times that of the human eye, that the earth has been million of years in the making, that a scum-covered pool is the home of countless millions of living organisms, but to realize the enormous meaning of these and other like facts we must give full play to our imagination. In fact, the whole universe is full of wonder, music, poetry, beauty, spiritual truth, songs of hope and joy—myriad voices which stir the memory, gladden the heart, and

arouse our sleeping souls-and they can all be ours if the imagination is stimulated to know, to feel, to see, to appreciate, to listen and to love. This ally will help us to realize: "How good is man's life, the mere living! How fit to employ all the heart and the soul and the senses forever in joy."

I know a man, a proofreader, who has never been outside the United States of America but who has made a very thorough study—as far as it can be made from books-of the great cities of the world—their history, government, political parties, economic and social life, buildings, streets, historic places, et cetera, and by visualizing his store of real knowledge he has made every part, section, and institution of these cities living realities in his world of thought. So the joy of the imagination is not confined to poets or artists, but is the servant of all who wish to see, and seeing live. No one who cultivates his power of imagination will find life dull or meaningless.

Imagination is the power to foresee. There is a saying, "to govern is to foresee." According to H. G.

Wells, one of the factors in the failure of the Roman Empire was "A grossly ignorant and unimaginative empire. It foresaw nothing." No man in high position has any sense of leadership who has not the imagination to look beyond the actual present and visualize to himself what it is he proposes to do, the obstacles to be overcome, the opposition to be met, and what will be the probable future effect of his program. "Hindsight" may be very instructive, but foresight born of a lively imagination to see realities is

what is needed, not only in government, but in the direction of all human life. 

LOVE AND FAITH 

Other allies are Love and Faith. These allies are as old as thought and as eternal. They have been the energizing powers and motive forces of man's life and work through all the ages. They are the most familiar words in our vocabulary and carry meanings the most profound, aspirations the most holy, longings the most intense, and results the most tremendous, because locked in the heart of these two ideas lies the need of humanity's present and the hope of the future of all civilization.

It is becoming more and more evident that the crass material philosophy of the present age, which men prophesied would bring us happiness and contentment, has proved an empty dream and is bankrupt in results. 

Modern science, dealing with purely material things, seemed to many destructive only, but the fear that science would kill all spiritual faith is passing. Science has increased man's mechanical power, given him a more intelligent understanding of the greatness and vastness of life, awakened him to a knowledge of a universe of majestic wonder and beauty, making it real to the understanding, and furnishing mental nourishment for our everyday life. But the spiritual ideals of all civilization since the world began have not been eclipsed, but still shine with undimmed luster and are being constantly revitalized with new power and significance, aided by the discoveries of science. 

Science and advancing knowledge may destroy many of our inherited notions and cherished beliefs, enlarge

our conceptions, change our ways and manner of living, bring into being new and valuable adjuncts to enhance life, but love and faith in essence will remain the same yesterday, today and forever-indispensable to all men and to science. 

Love is a human need. Rich or poor, high or low, learned or unlearned, whatever our station or mental equipment without love we are "tinkling symbols."

The yardstick of life is not mathematics or laboratory methods. Man is more than a machine, more than a physical system or a physiological process, more than an animal with raw appetites. We do not have to prove this. Every thinking, willing, loving man is his own proof. We know it, feel it, are conscious of it even though our understanding is very imperfect. 

Love is the supreme power in life-whether the material life or any other kind. It is the power that drives and holds us to all that is best and noblest; it is the basis of all our actions of moral and spiritual value. Love of God, country, home, goodness, truth, beauty, neighbor is the distinguishing passion of the good man's life. What he loves he pursues; what he loves he is, becomes, and remains. Nothing can take the place of love-neither knowledge, education, mental perception, material advancement, or any other thing. Other attainments, however, excellent in themselves, are barren without love. Science or education cannot produce love, but love can motivate them. Religion that is not centered in love is barbarism. The doctrine of Christ is love and loyalty to God, to neighbor, and to your own spirit. This is the heart and substance of his religion. 

All human experience proves that love is the strength of life; that its ways are ways of pleasantness

and profit; that it is the highest excellence and the fulfillment of the noblest law of being. Love is the divine expulsive force of evil in the inner life and is the power to keep and to hold. The possibility of all other graces and attainments is lodged in love. It is the foundation-stone of moral action, sacred honor, pure devotion, high performance in the pursuit of life and more abundant life.

Love is fundamental because it is conquering power. 

Love casts out fear, humbles pride, seeks to serve others rather than self, it cannot live contentedly in the same house with hatred, envy, jealousy, covetousness, impurity, immorality, evil thoughts or wrong intentions. It is the soul's chart and compass to overcoming power. Love is a living religion; it is the gospel of deliverance, the solace of the poor, the struggling, the discouraged, the heavy laden, the defeated; it is the inspiration of every soul that desires. life, usefulness, and the joy of personality; it is the hope of human society.

Man is wonderful in many ways, but especially wonderful in his stubbornness and blindness. Stupidity is bad, but the stupor of mental and moral blindness is fatal. The laws governing a courageous life are really very simple, our essential needs are very few, the deepest kind of happiness is not difficult to obtain. 

We all have capabilities and abilities sufficient to unlock the secrets of nature and of life to our understanding; there are life-allies knocking at the door of our intelligence and our reason-allies that would transform and enoble all activities, inspire all mental effort, lead into a new world of realization and possession, and yet how dead and dumb we are! Millions of men persist in living an unnatural and deficient

life-chasing happiness but never finding it because their whole desire and longing are devoted to the pursuit of the primitive, the emotional, and the material. 

They do not know the power of helpful allies. 

Life therefore becomes a stagnant pool rather than a spring of living water. Outside thrills of the superficial, the raw experiences of the sensualist, the selfishness of the egotist, the vanity of the materialist get us nowhere. There is an essential lack somewhere and no power on earth can ever supply that lack but the love that conquers. "Consciousness is a universe and its sun is love."

FAITH 

The lifting ally of Faith is locked in the heart of love. Courageous, far-flung, heroic faith has been a sustaining quality in the history of man and a prime energy in all his undertakings and accomplishments. 

We all have faith. It is not a power obtained from without, but a stimulating power within, and is just as much a part of our existence as love or hunger. 

It is man's heritage. Faith is beyond argument, statistics, or mathematics; it sees the reality of accomplishment. It is faith that is back of all discovery and of all material advance, as well as of all moral and spiritual values. The look of faith is a forward look. Not a field would be cultivated unless the farmer could visualize growing crops. It was faith that discovered the New World, faith that sailed the Mayflower, faith that energized the minds of Newton, Franklin, Mendel, the Wright brothers, Lindbergh. The history of religion, education, medicine, science in any sphere, is the story of man's believing

faith. Faith has often led where reason was dumb; has gone forth and conquered when reason halted. 

Without faith human life would perish and become a wilderness of despair; without faith the creation and development of systems and institutions, social and industrial progress, family relations and all human interrelationships would be impossible. Faith is man's common inheritance-a living thing, not a blind belief opposed to intelligence, reason, or science, but the inner consciousness in the reality of life, belief in the possibility of progress, and that all things, however mysterious or beyond present knowledge or proof, work together for good, and in a world of apparent contradiction and terrific evil sees the star of hope ever shining.

Faith is the ally of the common man. We must have faith in ourselves, in our possibilities, in our purposes, in life, as well as faith in the faith and work of others. The center of gravity must be in ourselves, not in outside things. We are not discoverers, explorers, inventors, scientists, breakers of new paths, or originators of new ideas. Our business is livingobtaining development of mind, heart, and soul. Here faith is an active force. It is creative, productive, fertile. A renewing agent. It ministers to calmness, confidence, courage, conviction, reliance, trust, faithfulness. Faith is a strong ally for the task of life, and its cultivation means that we are in the way of the life of wisdom.

When change and decay seem all around us, when the old is supplemented by the new, when men in high position sell their souls for a mess of pottage, when nations follow false gods and play the fool, when life and the world and even our own existence seen like a

cimera, absurd, fanciful, and unreal, when our daily life rises like a tragedy of triviality, when destiny looks hard, when doubt threatens to consume life, when moments of melancholy take hold of us, how we do need faith! Cultivate it. It will solve many a problem.

HOPE 

Another important ally is Hope. Hope is sustaining, stimulating, and inspiring expectation. Not vain. expectation apart from duty, but the hope of radiant, practical faith. It is an attitude of mind that in times. of storm and stress, doubt and difficulty, proves an anchor to the soul. Take hope out of man's thinking and human life would go into a ruinous depression from which there would be no resurrection. When misfortune comes crashing down, and even love and faith seem very weak, hope still lives. Visit the hospitals, the homes of poverty, prisons that separate man from life and sunshine, and see how hope lives eternal.

Hope is the consciousness that victory deferred may yet be obtained; that all experience, sacrifice, and suffering will prove a door of life through which, sooner or later, will enter good. 

Hope sustains faith, stimulates love, and inspires effort. It never gives up or surrenders. It sees success in failure, opportunity in difficulty, and a star shining in every mystery. 

Hope is the strength of duty. 

Hope never sees a time to quit. 

Hope is the mind's daily bread. 

Hope is the heart's good wishes.

Hope is the soul's life renewer. 

Hope is the sunshine of good-will. 

Hope is today's welcome to tomorrow.

Hope is the cheer leader of the world. 

Hope is the light shining in the darkness. 

Hope is inspiration to moral courage. 

Hope is the vision of the unseen. 

Hope is a believing optimist.

SILENCE 

Another ally worthy our attention is Silence. Man builds his own life, and if it is a life of any reality, the building-stones are truth, hope, faith, love, pa- .tience, sincerity, good-will, laid securely within the thinking soul in silent meditation as the days go on.

No doubt there are times when silence ceases to be a virtue, but it is a wise man who knows when those times are. The silent man who listens and weighs but reserves his judgment saves himself from many an unfortunate situation. The silent man is the superior man, for the idle shafts of unjust or cruel criticism do not alarm him or destroy his peace. The man who "goes off half-cocked" has not learned the power of silence. To grow the soul must not only have food, but times of silence to digest the food. How silent is the working of nature. How calm, solemn, constructive, productive. The mighty forest-how it grows in silence; the vines and flowers gladden the earth with their presence and beauty but you hear no sound; the planets move in their swift orbits but "their voice is not heard." Growth is silent. Illumination dawns in silence. Realization is the child of silence. 

The fine deeds of noble endeavor, the mighty works of immortality mature in silence. The soul that has no silent periods away from the noise and strife of

the world has not found the secret place of power where dwells the strength of life and the delight of discovery.

All great producers work in silence. A recent writer thus describes Einstein: "He recognizes fully the work of others, but his is a life of quiet meditation. He knows not when he works. A pencil, a paper, and his pipe are his tools. He is in no hurry to finish anything. He thinks things out." Every great thinker knows the value of silence and meditation-not to be expended on himself, but to consider the deep things of life. Thought is seldom found in tumult. 

If there is anything Americans like it is noise. "Get busy and keep going" is the theme of the national anthem. The more noise the more life. We are all so busy, occupied, and active with the passing show that we do not see the glory of the visible and the invisible by which we are surrounded. We have no time for thought, meditation, reverie, dreams, calmness, consideration, appreciation that come when the mind and heart are silent and we rise above the narrow horizon of our temporal lives. We have no time to feed our minds or for study to enrich our souls. 

Too busy to think! Too busy to live!

We observe the man who is consumed with the details of trivial things, who is a slave to success, who is filled with the pride and self-conceit of getting on in the world, who is constantly seeking the stimulation of effects, he is a man of noise, tumult, excitement, given to exaggerated views and hasty and thoughtless decisions. He lives on the fringe of things, in the scum of his materialism, roars his opinions like a Boanerges, and has no use for thoughtful silence.

The greater the noise the more evident the superficiality and the more shallow the character. 

Not so the man of rare and distinguished accomplishment. From Socrates to Einstein silence and quiet have been characteristic of the world's really great minds. No bluff, no bluster, no noisy appeals, no superficial deceptions. There is a distinct difference in the emphasis and in the manner of life. Here we have recognition of limitations, the sincere humility of greatness, open mindedness to truth and carefulness of statement, the results of silent thinking. Generally the strength of the individual life lies in the creative power of calm, silent thought that does not center in morbid introspection or unintelligent emotionalism, but which awakens the latent powers within for new productive work.

Work and activity always characterize the man alive, but they are the aroma of life, the offspring and outcome of his inner spirit, the expression of the realities of his own individuality formed and nurtured in the silences of life. "Let my soul walk softly in me

CHAPTER XVI COURAGE 

"Courage is the thing. All goes if courage goes." -J. M. Barrie.

Because of its literal truth Barrie's statement has had wide quotation and very definitely suggests the importance of this high quality as a saving principle in human life, deserving of careful consideration. 

The pessimist sees life as evil because it is war; the optimist sees life as good because it is war; the opportunist sees life as compromise because it is war; the courageous man sees life as good and evil, as war and compromise, but also as an arena in which he must face realities and be true to himself, to his personal convictions and his highest ideals. Courage is the thing. The noblest life of the race has always been distinguished by this fine virtue and everywhere the splendor of its nobility has been admired and praised by all races and nations. It is the universal red blood of life and therefore a proper subject for our consideration.

COURAGE A CHILD OF THE MIND 

Real courage is not a temporary emotion. Courage is built-up reserve power. It is rock character. Men break and "all goes" because when the burden becomes 162

The storms beat and the winds blow-sometimes fast and furious-and unless courage is built upon something more than physical strength, emotion, or mental ability we lose our grip and suffer loss. Like everything else about man courage is a growth nurtured and maintained by the law. of growth. It is the outward expression of an inner reality.

Courage is not a gift, it is not an accident, it is a fruit of being-clear thinking, will, honesty, sincerity, loyalty, love, self-control, conviction, tolerance, purpose, independence, and determination. Without courage when winter comes we wither and die, because we have not built the house of life upon an enduring foundation, and are in no condition to continue the journey. The heroic exploits of daily life spring from love and a fine sense of duty existing in the human soul. Courage is a discovery of existing reality. 

Courage is not ability to do what you can't avoid doing, but power to stand when you could run away. 

The convictions of the man of courage may suffer change, and often very properly so, but not until there is a change of mind. The healthy mind lives up to its highest and best views of life; it is not a reed shaken by every wind that blows, but with steadfast and firm resolution follows the light as it sees the light. The man who displays high courage in any emergency is simply running true to himself—he has the seeing, discerning, independent mind which responds to the call of duty within him, and is loyal where the coward would hesitate, evade, or show the white feather. Courage "is the thing" because it is

the strength of life, consciously and faithfully developed, and while it remains all is well. 

The dissolute life arises from a diseased and unadjusted mind, the man comes into captivity and there is no possible hope until the mind is awakened, desire quickened, and the will given new power. Then courage is developed within the soul and the individual is able to deny himself and follow the light of a new conviction. Courage then becomes the habit of the soul, and the life that was once disorganized and fearful becomes one of triumphal purpose.

COURAGE IS "THE THING" WE NEED 

We extol the man who faces death bravely, but how about the man who lives courageously? To face life's problems as they come along-in prosperity and depression, through all the changing scenes of timewith balanced mind, clear conscience, and clean reputation, is something higher and nobler than a grandstand play to bravely face the inevitable. It is one thing to be brave when you can't be anything else, but it is another story to show courage as a principle of life.

WASTED CAPITAL 

Time is capital which we waste ignorantly, wilfully, thoughtlessly, foolishly, and wickedly. "All men think all men mortal but themselves" and live as if they had a guaranteed mortgage on Time and never need worry about the mortgage being foreclosed. "O Time suspend thy flight" is a vain and hopeless request, for "Time and tide wait for no man." We can neither control its flight nor escape its toll. But we can and should sense its reality and realize how very important it is to faithfully use while it is ours. 

As we look over man's history-consider past ages, our own age, and our individual lives-not viewing history or life from any idealistic standpoint or yet from a pessimistic attitude, but from a common-sense viewpoint, we discern material progress and an upward trend, but at what a cost in waste of Time. 

This waste might be justly called humanity's cardinal sin.

From the beginning of history war has not only laid waste the earth, despoiled man of his life, his substance, his work and his happiness, but it has been an insane and asinine waste of Time. Of course it is easy to point out some good results that have followed

in war's wake. The Bible says, "God makes the wrath of man to praise him," and from his own devilish mischief man learns something. He has yet to learn, however, that war is a criminal waste of Time. Any attempt to justify war by its good results is like advocating a pestilence to bring about sanitary conditions; or making hell on earth to bring in the millennium. 

The evil results of war always greatly outnumber the good. The World War was a species of international barbarity not justified at the bar of reason or common sense. It was a supreme loss of Time as well as the destruction of national prosperity. It did not make the world safe for democracy, personal liberty, or foster good-will among the nations. It plunged the whole world into a mire of hate and jealousy, moral and spiritual deterioration, and material chaos, from which it will take humanity many long years to recover. War is inhuman, the hell-dog of brute desire and national aggrandizement. It robs men of their divine right to Time in which to pursue peace and happiness. War is a game where everybody loses.

No millennium in human affairs may ever be expected until men and nations get a saner and more intelligent idea of the worth and value of Time.

More and more men are waking up to this curse of waste of Time and life, and becoming alive to the nature of the sham gods of patriotic racket and military humbug used to lead them to their destruction.

It is appointed for all men once to die, but there is no reason why they should prematurely commit suicide.

The call to every intelligent person is to think of the things in which there is the virtue of life, and Time is one of these.

If people would think less and worry less about death and the future, and calmly and soberly think more about Time and the matters which concern their present welfare, happiness, and peace, life's rich veins of gold would pan out far better results. Time has been the friend of man, brought good out of evil, corrected many crooked ideas, and overthrown many pernicious ways of life. Time is not only the foe of error but the revealer and vindicator of truth. 

Evil forces and methods, false, low and corrupt ideas and practices, blind pursuits and unreasoning stupidity, devilish devices and laws of inhuman oppression have often seemed to have a stranglehold upon life. However, Time has always been on the side of reason, sanity, and righteousness and has

CHAPTER XVIII THE WELL-BALANCED LIFE 

If there is anything the average human life needs to make it successful it is balance. It is a proper prayer, "From the perfect man Good Lord deliver us. There is no such animal except in his own conceits, and then he is the devil to live with. However, the well-balanced life of symmetry in which there is the spirit of harmony and order, calmness, intelligence of action, humor, humaneness, power, and agreeableness of manner-something of an all-round development in mind, heart, and spirit-this is distinctly a mark of attainment and means much in getting the best out of life.

Life is not a conspiracy to engulf us with insurmountable difficulties or maddening perplexities. 

Difficulties and perplexities there are aplenty. What we greatly need is adjustment to life-adjustment of body, mind, and soul. As we grow life expands and we must expand with it or we get out of balance-to ourselves, to others, and to the social order. 

But to get this adjustment means courageous persistence. 

Many begin but few continue; many desire balance but lack perseverance. But here as everywhere we must use our brains. Balance is desirable but we must beware of trying to do the impossible. Many show lack of balance in trying to do things for which they are unfitted; have no adequate or sufficient foundation, no 177

natural inclination. Each one should carefully weigh his own life, study his own mind, capabilities, ambitions, and desires, have a plan of life suited to his individual station and requirements, and stick to it. 

The strong personality, ready to meet life-emergencies and take advantage of opportunities, who cannot be discouraged by difficulties, who stands up unafraid and undisturbed, is the man who has his physical, mental, and moral powers sympathetically developed. Of course, such complete development is ideal, which few reach, but some degree of the ideal is possible to all of us and worth striving for. 

The one-sided, poorly developed life is linked with the one-track mind. It is not to live but to lingerto be dependent. To know one thing well is good, very desirable, but to know only one thing is not so good. One should have a central idea in life and pursue it, but he should broaden his life by some secondary activities or he will lack balance. To be content with a very limited understanding of life and its ways is to belong to the unbalanced. To be able to judge only one side of a proposition, to be unable to perceive its relation to other things or its relative importance is not balance; it shows inadequacy. 

In life there are principles of action to be understood and evaluated, definite decisions to be made, ideas, ideals, and theories to be considered, life itself to be judged. How are we to know what is significant, good or bad, right or wrong, useful or useless?

It is sometimes difficult to say-mistake is always possible, but the decisions we make and the conclusions we arrive at will generally depend upon the wisdom that comes from an adjusted mind of balance.

BALANCE A HUMAN NEED 

Now, as we have already said, all things are not equally good, important, or desirable. Neither are all ways or methods or standards of life. What is good, necessary, or advisable for one man in his special situation may not be equally essential or needful for another, but whatever our life, our situation, or our problems, we need balance. 

Men look upon money as the chief possession, the one thing absolutely good and desirable because they think wealth will bring them all the satisfactions of life, which view experience proves to be false. They lack mental balance. "Seek ye first the kingdom of wealth and all other things will be added unto you or not greatly missed," is the popular philosophy of the unbalanced.

The lawyer may be well versed in legal science, but if in his appearance before the court he lacks poise, calmness, tact, self-control, he does not have balance and is at a great disadvantage.

The doctor may know medical science, but if he lacks personal judgment and insight in dealing with individual patients he does not have balance. 

The preacher may know theology, he may even have

the rare gift of knowing how to preach, but if out of the pulpit he lacks the high traits of a man and a gentleman, he does not have balance. I once inquired of a friend concerning his minister. "Well," he said, "he is a good preacher, but such a fool!"

The teacher who lacks imagination, enthusiasm, patience, the knowledge of the history of sciences related to his own, knowledge of life and of the human being, will not display that balance in his teaching that grips the minds of his students. 

The business man may be well up in the knowledge of his own special line, but if he lacks knowledge of men or how to treat his customers, he does not have balance.

The mechanic may be a proficient worker, but if he is without ambition or aspiration he lacks balance and is handicapped.

The women who is "a good housekeeper" may rejoice the heart of her husband and win the praise of her neighbors. Very well. However, if that is the extent of her activities and the scope of her skill and intelligence, she does not greatly magnify her calling as wife and mother. She has neither the knowledge nor the insight to discern the nature or the needs of her children. She should acquire a more balanced personality.

It is this lack of balance that leads to confusion of thought, weakness of understanding, and inefficiency in the work of life. 

Of course, one can spread his efforts out so thin as to achieve neither balance nor usefulness. Some persons are paragons of superficial knowledge. They know or seem to absorb something of everything, but really know little of anything. There is no balance

JUSTICE AND BALANCE 

Consider justice. Why is justice sometimes represented as "blind?" For the simple reason that justice in human action does not have balance. Whenever and wherever it is unbalanced there is injustice and wrong. Where there is no liberty for the individual, no spirit of humanity, no opportunity for adjustment to life, no equitable division of the profits of labor, no encouragement or opportunity for man to grow, develop, and expand in harmony with the spirit of his inner life, justice is a word of irony. The whole of life is unbalanced, disorganized, and disturbed. When justice becomes power-blind, gross, perverse, vindictive, brutal to that extent it ceases to be—it is unbalanced law.

Emerson wrote, "A perfect equity adjusts its balance in all parts of life-the dice of God are always loaded."

Plato says, "The just man does not permit the several elements within him to interfere with one another, or any of them to do the work of others; he sets in order his own inner life and is his own master and his own law and at peace with himself; when he . has become one entirely temperate and perfectly ad-

justed nature, then he proceeds to act, if he has to act, whether in a matter of property or in the treatment of the body, or in some affair of politics or private business; always thinking and calling that which he preserves and cooperates with this harmonious condition just and good action, and the knowledge which presides over it wisdom, and that which at any time impairs this condition he will call unjust action, and the opinion which presides over it ignorance." 

SHOULD HAVE A STANDARD 

To get the most out of life we must have some standard of life action and endeavor to live up to it. 

That demands loyalty and loyalty requires balance. 

The reason that man's life is so tempest-tossed, so turbulent, so disordered and disorganized is because he has no standard of guidance-he is mentally, physically, and morally unbalanced and unfitted for life. 

The less we have of an all-round development the weaker we are to achieve, the more turbulent our lives, and the greater the danger of defeat. 

Balance in life does not imply complacency of repose or attaining the fine feeling of perfection or superiority. It is a practical philosophy of well-being, the result of conscious and intelligent development leading to broader understanding and more intelligent action. Evil and ignorance separate man from effective behavior, throw him out of balance, and destroy life. Without balance we are incomplete, inharmonious, ill-matched, and unequal to the struggle for a successful fight. There is, therefore, no greater human wisdom than attaining balance of life.

"I pray you with all earnestness to prove, and know within your hearts, that all things lovely and righteous are possible for those who believe in their possibility and who determine that, for their part, they will make every day's work contribute to them."-

There is an apostolic admonition, "prove all things, hold fast that which is good." Not indefinite yearning and wishing but action. Now, before one ever starts with earnest purpose of heart to prove or to know, he must have something of a definite conviction that there are things "lovely and righteous" within his possibility of achievement, and which are worth working for. It is this belief that stirs the mind and encourages the heart to attempt and that characterizes the life of useful endeavor. Too many live in a dream world of wistful desire. The kingdom of good does not belong to a superior few; it is open to all, but only those reach it who work and labor to find it. 

John Ruskin and our own well-beloved Ben Franklin were both warm advocates of the philosophy of work, and the heavenly vision occupied much of their thought. It may be questioned, however, whether these worthies did not lay too much emphasis on work as the source of all comfort. Today we are probably getting a wider view of life than "work." Still it holds true that those who wish development of life must put forth brave effort. 

If asked to name the first and most distinguishing mark of a successful life the majority of us would probably say, "The Good Life." And properly so, if the definition is correct. If there is anything higher or better than true goodness that finds its joy in usefulness and balance only hair-splitting philosophers know what it is. Nietzsche thought he knew something better, but the heart as well as the mind of humanity responds to Jesus and not to Nietzsche. 

Might proclaims its right to subdue, crush, and eliminate, but goodness demands the right to live, to love, and to progress. 

Look up the synonyms under "Good" in your dictionary and note the scope and splendor of their meaning. No matter how great the changes in life, goodness never goes out of fashion. Goodness, however, is always active; it may be unobtrusive and unostentatious, but it is never inert. As Emerson said, "What is good is effective, generative, makes for itself room, food, and allies." Goodness and usefulness are joined together by a divine law of life and no man can separate them. To look back upon a life of goodness is to have an invisible sun.

The good man is the man of good-will, possessing the desire and motivated by the purpose to do good, live nobly, and combat wrong and injustice; who is controlled by the wholesome ambition to add his little to the well-being of all men. It is not necessary to tell me that the really useful life is good, that the good life is a useful one, or that the bad life is wasteful and useless. This is so selfevident as to need no argument. The tree is known by its fruit, both as to kind and quality. In some respects the life may be far from ideal, may not be immune from blemish, mistake, and misconception such as characterize all humans, but if goodness has been its governing principle and purpose then its manifestations will inevitably show in acts of benevolence and usefulness. As Amiel wrote in his Journal, "Our duty is to be useful, not according to our desires, but according to our powers," and in the performance of this kind of duty lies individual salvation.

The word "useful" may not be numbered among the so-called "most beautiful words" in our language, but it is a mighty good and expressive word for all that. 

One of the best and most desirable lessons any man learns is the importance and dignity of useful work.

Our life and work may not seem very important or even very profitable, but if grounded in goodness and loyalty it is sure to result in blessing to others and in peace of mind and strength of life to ourselves.

The world, and especially the present age, has small respect or regard for a goodness that isn't usefulnot simply busy, but serviceable. Negative goodness without usefulness is unthinkable, if it is good it is good for something; not a pseudo-goodness that is a sham and a pretense. 

Mr. J. P. Warburg, the wealthy New York banker, recently declared: "I could part with most of the things which I possess without any great heartache.

On the other hand, if I had to strip my life of the interests which have taken such a large share of my time, efforts mostly in the educational field, I would miss them intensely and life would very quickly become very drab and uninteresting."

Life is useful accomplishment, not the possession of things, however much these may contribute to our comfort and delight.

Now, the individual who is not functioning in some serviceable way-whose life has no distinctive mark of goodness and usefulness-is a cumberer of life, a destroyer of his own best interests—a thing existing.

A YARDSTICK OF MEASUREMENT 

Now, in the long run there will always be found a very intimate and real co-relation between a man's character and his deeds. We speak of a noble char-

acter, a good character, a base character-that is, we evaluate a man's deeds and vicariously transfer them to his character. A noble character is one who is wont to perform noble deeds. Thus the solid ground of the moral life is the useful and beneficent character of our work.

The work of the scientist, the painter, the sculptor, the musician, the scholar does not have its genesis in love of money or desire for fame, although it may sometimes bring both. The work may have been an "individual pleasure"-may have been primarily performed for itself alone and without any thought of remuneration or "utility," but if it has character and worth how far-reaching its results and service. The splendid cathedral, the beautiful painting, inspirational poetry, melodious music-how splendid and wonderful in themselves. What a testimony to the native genius and greatness of the mind of man. But is this the extent of their meaning? Not by any means. The high helpfulness, inspiration, and usefulness of these spiritual creations are simply incalculable. They glorify life. The work of the ancient Greeks in art and literature has been a saving power to man's life through all the centuries because of its intrinsic excellence and beauty.

Spiritual life never dies. The mental, moral, and spiritual ideas, ideals, and conceptions of men are as truly real, helpful, and useful as the purely mechanical and so-called practical things of life. The thought and work of the scientist primarily may be to establish and discover the facts of life or the truth of knowledge, but the advancement of knowledge and the establishment of the principles of law and order for man's guidance are valuable agencies in human society and

aid man in his career of usefulness. Nothing that is good and useful exists for itself alone. This is the law of life and the affirmation of experience. Thus can we measure our ordinary lives. No act, work, or belief is true because it is useful, but if it is true it will be useful. 

No man will live unto or for himself entirely if he desires the satisfactions of life. A fruit tree that produces only leaves is valueless except perhaps as a shade tree. It would be difficult to find even a shade-tree value for a useless life.

All things, as well as all lives, are neither good nor useful in precisely the same way, or to the same degree. The work of art is not useful after the manner of kitchen utensils. Nor is the everyday life to be measured with the yardstick of the unique life of genius. Usefulness, however, is a mighty good yardstick of life-measurement, and its existence or nonexistence is pretty fair evidence as to whether we are getting much or little out of life. 

The good life is measured by its usefulness, sincerity, and purpose, and should, therefore, be the aim of all intelligent men and women. It is one of the best ways ever found to make life real and worth living. It is also one of the best methods of securing and keeping an interest in life. It is the way to a happy and contented present. There is no interest or happiness in badness or in idleness, or in leisure that has no object but personal gratification. The idle man is an annoyance to himself and a bore and a burden to everyone else. Whatever our sphere or opportunity we all have capacity for useful service and there is abundant opportunity as well as universal need. 

The delight and joy of life does not begin or end

at 40 or 60 for the man or woman whose spirit is linked in interest and desire with the progress and work of humanity and who finds every day a new day of possibility and fruitfulness. We must discover, cultivate, and put into circulation the hidden resources of life which lie within us in order to give our lives power, purpose, and meaning, and give life the dignity of goodness and intelligent usefulness.

CHAPTER XX THE MINISTRATION OF BOOKS 

"A good book is the precious life blood of a master spirit embalmed and treasured up on purpose to a life beyond life."-Milton.

The influence of books upon the history, thought, life, and manners of mankind has been tremendous, and as subtle and varied as man's needs and interests. 

Books on religion and theology, scientific and mathematical theory, economics, psychology, philosophy, poetry, history, works of imaginative writing, discovery, analysis and criticism, books of the common life giving insight, cheer, comfort, and courage, have all had a very definite and lasting influence in energizing thought and advancing civilization. In the development of our individual lives there is great need to obtain a background of knowledge. This is the mission of good books.
