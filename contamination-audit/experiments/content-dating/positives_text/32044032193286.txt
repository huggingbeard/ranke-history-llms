EUTROPHICATION 

Lakes like almost everything else in nature, begin dying the moment they are born. For a lake, however, this inevitable process takes tens or hundreds of thousands of years unless something speeds it up. 

Something, or rather someone is: Man.

Man, with his ever growing output of wastes and his increasing tendency to meddle with nature by dredging, filling or channelizing lakes or watercourses, has shortened the lives of some lakes to a mere two or three human generations. 

The ageing of lakes--natural or premature--is called eutrophication (ũ trof i ka shun). It means merely that the lake is rich in nutrients, for instance nitrogen and phosphorous. MAN'S INFLUENCE UPON AGEING OF LAKES 

Natural Ageing, just described, takes thousands of years. Man's aggressive crimes against nature can shorten the process to a few tens of years. Poor agricultural and land management practices, sewage and, industrial wastes emptied into rivers and lakes, and the tampering with natural drainage patterns are ways in 3

which man's activities have speeded up the process of ageing.

Croplands which are fertilized regularly are important contributors of manmade nutrient materials into our lakes. 

Excess fertilizers wash into streams and flow into lakes after periods of heavy rains--adding to the natural nutrient loadings. 

Phosphorous and nitrogen, the key elements in fertilizers, are major components of human sewage--even more so now with the increased use of phosphorous-rich detergents for both laundry and dishwashing. Only in a very few ultra modern and up-to-date sewage treatment plants are these nutrients removed. Usually, nutrient-rich sewage effluent is poured into a nearby ditch, stream or lake where the nutrients promote the growth of algae or other aquatic plants and hasten the process of eutrophication. Many kinds of industrial wastes contain nutrients. Again they are removed infrequently and are often poured into nearby bodies of water. 

Impoundments, channelization, removal of fringing marsh areas, and related water management and flood control techniques also can contribute to overenrichment, either by reducing the flow of a waterway or by eliminating the "natural treatment plant" effect of the marsh or swamp. 

Under normal conditions marshes and swamps which frequently border a stream- -

especially in Florida--act as a filter trapping the nutrient material and utilizing much, if not most of it, for growth processes. Little reaches the stream lake. or

Channelization or diking of a waterway eliminates this filtering effect. Nutrients from runoff, from agriculture, or from waste treatment operations, flow directly into the waterway.

Overenrichment occurs. Ageing is speeded up. The death of the lake begins.

CONCLUSION 

Florida's water quality is threaten-

ed by excess nutrients. Even without added nutrient loads from man-made wastes, Florida's phosphorous-rich environment promotes eutrophication. It is doubtful that there are any young oligotrophic lakes in the state.

Several Florida Lakes of which Lake Apopka near Orlando is the most notable-are highly overe..riched today and are near death, or are so bad off they no longer are of any use to man. A project to drain 

Lake Apopka and to restrict the input of nutrients after refilling may put new life into one of Florida's most sadly treated lakes. If successful, it holds hope for other highly overenriched Florida Lakes. 

Advanced waste treatment with nutrient removal soon may become a requirement in many areas of Florida--especially those areas in which wastes are released into lakes. Land disposal spray irrigation Or ground water recharge with treated waste effluents already is used in some areas of the state and nation.
