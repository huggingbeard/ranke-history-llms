Foreword 

California timberland owners and operators have been given the opportunity to regulate their woods activities so as to insure a future source of timber for the people of the State and the continuance of the forest industry. To accomplish these ends the State 

Legislature, in 1945, declared that regulations were needed and wisely decided that the industry itself could best determine what practical actions should be taken to leave the land in a productive condition after logging and to protect present and future forest crops from destruction by fire, insects and disease. 

The North Sierra Pine District (see map, page 1) is one of the four forest districts outlined in the Forest Practice Act, Chapter 85, Statutes of 1945, as amended in 1947. The forest practice rules for this district have been prepared by a committee of forest land owners and operators, have received the voted approval of more than two-thirds the private timberland ownership of the district and were given the force of law by approval of the California State Board of Forestry on 

March 6, 1947.

The type of future the California forest industry is to enjoy is largely dependent upon the success of this program. Realizing this the forest practice committees in each of the forest districts spent several months in careful preparation of the rules. Final success of the program depends upon the operators and the men in the woods. The State Forester is charged with the administration of the Forest Practice Act. 

The State Division of Forestry will cooperate with operators who desire suggestions as to how they might best fit their operations to the rules.

Forest Practice Act 

Besides setting up the machinery for the preparation, approval and enforcement of rules of forest practice for the four forest districts, the Forest Practice Act also provides for amendments to the rules, the submission of alternate plans, and the registration of all timber operators engaged in the cutting and removal of timber or other forest products. 

Amendments will become law after public hearings, and a vote registering the approval of two-thirds of the forest land ownership, and finally, the approval of the State Board of Forestry. They may be initiated by a petition signed by 50 per cent of the private timber and cut-over land ownership. 

Alternate forest management plans may be submitted by forest land owners to the district forest practice committee. Upon receipt of the approval of the committee and the State Board of Forestry, the alternate plan will have the force of law on the property to which it applies.

Registration of timber operators with the State Forester is required annually. The fee for registration is $1 per year. (Ch. 85, Statutes of 1945, amended 1947.)

White Pine Blister Rust Control 

Minimizing damage to young growth sugar pine and leaving a seed source are the only on-the-job actions most loggers can take against white pine blister rust, the five-needle pine disease now in the sugar pine stands in the northern third of California.

Control work, under the direction of the U. S. 

Bureau of Entomology and Plant Quarantine of the U. S. Department of Agriculture, consists of eradicating all gooseberry and currant bushes, because they are necessary for the existence of the disease, which spends part of its life on them. The work is financed by the 

Federal Government, the State of California and contributions from private operators. [24]

Forest Insect Control 

Protection of forests in the North Sierra Pine Forest District against forest insect pests requires special measures to prevent or control attacks. There are certain things the timberland owner or operator can do to forestall insect damage or to minimize its severity; but once an outbreak has developed, the individual owner or operator can do very little on his own and must cooperate with adjoining owners and with the State Forester, as authorized by law, in applying uniform control measures designed to meet the special conditions of the situation. 

All of the commercial forest trees in the district have insect enemies capable of killing from a few trees to a high percentage of the stand. Ponderosa pine, particularly in northeastern California, often suffers heavy damage from the western pine beetle. 

Ips beetles frequently kill large patches of reproduction, saplings, poles and the top of larger trees. Sugar pine and lodgepole pine are killed by the mountain pine beetle; and Jeffrey pine by the 

Jeffrey pine beetle and flatheaded borers. White and red firs are killed by engraver beetles and flatheaded borers; while Douglas fir may be killed by the Douglas fir beetle and flatheaded borers.

Serious insect damage to these commercial forest trees can largely be avoided by following such good sanitation and forestry practices as: 

1. Eliminating insect breeding places by promptly salvaging windfalls, fire-weakened or killed trees, or trees cut and left in the woods; and dying trees currently infested with insect broods. Insect damage cannot be avoided by burning old logs, snags or forest debris, because tree-killing insects never breed or live in such material. 

2. Treating slash from occasional spring and early summer thinnings or logging operations so as to make it unsuitable as insect breeding material. This can be done by lopping and scattering slash so that it will dry out rapidly, or by spraying it with penetrating oil. 

3. Making periodic light sanitation-salvage cuttings of trees of poor thrift and declining vigor which are of "high risk" to beetle attack, and thus keeping forest stands in a healthy, vigorous, growing condition, free from trees which might act as focal centers of infestation. 

4. Applying silvicultural principles to cutting operations so as to remove a high percentage of "high risk" and insect susceptible trees. These are represented by certain of 

Keen's tree classes (as shown on page 16) or Dunning's 

Classes 4, 5, and 7.

If these preventative measures are neglected or fail to stop forest insect outbreaks from occurring, then direct control measures are needed. No blanket rules are applicable to these variable forest insect control problems, and each outbreak calls for individual attention before specific recommendations are made for control.

The California Risk Rating Classification, developed by Salman and 

Bongberg, divides trees of any age or crown conditions into four degrees of risk: (1) low, (2) moderate, (3) high and (4) very high. The degree of risk is measured by symptoms of poor health found in condition of top, twigs, crown and foliage, shortening of needles or poor color. Any tree may become weakened from a number of causes and show these symptoms, which may change rather rapidly. Weakened trees which become "high risk" rarely return to a full vigorous resistant condition. [25]

Volume Tables For convenient reference, the Division of Forestry is including certain volume tables in the appendix. To get net merchantable volume from these tables requires experience, since determinations of merchantable height, defect, accessibility, falling breakage, etc., must be made.

The tables show the rapidity with which the board foot volume of small trees increases with growth. For example, a tree may be 18 inches D. B. H. at 60 years of age and grow four inches in diameter and about double in volume in the next 10 years.
