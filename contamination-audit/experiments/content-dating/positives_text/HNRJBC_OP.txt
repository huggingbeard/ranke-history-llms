CHAPTER II 

Unemployment

One of the first facts that Winthrope More Daniels taught us at Princeton forty years ago is that a nation that does not buy cannot sell. A child should have been able to see this, but the Republicans were less intelligent than children. 

What would be the result of a nation's selling more than it bought? With what would other nations pay the "balance of trade"? Not with gold or other precious metals because (1) other nations soon would exhaust their supply of metals and (2) in the hands of a monopolizing nation the value of such metals would decline through losing their value as mediums of exchange. Obviously the "balance" would have to be paid with goods. Can you not see that fundamentally money is barter? "But", maintained the Republicans of that time, "we do enjoy a favorable balance". We did not. That "balance" was made up by what we paid in the form of interest on American securities owned in Europe, by what we paid English ships to haul our goods, and in other simple forms, which however were too dark and obscure for Republicans' brains.

So, after the First World War the Republicans (1) ignored the fact that Europe was bankrupt, (2) raised tariff rates, and (3) did not warn us to shift from the production of goods we had been exporting to production of new goods for domestic consumption, for which consumption we should have lost no time in educating Americans. (1), (2), and (3) would produce a depression in any man's world. (If there ever has been a man in American history who could unscramble the tariff eggs donated by the Republicans, I believe that man is Cordell Hull; but the dice are loaded against him and are becoming more so every day.

Of course we must not expect Argentina and Germany not to trade with each other, because Argentina overproduces what the U. S. overproduces and what Germany needs. But we can develop South America's rubber, tin, and cinchona.)

Labor went to school to the tariff and other rackets and decided that labor could sell without buying. Accordingly, laborers organized and brought about a situation in which a member of a union could swap one hour's work by himself for several hours' work by a non-union man who was just as honest, intelligent, and industrious as the member. 

If you will examine the facts, you will find that the nonunion labor required for producing enough cantaloupes to buy one radio is several times as much as the union labor required for making the radio. This not only deprives thousands of cantaloupe growers of owning radios but also throws out of work thousands of radio makers who would be enjoying cantaloupes if the hoggishness of their fellow radio makers would let them swap radios for cantaloupes on something like an hour for hour basis.*

The fundamental explanation of the preceding is found in practically any standard text-book on elementary economics where it is pointed out that an unhampered law of supply, demand, and competition fixes the price of an article where the greatest number of transactions will take place. It makes no difference whether prices are "high" or "low" so long as they bear a fair relation to one another. 

But if prices of some articles are artificially high while prices of other articles remain at their normal level, naturally people producing the latter articles will not buy very much from people producing the former, because fundamentally all money is barter. *If cantaloupes don't give a true illustration, make it butter. My critics generally are more interested in my illustrations than in the points illustrated.

If the producers of cantaloupes should organize and swap cantaloupes for radios on an hour for hour basis, the makers of radios would lose the "advantage" they formerly enjoyed. 

They never owed that "advantage" solely to their being organized; they owed it largely to the fact that the cantaloupe growers were not organized. They were grafting on the fact that others of us were not organized, just as our manufacturers of a quarter of a century ago were grafting on the fact that other nations did not employ tariffs. 

If the makers of radios reply that the growers of cantaloupes (sometimes) have gardens, cows, etc., to help out the price of cantaloupes, the answer is yes if the cantaloupe growers choose to milk the cow and hoe the garden after they have done their regular eleven hours' work in the field with cantaloupes, corn, and cotton. 

Shall we abolish labor unions? No, because at present such unions are necessary evils. We should abolish the "evil", and then the "necessary" should vanish automatically. If we should throw some such institution as CCC open to everybody, this would enable a worker to walk out on an unreasonable boss without the worker's having to be a slave to a union. The details of the plan are found in Chapter IV of my "Whither, World?" and Chapter III of my "On Being One's Own Publisher".

Where would we get the money for such a CCC? In the first place, I don't believe very many people will have to apply to CCC for work after we shall have re-established the proper workings of the Law of Supply, Demand, and Competition. In the second place, most of the work done by CCC would be (a) on things that would not have been done by private industry and (b) self-liquidating. In the third place, graduated income and other graduated taxes would raise enormous sums if employers paid their workers too low. Not only would such taxes take care of the hoggish rich but also they should equalize the unfairness between

the radio maker and the cantaloupe grower. Exemption for married couples should be reduced to $1,500, though liberal allowance for dependents should be retained. The majority of voters and legislators are married; therefore, in matter of taxes, army service, and other duties of every citizen the married are pimping the unmarried. 

Our statesmen could learn a great deal by studying the efficiency of Germany, especially of the pre-First World

War Germany of the Kaiser. The government should supervise those of our industries that are necessarily large scale for the purposes of (1) eliminating useless duplication and other waste and (2) enabling a large part of Americans each to own his own little job as did the pre-First World War Frenchman but with a somewhat better material living than that of the Frenchman.

So vast are America's unused natural resources that the American's job could be much more of what some of my books have termed a "Crusoe's Peninsula" than was that of the Frenchman. My one-teacher private school has been an excellent example of such a "peninsula”. Instead of helping individuals to solve unemployment by creating for themselves Crusoe's peninsulas, the New Deal seems to have a spite at the individual who is in business for himself no matter how small the business may be. Let us consider two individuals one of whom worked hard and saved his money while the other demanded a six hour work day and spent what should have been his savings on beer, tobacco, and gasoline for joy riding. The former, whom we shall term an ant, goes into business for himself and in the course of time needs a helper; so, he gives a job to the grasshopper, who was on the edge of starvation when he met the ant. During the weeks, months, and perhaps years in which the business shows a net loss, the ant pays the grasshopper's wages promptly every Saturday. One would think the New Deal would appreciate the ant. That is not the impression I have received. 

At sixty-five the grasshopper retires on an old age pension. A couple of years later the ant becomes sixty-five and retires. He has enough to buy several acres, on which

he builds a cottage and makes a garden, and have an annual cash income of several hundred dollars from investments. 

To his dying day he, though not producing a money paying crop, is taxed that the grasshopper may receive his pension promptly.

But what, you may ask, shall we do about the grasshopper? Would reply that the old poor house gave a bed, bread, and milk; whereas a pension buys beer, tobacco, and gasoline. Also the poor house took the needy; the pension seems to think that there is something magic about the age of sixty-five. It has been my observation that by the time deserving people are sixty-five most of them either have worked themselves to death and are in their graves or have provided for their remaining years. A deserving thirty or forty year old widow who has been left with several children and several thousand dollars by a deserving man, is far more deserving of a pension than is a sixty-six year old pimp. Franz Schubert gave the world more than a billion dollars' worth of music and died virtually of starvation at thirty-two; Robert Burns gave us more than a billion dollars' worth of poetry and died virtually of starvation at thirty-six; Columbus gave the world a new continent and died a pauper; Jefferson gave the world democracy and died bankrupt. Wilson while a teacher applied for a Carnegie pension, for which I condemned him sharply. 

After his death I remembered the reason he gave. The gist of it seemed to be that he wanted to work for humanity and did not want his wife and daughters to be in want as a result of any game he might play. I still doubt the wisdom of his application for a pension; but if Wilson had had the price of radio in his day, it is my opinion that neither the depression nor the present war would have occurred.

If the government would do something about such things, it could force grasshoppers to earn their livings on Crusoe's peninsulas. I know old people who are making their livings on what are virtually Crusoe's islands. I strongly advocate taxing dogs-in-the-manger.

CHAPTER III 

International Affairs

Germany started the First World War. Her excuses were that (1) she needed colonies, or what she termed "a place in the sun", and (2) if she had not hit the first lick England, Russia, and France would have. 

Why did she need colonies? The entire world was open to her emigrants. Until the First War, we of the United 

States not only welcomed but practically solicited Nordic immigrants. We were becoming uneasy at our receiving so many immigrants from Southern and Eastern Europe and wanted Nordics to neutralize them. Other parts of the 

Western Hemisphere were equally open to immigrants from 

Germany. Why, pray, should Germany have to rob France,

Russia, and England of deserts, tundras, and overpopulated regions when the Western Hemisphere with unlimited natural riches and comparatively little population was Germany's for the entry?

If she replies that she did not like the Western Hemisphere's inefficiency, I was one of the many Americans who deplored that inefficiency and begged my country to study 

German efficiency. One of my reasons for wanting millions of Germans to emigrate to the United States was that I wanted them as allies to help me induce America to employ governmental supervision of large scale industries for the purposes of eliminating useless duplication and other waste and enabling the majority of Americans each to own his own little job somewhat as did the Frenchman of a quarter of a century ago.

Perhaps Germans will reply that our tariffs kept her from exchanging her goods for ours. (1) Latin America and 

Canada had virtually no tariffs at that time and (2) we followers of such Democrats as Cleveland and Wilson were trying hard to reduce our tariffs while most German-

Americans were voting the Republican ticket. (3) A nation has no right to spawn more babies than it can feed and then expect other nations, which are trying to maintain decent standards of living, to conform to the baby spawners' ideals. 

I admit that the populations of rich countries that are not overcrowded become fat, lazy, complacent, soft, and corrupt. America owes her past successes, not to the superiority of her people but, to her natural wealth. On the other hand, Germany and Japan owe their points of superiority, not to the superiority of their people but, to natural unproductiveness of their land and overpopulation that forced Germans and Japanese to be frugal, energetic, and efficient. 

Place Americans in Germany and Japan and place Germans and Japanese in America, and within a generation or two the Americans would be efficient and the Germans and Japs would be soft.

From the preceding it is obvious that we are all in the same world together and should cooperate; but examine history and you will note (1) that Germans don't seem to enjoy a thing unless they get it by fighting and (2) that they were not satisfied with becoming equal citizens in the melting pots of the Western Hemisphere but wanted to run the Western Hemisphere as directed from Berlin. Now, we of English and Scotch descent have no idea of letting the British Empire run our government for us; but we are used to Anglo-Saxon ideals, prefer to keep them until German-Americans or some other bona fide American citizens can change them by democratic balloting, believe we can vindicate them in debate, and (I hope) still have guts enough to fight for them.

As to Germans' claim that England, Russia, and France were planning to attack her, when we Americans replied that if Germany had waited until this was proved our sympathy and cooperation would have gone to Germany, Germans forgot the claim and contemptuously replied that they

could win the war without America's sympathy and cooperation.

Also again, for years before the First World War German army officers drank to "Der Tag". Just what was "Der Tag"? 

Also still again, Germany's conduct of the Second World

War makes her the wolf who, when he could not find an excuse for eating the lamb, said, "I'll just have to eat you without an excuse".

Within their own "greatest number" Germans are perhaps the most ethical-minded people on earth. Their socialmindedness could well be copied by Americans. The trouble is that Nordics are their "greatest number" in the ethical criterion, greatest good of the greatest number. 

Within their own "greatest number" I have reason to believe that Germans are more honest than Americans. (If they are not, they are damned crooks.) Many Latin Americans say they would rather do business with Germans than Americans, not only because Germans give them better bargains but also because Americans are worse cheats than Germans. For instance, Latins say an American will cause them to stock up on American goods and then send Americans into the field as competitors with the Latins. They say the American will even set the price at which the Latins must retail American goods and that then the Americans will undersell their own customers. The Latins say that big Americans exploiters of Latin resources resort to every imaginable form of tax-dodging. And we all know that is exactly what our big American business concerns did to their own government back in "the glorious days" of Republican rule. With these divinely appointed guardians doing such things to their own countrymen, it stands to reason that they would do the same to their weaker neighbors if they could get away with it.

Then we have England's forcing opium on China and her war on the South African Boers.

But that war was forty years ago, and even then England's treatment of the conquered Boers showed an encouraging contrast with the North's treatment of the conquered South in our own country. A few of us in our own country and in other democracies really were trying to make this a better world by democratic methods, an attempt that would put us into concentration camps in the totalitarian countries.

Parenthetically, I cannot understand why English planes do not shower the German people with leaflets asking, "Why do your rulers refuse to let you read and tune in on what other peoples have to say, as do England's rulers?"

There never will be peace in this world until racial conceit shall have been abolished. One of the most fortunate experiences of my life is that I was reared literally in two churches (Southern Baptist and self satisfied Presbyterian) and virtually in three others (rural Methodist, Catholic, and the "non progressive" branch of the "Campbellite", or "Christian"). I know it to be a fact that millons of children in the Southern Baptist Church were reared to believe that the Baptist was the only right church and that rural Methodists believed that the Protestant was the only right church and that non-progressive Campbellites would rather proselyte than convert; and I have every reason to believe that millions of American Catholics are still being reared in the belief that the Catholic not only is the best church but also that Catholics should remain aloof from the American melting pot; and I also have reason to believe that Jews are even more aloof and that, while they are beginning to doubt that God told Abraham he was It, they are nevertheless sure that God Himself is a Jew.

I was a Democrat automatically, and when I reached Princeton I discovered that my classmates not only were

Republicans automatically but that with all the self satisfaction of Presbyterians and Episcopalians they were convinced that Republicans, or patricians, were the divinely appointed guardians of others. Be it said to the glory of our professors, they were liberal in both religion and politics; and I never shall forget the spoiled baby indignation of some of my classmates when the professors would exhibit broad-mindedness.

Now, if youths reared in America, where we have freedom of discussion and can read anything we choose, should grow up jelled, what is to be expected in Europe, where 

Arthur E. Morgan says the envies, jeolousies, and hates, are ancient, many, and varied?

Hitler is on the wrong track. Europe must become the melting pot that we once thought America was. Oh, I am willing for small fractions of races to remain "pure", in order that every "pure" race and every melting pot may serve as "yardsticks" to one another. I have read that it is, or was, a penitentiary offense to take any stock of cattle except jersey to the Island of Jersey, but we know that elsewhere jerseys may be mixed with other breeds. Similarly, I am willing for the states north of the Rhine and the Upper Danube to be "pure" German, for the Japs to have the Islands of Japan, for the Chinese to have China, for the negroes to have a large state in Africa, etc.; and then 

I want the other countries to be melting pots. I am a great believer in "yardsticks". 

Here I must issue a warning to the American negro. Just as the Jew carries pride of race to extreme, so does the American negro carry lack of pride of race to extreme. I once belonged to a club of thirty members in which the most popular member was a Jew; and when we spoke of him as "that damned Shenee", he seemed complimented. I have known Dutchmen who, when asked what is the difference between an Amsterdam Dutchman and any other

damn Dutchman, would cuss but take it in good spirit. I have known Irishmen who seemed pleased when I would begin a story with, "Once there was an old Irishman" and end with a joke by no means complimentary to Irish intelligence. But the American negro is so racially sensitive that he is a nuisance. The only way to get racial equality and racial superiority is to begin by having a pride of race. 

Parenthetically, I'll fight to give the negro economic equality. Granted that, he should tell social equality to go to hell. 

When he does this, social equality will arrive automatically. 

I wish a few Jews would marry negroes, that I might see whether their offspring would suffer from racial conceit or inferiority complex.

Returning to Germany, the Allies could have destroyed 

Germany in 1918. But Germany asked for mercy and received it. The Versailles Treaty was far milder than what 

Germany had planned for the Allies, and it was gradually softened as memories of German atrocities faded into the background. What has been Germany's appreciation of that mercy? The Second World War, in which she has lied and stolen and boasted of it.

England dared not back the United States when Stimson was trying to keep Japan from raping China, because (1) the United States, like Russia, is a quitter who walks out in the middle of jobs and (2) England had a rearmed Germany at her rear. France dared not help England to keep 

Italy from raping Ethiopia because a rearmed Germany was at France's rear. No one could help Finland against 

Russia because Germany was cultivating the cooperation of the world's scoundrels. Wherever we find hell, we find Germany at the bottom of, or at least connected with, it. 

Parenthetically, this makes our isolationists, pacifists, and sob sisters generally, allies of hell. H. R. Cromwell delighted us by terming them trojan jackasses. A friend

of mine terms them Hitler's-suckers. But in the final analysis, “allies of hell" seems to me to describe them best; for, Germany's occupation all down the centuries has been to "lift the lid off hell".

Races should be encouraged to intermarry, and their offspring should be taught to think of themselves as belonging, not to either race but, to both. A simple world language should be created and taught in all schools. Some kind of league of nations should enforce freedom of discussion in all nations. A citizen should be allowed to tune in his radio on anybody and-Massachusetts take note-to buy and read any book.

The United States should maintain bureaus in other countries to examine prospective immigrants and should pass only those who give promise of accepting the melting pot in good faith. Females of races that do not mix readily with whites should be refused entry for permanent residence, but those already here should be allowed to remain if they accept the melting pot according to their opportunities. 

If I were a Frenchman and should be asked from what country I hailed, I suspect I should borrow from the 

Arkansan and reply, "France; now laugh, damn you!" Yet the United States is in exactly the condition France was in at the outbreak of the war. France's government was in the hands of patricians, and her productiveness was in the hands of organized labor. From 1920 to 1932 (and most of the preceding seventy-five years) the United States was run for the benefit of patricians, and since 1932 it has been run for the benefit of labor rackets. Alabama erected a statue to the boll-weevil; for similar reasons America some day may erect a statue to Adolph Hitler. 

English patricians gave us Munich; American patricians of 1920-28 gave us the depression and the Second World

War; God only knows what the patricians of today, who include John L. Lewis and William Green, will give us. Which brings us to

Conscription. I have heard colossal lies in my day. One, for instance, is that Wilson "promised to keep out of war" when he made no such promise. But he did tell Germany that America "would omit no word or deed" if she persisted in her unrestricted submarine policy, and America heard him. Moreover, it was Lodge and followers who called him a coward when he did not demand war at the sinking of the Lusitania and at many other points. The 

Republicans' policy was to find what Wilson wanted and then to oppose it. I am far from approving the New Deal, but the Republicans' policy is to find what Roosevelt wants and then oppose it.

Of all the lies I have heard, perhaps the most colossal is that conscription is "undemocratic"; for, the volunteer system is just about the most undemocratic thing I can imagine. Why should one man volunteer to fight for the preservation of another man's liberties? For instance, organized labor probably has more to lose through Hitlerism than any other group; yet labor wants farmers and professional men and clerks to fight to preserve labor's racket. 

Not only must we have universal conscription, if and when we need an appreciable addition to our army, but "exemptions" should be virtually abolished. The American woman knows how to support a family. Millions of them are doing it for pimp fathers right now. All the more easily can the average wife of a soldier do this if given a small pension. And as to "essential" occupations, see the paragraph on "promotions" on page 25. Even if a man truly is essential to a factory, let him first be drafted into the army and then be loaned to the factory. "Essential" occupations are just another labor union racket.

Another group who are trying to retain a racket are college undergraduates. These youngsters have lived with silver spoons in their mouths until it has never occurred to them that they should contribute. Their job is to receive. 

Don't be deceived by the claim that many are "working their way through college". If a boy sells a package of cigarets on a commission, he is classed as "partially self supporting". A friend of mine told me he earned $2.60 at 

Princeton and went to New York seven times on the proceeds. But I would let a good student finish out a year. 

Every man, woman, child, and piece of property should be registered (not necessarily drafted). Youngsters should be allowed to remain in school if they study hard. If they loaf they should be drafted to do civilian work under military discipline.

There are plenty of things that I, sixty-one and suffering from a chronic ailment, could do if unhampered by the smug, complacent, hidebound traditions of red tape and professionals. I regard Henry Ford and Charles Lindbergh as spoiled babies if not "nuts"; but I understood exactly

Ford meant when he said he could turn out a thousand planes a day if allowed to do it his way. Lindbergh also understands such methods.

Until six months ago, the French army was considered the best in the world because the individual soldier was trained to ask "What is essential?" and to use his own judgment. He was defeated because his leaders asked, "What does tradition advise?" while the German's leaders were asking, "What is essential?"

The one time when I was really proud of Princeton was when young Gorin wrote "Patriotism Prepaid", which advocates bonuses to future veterans so that they can enjoy the bonuses before being killed in war. Now I discover that many of Gorin's followers intended to collect in ad-

vance and then refuse to fight. I hereby apologize for the uncomplimentary things I said of the veterans of the First World War, because it seems that they are going to have to fight two wars because the advocates of bonuses in advance refuse to fight one. Parenthetically, it is hard for me to believe that babies of men who marry to escape conscription are not born with yellow stripes down their backs.

By the way, I am a conscientious objector. My conscience forbids me to fight to retain liberty for those who refuse to fight for themselves. Therefore I am advising young men not to volunteer unless they have their own selfish reasons for doing so. Parenthetically, would a professed agnostic be exempted if his conscience forbade him to fight?

Either we must buy an island on the other side of the earth and send to it those who "refuse to fight", thereby leaving them to face Hitler without an intervening group who stand between them and Hitler or we must swap those Americans who refuse to fight to Hitler for those Czechs and others who are raring to go. We who will fight, should not be left standing between those who refuse to fight and Hitler.

Now, if the youngsters' objection to fighting is fear that 

Gerald Nye, Rush Holt, Bennett Clark, Ham Fish, Boake

Carter, and Charles Lindbergh will do a Leopold or throw away the fruits of victory after a second war as did Lodge, Borah, Johnson, Reed, and the Park Avenue resident who said she favored a league to prevent war but that she would would wreck the world to keep Wilson from getting any credit for it, I don't blame them. I myself, if drafted, intend to demand promise of satisfactory after-victory proceedings and a guarantee that those making the promise are not lying. 

How do the candidates compare on foreign affairs? Will-

kie and Roosevelt both are sound in expressed policies; but 

Roosevelt and Hull have the record, while Willkie has only words and, at times, contradictory words. 

We must give this chapter to the Democrats. 

Can England defeat Germany? "On paper" the answer is no. Actually, England is licking Germany and has been doing so consistently from the fight in Belgium to this date, Sept. 20.

Can England indefinitely endure the pounding London is receiving if after the destruction of London the pounding is shifted to other English cities? Again the answer is no.

England must take mastery in the air from Germany and carry the offensive into Germany.

Here the United States may hold the deciding power. If we shall furnish England unlimited planes, ships, ammunition, etc., England should win if she can hold through this winter and if our isolationists can be kept from throwing wrenches into the machinery indefinitely. 

Of course the time may come when we shall have to extend credit to England, which means gift unless we accept territory in payment. And the time may come when I shall advise our young men to enlist in Canada's army for such work as aviation. I should be doing that right now but for my prejudice against seeing thoroughbreds die that the followers of Lewis and Green may "yield none of the gains made by labor" and that undergraduates may continue to go to New York on week-ends when not attending dances. at Vassar and Smith.

We cannot afford to let England lose; and when England wins, Germany must be rendered incapable of continuing her at least two thousand years of lifting the lid off hell. 

In case of victory shall we destroy Germany? Among

points for the negative is the fact that the democracies and China seem to need Germany and Japan for the same reasons for which shortsighted dogs need fleas. 

In case Germany wins and the worst we fear becomes realized, I here remind Americans to remember this: you asked for it. Walking out on Wilson without getting a substitute is merely one of our blunders. Our late so termed neutrality law that caused manufacturers of war implements to shut down when they should have been going at full blast is another.

Sept. 22.-I have just read Willkie's six point program on foreign affairs delivered at San Francisco. I agree with it; and it is fairly definite, thanks to the fact that it is what a majority of Americans, including Roosevelt, want. 

Sept. 27.-Time Magazine has just arrived and contains some excellent extracts from the San Francisco speech that were omitted by our local papers. I wish the Republicans were not such cowards and would put their man on the air. 

Would our entry into the war bankrupt us? Probably but not necessarily. If we should put our ten millions of unemployed to work by the system of promotions mentioned on page 25, if we would cease to buy needless things, and if we would institute efficiency, we should be able to win the war and be in better fundamental condition at its close than now.

As present indications are that Roosevelt will remain in Washington, I must point out a few facts to him. Recently 

I saw a picture of a superintendent showing a friend over a factory. They were in a room containing five people. The superintendent said, "These are our book-keepers; this one attends to the firm's business, the other four attend to our dealings with the government".

When social security was first instituted, a book-keeper friend of mine wrote to the government asking whether his institution would be included. He received a negative reply, which he wisely filed. Two years later the government's local office informed him that he had been neglecting the law, that all of the institution's employees would have to pay back dues with two years' interest at six per cent. The book-keeper produced the government's letter, and the official replied that the government could not be responsible for an error on the part of one of its clerks. The bookkeeper replied that he owned government bonds that paid him only three per cent. On what grounds did the government demand six per cent on its own error? The official replied that that was no affair of his. My friend knew some law and replied flatly that he would not pay his six per cent. 

But he had to get in touch not only with all of his institution's employees but also with those who had quit during the past two years. Some of them did not have the dues and interest. Others were on the point of taking a much needed vacation, which they would lose if they paid. 

After a year or so of this the government decided that its first reply was correct, that the institution did not come under social security, upon which my friend had to refund all he had collected and fill out countless complicated forms a second time.

Let us say the publication and distribution of this brochure will cost me three hundred dollars and that I shall sell thirty dollars' worth. How shall I mention it on my income tax? If I ask our local office they will frown, groan, and tell me to divide three hundred by a thousand (copies published), call that cost, and subtract it from the price of the book. If I mention that I shall begin by sending out several hundred gift copies with personal letters, they will tell me to subtract that as advertising. If I add that I'll have several hundred left on my hands, most of which I'll

give to the Salvation Army as waste paper, they virtually complain that I never should have begun such an annoying and complicated business. 

To save myself annoyance, I don't mention the books on my tax sheet, thereby giving the government what could have been deducted as loss. In swearing to the truth of my report I add, "except that in annoying figures I rendered general estimates, making sure the discrepancy is in the government's favor", and the tax office appear to think that is correct provided I can prove the discrepancy favors the government.

Let us assume that some department of the government takes a dislike to something I write and demands an itemized account on my publishing business. Will the government then refund what I have been giving it all these years? 

Probably not. It may even tell me I broke the law. More than frequently, various administrations have been accused of employing such acts as punishment which they can not employ so directly as does Hitler.

Some years ago the government demanded my income for a year five years previous. I made an estimate and discovered that I should have paid $3.15 when I had paid nothing. After five or six hours of exasperating work, I noted on that year's (private school) roll the name of a boy to whom I had given the tuition. If I had been challenged within two years of the year in question, I should have spotted this at once.

Is a government like that sufficiently efficient to lick Hitler? Willkie says no, and then he expresses sympathy for those who stage sit-down strikes while the dictators have a gun at Uncle Sam's ear.

CHAPTER IV Traditions 

In normal times-sound arguments can be presented in favor of one presidential term of six years, sound arguments can be presented for only two terms of four years each, and sound arguments can be given in favor of reelecting a man indefinitely so long as he gives good service. 

The arguments for each of these so nearly equals the arguments for each of the other two, in normal times, that it would be hard to decide which is best. 

These are not normal times. Therefore, the don't swap horses while crossing a stream tradition outweighs the two two term tradition. Most Republicans admit that had 

Lincoln been up for a third term in 1864 he should have been given the third term, and most Democrats admit that had Wilson been up for a third term in 1916 he should have been given the third term. Most Independents admit that 

Roosevelt should be given a third term in 1940 if Roosevelt is the best man we can find for the job. Whether he is the best man, belongs to this entire work and not to this chapter only. I am far from saying Roosevelt is the best man for the job; but I believe I can prove that if on his own merits he deserved forty-six states in 1936 he deserves all fortyeight in 1940-assuming that Willkie's speeches yet to be made are no better than his speech of acceptance. Granting no difference between Landon and Willkie, there is more reason for reelecting Roosevelt in 1940 than there was for reelecting him in 1936; and there was more reason for reelecting him in 1936 than for electing him in 1932. The biggest reason for electing him in 1932 was Hoover, the biggest reason for reelecting him in 1936 was Landon, I hope the biggest reason for reelecting him in 1940 does not prove to be Willkie.

Another tradition Willkie is appealing to is the old custom

of face to face joint debates. Debating is my hobby, but a joint debate can be conducted over the radio, with several days intervening between a speech and the reply, better than face to face because it gives each debater time in which to size up his opponent's last speech. Willkie has laid himself open to the suspicion that he intended to capitalize the fact that a fool can ask more questions in a minute than a wise man can answer in a thousand years, at the same time not giving Roosevelt time to go home and look up the answers. For instance, I suggested that Willkie ask to what extent the work of WPA is expected to be self-liquidating. Roosevelt should be given time to present a statement. 

Willkie can point out that in seven years of peace and with unlimited billions of dollars Roosevelt failed to solve unemployment and the depression. Then he can ask, Should we trust Roosevelt's (lack of ?) executive ability to conduct a possible if not probable war when he has failed in peace? I hope he does ask this. But Roosevelt must be given time to prepare an answer.

A face to face debate gives an unfair advantage to the debater who is feeling physically well at the time of the debate. Willkie is younger, stronger, and has less responsibility than Roosevelt. Therefore it seems a little unsportsmanlike for him to challenge his opponent to a physical endurance contest. 

Suppose Roosevelt agreed to a date for a debate and then a matter of state necessitated his canceling it. The opposition would say he lost his nerve. Correctly did Ickes say that the President could not adjourn the Battle of Britain to keep a date with a politician.

I said a radio debate with several days intervening between speeches and replies disseminates truth better than does a face to face debate. Can it be that Willkie is afraid of true debate, and wanted to forestall it by challenging an

older and weaker man to a physical endurance contest? 

If Willkie will tell me what he will do and how he will do it, he will get my vote if his what and how impress me as being better than Roosevelt's whats and hows. I can add that I rate some of Roosevelt's whats and hows very low. 

But at this writing Willkie has not shown enough superiority to justify our swapping horses in a crisis.

CHAPTER V The Candidates Personally 

For some years there has been in Texas a flour salesman named Lee O'Daniel. On the side he gave us hillbilly songs and the Ten Commandments over the radio. Both of these appealed to a large class of Texans. One day he expressed his disgust at professional politicians and remarked that he believed even he would make a better governor than any of them. His hearers called his hand by telling him to enter the race. He knew nothing about the job, and I believe he had reasons for preferring not to run for the office. But he lacked the nerve to back out, ran, and received more votes than eleven opponents combined. 

Two years in office proved, what he himself had feared, that he knew nothing about the job; and had the professionals given him a square deal he might not have run for a second term. But he was persecuted even when he happened to be right and responded by seeking a second term. 

He had five opponents. As my pencil poised over the names of the six candidates, I was unable to vote for any one of them. Had the law required me to vote, I should have voted for O'Daniel; but, being free, I simply could not vote for any of the six. Without my help O'Daniel got more votes than the other five combined. 

In 1928 I realized Herbert Hoover's limitations but voted for him because I feared Smith's views on immigration and birth control. He proved even weaker than I had feared and in addition to weakness ignored the Hoovercrats to whom he owed his election. He seemed deaf to all except those who Wilson said imagine they are the divinely appointed guardians of the rest of us, commonly known as patricians or autocrats.

In 1932 I eagerly awaited Roosevelt's speech of accept-

ance. It proved devoid of constructiveness. He said he would "experiment". As I had just seen a movie in which the girl, having been made pregnant by the villain, married the villain "because there was nothing else to do", I voted for Hoover a second time on the grounds that the country was pregnant by twelve years of Republicans and might as well as stick to ills we knew as flee to those we knew not. 

Roosevelt's first experiments caused me to rave; but since then I have learned that at the time he took office parts of the nation probably were within several months of revolution, and I have mollified my criticisms a little. Nevertheless I should have voted for Landon if he had shown an understanding of conditions and an independence of Republicans. As he showed neither, I again stuck to ills I knew of and voted for Roosevelt in 1936.

CHAPTER VI Fundamentals 

According to the latest figures I have received, the United States contains fifteen millions of high school graduates, three millions of college graduates, and half a million or so who have pursued even higher academic education; yet the nation is a flop in its internal economics, its international relations (in spite of first Stimson's and then Hull's heroic efforts to put us on the right track), its ethical standards, and its spiritual ideals. I am confident that ten thousand truly educated individuals could have made the United States an example of successful democracy that would have forestalled the advent of dictators if the ten thousand could have found channels through which to reach one another and the general public. But we didn't have the ten thousand and we didn't have such channels. When young I found it hard to believe that Abraham could not find ten good men in Sodom and Gomorrah. Now I wonder whether there are ten truly educated men in the United States. 

In light of the failures of a nation that had the world's richest natural resources in proportion to its population, no dangerous neighbors until recently, and the most schoolroom education in history, do you not think it is time we changed our definition of education?

The first requirement for a child's true education is parents whose education has been sufficiently generalized to enable them to give the child in its earliest years the right bird's-eye-views. The following is quoted from my brochure, "Paid Tellers Don't Tell": 

Six blind men went to "see" an elephant. That night the one who had felt the elephant's side expressed surprise that an elephant should be so much like a wall. The one who had embraced a leg corrected the first with the remark that an elephant is very much like a tree. He who had grasped the

tail said an elephant is very much like a rope; the one who had grasped the trunk said snake expressed the elephant better than rope; the one who had stroked a tusk said the elephant was like a spear; and he of the ear experience likened the elephant to a fan. An argument ensued that ended in a free for all fight.

When the six awoke the next morning they were amazed and delighted to discover that their sight had been miraculously restored while they had slept. After a few minutes of rejoicing one raised the question as to what object they should go to see. A man who knew of their argument about the elephant suggested that they give an elephant their first inspection. This seemed to scare them. Not one of the six seemed in a hurry to see a real elephant. But under pressure from the man who had suggested it, they set out for the zoo. 

It so happened that the very first animal to attract their attention was an elephant. Upon their asking the keeper what it was he told them. Immediately all six denounced the keeper as a liar; and when he refused to retract, they attacked and might have injured him had the elephant not interfered. The men were still blind-intellectually. On partial views they might differ among themselves to the point of burning one another at the stake; but they were a hundred per cent united that the man who had seen a whole elephant was a liar, a heretic, an infidel, hated by God, and deserving of immediate extermination. 

The world contains thousands of different religions, thousands of different political parties, thousands of different definitions of education, and each blindly assumes that it is right; but they are united in the belief that the man who has seen a whole elephant is wrong. Therefore, in concluding the Willkie vs. Roosevelt argument, this writer will, as usual, expose himself to the charge of "not sticking to his subject" by pointing out to the candidates that their views on and what they will do about education and dissemination of truth are far more fundamentally necessary to their equipment for the job they are seeking than are their views on such comparatively superficial subjects as the two-term tradition. Indeed we never shall solve even such fundamental problems as unemployment and the end-

ing of war until we shall have solved the still more fundamental problems underlying a correction of our definition of education.

In addition to being generalized, parents and teachers must be able to discriminate or separate the essential from the non-essential, the important from the unimportant. My former publications have harped on this incessantly, but I wish to add some up-to-date illustrations. In my book, "An Outline for Self Education", published back in 1926 when some thought we had ended war and made the world safe for democracy, I ask, What is "essential" to successful conduct of war? and reply, "I would instruct my Henry

Fords to exercise a speed that would smother the enemy with numerical superiority of aeroplanes, artillery, and (if permissible) poison gas. These I believe now to be the 'essentials'."

But on another page in the same book I add prophetically, "Would Henry Ford have pulled his peace ship stunt if he had not been living to the tune that history is bunk?” 

In "Whipped America", published well before "Munich", I answer, What is essential? with, "I think the most decent thing Franklin D. Roosevelt ever did was his 'quarantine' speech; but entirely aside from that, the best way to keep bullies in their place is to be able to lick them. If we are going to insist on 'splendid isolation', we should write our tariffs to make our country self-sustaining in case of blockade, should perfect 'on paper' plans, should fill the air with planes and the sea with submarines, should fortify a hundred or so of our most important harbors, should have at least a few floating fortresses called battleships, should perfect the anti-aircraft gun, should have at least the foundations for unlimited munition factories, should teach war in our schools and CCC camps. I admit it is a shame so to waste good money; but you asked for it when you lied about a war to end war.”

I regret exceedingly that I neglected to add in print what I had said often, that the next war would be won by coordination of different branches of warfare, though I should confess that I always illustrated this with a naval battle such as Traflagar. Just as an expert football quarterback "mixes 'em up" as he calls plays, so would I have battleships, cruisers, submarines, destroyers, and planes to cooperate in battle. It remained for the Germans who by nature are so "dumb" that they must ask, "What is essential?", to coordinate in land battles.

When Roosevelt denied having said that the Rhine was our frontier, I published that if he didn't say it, I'd say it for him. At this writing the English Channel is our frontier. 

If our trojan jackasses have their way, the Rio Grande and the St. Lawrence soon will be our frontier. 

But the point I want to note is that, while we may not agree with the Germans' aim, or what ethics terms "greatest number", we must admit that they know how to ask and answer, "What is essential to the attainment of our aim?"

The application of "What is essential?" to education in general is discussed in my works on Self Education. The person who asks this question, "works from the center" of his subject and thereby avoids wasting effort and time on those major, sub, and sub-sub branches that are non-essential to his objectives. A boy making a radio or an aeroplane asks "What is essential?" and thereby wastes little time.

Fifty years ago I made water-run mills, boats, and tents by asking "What is essential?" Then I went to college and almost flunked physics, chemistry, psychology, and economics by following the professors instead of staying ahead of them with "This is essential to what?" and "What is essential to this?" 

Once an eleventh grader asked me to prepare him for

Yale's entrance examination in physics. I told him I knew no physics but that we would get books and recite, I with the book opened and he with it closed. He passed. I had learned to ask for essentials. Since then my success in putting boys through physics examinations has been such that I receive requests to tutor in the subject and should receive many such if it were not known that I would refuse, though even now I could not make twenty-five per cent on a College Board examination on physics if my book were closed.

While I was teaching the eleventh grader, there was in my school a seventh grader who flunked Latin and algebra but who worked continually in his father's physics laboratory. When the eleventh grader and I got stuck, the seventh grader would help us out. He had studied physics the essential and self education route. 

Thus "This is essential to what?” and “What is essential to this?" guide us to simplicity; while theologians, pedagogues, doctors, lawyers, carpenters, repair men and other professionals steer us into complexity. Complexity makes more and bigger jobs for specialists. 

Self discipline in "This is essential to what?" and "What is essential to this?" gives one a thought background that enables him to form responsible opinions for himself. Aside from prejudices, few people have either the ability or the courage to do their own thinking. I do not know to what extent Fort Worth represents America in this, but all that Fort Worth knows is what somebody tells it; and the "bigger" the somebody, the more blindly Fort Worth believes. 

If elephants and whales could talk, they would run Fort Worth.

Parenthetically, American education is ceasing to give even an information background. My parents quit school in the ninth and tenth grades; yet they instantly could

catch practically any allusion to the Bible, Pilgrim's Progress, Shakespeare, Scott, Dickens, the ancient philosophies. 

The average college graduate can't do this. 

When I first began to publish works like this one, I was surprised that readers found them difficult reading. Then 

I discovered that modern Americans, including college graduates, possessed little information background and practically no really deep thought background. 

Finally, "This is essential to what?" improves one's sense of proportion and removes pettiness from his character. 

When Jugurtha left Rome, he shook his fist at it and said, "Venal city, you would sell yourself if you could find a bidder"; and we can't pronounce Jugurtha a "sore head", because shortly after that Rome fell as a result of its venality. I studied Shakespeare's Julius Caesar in prep school, in freshman at college, and in junior, and have taught it thirty-eight years. America looks like a print of Rome to me.

In addition to being (1) generalized and (2) able to discriminate, parents must (3) know the value of thoroughness. Thoroughness, or knowledge of where what one knows stops and where what he does not know begins, creates executive ability in one, especially if he takes part in fact-marshaling, issue-joining debate and now and then writes a long composition in preparation for which he had to seek, find, select, organize, marshal, focus, and hammer facts for the accomplishment of practical objectives. 

Not only should youngsters even in the lower grades be held to thoroughness, but high school students should be required to take examinations written and graded by educators who in no way are connected with the student or his teachers. The papers should be signed with a number, thereby concealing the identity of the student from the grader.

My school has received boys who had been making C's and D's in a high school and who, though they swore I was working them to death, had to attend my "Saturday schools" to make up poor work. Then when they returned to high school and made A's and B's, they reported that the "high school had made great improvement". They still didn't know that they didn't know.

The College Entrance Examination Board is an example of what I mean. I wish the government would issue a high school diploma. I am a great believer in "yardsticks". The government might arrange with the College Board to write and conduct examinations; but while the Board would continue to grade papers for the colleges, it should send the papers seeking a government diploma to the government, which would employ examiners to grade absolutely. That is, if the candidate correctly answered seven of ten questions, his grade would be seventy. ("Sliding scale" grading is a racket.)

If the candidate took examinations in the twenty-one units I shall outline below, making not less than fifty on any unit and not less than seventy-five on (a) his major subject and (b) his general average, the government should issue him a diploma.

This diploma would be a "yardstick" to be compared with diplomas issued by schools. I think there should be public schools and private schools, socialized medicine and privately practicing physicians, government supplied lawyers for defense as well as for prosecution and privately practicing lawyers, several railroads run by the government and several owned and managed by the operatives and the rest owned and managed by capitalists. And the government should not fudge by subsidizing its railroads: it should make its roads pay their own way. 

I shall now outline a twelve year course that I believe

offers all the school room education needed. (In other words, I think ninety, if not ninety-nine, per cent of our colleges should be abolished.) The first seven years should give a mastery of such tools as the three R's and a general knowledge of geography, U. S. History, and grammar. The Germans' use of their knowledge of geography is an important explanation of their success in war. 

The high school curriculum covers five years. As it is assumed the student will have four studies per year, I shall devote a single paragraph to each of his four "courses". 

The high school curriculum now follows: 

1. Two years of algebra, one of plane geometry, one of solid geometry and plane trigonometry, leaving the fifth year for physics. 

2. Four years of ancient, medieval, modern, and a combination English-U. S. history, leaving the fifth year for economics principles. In the history special attention should be given to (a) economic development, thereby leaving the straight economics year uncluttered by anything that would detract attention from principles and (b) why races grow old, sicken, and decay. 

3. Five years of English. The student's mastery of grammar should be made perfect, and a simple text on rhetoric and composition should be studied during the second or third year. My favorite was Butler's "School English", now apparently out of print. Genung's Rhetoric also was so good that I imagine it is out of print. Each year the student should take part in several of the debates and should write several of the long compositions mentioned above under executive ability. Each year he should study two examples of choice literature on class and each vacation he should read several. If two samples of literature per year on class are not sufficient for making the pupil become a reader, then there is something wrong with either him or his teach-

ers. For the fall term of his first year I recommend a book of selected short poems; for the spring term, an interesting prose story. Fall of second year, Scott's Lady of the Lake; spring, a prose story. Fall of third year, Burns' poems followed by Carlyle's Essay on Burns; spring, Franklin's Autobiography. Fall of fourth year, Burke's Speech on Conciliation coordinated with the debating; spring, Shakespeare's 

Julius Caesar with several weeks left for Goldsmith's Deserted Village. Julius Caesar should show why and how races decline; Deserted Village should introduce the economics of the fifth year. Fifth year's English books should be left to the teacher. 

4. Two years of Latin the way "Old Sawney" Webb used to teach it followed by one year of German with emphasis on German grammar. Latin is the mother and German is the father of the languages of Western Europe. The student who is given these three years right can get a book and learn French, Spanish, or Italian without a personal instructor. If we ever get intelligence and character enough to establish and learn a world language, these three years can be devoted to something else. I believe I can construct an inflected language that, except for breadth of vocabulary, can be learned well in a month by the average person if he already knows sentence construction. I believe this language would be more efficient than Latin and so beautiful as Greek. The fourth year of this paragraph, or "course", should be devoted to chemistry and the fifth to physiology. 

The chemistry should close with a chapter on the more common medicines and poisons. The physiology should contain a chapter on reproduction and should close with an introduction to general biology, the biology containing a paragraph on each of the score or so of more common diseases. 

The medicines should be grouped in families (the purgatives, including laxatives and emitics; the sedatives; the stimulants; the disenfectants, internal like quinnine as well as external like iodine; the salves, etc.). The ailments also

should be grouped in families according to treatment (wounds, germs, growths, etc.). The best way of grouping diseases is to explain "the fundamental" of each, or how each works and how to combat its workings. For instance, "the fundamental" of tuberculosis is (a) to build up general vitality and (b) get rid of the germs. 

Throughout the youngster's entire career he should be kept face to face with honest ethics, "professional" ethics being just about the most dishonest thing I know of. Every college course in ethics of my acquaintance left its graduates just as liable to steal watermelons as they were when they began the course. Recently a family informed me beamingly that a certain "big" school was considering granting my school's work in ethics a half-year credit toward graduation. For this the family congratulated me heartily. But none of its members had seemed to appreciate my ethics' contribution toward making their son and brother truthful, honest, and considerate.

The college undergraduates of today are hysterical at the thought of the United States' getting into a war. I really believe a large fraction of them would weep screamingly if they should be drafted for the actual facing of bullets. But so far as I can find, none is devoting study to a way of abolishing war. Most of them were reared on the dogma that Wilson was wrong, and few seem to realize that either Wilson was right or someone must find a substitute for Wilson's plan. In 1920, while our divinely appointed guardians were campaigning for Harding, they told us, "A league to prevent war is all right, but we will do the job much better than Wilson is doing it. Just leave it to us." 

We left it to them, and look what we got. 

I am confident that terror due to my saying some fighting cannot be avoided explains an anynomous letter I received. It was signed "a Princeton junior" and was repre-

sentative of most of the signed letters I receive from undergraduates. It said, along with much more, "You know absolutely nothing about economics, history, and politics; so, quit writing on subjects of which you are wholly ignorant. 

Also quit mentioning that you went to Princeton. Princeton is ashamed of you."

I hereby absolve Princeton of all credit for anything I may say or do. As to economics, until the depression made the subject a fad, Princeton seniors regularly voted the course in economics principles "most useless" and "dullest" course in college; and even now most college economics is a superficial fad. My treatment of the subject has a practical aim, which explains why the youngster does not recognize it as economics. For the same reason academicians and their pupils don't recognize "my" ethics as being ethics. "My" ethics is "not ethics" because it teaches boys to play the game of life honestly.

In the same letter our anonymous junior writes, "After having read some of the best writers of the English, French, Latin, and German languages I can readily appreciate why your booklets are considered of little merit. Your style of writing is highly offensive and insulting to the intelligence of an educated person." 

Note the youngster's academic criterion—"best writers".

I hazard the guess that not once in his entire career has he been given a point on writing for accomplishment of objectives. The speech Shakespeare has Mark Anthony to make over Caesar's dead body violates academic sequence, coherence, and unity; yet it is the greatest speech ever penned when judged by objective. Scott's Lady of the Lake murders academic rhetoric so freely that even I notice it; yet it is by far so much my favorite picture of the lakes and mountains amidst which I have spent sixteen summers that I can recite nearly half of it from memory as a result of having read it aloud so often.

(When I was a boy my very literary old maid aunt told me that "the critics did not rate Lady of the Lake very high" and that I should devote more attention to the better writers. Like my aunt, the College Board drives he-men to hysterics with sissy "literature" that ain't worth a damn.) 

It may interest our junior to know that practically every writer for accomplishment (including many nationally known writers) who has read any of my work has expressed envy of my style, an instructor in the junior's own college having said that my words are things and not just words. 

I spent a quarter of a century acquiring that style of writing because I had noted that the vaccinations of others were not "taking". My writings say to the guilty reader, "This means you", and, "Thou art the man", which explains why they are "offensive and insulting". The most "offensive and insulting" writing on earth is a cap that fits. 

A form of writing that produces venom equal to that produced by a cap that fits, is that which produces practical results. Recently I ran the following as a paid advertisement in one of our local papers: 

DON'T VOLUNTEER 

unless you have your own selfish reasons for doing so. There is no reason why thoroughbreds should die or fight that yellow dogs who oppose draft laws may remain at home and produce the next generation.

Those who objected to the notice of course attacked "yellow dogs" as being "offensive and insulting", but the substance of their conversation was aimed at the headline-DON'T VOLUNTEER. That notice was calculated to help get the draft law passed, and there was where the venom really centered. 

Parenthetically, I wish here to suggest what I forgot in the chapter on foreign affairs, that the draft age be lowered to eighteen, or the year between graduation from high

school and entry into college. Not only will that be less upsetting than any other year, now that college freshman is no longer a continuation of high school senior year studies, but high school boys impress me as being less soft than college undergraduates, a probable cause being that the younger boys have not had their bills paid by someone other than themselves until they have become professional receivers who are indignant at the suggestion that they give. 

A reader sent a letter to a magazine complaining against the high cost of dying (funerals). The editor added a footnote saying he would like to hear from other readers on the subject. Immediately I wrote a letter proposing that we act instead of talking, and pointed out that if a municipality will burn a thousand pound horse for five dollars there is no reason why it should not do the same for a hundred and fifty pound man. I added that I hoped Fort Worth would be doing that before I die. My letter was returned with a very sweet personal note from the editor saying he had now published the quota on the subject. Actually he had published three or four when usually he published a dozen on a popular subject. Not only do I believe that the undertakers made it worth while for the editor to desist, but also my letter was focused on results. It not only said, "Let's do something", but it told what to do and how to do it. And my letter was the kind that virtually is never published. 

The explanations are (1) racketeers don't like such letters and racketeers advertise and (2) American education has paralyzed its products for execution until to them the truly practical sounds "theoretical and visionary". 

Before leaving the high cost of dying, doctors collect thousands of dollars keeping dying people alive. If I ever become convinced that my life has a minus sign in front of it, I hope I shall have character enough to take chloroform. 

But this is not meant as a suggestion that parents who have done more than their duty to their children take chloroform.

Parents owe it to their children to see them through a free high school amidst wholesome surroundings. Children owe for all they receive beyond that. 

Continues the anonymous one, "The first essential of a scholar is to say a lot in a few words. You reverse the process.' 

Perhaps the most frequently made criticism of my style by (friendly) reviewers is that my sentences are too pregnant and thereby give mental indigestion to simple minded readers. 

To repeat, my guess is that the thing in my books that makes the junior rave is that I advocate military conscription by lot. Parenthetically, I am sure he is not working on a substitute for Wilson's plan. That would be working with a practical objective, which is taboo in American academic education.

During the past year I have contacted about a hundred undergraduates, holding elaborate personal conversations with some of them. Although the ones I have contacted personally are much above average in natural ability, they are being miseducated. About a year ago, one who was both majoring in economics and selling advertising for a college publication, let me read a paper he had written on "The Economics of Advertising". It was the best thing of its kind I ever have seen. Not only was he a master of both the economic theory and the practical of his task but he had fused correct theory and practice into one, which is the only true education.

At the time I returned his paper I suggested that he give his attention to unemployment. I have just had a talk with him on this subject and regret to have to add that he has not solved unemployment. He had learned the economics of advertising as my seventh grader had learned physics; he had learned unemployment the academic way, with the

result that his views were general, vague, and lacking in connecting links with the existing political set-up. But be it added to his academic credit, he did not rob Peter to pay 

Paul, as do the politicians. 

A decade ago Senator Thomas F. Bayard of Delaware said, "Americans must think or lose their liberty". Since then, Russians, Italians, Germans, Chinese, Japanese, Norwegians, Belgians, Dutch, French, and others have lost their liberty as a result of their having refused to think; and unless the boll-weevil saves us, I think Americans will follow.

To think, one must stand alone. Parents are afraid for their children to stand alone and are encouraging them to be molded in the uniformity of the majority. Educators are cooperating with parents. In Fort Worth, the bait is to get youngsters interested in the veneer of extra-curriculum activities and by so doing keep the youngsters blind to the fact that they are neglecting essentiality, thoroughness, and the right generalization.

Americans can't take it. A teacher tells me of a boy who quit school rather than take a whipping. Ten years later the boy took a hanging. American youth refused to take that which was difficult. This encouraged the dictators to the point where Americans are having to take war. 

Roy Helton's great article correctly traces our evils to the American woman. He points out that her natural desire for security-and let me add her desire for veneer-has done for the American man what Eve's desire to be a wise guy did for Adam. For a decade there has been lying unpublished in my desk a manuscript with the title, "Outwitting Cupid". I have held it because to have published it merely would have furnished more "proof that Sims is a nut". So early as 1930, in "History Repeats", I pointed out that if a man wants to accomplish something truly worth

while he must either not marry or marry a self supporting wife who practices birth control. When a man gets an 

American woman and her anthropoids tied around his neck he is done for so far as his being anything but a draught horse in the day time and a buggy horse at night goes.

This is nothing new. In the Delineator a quarter of a century ago, Mrs. Wilson Woodrow had an article with the title, "The American Husband", that pointed out how the American woman regards a man as being nothing but a provider. Mrs. Woodrow pretended to think the men liked this, but she knew better. I tried to make her follow up the theme, but I think women intimidated her into dropping it. 

I have repeatedly published that the American "man" is merely a male woman. The female has emasculated him. Of course it is his own fault. He is so desirous of giving his wife and children a fair deal that he has spoiled them rotten when he should have set their posteriors on fire with a strap. 

Elsie Robinson and other women are approving Helton's article; but I fear the explanation is not so much their love for the greatest good of the greatest number as it is their fear that the article may cause a few men to shun marriage. 

During my fifteen years as author-publisher, probably more than a hundred readers have expressed verbal reactions to my books, and more than another hundred have betrayed their reactions in their attempts to conceal what they thought. Many have complimented, many have denounced, many have evaded, but not five of several hundred have betrayed interest in the greatest good of the greatest number, which has been the aim of practically my every effort in my publications. No matter how a reader may disapprove of my work, it would be impossible for that reader to discuss my work without betraying an interest in the greatest good of the greatest number if he were interested in the greatest good of the greatest number. No such in-

terest is betrayed. Even those who are most friendly devote their suggestions to helping me personally with no hint on their part focused on serving humanity.

During the same fifteen years I have made the same discovery in connection with my school. There was a time when parents rebuked their sons for trying merely to "get by". Now parents are worse than their sons in wanting the sons to get by. I have explained this elsewhere. Employers have discovered that while it means little for a boy to possess a diploma, the intrinsic value of which generally is worthless, it does mean something for him not to possess one because nowadays any bum can get one. Therefore parents shriek hysterically for "credits" leading to a sheepskin that employers more and more are expecting.

The educators are capitalizing this racket. They even are trying to get the war department to admit to aviation only those possessing a college degree. If the teachers of history taught honestly, youngsters would discover that great generals usually won their military success by violating the traditions and conventions of military teachers. I particularly recommend a study of the Confederate general, Nathan Bedford Forrest, who owed much of his remarkable success to asking what West Point would recommend and then doing the opposite.

The fundamental result of all the preceding is that parents would rather their sons get "credits" and diplomas than for them to know arithmetic and have character. 

The delirium of the first decade after the First World War and the depression of the second decade, resulted in parents' as well as sons' being whipped. Nearly every person I know, is cowed, with the result that most people prefer great risk of failure along with the majority to the chance of true success through being right but alone. 

A few weeks after I married, several of my married

friends gave me a stag dinner. In the course of the evening one of them complained about some of his wife's ways. 

Each of the others added the same complaint against his wife. Then one said, "The only way to get along with them is to pay no attention to them", to which all the others subscribed. I wondered if the conversation had been staged for my benefit.

I have not needed the advice in my married life, but I have come to practice it in my dealings with adults. I am confident that the quality of my teaching has improved every year, and my disposition has mellowed with age in my dealings with boys. But in my dealings with adults I have come more and more to "pay no 'tention to 'em". I still think enough of my near relatives and several of my best friends to disagree with them, but beyond that I "pay no 'tention" to most people.

If only our professional disseminators of "news" were honestly ethical, I believe we few individual problem-solvers could do much to correct the deplorable condition our nation finds itself in, because "if all human liberties except freedom and dissemination of discussion should be chained, that one would unchain all the others". An editor of a nationally read publication writes me in defense of his publication, "It is necessary for us to keep a large number of readers. A publication that does not have a large number of readers is, ipso facto, no publication. As a consequence, we must use a lot of finesse in what we print. At present we seem to be one of the most capable on the market."

When I replied that he might be able to use some of my ideas if he would specify that they were "the ideas of the writer and not of the publication printing them", he wrote `back, "You put things too bluntly for most readers, especially the ones who would profit most", the italics being inserted by me.

My whole object is to help people to "profit". Note that a publication boasts that it became one of the nation's greatest by not helping people to profit. 

All of the publications of my acquaintance that quote Dorothy Thompson specify that the ideas she expresses are "hers" and not the ideas of the publication printing them. 

Perhaps that is why "her" ideas are good.

The publications say Thompson's writings are "controversial". At first this puzzled me. Then I discovered that most people consider fact-marshaling and issue-joining "controversial". Have you ever stopped to consider what kind of world this would be if we should abolish factmarshaling and issue-joining, or "controversy"? It would be a paradise for those weaklings and those crooks who preach that we should "find no evil, see no evil, hear no evil, and say no evil". That is, it would be their paradise until it became a hell through their not being saved from themselves by those of us who find truth, see truth, hear truth, and speak truth. Hitler got where he is through our isolationists', pacifists', and other sob-sisters' seeing, hearing, and saying no truth.

Our editor of the "successful" publication continues, "The best teachers are usually blunt, but the pupils have to take it. Outside of the classroom, most people are dumb, very much so. Those that can understand wont, and those that cant, it is a waste of time to try. [In defense of our editor's rhetoric, remember that I am quoting a hurriedly written letter.] "I am sorry for anyone who has to do mass education. 

As you say, most want it in packages. I think you should note, that so-called training of LEADERS, is purely fiction. 

A man is a natural leader or he is not. When they come capable we are lucky. WE SHOULD TRAIN MEN TO

FOLLOW their leaders. A leader simply has to be more

capable than his followers to survive. "As for THINKERS, well, they are hard to find. Most people follow blindly what they learned when they were five years old. A surprising statement, but most people do not deviate much from what they learn at the time when intelligence first comes to them. "Most people want confirmation. OR, nonsense. It hurts most people to think."

Not even I, who for a quarter of a century have been denounced as a pessimist and a misanthrope, am that pessimistic. I think that the average person is sufficiently intelligent to become both a follower and a leader if he is trained from childhood to separate the essential from the non-essential and if his schools did not turn him out from their mold of uniformity as a robot. 

Nevertheless, I must admit that our editor is substantiated by the late Willis J. Abbot, who said that a newspaper not carrying advertisements would have to specialize in the superficial and the sensational in order to get enough subscribers to make the paper pay. Our editor is substantiated by Dale Carnegie, who turns out best sellers. He is substantiated by the records of three millions of American college graduates. Now that I think of it, he is substantiated by my own findings.

But I still think the editor and I should try to do something about it. I think even Roosevelt and Willkie should try to do something about it. One of my two major suggestions to our next president is the above explained "yardstick" in the form of a government-issued diploma. The other is aids in self education. Not only should the person who had to quit school early have such aids but the high school and particularly the college graduate need them sorely. Most of what "college" education I have that is worth having was got outside of college. The best that college can

do is to plant the right educative seed the right way, and the colleges are doing a very poor job at this. 

The government's contribution to self educators should be (1) the right books on self education, (2) public libraries, public laboratories, and properly guided debating clubs, (3) educators who not only would grade the papers and speeches of self educators but also would, for a small payment, send the self educator copies of his papers with errors marked, criticism, and suggestions.
