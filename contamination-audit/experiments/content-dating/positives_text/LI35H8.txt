The Place of Field Crop Production in New Jersey

HOWARD B. SPRAGUE, Agronomist 

Why should there be real interest in the production of grains, hay, and pasture in New Jersey? Does the future success of the dairy and poultry industries rest on growth of feed on the farm? Is not the agriculture of this state independent of local production of such crops? Why should the 

New Jersey farmer concern himself with the growth of feeds when a surplus is grown in the Middle West? Are not our local problems primarily those of distribution and marketing, rather than of production? The correct answers to these and similar questions are of great importance, not only to the individual farmers, but also to those charged with the various phases of agricultural leadership.

Permanence of Agriculture in New Jersey 

One of the first considerations is whether agriculture is doomed to early extinction in New Jersey, as some have predicted, or whether it is comparatively stable. Table 1, which shows the relative acreage of crop land in certain representative states, indicates New Jersey is maintaining its acreage of crop land fully as well as the two large neighboring states of Pennsylvania and New York. The reduction in crop acreage has been caused in part by the continued replacement of horses by autos and tractors, with corresponding reduction in acreages for feed production, but more particularly by the abandonment of certain types of land. The movement is described by economists of the U. S. Department of Agriculture as occurring throughout the Northeastern states, where less productive land is reverting to forest, or being devoted to other purposes than crop production. This adjustment should be healthy since the remaining farms on relatively good land should be more profitable.

For the United States as a whole, there has been a continuous reduction in acres of crops per capita since 1920, partly as a result of the shift from horse to machine power, and partly as a result of the increased efficiency on the part of farm operators. The reduction has not occurred uniformly, since certain of the plains states actually show increases. Restriction of agriculture to more efficient soil types in the states where land retirement is occurring should certainly cause no concern.

Local Feed Production 

There is no doubt that the use of cows, poultry, swine, and sheep to convert unpalatable or low grade plant materials into concentrated palatable foodstuffs, is an indispensable part of American agriculture. Nevertheless, these animal industries should not lose sight of the foundation for such phases of agriculture. Neither should the agricultural leaders ignore the fundamental importance of local feed production in maintaining this type of farming in the various regions where it now exists. Field crops for feed production, aside from those grown for cash sale, would have nearly as great a monetary value as all fruits, vegetables, potatoes, nursery, and greenhouse products

At present, the farm value of field crops equals 55.0 per cent of the total farm value of all horticultural products. 

The potential danger in ignoring the source of feed for animal production is shown in Table 5. The average farm prices for corn, oats, wheat, and hay are far higher in the East than in the Middle West, and hay is notably higher in New Jersey than in the neighboring states of New York and Pennsylvania. 

The total cost (for the period 1925-1929) of such feeds normally required per dairy cow ranged from $45 to $50 in the middle western states to about $67 in New Jersey. Eastern farmers have been able to operate successfully, because of certain advantages in local markets for the perishable products of milk and eggs. Solution of transportation problems which would permit middle western farmers to place such products in the eastern markets in good condition, will force the eastern livestock farmer either to more efficient production of feed, or to ultimate bankruptcy. 

Feed Costs in Milk Production 

The true importance of feed in producing animal products is frequently overlooked, in spite of the fact many farmers complain that they appear to be working entirely for the benefit of the feed dealers. The data in Table 

6, which covers six different states, indicate that feed costs make up 50 to 60 per cent of the total cost of producting milk. Figures for high and low producing cows in New York indicate that although higher milk production is greatly to be desired, the relative proportion of the total cost attributed to feed is much higher for the more productive cows. It is worthy of note that these feed costs represent farm values of crops in states where the feed is grown locally. The only exception is New York, and the data from 

Cornell University show that the ratio of milk prices to feed costs was quite different for that period than has existed since 1921. One may conclude therefore, that feed actually makes up considerably more than 60 per cent of the total cost of producing milk in New Jersey, since a considerable portion of feed is shipped into the state.

The average value of farm land and buildings used for crop production in New Jersey was $79.87 in 1930, in comparison with $78.58 in Pennsylvania, and $124.18 for Iowa. The factor of land value is certainly not responsible for higher costs of production in New Jersey. The monthly wage paid male farm labor per month, with board, is reported as $47.31 for New Jersey by the 1930 census, $45.87 for New York, and $44.93 for Iowa. Such differences as exist in cost of farm labor between the states named could hardly account for substantial increases in cost of producing crops. With regard to fertilizer, the middle western states have a definite advantage, but a comparison between eastern states shows only minor differences. Ohio averages 31.5 pounds of commercial fertilizer per acre, Pennsylvania 43.4 pounds, New York 27.8 pounds, and New Jersey 47.7 pounds per acre for counties where fruit and vegetable growing is of minor significance. 

New Jersey Could Lower Feed Production Costs. 

Unless more important factors can be found than acre yields, land values, labor costs, and fertilizer consumption, the conclusion must be accepted that high costs of crop production, wherever they actually exist in New Jersey, are the result of inefficient farming practices. There appears to be no fundamental reason why feed crops of the types required by the livestock industry cannot be produced in New Jersey at costs comparable with those of the surplus feed producing states, or at least, considerably less than the price of feeds purchased in the open market in the East. As noted above, the market value of feeds in New Jersey is equal to the farm price in the middle western feed-surplus states, plus transportation costs, handling charges, advertising charges, and commissions.

Great opportunities for reduction in costs per unit of crop grown are possible in New Jersey, by means of increasing acre yields. The effectiveness of reducing unit costs by improving yields is shown in Table 8. These data, which apply to the country as a whole, demonstrate that the net cost per bushel of corn, wheat, and oats, may be fully 50 per cent less where yields are reasonably good than when yields are below average. 

There are three principal methods of improving yields; (1) by growing the various crop on suitable soil types, (2) by planting improved varieties which are adapted to the region, and (3) by utilizing efficient cultural methods, such as time and method of planting, proper fertilization, tillage, and harvest. 

The first two methods entail little or no extra expense, and the enhanced

13 yields obtained by their adoption are practically clear gain. Efficient cultural practices may include the use of additional labor, lime, or fertilizer, but substantial increases in yield frequently require no more than an intelligent use of the labor and materials usually applied. 

Substantial improvements in crop production could become effective immediately by the general application of information already collected. The entire state has been covered by the soil survey, and it is six years since the general suitability of soil types to the growth of various crops was published. 

The results of ten years of plant improvement and variety tests have been printed in a series of bulletins and circulars. The general planting of the improved varieties now available would increase crop yields of the state 10 to 20 per cent without any appreciable increase in acre costs, and consequently, reduce costs per unit of crop. The cultural and fertilizer requirements and soil treatments for each of the important crops have been studied for many years, and, although the findings have been reported in bulletins,, circulars, and popular articles, they have been very inadequately applied to actual crop production. A state-wide survey of permanent pastures has shown that average yields of herbage may be more than doubled by improved management practices with very little additional cost. Although investigations must be continued on all phases of production to meet improvements developed in other crop regions, the fact remains that very substantial increases in crop yields and corresponding reductions in unit cost of feed produced, could be realized by utilizing the facts now at hand. 

Put the Land to Work 

The present size of farms will usually be adequate for any renewed interest that may be developed in crop production. The average size of general farms was 75 acres in the 1930 census, 106 to 125 acres for cash grain and crop specialty farms, and 122 acres for dairy farms. The average size of poultry farms was 31 acres, which provides a substantial acreage for other uses than yards and buildings. Effective use of suitable land in the farm also would indirectly benefit the situation, by providing adequate returns from the land on which taxes and interest on investment must be paid, by more equal distribution of labor throughout the year, and by more complete utilization of the machinery and equipment on hand. 

One of the most inadequately developed farm resources in New Jersey is the permanent pasture land. Studies such as that of Prof. E. G. Misner for New York (see attached supplement), and those of Prof. J. W. White in Pennsylvania, as well as surveys conducted in New Jersey (Circular 141, N. J. State Dept. Agr.), show the high feed value of these lands, in spite of their general neglect. 1

It seems clear that the production of field crops in New Jersey should continue to play the fundamentally important role of supplying feed at reasonable cost for the livestock industries. A realization of the necessity for producing such feed locally in order to compete successfully with other regions, will in large measure offset the psychological handicap of not selling these crops for cash. There is ample reason to believe that costs of producing grains, hay, forage, and pasture crops, may be reduced to levels comparable with middle western states, by utilization of information already at hand. 

Likewise, there will continue to be a need for improvement of field crops, studies on cultural practices, and soil treatment, insect and disease control, supplemented by economic studies. The problems of field crop production and the distribution of information merit more attention than has been paid to them by agricultural leaders in recent years.
