CHAPTER I 

RUCE NEVILL stopped suddenly as a sharp but slightly muffled report came to his ears. 

Instantly alert, he straightened, withdrew his hands from his trousers pockets, and, placing his arms akimbo, cocked his head to one side, listening. 

For the moment as he listened, his senses mechanically aroused by the interruption, his clean-shaved, fresh-coloured countenance had shed the gloom so illsuited to its habitual brightness. 

Well aware of the difficulty of locating the sound of a shot, a difficulty that increases with each second of time, he stared round, half doubting his quickly-aroused suspicions, and, after reflecting that parks are so massed into oneness in the dark that he might have been standing in Kensington Gardens, he slowly returned his hands to his pockets and shrugged his shoulders. "If it weren't ridiculous I'd say that was a smallcalibre revolver," was the thought that passed through his mind; then he continued his aimless stroll in the warm night air up the Avenue of Victory in Kisilev Park. He was beginning to doubt the success of his visit to Bucharest. The city's exotic strangeness, its semi-oriental atmosphere, its unrest-these were to com- 1

bine to charm in him forgetfulness of the recent past, to provide an anodyne of which he felt at the present time desperately in need. But the effect had been completely otherwise. Bucharest bored him-it seemed to him tawdry and irritating, at once reminiscent of thirdrate opera bouffe and of a thieves' kitchen. Romantic and flamboyant, it was also petty and drab. It had no consistency of style. It was neither East nor West-the twain met but did not mix. The interest he had expected it to awake in him had so far continued in a profound slumber. Nor did it seem likely ever to be awakened now.

The question is, he communed with himself, what should I try now? Will boredom keep me company wherever I go? How to recover any sort of interest in life-that's my problem. If only- 

Again he heard a similar report.

He could have sworn it was followed by a woman's cry. But was it? He halted to listen. Everything was again silent. In the night, alone, the ears play tricks.

Then from one of the bypaths, not twenty yards ahead, a figure came hurrying—a man. 

As he came towards Bruce Nevill he slackened pace, but kept his face averted. 

Bruce could not on the instant decide what to do. 

Should he collar and question him? He could not speak a word of the language. Yet if he let him go.... 2

The man had already reached and now had passed him. 

What the devil does it matter, Bruce appeased his conscience. Why should I interfere in their damned tuppence-ha'penny political quarrels? No doubt that's what it was. Safer to keep out of it. A little fillip in life wouldn't do me any harm, but why deliberately seek a sudden, violent and useless end?

Nevertheless, curiosity and a growing feeling of apprehension took him to the bypath from which the man had emerged to the main thoroughfare. 

What he saw made him exclaim aloud. He glanced back; the precipitate pedestrian had disappeared. He quickened his pace. On the ground a little way ahead two bodies were lying. He broke into a run to reach them. One was the body of a smartly-clad girl, the other that of a middle-aged man of the peasant type in native garb.

Bruce dropped to his knees beside them. The girl was alive even in the half-darkness he could distinguish the movement of her breathing. But the man was dead-of that there was no doubt. heart-beat Bruce sensed his hand become wet and sticky with blood; the man was shot through the chest. One of his legs was twisted curiously. "Winged him and then finished him off," reflected Nevill as he turned his attention to the girl again.

At her side his hand alighted on an automatic pistol.

Unreflecting, he thrust it into his pocket. Then he raised the girl to a sitting posture and sought to discover her injury. At first he could find none. Her fashionable, close-fitting hat was somewhat awry; he took it off her head and then saw that blood was trickling from a wound in her scalp.

Even under the confusion of the encounter Bruce found himself bitterly regretting that he had not tackled the man he had seen hurrying from the park; in subsequent investigation, that was likely to be held against him as a piece of supreme foolishness. Of course, it was probable that the girl would be able to enlighten the police as to what precisely had happened; she was not dangerously hurt and was bound to recover consciousness.

In the meantime, what was best to be done? He wondered whether he dare risk leaving the girl where she was while he went for assistance, or whether he should carry her with him. He would have to get aid of some sort, and have her injury attended to. Yet he had noticed that the park at this hour was almost deserted. Devil take it! Where could he find a policeman, and, having found him, explain to him what he wanted him for?

While thus his thoughts proceeded he felt the girl stir against his supporting arm. A sound-half-sigh, half-moan escaped her. Looking into her face he saw her eyelids flutter.

"No, no," she murmured, still plainly not back to consciousness, "it's murder!" Her low voice was filled with abject fear and horror. "English!" exclaimed Bruce in astonishment.

He studied her carefully. Her head, resting against his shoulder as he knelt, was beautifully shaped, its short, dark hair wavy and shining even under the starlight.

Her face was an exquisite oval, the features Grecian in their regularity, the eyes, as they slowly opened, lustrous and large. "A beauty-and a thoroughbred," was Bruce

Nevill's mental summing-up. 

The girl's eyes looked upward into the face of the young man bending over her. No alarm or disturbance showed in her gaze at first. She saw a good-looking, frank-eyed, well-groomed young man, his expression one of intense solicitude. He was dressed in old but perfectly-cut tweeds worn with the unstudied carelessness that specially marks the Englishman abroad. "What is it? Where am I?" she jerked out. She shook her head fretfully as though to throw off a mental fog and bring realisation. Then-"I-I can't," she stammered, and, closing her eyes, fell silent again. Dismay was perhaps his uppermost feeling. He realised, only too well, that he could hardly escape becoming entangled in a tragedy that at best must have uncomfortable consequences for him in the shape of police interrogation and suspicion. Uneasily he foresaw himself spending a night or two in a Bucharest gaol. And that might be the least of it. He shrugged his shoulders at the prospect-but come what might he could not desert the girl. As he made to move off with her he put the fragment of envelope and letter securely in his pocket.

It was a mysterious business. This letter now. The envelope had been addressed to the girl, from all indications, yet it was to the Prime Minister that the letter was addressed. Why had the envelope, too, not been addressed to him? Had the envelope by any chance 10

contained a covering letter which had disappeared with the main portion of that addressed to the Prime Minister? 

He stopped in his forward movement, turned, and again scrutinised the ground where the tragedy had been enacted, dropping to his knees beside the dead man to see if near his body there lay anything which might provide an explanation of the mystery. He was well aware of the risk he was taking in thus investigating matters on his own account before the arrival of police officials, but desire to aid this helpless girl, combined with curiosity as to an affair which obviously had more than ordinary complications, made him accept the risk. 

His scrutiny yielded no result. He rose to his feet, took the girl's arm, and together they stepped slowly along the tree-darkened path. He did not again speak to her.

As they moved on a faint sound behind them made 

Bruce wheel round. Two men had approached stealthily and swiftly from the shadow of the trees. Even as he turned they threw themselves upon him converging from each side. He flung himself away from the girl and leapt swiftly forward between them. Baffled so unexpectedly in their immediate onslaught they swung round, but Bruce Nevill had already turned.

In the use of his hands he had been well schooled. 

Without a second's hesitation or manoeuvring he sprang at one of the men, and in the same movement swung his left for the point of his jaw. In the uncertain light 11

his fist missed the mark but the blow sent his opponent staggering. Some instinct warned Bruce that the matter was beyond settlement by honest fisticuffs. He drew from his pocket the pistol he had picked up, presented it ostentatiously at the other and fired. But he fired carefully to miss.

The effect was almost ludicrous. At the report the second of the two men cried out incomprehensibly in a tongue unknown to Bruce-he judged it was Rumanian -and without more ado dived back into the shadow from which they had sprung. The one Bruce had struck quickly followed. He heard them crashing through the thicket in their startled rush.

He was in no mood, however, to appreciate the ludicrous aspect of their retreat. This new adventure startled and irritated him because of its utter fatuity. "For God's sake," he said to the dazed girl, as he drew her arm into the support of his own, "let's get back to the world of law and order!"

CHAPTER XII 

THAT squalid little estaminet, "The Thirsty Camel,” although in the rue St. Julien-le-Pauvre, so beloved of artists and etchers on the prowl for picturesque "bits," has in itself nothing in the least picturesque to recommend it. There is, however, about it something unusual. 

The visitor enters a dirty café-bar that differs not at all from any of the hundreds of similar establishments with which Paris is afflicted or blessed. If he is venturesome, however, he will find that down a flight of winding, narrow stone steps is a chamber, which, in medieval days, when the whole Isle-de-Paris was under the sway of the Cathedral of Notre Dame, did duty as a dungeon. 

Its stone walls are dank with the percolating waters of the Seine, and the atmosphere of the place is unpleasantly suggestive of ooze and mud. To-day it is precisely as it was hundreds of years ago, except that it is lit by oil lamps, is furnished with tables and chairs, and is frequented voluntarily. What in olden times was a haunt of doom and despair is now a haunt of pleasure, the astute proprietor of the property having been inspired to pander to the craze for novelty by converting the dungeon into a drinking den. The cabaret shows that on occasional evenings are there presented, however 169

-mediocre performers, attired in medieval costume, 16citing the ballads of Villon and singing pseudo-fourteenth century lays serve only to cover other activities less innocuous; the real habitué has more pressing interests than the poems of the long-dead Villón and his contemporaries.

It was to this curious resort that Superintendent Daniels' investigations had led him. It had been imperatively impressed on him before he left London, although in point of fact it was not so much an instruction as an official sanction, that if it were at all possible in no way was he to ask help from or encourage the intrusion of the French authorities in the most delicate and vitally important case on which he was engaged. 

The risk was clear; it was imperative that the Air Ministry's missing papers should not even for a brief period fall into the hands of the representatives of any nation but that to which they rightly belonged. Otherwise the temptation offered was hardly likely to be resisted. 

Only for such a reason would Superintendent Daniels have assumed the risk he now ran-for it was a risk, a grave risk, but the fact that he was fully conscious of that in no way affected his resolution. He had experienced no great difficulty in discovering that this was a frequent rendezvous of members of the Rosseger gang, and it had seemed an obvious thing to him that he should put his discovery to an immediate test.
