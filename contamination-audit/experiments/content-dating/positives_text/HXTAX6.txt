INTRODUCTION THIS 

HIS IS NOT an autobiography; neither is it a formal biography of my father and mother. It is merely an attempt to tell simply the story of the sixty years (18771937) during which they labored together for the upbuilding of the kingdom of God in the earth.

Those who knew them will find nothing new in these pages. It is hoped that those to whom they were strangers will find here the picture of two very lovable persons whose single-hearted devotion to the advancement of the gospel of Jesus Christ made them a blessing to a multitude of their fellows. 

In these days when civilization is crashing in ruin it may be that we shall find a worthwhile lesson in these two lives builded on the faith that there is no hope for a sin-sick world save in the Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of the Living God. 

-Charles P. Chipman.

LIFE'S MORNING 

When morning gilds the skies, My heart awaking cries, "May Jesus Christ be praised."

I Connecticut,

F EITHER YOU or I had been present in the railroad 

January 2, 1877, we would have seen there a slender young man of twenty-two. Outside a real blizzard was raging and train service between New London and Providence was almost at a standstill. But the young man, whose name was 

William Pendleton Chipman, was not to be daunted by anything so trivial as a snowstorm and a disrupted railroad schedule. His ordination as pastor of the Quidnessett 

Baptist Church in North Kingstown, Rhode Island, had been arranged for the following day and he proposed to be present, even if no one else were there. So he was waiting for the afternoon train to Providence.

The outlook was not encouraging. The long hours dragged themselves away. Towards supper time a train came in from Providence. When the conductor entered the station to confer with the telegraph operator young Chipman recognized an old acquaintance and promptly inquired what were the chances of his getting to his destination that night. "I'll tell you, Mr. Chipman," replied the conductor, "as soon as I get to Groton Bank (in those days the local trains went no further than the eastern shore of the Thames river; only the through trains crossed the ferry to New 

London) I shall turn around and start for Providence. I ought to be back here in a couple of hours."

That promised something definite and the young minister prepared for another period of waiting. Slightly more than two hours passed before his patience was rewarded. Then the train appeared once more, headed for 

Providence, and he climbed its snow laden steps. The interior of the car was dimly lighted by kerosene lamps and the few passengers were seated in the end nearest the coal stove. Presently the train started with a jerk and moved slowly off into the night. Young Chipman was on his way at last.

But that was only a beginning. At times progress threatened to cease altogether. Some hours later the train

reached Kingston, Rhode Island, twenty-five miles from Mystic. Whether it would proceed any farther that night seemed problematical. The crew raided a country store near the station and the weary passengers were regaled with hot coffee, crackers and cheese.

Just what refreshments the locomotive received is not known. Apparently it was invigorated by its long rest. It was also reinforced by a second locomotive, and the train started on once more. Several hours were spent in bucking snow drifts piled on the tracks. At times the locomotives were uncoupled and went on ahead alone, to return and pick up the cars after the way had been cleared. 

It was well past midnight when the conductor announced to young Chipman that they were nearing Davisville, where he was supposed to leave the train. "You'd better go on with me to Providence," said the conductor, "and return on the first train in the morning. 

I'd rather not risk stopping at Davisville, for we may have difficulty in getting started again. The extra ride won't cost you anything, and the company will provide you with a room for what's left of the night.' 99 "But I may not get back in time if I do that," objected the young man. "You are supposed to stop at Davisville, and I want to get off there."

The conductor demurred again, but the passenger was insistent. Finally a compromise was reached. The train did not actually stop, but slowed down sufficiently so that William jumped safely into the snow piled upon the platform, and the train chugged off into the darkness. 

Of course the station was closed; there was no shelter there for the weary traveller. The house where he was supposed to be passing the night was a half mile away and the storm still raged. Through snow that was waist deep

William floundered up the deserted street of the tiny village, with no friendly lights to show the way. It was not child's play, but he reached his destination at last.

His host had long since given up expecting the pastorto-be, and the family were all sleeping soundly. When aroused by repeated peals of the doorbell, however, they gave the tired young minister a cordial welcome and warm blankets for his bed, where he was soon asleep.

With the coming of day the storm ceased, the sun shone brightly, and somehow the delegates to the ordaining council from the neighboring Baptist churches got to the Quidnessett meeting house through the drifted roads, though more than one sleigh was overturned on the way, spilling its occupants into the snow.

At the preliminary session of the council William Chipman underwent a rigid examination on his Christian experience and his religious beliefs. Just what questions were asked it would be impossible to determine now. The gist of the candidate's replies can be quite accurately gauged, however, for he never departed from his early faith during all the years of his ministry. It is enough for the present to know that he adhered to the conservative, evangelical theology and successfully passed the ordeal. 

On the afternoon of January 3, 1877, he was duly ordained to the Baptist ministry.

Among those who were present at the ordination was a young woman of twenty-one, a member of the First North Kingstown Baptist church at Allenton, seven miles away. 

Her name was Lillie Phillips, and she had a special interest in the outcome of the sessions that winter's day, for she was engaged to be married to the candidate.

Three weeks later, on January 23, 1877, the wedding took place in the Phillips homestead at Allenton, which had been in the family since colonial days, and where the bride had been born. Only one of all that wedding party is still living after sixty-three years, the youngest brother of the bride. After a brief trip to Baltimore and Washington, Mr. and Mrs. Chipman returned to Davisville and began housekeeping in the parsonage there. 

II 

Both William Chipman and Lillie Phillips Chipman were of old New England stock, and both were descendants of 

Mayflower passengers.

William was born in Mystic (now Old Mystic), Connecticut, on May 11, 1854, the son of Charles Packer 

Chipman and Catherine Pendleton Chipman. The father was a lifelong resident of Groton (Mystic lies partly in the

town of Groton and partly in the town of Stonington), and for many years conducted a general store in the village. 

He was prominent in town affairs, serving as selectman and justice of the peace. He was descended from that John 

Chipman who settled on Cape Cod and married Hope 

Howland, daughter of John Howland of the Mayflower company. William's mother was also of colonial stock. 

Her paternal grandfather served as a soldier in the revolutionary forces.

William was brought up in a godly home which was located directly across the street from the meeting house of the oldest Baptist church in Connecticut, the First Baptist Church of Groton. At the age of twelve years he was baptized into the membership of that church. The baptism took place in the pond just back of his home, and since it was midwinter, a hole had been cut through the ice to permit the candidates to enter the water. 

As he grew into young manhood he dedicated himself to the ministry. His preparation for college was completed at the Mystic Valley institute, and he entered Brown University in the fall of 1871. He was an excellent student and stood well toward the top of the class. During his college years he acted as pulpit supply in various Baptist churches in the vicinity of Providence, one of which was at 

Allenton. There he met Lillie Phillips. Friendship soon ripened into a love which was to deepen as the years passed. 

Before his graduation from Brown in 1875 William had been accepted as the betrothed of the young lady. 

A year of further study at the Rochester (N. Y.) Theological Seminary followed his college course. Then came the invitation to become the pastor of the Quidnessett church and its acceptance. 

Lillie Phillips was the daughter of William Browning 

Phillips, a prosperous farmer of North Kingstown, and was born in Allenton on May 4, 1855. The Phillips family was among the earliest settlers in the town, and the original Phillips homestead, sometimes known as "Mobray

Castle," built about 1698, is still standing, somewhat spoiled by modern alterations, on alternate Route 1 (Boston 

Post Road) near what used to be the Belleville railroad station in the days when the Newport and Wickford Rail-

road furnished the most direct route between New York and Newport. The house at Allenton, two miles distant, was built by the family on another farm at a somewhat later date than the homestead at Belleville. The Allenton house was burned about fifteen years ago. 

Through his mother William Browning Phillips was descended from that Mistress Anne Hutchinson of stormy memory, whose statue stands in front of the Massachusetts State House. Lillie Phillips's mother was Mary Ann 

Brett, who traced her ancestry to John and Priscilla Alden.

Lillie Phillips was educated in the public schools of her native town and at East Greenwich Academy in nearby East Greenwich. At an early age she became a member of the church at Allenton, and as she grew into young womanhood she took an active part in the work of the church, an excellent training for her duties as a minister's wife. 

III 

The parsonage at Davisville still stands and looks outwardly very much as it did in 1877 when the Chipmans began their homemaking there. The same white picket fence separates the front yard from the street and the same small white stable (later a garage) remained at the rear until the hurricane of 1938 demolished it, as it did many a more notable landmark throughout New England. The parsonage is one of those deceptive houses which appear small but are actually roomy. The ell, which extends parallel with the road, contained a kitchen and dining room; the main house, a sitting room, front hall, and two other rooms on the first floor, with three bed rooms above. 

The village also has altered but little with the passing years. The house to which William Chipman made his way through the drifts on that stormy January night is still standing, unchanged outwardly. The railroad station is gone the automobile and the motor bus are responsible for that. The mill and the post office have disappeared, too, together with one or two of the older houses. A tiny library has been erected just below the parsonage, and the highway crosses the railroad tracks on an "overpass."

The meeting house in which the youthful minister was ordained has gone. Under his leadership it was raised and rebuilt with a vestry below and the auditorium (they would have been shocked had anyone called it a "sanctuary" in those days) on the second floor. Many years later it was burned, but a new building of concrete blocks, otherwise very much like the older one, stands on the same site. It is not in the village of Davisville, but is located on the state highway (Route 1) near the bridge over the railroad not far from the boundary between the towns of 

North Kingstown and East Greenwich.

The Quidnessett Baptist Church was a typical rural church of that period. The meeting house stood near the center of a considerable farming section which contained three small hamlets, all about equidistant from the place of worship. The congregation was made up almost entirely of families of early colonial stock. Whatever problems it presented to its new minister were not complicated by differences of race or color. Unlike the majority of rural churches, the Quidnessett church remains to this day a strong and active body.

The Chipmans were not only young, they seemed even younger than they were because of their small stature. 

William stood only five foot four, and his wife was a bit shorter. Both were slender; he weighed about one hundred and twenty-five pounds, she a few pounds less. They were good looking young folks: William with ruddy complexion, regular features, hair that was almost black and inclined to wave, eyes blue with a merry twinkle lurking in their depths. Mrs. Chipman's hair was also dark, her eyes grey, her features and complexion good, her face indicative of strength of character.

As he grew older William grew stouter, but his wife never weighed more than when she was married and later in life her weight was well under one hundred pounds. 

When William, for the protection of his throat, raised a beard in his late thirties, it was not black but red-brown, as was also his mustache. 

William often told with great enjoyment an incident which occurred in the early days of his pastorate. The 

Quidnessett Sunday School united with the school of a

neighboring church in a joint picnic. In the course of the day the pastor of the other church who, for some reason, had failed to meet young Chipman, asked a member of the Quidnessett flock: 

"Where is your pastor? I haven't seen him here." 

The reply came quickly: "There he is, right yonder."

The older minister looked in the direction indicated and then burst out. "What? That BOY?"

The good woman to whom he was speaking answered quietly, though doubtless with an inward chuckle: "Why! don't you know that the Good Book says, 'a little child shall lead them?'

The little child and his wife possessed a good stock of common sense and an unusual faculty for making friends, qualities which compensated in large measure for their lack of experience.

The apostle Paul declared that a bishop "must be given to hospitality." Taking the word bishop in the same sense in which Paul was using it, as denoting the pastor of a local church, William Chipman certainly qualified in that respect, for he and his wife were given to hospitality to an unusual degree. The trait persisted through the years without diminution. There was always room for one more at their table or a bed for the unexpected overnight guest, even if the members of the family had to put up with some uncomfortable make-shift.

It was not long after the young people had established themselves in the new home that William was called upon to marry a couple who came to the parsonage for the ceremony. They were self-conscious and a bit nervousnot an unusual circumstance. After her husband had pronounced them man and wife, Mrs. Chipman asked them to be seated for a moment and hurried to the kitchen. 

She returned shortly with hot coffee and some of her own wedding cake for the bride and groom. While they were eating she and William told of their own recent marriage and succeeded in putting their guests at their ease. As they stood up to leave, the groom took from his vest pocket a bill-evidently the fee prepared for the minister—looked

at it and looked at Mrs. Chipman, then took a second bill from another pocket and handed both-to the minister's wife. A case of "cast thy bread upon the waters" with a most unexpected and immediate return.

William was in these early years, as always throughout his ministry, preeminently a pastor. Though a preacher of more than ordinary ability whose sermons were practical rather than dogmatic, it was as a shepherd of the flock that he was most successful. Both he and Mrs. Chipman were always ready to enter with tact and sympathy into the joys and sorrows of their people, to share their burdens, to help in the solution of their problems, personal or family. 

Through frequent calls they came to know each family intimately in the home and to understand the strength and weakness of each individual. The quiet, busy days of that first pastorate were teaching the young minister and his wife many a lesson of which they were to make good use in the years to come.

There was nothing monotonous about the life the Chipmans led in those early years at Quidnessett. Besides the regular Sunday and midweek services at the meeting house there were district prayer meetings of a more intimate character in the homes of the members. And there were the special meetings of the women of the congregation where the minister's wife found a separate work to do. 

Then there were the long rides up and down the pleasant country roads as the pastoral work of the church called them to the homes of their parishioners. There were other rides, too, not so pleasant, when the minister was called forth in bitter winter storms to carry help and comfort to the sick or the dying.

It was in the Davisville parsonage, on January 10, 1878, that the first child of William and Lillie Chipman was born, a boy, to whom was given the name Charles Phillips Chipman. 

Of course, the minister "kept" a horse. How else could he make the rounds among the members of his church, or even get to and from the meeting house on Sundays? 

So a spirited young mare named Nell was installed in the stable. She used to get lonesome there sometimes in the night and would pound against the side of her stall until

she waked her owner. Then he would-please do not call him an uncomplimentary name get up, dress, go to the stable, give Nell a wisp of hay or a bit of sugar, pet her a little, and then go back to his interrupted slumbers. Trivial as such an incident may seem, it is worthy of notice because it illustrates a trait which marked William Chipman throughout life, his readiness to put aside his own convenience for the benefit of man or beast whenever the opportunity offered.

Mrs. Chipman was quite as capable of harnessing Nell and of driving her as was her husband. Often she would take her young son on the seat beside her and drive the seven miles to Allenton to visit the old home. 

One of my few memories of the years at Quidnessett is of being aroused from sleep at the close of the evening service in the vestry of the meeting house. Then, tucked between my parents on the seat of the buggy, of listening, half asleep, half awake, to the clop-clop of the mare's feet on the ground and father's oft-repeated admonition: "Get up, Nell, get up!" as we rode home through the night.

IV 

It was our Lord who said: "Whatsoever ye shall ask in my name, that will I do." And it was the beloved disciple who wrote many years later: "Whatsoever we ask, we know that we shall have the petitions that we desired of Him.” 

Among the membership of the Quidnessett church there was an aged woman whose faith was of the kind described by the apostle. Her son ran away from home as a boy. For that lost boy the mother prayed constantly until she received the assurance that she would live to welcome the wanderer home again and that he, too, would become a follower of her own Master. With a faith that seemed to transcend all reason she clung to that assurance, though forty years had passed with no word from the prodigal. 

At last she lay dying, but even then her faith did not falter. 

One day William Chipman went to pay what he felt sure was to be his last call on this aged mother in Israel.

She greeted him with a smile and the triumphant announcement: "My boy has come home! my boy has come home!"

Her pastor may be pardoned for thinking that she was delirious; but inquiry proved that she spoke the truth. Her son had arrived the evening before, a man past middle life. What the mother did not know before she died was that he had returned, not a professed follower of Christ, but a man of evil habits.

That was not the end of the story, however. Some months later the minister was holding cottage prayer meetings in various homes, and in one of those meetings that middle aged prodigal met his mother's Savior and accepted him as his own. His changed life thereafter was one of those miracles which forever witness to the reality of the second birth and to the truth of the apostle's declaration that "where sin abounded, grace did much more abound; that as sin hath reigned unto death, even so might grace reign through righteousness unto eternal life by 

Jesus Christ."

That transformed life within was noticeably manifested in the man's countenance, and one of my childhood memories is of looking up from the floor, where I was playing, as he passed through the room with my father, and of the sense of awe which filled my childish heart as I gazed and said to myself: "That is a saint."

Early in the Quidnessett pastorate a young man in the community was converted under William Chipman's ministry, and experienced what was known as "the call to preach." He came from one of the poorer families, rather looked down upon by the more prosperous, and he had only a common school education. Many felt that he was mistaken about his calling.

But his pastor had faith both in the soundness of the conversion and in the reality of the call to the ministry. 

He arranged for the young man to secure further education, and, indeed, gave some financial assistance. During those years of preparation the young man, then away at school in another state, once wrote to his pastor saying that in order to complete the school year he would need a small sum of money which he saw no way of securing. Could the pastor come to his aid?

It so happened that William Chipman could not spare the necessary amount at that time and did not know where to get it. He and his wife knelt and asked their Lord to show them the way. Then they went about the duties of the day in the assurance that their prayer would be answered. 

That afternoon while making pastoral calls they came to a home where the wife was a member of the church but her husband was not. The husband was not present during their call, but as they were about to leave he came from the barn where he had been at work. Placing an envelope in the minister's hand, he said: "There's something I've been wanting to give you.

Use it for yourself," and he went back to his work. 

When opened, the envelope was found to contain the exact amount needed by the young ministerial student. 

It was promptly forwarded to him and he was able to finish the school year. Eventually he completed his education and came before a council as a candidate for ordination. 

Again there was opposition, over which his pastor's faith and tact were victorious. Through a long life of effective service that young man vindicated his pastor's confidence and put to shame those who doubted.

Another young man, who was at the time a clerk in the country store at Allenton, felt a similar call to become a minister of the gospel. He had a high school training and had taught school for a time. He realized the need of further education if he were to enter the ministry, but was unable to finance even a short period in college or seminary. He turned for counsel to William Chipman, who advised certain lines of study and acted as tutor. He also recommended the young man to his first charge, married him, and assisted in his ordination. William lived to see his protege become a nationally known figure, the recipient of honorary degrees from more than one college. VI Father had a natural gift for story telling and mother's younger brothers would often tease him to "spin a yarn" for them. He was always willing to oblige them. On one

20 

occasion, after he had finished an unusually interesting tale, Grandmother Phillips, who had been one of the audience, said: "Will, why don't you write out that story and send it to the Youth's Companion? It's as good as some of the stories they print and I believe they would publish it." 

After some demur, father allowed himself to be persuaded. The story was written; was sent to the Youth's 

Companion; was accepted and published. That was the beginning of a series of short stories, essays, serials, and books, written at odd times as opportunity served, which did not end until the final serial was published fifty years later.

VII 

On the shore of Narragansett Bay, about four miles south of Wickford and a dozen miles from Davisville, near where the new bridge from the mainland to Conanicut Island (Jamestown) has been built, there is a strip of sandy beach. At the time of the Quidnessett pastorate it was known as Plum Beach because of the beach plums which grew there. It was not far from the site of the old 

North Ferry to Jamestown of colonial days. Indeed, the ruins of the old stone wharf were then in existence, and the cellars of two or three houses were still to be found nearby. 

Only two houses were still standing, however; an old farm house, which was located about halfway between the highway at the top of the hill and the ruined wharf at the bottom, and a small two-room cottage a short distance to the north of the larger dwelling. 

One summer when I was not more than three years old father and mother spent their vacation, together with 

Grandfather and Grandmother Phillips and other members of mother's family, camping at Plum Beach. From the margin of the beach the shore rose steeply to the height of twelve feet or more, and at the top of this bank there was a level, grass-covered space where the tents were pitched. A spring a short distance away furnished sufficient water for drinking and cooking. Altogether it was an attractive spot for camping.

The first night in camp was one never to be forgotten by the campers. It was a frequent subject of conversation in the family for years afterwards. A severe thunderstorm, or series of storms, began soon after nightfall and continued until dawn. The heavy downpour was accompanied by high winds which threatened to sweep the tents into 

Narragansett Bay. All night long the men of the party stood holding the tent poles to prevent them from being uprooted by the gale. The rain came through the unseasoned canvas, soaking campers and camp equipment alike. More than once some one was heard to declare that come morning they should pack up and return to their homes. 

But with morning the sun shone brightly, the winds ceased, and opinions changed with the weather. Possessions were spread to dry in the sun, planking was secured and floors laid in the tents, and all thoughts of abandoning the outing were gone. During the day friends from the village, who had been alarmed for the safety of the campers, came to see how they were faring and stayed to lend their help in repairing the damage. For the remainder of the two weeks the weather was so delightful that the experiences of that first night could be viewed, if not with pleasure, at least without concern.

So much in love with Plum Beach did they become that before the next summer arrived Grandfather Phillips and father had purchased for a small sum the two room cottage already mentioned, with its two acres of land, and vacations at Plum Beach became the accepted thing. The little house was remodelled so that it contained six rooms, all on one floor: kitchen, living room, and four bed rooms. 

A piazza stretched the full length of the front and south end, a total distance of ninety feet. A barn was built, and still later a well was dug, when it was found that the two springs in the lot sometimes went dry.

There, for many years, it was the custom for father to spend the whole or at least a part of his yearly vacation. 

We were never alone. Of course, Grandfather and Grandmother Phillips were always with us; Grandfather Chipman, whom it was difficult to entice away from home, came once before his early death; father's sisters and other relatives

came; and friends of the family galore learned the delights of Plum Beach in summer. The cottage was nearly always filled to capacity, and it was surprising how elastic those six rooms proved to be. There is a tradition that on one occasion nineteen persons spent the night there women and girls in the sleeping rooms, men and boys on the living room floor.

It was really a restful spot-when there were not too many guests for the only house in sight was the old farm house in the adjoining lot. The old lady who lived there, and the five boarders who spent their summers with her, were quiet folk and good neighbors. There was excellent bathing at Plum Beach within easy walking distance, and tolerable fishing from the old wharf or from the cliffs a little farther down the bay.

VIII 

Father and mother had not only won the respect and confidence of the Quidnessett community, but had endeared themselves to both old and young to an extent of which they were unaware. At the end of three years they were to have very convincing evidence of the real state of affairs. 

Father received an invitation to become the pastor of a village church on Cap Cod, which he seriously considered accepting.

When the members of the church learned what was impending they descended in a body upon the parsonage, made such strong protests, and displayed such sincere grief at the prospect of losing their pastor and his wife, that the call was declined, to the satisfaction of all except the church on Cape Cod. Three and a half years later father did resign his pastorate. By that time the bonds of friendship had been so strongly cemented that the mere removal of the Chipmans from the community did not sever those bonds. Members of the Quidnessett church were welcome visitors in our home for fifty years; after that the infirmities of age kept them from coming, but correspondence continued as long as father and mother lived. IX

It was in the summer of 1883 that father accepted a call to become pastor of the church in Peabody, Massachusetts. A reluctant good-by was said to the friends of 

Quidnessett and a new home was set up in a rented house in Peabody, since the church owned no parsonage. The pastorate there was destined to be short. It came to an end before father and mother had time to prove their ability to cope with conditions in a large industrial community, but not before they had again shown their talent for winning new friends.

Scarcely a year had passed when the east winds of the Massachusetts coast proved mother's undoing. A severe cold developed into pneumonia; that passed, but left a condition which her physicians diagnosed as consumption. 

They shook their heads gravely and mentioned a possible six months before the end. They suggested an immediate removal to the milder climate of the South as a means of prolonging life, without any promise of a permanent cure. 

Surely a time had now come which would test the faith of my parents in the loving-kindness of the God to whom they had dedicated their lives; a time which would also

demonstrate the power of that faith to heal a sick body as well as to save a sin-sick soul.

Nor was mother's illness the only trial of faith which they were to face at that critical time. In November, 1884, Grandfather Chipman died after an illness of several months. Father had already obtained a leave of absence from the Peabody church and shortly after grandfather's death we started South-father, mother, an aunt of my mother, and myself.

We made a short stop in Richmond, Virginia, where on our arrival they were celebrating the election of the first Democratic president since the civil war. We watched the proceedings from the window of our rooms in the hotel. 

On the Sunday morning father preached for the pastor of the leading colored Baptist church in the city. The striking and, to me, strange appearance of a number of candidates for baptism robed in white, made a lasting impression on my childish mind. 

From Richmond we went on to Columbia, South Carolina. There the state fair was in progress. The hotels were filled to overflowing, but we at last found quarters in a boarding house, highly recommended but nevertheless a comfortless resting place because of the lively little lodgers already in possession of the beds. 

The next day we resumed our journey to Clinton in the same state, where rooms had been secured in advance. 

There we remained for some weeks and, as usual, father and mother quickly made many friends, among them the family of the president of the small Presbyterian college located in the town.

After father's arrival in Clinton he had resigned as pastor of the church at Peabody, so when late in December he received from the church at Barnwell Courthouse an invitation to become its acting pastor, he accepted. 

The time came for us to go to Barnwell, but a remittance which should have arrived from the North failed to come and we had no money for our journey. Father was at the 

Post Office each time a mail was due, hoping to find his check in his mailbox, but each time he was disappointed. 

My parents took their plight to their Lord in prayer, asking that he would show them what to do.

After several days of suspense, one morning as father was leaving the Post Office one of the leading merchants of the town stopped him and said: "Mr. Chipman, could I see you for a moment at my office?" 

In some surprise father replied, "Certainly, sir," and accompanied the gentleman to his office in the rear of his store nearby. When he had asked father to be seated, the merchant closed the door of the office and said: "Please pardon me, Mr. Chipman, if I ask you a very personal question. Are you not awaiting funds from the 

North which have failed to arrive?" 

Father answered: "Yes, sir; but I do not see how you could know it." 

The merchant smiled as he said: "By watching your face each day at the Post Office. The disappointment you showed so plainly when you failed to get the letter you were expecting told me your story. May I ask another personal question? How much do you need?"

Father explained briefly the situation: his invitation to the Barnwell church, his acceptance, the failure of the expected remittance to arrive, and the amount which would be necessary to take the family to Barnwell. 

Then came the most surprising part of the interview.

The merchant went to his safe, took from it several bills, and extended them to father with the remark: "There, Mr. Chipman, is the money you require. Take it, go to Barnwell, and when you receive your remittance send me a check for the amount.” "But I am almost a stranger to you, sir," objected father. "Why should you lend me this money?" 

Again the gentleman smiled. "You have been here long enough for me to know you for an honest man," he said. "And I am sometimes glad to be able to give aid to a brother in need." "I thank you most heartily," said father, "for I have been in an embarrassing position and hardly knew how to explain to the church in Barnwell the reason for my delay. 

I will take the money because I see no other way out. But

until I can repay you shall I not give you my note, or, at least a memorandum of the amount?"

The merchant drew himself up stiffly and asked: "Are you not a gentleman, sir?" 

Somewhat nonplussed, father replied: "Why, I trust so." "Then your word is enough. Tell Mrs. Chipman that

I let you have the money. If anything should happen to you, she will see that I am reimbursed." And he bowed father out of his office.

The next day we were on our way to Barnwell Courthouse.

When we arrived in Barnwell father found his check awaiting him there, sent to that address by some misunderstanding. With a thankful heart he was able to make immediate repayment to the "friend in need" at Clinton. 

With true Southern hospitality a member of the Barnwell church, a prominent citizen of the village, took us into his home as guests. There we remained while father and mother were making habitable three rooms of the parsonage with the minimum of inexpensive furniture. 

In those three rooms we passed very comfortably the months which followed.

The Barnwell parsonage was very different from any other in which we had lived before or were to live afterwards. It had no cellar, but stood on posts which raised it several feet above the ground. Its five rooms were all on one floor and were heated only by fireplaces. Across the front there was a wide verandah, where stood a jouncing board. This was simply a wide, heavy plank supported at each end by an upright. By proper manipulation a person seated upon the plank could cause it to spring up and down with a not unpleasing motion-a southern substitute, perhaps, for the New England rocking chair. 

The main house contained four rooms. The two facing the street were the larger, and opened upon the verandah. 

The two smaller rooms in the rear were separated by a large central hall, to which those in front also had access.

There was no partition at the back of this hall, but a sort of barred gate which extended from floor to ceiling between the hall and a rear verandah. At one end of this verandah was another room occupying an ell at right angles to the main house. This ell was intended for use as a sleeping room, the kitchen being located in one of the several small cabins which were clustered in the back yard. But as we were to have no servants, the ell served as a combination kitchen and dining room during our residence. 

One of the large front rooms had been furnished as a bed-sitting-room; the smaller room behind it, was opened on the central hall, as a bed room. Thus there were two unoccupied rooms at one end of the house. The fact that our nearest neighbor in that direction was the county jail may have influenced mother in her choice of the rooms to be used. But our neighbors there never caused us any annoyance, though we could often hear the negro prisoners singing hymns and spirituals in their cells. A greater drawback was to be found in the rats which infested the house. 

They sometimes went so far as to jump on our beds during the night. 

Marketing in Barnwell presented some aspects new to us. Refrigeration was almost unknown, and fresh meat, other than fowl, was to be had only when some neighboring planter butchered and brought to the village such portions of the animal as he could not use at once. In the town square a large bell was suspended and this bell the planter rang to announce his wares. Thereupon citizens who were minded to eat meat for dinner hastened to the square and bought-if they were fortunate enough to arrive before the supply was exhausted. The same bell served as a fire alarm, but how the townspeople distinguished between meat and fire I do not know. 

Before he left for the South father had been warned that he, as a Northerner, might be looked upon with suspicion. Actual experience proved the warning to be unnecessary. How much of the cordiality and friendship which met the family everywhere was due to Southern hospitality and how much was the result of the tact and friendliness of my parents, I shall never know. That they were made welcome and found many warm friends wherever we went, is indisputable.

Mother's New England housekeeping methods were a constant surprise to her Barnwell acquaintance, accustomed as they were to have negro servants perform every household task.

Contrary to the opinion of mother's physicians in Massachusetts, her health improved steadily and in May, 1885, the Barnwell doctor announced that it would be safe for her to return North. For that reason, and also because his duties as executor of Grandfather Chipman's estate made his presence in Mystic necessary, father declined the invitation of the Barnwell church to become its permanent pastor, and preparations were made for our return to New England.

We travelled by train to Charleston, where we took the steamer for New York. That voyage was one we long remembered. Father had secured tickets and stateroom reservations from the local agent in Barnwell. The purser's office on the steamer remained closed until after the boat had left the dock. When it opened, father discovered that there were no rooms on the boat corresponding to the numbers which had been assigned to him when he purchased his tickets.

It appeared that several passengers were in the same unpleasant situation. The best that the purser could offer us was a couple of mattresses laid on the floor in a recess at the foot of the stairway leading to the lower saloon. 

That was not our worst trouble, however. Hardly had the steamer left the harbor when we ran into a severe storm-the most severe in her experience of several years, the stewardess declared. Father was the first to succumb and the last to recover-in fact he was barely able to sit up when the boat docked in New York, and it was not until the following morning that we were able to go ashore. For that last night on board we were given staterooms and slept in comfort.

XI 

During the next eighteen months father was occupied with the settlement of Grandfather Chipman's estate. 

Grandfather had conducted a general store in Mystic for many years, and the collection of outstanding accounts

took a considerable time, as did the sale of the business itself.

For the summer of 1885 mother and I were at Plum Beach. The early fall of the same year found us with 

Grandfather and Grandmother Phillips in Wickford, for

Grandfather had left the farm at Allenton to the management of his two older sons and had moved to the village. 

It was in Wickford that my brother, William Browning

Chipman, was born on October 4, 1885. Later we joined father in Mystic with the idea of spending the winter with Grandmother Chipman and father's two sisters. But before the winter was over I was left alone for a time, as father and mother called back to Wickford by the illness and death of mother's only sister, a girl of eighteen.

We were at Plum Beach again in the summer of 1886; and it was during that summer that I quite innocently caused the family, and the neighboring farmers as well, what was for them a rather harrowing experience. 

The nearest railroad station was at Wickford, four miles to the north. And on a certain afternoon in that summer of 1886 mother's youngest brother and his fiancee were to arrive at Wickford on a late afternoon train. 

Father drove to the village to meet them and was expected to return with the guests in time for a late supper. 

I asked that I might go with him, but for some reason which I have now forgotten he decided that it was best for me to remain at home. He promised me, however, that 

I might come to the gate at the top of the hill, where the wagon track to the cottage left the highway, to meet him there on his return.

About the time father was expected to arrive I asked mother's permission to climb the hill to the promised rendezvous at the gate. It was readily given, and grasping a small stick which I often carried as a cane I started off. I was a timid youngster and was mortally afraid of the cows of which there were a number in the pasture through which I must pass on my way up the hill. Hence the stick, which was destined to play an important part in the happenings of that evening. When a short distance from the cottage I turned and called back to mother, who was sitting on the porch:

"If father doesn't come right away, I'll walk along the road until I meet him."

And right there the trouble began, for mother misunderstood me and called out in reply: "All right."

When I reached the gate father was nowhere in sight. 

After waiting what seemed to me to be a long time but was probably only a few minutes, I set out along the road in the direction of Wickford. It was still day, though the sun was near setting, and timid as I was I nevertheless had courage enough to trudge along the edge of the highway, for I was confident I should see the carriage coming at any minute.

But no carriage appeared. Farther and farther from 

Plum Beach I walked until it would have required more courage than I possessed to turn back. So on I went, through the tiny village of Hamilton with its one country store and post office. The sun had set now and twilight had fallen, and still I pushed ahead until at last the Wickford station came into view. Surely, I thought, I shall find father there! But no, the station was closed and not a vehicle was to be seen.

Now on the same street where Grandfather and Grandmother Phillips lived (though they, of course, were at Plum Beach and the house was closed) there also lived the father and mother of the wife of mother's oldest brother.

I knew them well, and so with lagging feet I went on through the village streets, keeping a sharp eye out for a possible glimpse of father, to knock at their door.

Mrs. Congdon, after considerable questioning, got an inkling of what had occurred. She thereupon decided that we ought to go to the station to make sure that father was not there. So back I went in her company. The station master had returned from supper by that time and was able to shed some light on the question of father's movements.

Mrs. Congdon was told that father had indeed come to the station, but when he was informed that the train was an hour late and would go through to Wickford Landing without stopping at the Wickford station, he had driven on down to the Landing to meet his guests there.

"The train went through here some time ago," the station master added, "and Mr. Chipman is probably on his way back to Plum Beach now."

As there was nothing else we could do at the moment we returned to Mrs. Congdon's home, where I was given some supper and put upon the sofa in the sitting room, where I promptly fell asleep, serenely confident that father would know where to find me. But Mrs. Congdon did not share my confidence, and settled herself for a long period of anxious waiting, for there was no telephone available in those days.

My own part in the adventure had been quite prosaic, but back at Plum Beach there was a very different story. 

When it began to grow dark and I did not return, mother began to be alarmed. She had not understood me when I called back to her that I would go on up the highway to meet father, but had thought I was merely assuring her that I would not be afraid of the cows in the pasture. And knowing how timid I was, she did not once suspect that I would venture beyond the gate at the top of the hill. 

At first Grandfather pooh-pooh-ed her fears; but when night had come and I was still absent, he, too, began to show anxiety and a search was instituted. As the evening went on all the neighbors within the radius of a mile were called on for aid. Men and women, lanterns in hand, searched pastures, beach, cliffs, and highway to no avail. 

To add to their alarm a thick fog came stealing up the bay, blotting out all landmarks, and rendering lanterns of little use. Some one remembered seeing a company of gipsies pass down the highway just before dark, and promptly made the suggestion that they had carried me off. 

Meanwhile father was nearing Plum Beach with his passengers, when one of the neighbors halted the carriage and, holding up his lantern, asked: "Is that you, Mr. Chipman?"

Receiving an affirmative reply, he went on eagerly: "Is Charlie with you?" "No," father answered in much surprise, "why do you ask?"

And the neighbor blurted out: "Then he's lost!" "What do you mean?" father demanded in consternation. Having learned the meagre facts the man could give, father wasted no time in further talk, but urged on the horse and soon arrived at the cottage, to have the story confirmed. 

What a welcome that was for my uncle and his fiancee!

The young lady-a stranger to all-found herself abandoned by her husband-to-be, for he and father, after getting all the available information; started back towards Wickford, believing that the missing boy would be found in that direction rather than in the pastures along the shore. 

It was my makeshift cane which gave them their first clue. Mother had told them that I had taken the stick for protection against the cows, and when they reached the highway my uncle got out of the carriage and with his lantern in his hand examined the ground at the edge of the road. He soon found the regularly spaced circles in the dust where I had planted my cane with each step that I took. 

He returned to the carriage and they drove on, stopping at intervals to make sure the tell-tale circles were still to be found beside the highway. One other clue they obtained, for as I was passing through Hamilton a woman had seen me from her dooryard as I passed and had recognized me. 

When father stopped at the house to make inquiry, she told him what she had seen.

Assured now that they were on the right track, father and my uncle reasoned that if I had kept on to Wickford, there would be but one house where I would go for shelter. 

Accordingly they drove directly of Mrs. Congdon's. With their arrival my faith was justified and the good woman's mounting anxiety came to an end. 

Once more the horse's head was turned towards Plum

Beach and we hurried back to the cottage. Just how much irritation was mingled with the relief of our neighbors upon my safe return, I never knew. But I do know that my own explanation was accepted by the family, and there were no words of reproof for me. We sat down to supper about midnight, and the adventure was over. The little cane, however, was kept by my parents for many years

in appreciation of the part it had played in locating the "lost" boy, who had not for one moment looked upon himself in that light.

XII 

The following fall father, having completed his duties as executor, accepted a call to the church in Walton, New York. The chief reason for his decision, aside from his assurance that his Lord had a work for him there, was a hope that the climate of the Catskills would complete the restoration of mother's health begun by the winter we had spent in the South. As a matter of fact, though there was no further threat of tuberculosis, mother was never again in robust health.

To one member of the family, at least, Walton seemed to be an almost infinite distance from the familiar New England scene. We travelled to New York by the Stonington Line steamer, and took the early morning train from 

Weehawken via the New York, Ontario and Western

Railroad. The journey lasted nearly all day and we did not arrive at our destination until mid-afternoon. 

Walton had been settled very largely by New Englanders, and the thriving village retained many of the characteristics of the land from which they had come. 

The parsonage, which was located in the rear of the church, facing Townsend Street, was a new house, containing ten rooms. Of these my parents made use of only eight.

The meeting house was an unpretentious wooden building of a single story, and the church was outnumbered in membership by several of the numerous Protestant churches of the village. Though the meeting house was in 

Walton, the majority of the members lived on farms, some of them miles away among the hills. One deacon's home was twelve miles from town, but that did not prevent his family from attending services regularly.

Like many of their fellows of that day, these farmers, though obtaining a comfortable living from the land, had only small money incomes. Consequently they were unable to pay their pastor an adequate salary. But they could and did contribute liberally to his support in other ways.

Saturday was the day on which they came to town for such groceries and other necessities as they were unable to raise for themselves. Few were the Saturdays when one or more of them did not appear at the parsonage with a chicken, or eggs, or fruit and vegetables, or, in season, with fresh pork or buckwheat flour, or maple sugar and syrup.

And those who came were usually guests at the pastor's dinner table.

Nor were these weekly offerings in kind the only additions to the minister's salary. There was also the annual donation party early in the winter, when a large part of the church membership descended upon the parsonage laden with supplies which stocked the pastor's pantry and cellar for weeks to come. They brought their supper with them, and stayed for an evening of social entertainment. 

After the last guest had gone the pastor and his wife were sure to find on the table in the dining room a plate piled with small bills and silver coins. They were large hearted, loyal folk, those members of the Walton church.

Since his ministry was so largely with people outside the village, father's duties were very similar to those in the Quidnessett pastorate. There were the same long rides over the country roads-in this case among the mountains instead of along the seashore-in making his pastoral calls, and the neighborhood prayer meetings held in homes of parishioners or in the one-room rural school houses. And in all the varying experiences of their lives the little flock found their pastor and his wife full of faith and love and understanding, sharing burdens, helping to solve problems, bringing healing to broken hearts, courage to fainting spirits, and pointing sin-sick souls to the great physician.

XIII 

An event which might easily have ended in tragedy but which actually bordered on comedy occurred, if I remember rightly, during our first winter in the Walton parsonage. My aunt, father's twin sister, was visiting us at the time. Father and mother and my small brother occupied a room on the first floor. My aunt and I slept in a room above the kitchen which was heated by means of a register in the floor directly over the range.

Early one Sunday morning, not very long after midnight, my aunt and I were awakened by a noise in the kitchen. Thinking that someone was ill, my aunt slipped out of bed and called down through the register: "Is that you, Will? What is the matter? Are you sick?"

A man's voice, which we both knew at once was not my father's, answered: "Yes. I'm sick."

My aunt ran from the room, down the hall to the front of the house, and called out from the head of the stairs: "Will! Will! there's a man in the kitchen!"

But mother had already heard the intruder and had aroused father. Together they started for the kitchen. 

When they reached the dining room they discovered that the house-breaker had closed the door between that room and the kitchen. He heard them coming and grasped the doorknob on his side at the same instant that father gripped it on his side. The unknown pulled with all his strength; father resisted for a moment and then suddenly loosed his hold, with the result that the door flew open, throwing the intruder off his balance. 

Before he could recover himself, father was upon him, grabbed him by the throat with a grip which completely shut off his breath (for despite his occupation father had the strongest hands of anyone I have ever known) and hustled him across the kitchen to the back door, which mother with excellent team work had already flung open. 

Still holding the man's throat with one hand father seized the seat of his trousers with the other and actually threw the fellow clear of the back steps into a pile of stones which lay beyond. 

Father had no weapon whatever, but despite that fact and probably with the idea of frightening the man, mother called out excitedly: "Shoot him, Will, shoot him!”

Her cry was quite unnecessary. The intruder had already had enough and picked himself up from the pile of stones to run off into the darkness as fast as he could go. 

Our unwelcomed visitor had taken off his shoes when he entered the kitchen and, of course, he left them there when he made his involuntary exit. They were the only

clue to his identity but, as it happened, they were all that was needed. The superintendent of our Sunday School was also the chief of the village police. To him father went at the opening of the session that morning and told him what had occurred. "I think I know your man," the superintendent said at once, "and if so he is a local chap who has just been released from jail where he served a term for stealing. He was hanging around the railroad station late last evening, and the policeman on duty in that part of the town warned him to move on. I will investigate later and report what 

I find."

The sequel was a laughable anti-climax. The man when interviewed by the chief of police admitted his guilt, but made the plea that he had been so roughly handled by the parson he ought to be let off without further punishment. 

He even went farther and stated it was his opinion that father ought to return his shoes!

Father was reluctant to bring a charge against the fellow and disliked the idea of the publicity which would follow such a course. He agreed to drop the matter and turned the shoes over to the chief of police. Later on a relative of the house-breaker, a respected member of our church, brought father a bushel of potatoes as an offering on behalf of the culprit.

Father did not escape publicity, however. The chief of police thought the story too good to keep, and the "little minister" became the village hero for a season. One other result there was, and that a most satisfactory one to mother. 

A new and substantial fastening was placed on the kitchen door to replace the flimsy lock which the intruder had forced by a powerful thrust with his shoulder. 

XIV 

No story which included the winter of 1888 in its scope would be complete without some reference to the famous blizzard. Father had cause to remember that storm because of two events, one of which was a personal experience of its severity while the other concerned him only after it was over.

On certain Sunday afternoons of each month father held a preaching service in the school house at South 

Walton, seven miles away. On that March Sunday in 

1888 he took me along with him. While the service was in progress the storm began, and when we started for home it was snowing hard. As we went on the storm increased in violence, and father put me down on the floor of the sleigh at his feet where I was covered by the lap robe and so protected from the bitter cold and the stinging snow flakes.

After the first few miles father found himself unable to distinguish the road through the billowing masses of snow. His hands were so stiff from the cold that he could with difficulty retain the reins. So he was obliged to give the horse his head in the hope that the animal would be able to find his way home. That faith was justified and at long last we found ourselves safe at the parsonage, to the great relief of an anxious family. 

Later in the week, after the blizzard was over but before the roads leading from the village into the surrounding mountains had been broken out, father was called upon to take part in the concluding act of a real tragedy of that memorable storm.

Far up in the hills outside the town in a log housefor there were still log houses there in those days-there lived a man, his wife, and several children. On the Saturday before the storm began this man came to Walton to purchase food for his family.

On reaching the village and before making his purchases, he entered a saloon to buy a drink. Having had one, he wanted another, then a third, and finally ended in a druken stupor from which he did not recover until the storm was at its height and an immediate return to his home in the hills was out of the question. 

Meanwhile, back in the log house his wife, who had been ailing, grew rapidly worse. The frightened children, the oldest of whom was in his early 'teens, did what they could. Against the outer door the snow piled in a huge drift which prevented them from opening it. The scanty supply of fuel was exhausted and the fire went out. Cold and hungry-for there was no food left-they huddled

together for comfort. They saw their mother die, helpless to fetch aid.

When at length the storm was over the oldest child clambered out a window and made his way through the drifts to the nearest neighbor. While other members of his family returned to the log house with the bereaved child, the neighbor started for the village on a wood sled. The journey was long and arduous, but he arrived in Walton at last. There he hunted up the husband, sober now; together they visited the undertaker, and then came for father.

Taking the coffin for the dead woman with them on the sled, the four men returned to the mountain home. A brief service was held there, and then with the same escort the wife's body was brought back to the village on the sled and placed in the vault to await burial when the weather would permit.

XV 

Of the many friends made during the Walton pastorate, two stand out because of the frequent contacts through the succeeding half century, and the closeness of the tie which bound them to father and mother-a common dedication to the work of the Master. 

They were a young man and his wife who were devoted members of the church, interested in all good enterprises. He was about father's age and was employed in one of the local factories, but felt that the great commission demanded of him a more complete surrender of his time and talents to the spread of the gospel. 

Upon the advice of father he first identified himself with the local Young Men's Christian Association. He demonstrated such an aptitude for work with youth that he was made the local secretary.

His success in that position in Walton lead to his promotion to similar work in larger communities. But he felt that the work of the Association was not what his 

Lord required of him, and again consulted with father, who was no longer in Walton, with the result that he entered the Baptist ministry and was a successful pastor until his death many years later.

XVI 

In the spring of 1888 father received a call to the pastorate of the Central Baptist church in Pawling, New York, which he accepted. There was one incident in connection with the call which was, as one prefers to see it, either amusing or significant. In the membership of the Pawling church there was one dear old lady, who afterwards became a very good friend of the family. In the meeting which met to consider the question of extending a call to father, this old lady announced that she had been asking the Lord to direct the church in its choice of a pastor, and that the Lord had responded by showing her in a vision the man whom He had chosen for the place. "And," she continued, "when I saw Mr. Chipman in the pulpit I immediately recognized him as the man whom I had seen in my vision, so I am sure that he is the man whom we should call." We never knew whether her statement influenced the vote of the church in any way. 

The Walton church used every possible means to persuade father to reconsider his decision, but he felt convinced that there was a larger field of usefulness in Pawling and that the time had come for his work in Walton to end, and he refused to remain with the Walton church. 

Pawling, which is situated about sixty miles north of New York city and adjoins the state of Connecticut on its eastern border, was not so well known as it is to-day, although it was even then a popular summer resort. 

We were all pleased with the prospect of returning to be so much nearer the New England relatives and looked forward to the new home with eager curiosity. The change was not made without mishap, however. The village of 

Pawling is located on the Harlem Branch of the New York Central, but there was another station of the same name in the western part of the town, on what was then known as the New York and New England Railroad. Between it and the village there was a high hill, locally known as "the mountain.”

By some misunderstanding the car in which our household goods were shipped was routed via the New York and New England and the car was sidetracked at their station instead of going forward to the station in the

village. The result was that all our furniture had to be carted over the mountain at considerable expense. That was not the worst of it. The truckman who did the hauling was not accustomed to handling furniture, did not load the goods as carefully as he should have done, and the result was that on the trip up the hill the load shifted to the rear of the wagon, and when the descent began it slid forward again. It was a broken and marred lot of furniture that finally arrived at the parsonage. 

The parsonage was situated near the center of the village, in a large yard with fruit and shade trees and an extensive garden, with a barn and chicken run at the extreme rear. The house itself had originally been a storyand-a-half structure, but had been raised bodily and another story added below. There were four rooms, a large hall and a pantry on the ground floor. On the second floor were four rooms of corresponding size and arrangement, while on the third floor there was one large room, four smaller ones, and two very large closets. The large room on the top floor made an excellent play room for my brother and myself. The remaining rooms were unoccupied, except at such times as we kept a maid, when one of these rooms was assigned to her. 

The meeting house stood on the eastern edge of the village, about five minutes' walk from the parsonage. It was a comparatively new structure, built of wood in the gothic style, the interior finished to the roof with exposed beams. The walls were handsomely frescoed and the windows were of colored glass. The vestry was in the rear of the auditorium on the same level as the pulpit, and could be made a part of the larger room by rolling back the doors between. At the rear of the meeting house was a comfortable dwelling for the use of the sexton, an excellent arrangement which was, so far as our experience went, unique among village churches.

XVII 

The Pawling parish was much more compact than the widely scattered Walton field, although a goodly number of the members of the church lived on farms outside the village. These were all readily reached over good roads,

and father's pastoral duties were much less arduous, so that he had more time for story-writing. It was during this period that his first religious novel, "The Black Forge Mills," was published by the American Baptist Publication Society. This book so impressed the president of the 

Presbyterian College of South Carolina, whom father had met during his sojourn in Clinton, that he persuaded the trustees of the college to confer upon father the degree of 

Doctor of Divinity. 

Of course, family devotions, morning and evening, were a regular part of our daily routine; but they were not the only occasions when the family gathered for prayer or praise. At one time when, for some special reason, we were somewhat pressed for funds, father received a letter from one of his publishers which contained a check for an amount considerably larger than he had expected. Hurrying home from the post office, he called the household together and while we all knelt father poured out in prayer his gratitude for this timely relief. Among other special occasions I recall another very vividly when, many years later, an impromptu prayer service followed the receipt of a check in payment for one of the last stories father wrote.

XVIII Near the parsonage, there being but one house between, stood the Roman Catholic church. One Sunday evening at the Christmas season we had just finished our supper and Grandmother Phillips stepped out on the rear porch to hang the dish towels on the line there. Glancing towards the Catholic church she saw a red glow through the windows and called excitedly to father that the church was on fire. 

Out the front door and up the street father raced.

Reaching the church he found the door unlocked but no one inside. A fire was blazing merrily in the Christmas green around the manger in which the infant Savior lay. 

Without stopping to think that he might be committing sacrilege, he threw open the nearest window, picked up the blazing manger, and tossed it bodily outside.

Just at that moment the priest in charge of the parish entered the building in great excitement. To him father explained what he had done, adding: "I'm afraid I may have done something of which you will not approve, but my only thought was to save your church from destruction.' 

To which the priest replied: "You did just the right thing, and I am very grateful to you."

At the next service of his church he explained to his congregation what had happened and told them they ought to show their gratitude by liberally patronizing the next bazaar held by the Baptists. 

This same priest and father subsequently became very friendly and on one occasion when discussing religion with father the priest remarked: "We Catholics and you Baptists are the only consistent churches, at opposite extremes. You reject all authority outside the local church and each church is independent, while we accept the authority of the Pope as head of the church. All churches of other denominations are to be found somewhere between us.'

XIX 

Of course, the parsonage was heated by stoves. In the living room, or the sitting room as it was known in those days, there was a large base-burner containing a hopper into which the coal was dumped at the top, to feed into the fire below. This stove heated not only the sitting room and the adjoining bed-room, but a pipe lead from an air chamber in the stove to a register in the floor of the room above, heating that room as well.

One winter night when Grandfather and Grandmother Phillips were occupying the first floor bed-room in the rear of the sitting room, something went wrong with the dampers of the stove. As a result the gas from the burning coal came out into the sitting room instead of going up the chimney. Enough of the gas found its way into the adjoining chamber so that when mother came down stairs the next morning she found grandfather unconscious and grand-

mother nearly so. It was only by prompt measures, supplemented by the services of the family physician, that the two victims were saved from an untimely death. 

Thereafter, whenever grandfather and grandmother were with us they occupied a bed-room on the second floor which was heated by a wood-burning stove. XX The years at Pawling passed very quietly. They were probably the least eventful of any in all father's pastorates. 

There was the same round of pastoral duties, with the same problems of sickness and death and sin to be met, and my parents met them with the same love and faith which characterized their work elsewhere. But there were few outstanding events to mark the months as they passed. 

Father gained a reputation as a gardener which was perhaps due as much to the fertile soil of the ground as to his own skill, although he took pride in doing his gardening well, as he did in all to which he put his hand. In none of his other pastorates did he have an opportunity to display his ability as a tiller of the soil in this way. 

One year he tried his hand at raising chickens, with results which through no fault of his were far from gratifying. A friend presented him with a setting of eggs from an especially fine strain of buff cochins. If I remember rightly, every egg hatched and the chicks throve well.

When the time came for our vacation father arranged with a neighbor to care for the chickens, which had by then reached the broiler stage of their development, while we were away, but the night before we left a chicken thief carried off all but one of them. 

It was a bright moonlit night and a friend who was visiting us at the time heard the chickens squaking, rose from bed, and looking out of the rear window of her room, saw the man as he calmly wrung each chicken's neck, put it in his bag, and went back for the next. Why she did not arouse the family we never really knew, although she had some unconvincing reason to offer in the morning when she announced what she had seen. The following night, after we had gone, the same thief, or another,

came for the lone bird which had been overlooked on the first night. That ended father's career as a raiser of fancy fowl. 

It was during these years at Pawling that father had the first serious illness of his married life. He was taken quite suddenly with excruciating pains in his abdomen. 

The family physician diagnosed the trouble as enteritis and the treatment consisted, in addition to internal remedies, in hot applications externally. Father was very seriously ill for some time, but in spite of a treatment which is contrary to present medical practice, he eventually recovered. The physician very generously stated that in his opinion the recovery was due more to mother's excellent nursing than to his own treatment of the case. It is quite possible that he was right.

XXI 

It will be remembered that while at Quidnessett father had encouraged a young man there to enter the ministry. 

In 1892 that young man was pastor of a village church in southern Connecticut. At that time the Baptist church in nearby Essex was pastorless. It happened that the organist of the Essex church was a friend of father's protege. 

One day the organist chanced to call on his ministerial friend and the conversation turned to the situation in 

Essex. Going to the mantel of his sitting room, the minister picked up a photograph of father which was reposing there and handed it to the organist, remarking: "There's just the man for the Essex church, if they can get him." 

Said the organist: "I like his looks. Who is he? What is his address?" 

The questions were answered; father's name and address were written down; and then the organist put the photograph in his pocket with the remark: "I'll take this along and show it to the members of the pulpit committee." 

Show the photograph to the committee he did. They agreed that they liked the looks of the man in the picture. 

They wrote and asked if he would come and preach for the Essex church as a candidate. He would and he did. The committee and the church liked the man himself even better than they had liked his likeness. They called him to become their pastor, and he accepted. As a result, the 

Pawling pastorate came to an end on April 20, 1892. 

XXII 

Essex is an old town on the western bank of the Connecticut river a few miles inland from Long Island Sound. 

Originally a part of Saybrook, it was set off as an independent town near the middle of the nineteenth century. 

The village of Essex lies on the river bank; the railroad station (no longer of importance, but then served by frequent trains to and from Hartford) is about a mile to the west; beyond is the village of Centerbrook, and still farther to the west is Ivoryton. The chief industry in 1892 was the

50 

factory in Ivoryton where piano keyboards and actions were made. There were numerous other small industries scattered about the town.

At that time the churches of Essex were clustered at the top of the hill overlooking the river and coves. At the southern end of Prospect Street, which runs north and south on the hilltop, were the Congregational church, facing the east; the Methodist church on the east side of 

Prospect Street, facing the Congregational; and the 

Episcopal, facing the Methodist from the west side of Prospect Street. The Baptist meeting house stood farther north, on the west side of the street, with the parsonage adjoining on the north.

The church, the second building occupied by the Baptists of Essex, was built about the time that Essex was set off as a separate town and was a replica of a Presbyterian church on Long Island. It was a strange architectural combination: the front consisted of two pylons between which rose a spire nearly one hundred and fifty feet in height. An uncovered platform, reached by a long flight of steps, stretched clear across the front at the level of the auditorium. The effect was impressive in spite of its oddity. 

Back of the pylons and the base of the tower, which together formed the vestibule, was the main body of the building, a regulation New England meeting house, with a gallery across the rear and down each side. Below the auditorium, entered by a door in the south pylon, there was a commodius and well-lighted vestry, a ladies' parlor, and a kitchen.

In 1892 the auditorium had already been partly modernized. The old square pews had been replaced by handsome and comfortable seats of oak in a semi-circular arrangement; a pipe organ of more than ordinary tone enclosed in a beautiful cypress case had been installed back of the platform. There was no pulpit, but a reading desk stood at one end of the platform. During father's pastorate the renovation of the room was completed by cutting down the galleries to a horseshoe across the rear, and the redecoration of walls and ceiling in colors to harmonize with the organ pipes. When thus completed, the auditorium was one of the most beautiful I have ever seen in a village church.

The parsonage stood in a large yard in which were seven apple trees, two cherry trees, a quince bush, and several shade trees. There was also a small garden plot. 

The parsonage was a plain white house, the main portion with the gable towards the street and an ell extending at right angles towards the church. The ell contained the kitchen, two large pantries and a sink room. To the rear was another one-story ell, unfinished, which served as summer kitchen, woodhouse, and store room. Besides the kitchen there were on the first floor front and back parlors and a dining room. One the second floor there were five rooms. One feature of that floor was the smallest hallway that ever was built, I feel confident. It was about eighteen inches wide and perhaps five feet long, extending from the back hall of the upper ell to the room in the main portion of the house which father used as his study. It was familiarly referred to in the family as "the straight and narrow way.' 

Such was the dwelling which was to be our home for eight years.

XXIII Those eight years were fruitful and happy ones in the ministry of my parents and prosperous years for the church. 

Almost immediately father suggested the formation of a Christian Endeavor Society and the resulting organization soon became an important training ground for the young folks. Father was always a more than average preacher, who held fast to the evangelical gospel message, but his strongest asset was always found in his conscientious and thorough pastoral activities. His faculty of making and keeping friends-which was shared throughout life by mother-soon made a large place for both not only in the life of the church but in that of the entire community as well.

The parsonage soon became the gathering place for young and old. After the Sunday evening preaching service or after the midweek prayer meeting it was quite often the case that half a dozen or more of the church folk would adjourn to the parsonage for a social hour. Members of the congregation would frequently drop in unannounced for dinner or for supper or to spend the day.

Most of those who came made use of the side door, which opened directly into the dining room. Once or twice a year these same friends would come to the front door, dressed in their best, with calling cards, to make the formal calls which they deemed a duty; for the rest of the time they were happy to run in as opportunity offered for a friendly "visit," all formality forgotten. The parsonage was as popular with the young folks as with their elders and they were constantly running in and out at all times. A number of them began to call father and mother "Uncle Will" and "Aunt Lillie," practice which did not end when we left Essex but continued as long as father and mother lived and until the young folks were no longer young but were well into middle life with children of their own.

They were never afraid to ask favors of their pastor or his wife. On one occasion when the young people were having a social gathering of some sort in the vestry and mother was at home, there came a rap at the door. Mother answered the knock to see one of the 'teen-age boys standing there holding on to the seat of his trousers with one hand. "Mrs. C-C-Chipman," he faltered, "I've just torn my trousers, and I don't want to go home and miss the fun. 

Could you fix me up So I can go back to the social?" "Come in, and let me see what I can do," was mother's answer.

He came in, mother took needle and thread, sewed up his torn trousers, and sent him back to the vestry rejoicing. 

XXIV 

In other years, as so often has unfortunately been the case in village communities, there had been considerable rivalry between the three larger churches in Essex; the 

Episcopal body was smaller and was inclined to hold aloof from the rest. But during the years from 1892 to 1900 a happy situation existed in which the pastors of the three churches worked together in a perfect harmony which extended to their several congregations to such an extent that the former rivalries were forgotten for the time being.

Out of this condition there came one of those rare spiritual manifestations, a spontaneous revival of religious interest. It was the custom for the three churches to unite each year in union prayer services during the week of prayer at the beginning of January. In the year of which 

I speak the attendance was unusually large at these union services and a number of persons expressed a desire to enter upon the Christian life. At the end of the week all three pastors were agreed that the interest manifested made it desirable that the union meetings should continue for another week. After consultation with their church officers they announced their decision. After that there was no stopping the services. 

Night after night for thirteen weeks the churches were filled. Storms could not keep folks away. With snow knee deep men and women donned rubber boots and waded to meeting. On Sunday afternoons special services were held for children, for men, and for women. Because the strain of such long continued effort became a burden too great for their strength, the pastors after some weeks secured speakers from out of town for the Sunday evening services. But no professional evangelists were called in for the weekday services or for the group meetings on Sunday afternoons.

The date of the annual fireman's ball on Washington's birthday came at the height of the revival. This had always been one of the most important social events of the winter, and the committee in charge went ahead with the usual preparations. They found to their chagrin, however, that no one was interested and eventually the ball was abandoned.

There was no undue excitement, there were no unseemly displays of emotionalism. It was simply a quiet, deeply spiritual awakening of the entire community, which resulted in a large number of conversions and the invigorating of all three churches. XXV 

On September 26, 1892, my sister Jennie was born in the Essex parsonage. She was a beautiful child, of a singularly sweet and serious disposition. As soon as she could

toddle she displayed a great interest in helping mother about the house. At family prayers she wanted a little testament to hold, and when she was old enough to memorize a short verse she was always ready with one when her turn came.

I was studying German in high school at the time, and she seemed to be fascinated by the sound of the German poems which I was required to commit to memory as a part of the course. She used to climb up in my lap and repeat the first word or two of a poem and then settle back in my arms for me to go on to the end.

She was with us for three years and a half, and then God called her home. She was ailing for some weeks, but sat at the supper table with us the night before her death. 

In the morning she said to mother: "Me can't get up this morning, Mama." Three hours later she was dead.

New England folk are always at their best when called upon to minister to those in sorrow; but the love and sympathy displayed by the whole community at this time were a striking tribute to the place which father and mother had won in their hearts. "She was ours as well as yours," they said, "your loss is ours also." And they showed that they meant what they said by every kindness which it was possible for them to manifest. 

We carried the little body to Allenton, Rhode Island, and there where four generations of mother's family rest we buried it to await the resurrection morning. 

Forty-four years later, after mother had gone to join

Jennie in the Father's house, I found in mother's book of daily devotions a clipping cut from the local paper, telling of Jennie's death.

There were no more children born to father and mother. XXVI Father had saved the Catholic church in Pawling from destruction by fire. Mother performed the same service for our own church in Essex, and with as little thought of self.

A social was in progress in the vestry one evening. In the ladies' parlor a lamp had been placed near an open

window. A sudden gust of wind blew the long curtains outwards into the room. They swept the chimney from the lamp and the naked flame ignited the curtains. As they blazed up some one cried out in alarm, but none had the presence of mind to act. That is no one but mother. She ran to the window, calmly pulled the blazing curtains from their fastenings, rolled them up, to smother the fire with her hands. It was all over in a moment, and mother escaped without serious burns. Fortunately the crowd in the main vestry knew nothing of what was happening until the danger was past.

XXVII Father was a man of unusually orderly habits and always planned his work with great care. His salary was never large enough, even when eked out by the earnings from his writings, to enable him to purchase many books. 

He made up for this lack by a most systematic use of such books and periodicals as were available. Each book or magazine was carefully read; each chapter or article was given a scripture reference according to the theme treated; and then entered in a large ledger under the Bible book and verse which it illustrated or upon which it had some bearing. Thus all the material on any given text was instantly available by reference to that index.

His engagements and schedule of pastoral calls were listed on a daily calendar so that each morning upon entering his study he knew exactly what were the duties awaiting him for that day. Father very seldom read a sermon. He resorted to that practice only on those rare occasions when he wished to be sure of the exact wording used. For, although he could memorize a subject and its subdivisions so well that he could preach effectively without notes, strangely enough he had great difficulty in repeating even a passage of scripture verbatim. It was necessary for him to write out such passages on cards or slips of paper and when the time came for the quotation to read it from the slip.

In contrast to this inability to memorize words, he had the faculty of remembering the name and number of any hymn in the hymnal in use in the midweek services. He

56 

never referred to the book which lay upon the vestry desk, but would announce: "Let us sing hymn number thirty, 'I love to tell the story'," or whatever the number and title might be. And he could always recall the appropriate hymn for any given situation, so that the hymns bound together the testimonies and prayers into one harmonious whole.

As I have been writing these paragraphs there has come to me a voice out of the past which may be of interest just here. One Sunday afternoon in January, 1895, one of the young ladies of the congregation, in her early 'teens, was writing to an older sister who was away from home in another town. A part of that letter, which escaped destruction through all the years, reads as follows: "Papa wants me to write about Mr. Chipman's sermon this A. M. He didn't have room in his letter. The text was 

I Samuel 10: part of the 21st verse: 'Saul, the son of Kish.'

It was a good sermon and the main point was that Saul sinned away his day of grace. His sin was disobeying God's command. He said that now people could sin their day of grace away even before their death came. Papa wishes you could have heard it."

Since father's salary was never large it always required careful management to make both ends meet. 

Besides the expenses of a family with growing children, there were always the outside demands which are made upon a minister's purse.

Mother was the manager and to her father largely left the problem of making the money available cover the expenses which must be met. How she did it, I have often wondered since I have been of an age to understand her difficulties. In spite of her frail health, we seldom had a maid. Mother usually did the housework herself with such assistance as we children were able to render. She was a natural cook of the old-fashioned type not tied down to a recipe book. She knew how to make good use of whatever material was available, and from a bit of this and a bit of that to concoct savory meals at a minimum of expense. 

There were, too, the constant demands made on her time by her share-and that no small one-in the pastoral work: the numerous calls to be made, the frequent women's

meetings at the parsonage or at the vestry, the teaching of a Sunday School class, to say nothing of the constant stream of callers at home. But she always found time for reading aloud to us children when we were small; for sewing (she usually made all her own clothing) and mending; and for an interminable amount of knitting, crocheting, and embroidery. For at Christmas there must always be a gift ready for each of the numerous relatives, both father's and her own, which must be made for there was little or no money to spend for them.

For several weeks before the Christmas packages were made up and sent off the spare room looked like a gift shop, with its growing array of gifts, arranged on the bed, chairs and tables, in orderly groups, the gifts for each family in a separate collection.

Just as father was orderly and methodical in all of his parish work, so was mother orderly and methodical in her housekeeping, and the whole house was always spick and span from attic to cellar. Often when church officials could not see their way to repaper some room of the parsonage, mother did the job herself and no professional paperhanger could have done better.

In the matter of family discipline my parents displayed the same team-work which characterized their other activities. There was never any disagreement between them as to the proper course to be taken in any given case. Neither demurred at any punishment administered or suggested by the other, and there was never any appeal from a decision once made by either. And while the standards of conduct which they set up were decidedly strict according to present day ideas, they were never unreasonable nor were the punishments meted out unjust or unduly severe.

XXVIII A large part of the success of both parents in their chosen calling was due to their ready sympathy, their understanding of human nature, their tact, and their failure to take offence even under provocation. They were always ready to go half way in meeting the other fellow.

I have already told how, in those early years at Quidnessett, father used to get out of bed at night to minister to a lonely horse. He was quite as willing to do the same for any member of his congregation when the need arose. 

I remember particularly one occasion when an elderly man-he was nearly eighty, a good man, but somewhat excitable at times-aroused us at four o'clock on a Sunday morning. Father dressed, admitted the caller, listened to his story, reasoned with him, and at six o'clock sent him on his way, calm and contented.

During the depression of 1893 there were many men tramping the roads who were not professional tramps. 

They had been thrown out of work at some distance from home and relatives. Without funds they were walking back in the hope of finding employment or assistance where they were known. Such unfortunates were never turned away when they knocked at the parsonage door, nor were they given a mere handout. 

They were invited into our kitchen, given a warm and substantial meal, treated as guests and not as beggars. 

Often father would draw from them their story, make some practical suggestions, while mother would find some bit of clothing to take the place of an article obviously past its usefulness. Money was not given, both because it was not available and because it would often have been unwise to give it.

One such wanderer who came in early spring was an elderly man with a daughter somewhere in Massachusetts who was willing to give him a home. "But," said he, "I can't go to my daughter in these rags. Is there any way in which you can help me to earn enough money to buy a new outfit?"

Impressed by the man's sincerity, father promised to see what could be done. He consulted with the women of the church. They were planning to give the building its annual spring cleaning, and readily agreed to give the old man a trial. He worked faithfully, if slowly, until he had completed the task to the satisfaction of the ladies. By that time he had won the sympathy of others and odd jobs were forthcoming which kept him busy for a month. At the end of that time he came to father with beaming face.

"See," he said proudly, pointing to his neat clothing, "I have earned enough to buy what I needed, to pay my fare to my daughter's, and to leave a little besides, so that I shall not arrive with my pocket empty."

XXIX It was during the Essex pastorate that father one summer received an invitation to read some of his stories to the boys in attendance at the Good Will Assembly, which at that time was held each year in connection with the Good Will Homes for Boys on the Kennebec river at what is now Hinckley, Maine. 

In order to help defray father's expenses the supervisor of the Homes arranged for father to preach in Lewiston on the Sunday before the Assembly began, and in 

Skowhegan the following Sunday. It happened that the

Lewiston church was pastorless and they were so favorably impressed with what they saw of father that on Monday the pulpit committee came to him and asked if he would consider a call to the pastorate if one were extended to him. Father very promptly replied that he had not come to them as a candidate, that he still had a work to do in Essex, and could not consider a call to become the pastor of the Lewiston church.

There was a rather amusing incident connected with that Sunday. In announcing the services, the Lewiston 

Journal of Saturday made the statement that a certain well known citizen of the state had said, "Doctor Chipman is one of the brightest men I know." It so happened that on Monday afternoon father met the gentleman to whom the remark had been attributed and thanked him for his kind words. To which the gentleman replied somewhat brusquely: "I never said it!" And that was that. 

Father had a most enjoyable week at the Assembly, making friends with the youngsters and reading one of his short stories every day to them as they gathered in the large assembly tent. Their satisfaction was expressed in ways which left no doubt of its sincerity.

It cannot be said that mother had as enjoyable a vacation as father had that summer. While he and I were in

Maine she, together with my brother and sister, was visiting in Allenton. Soon after their arrival Will had the misfortune to come in contact with poison ivy, with results which lasted until we had rejoined them on Our return from Maine.

XXX Father's activities in Essex were not confined to his pastoral duties. He was prominently identified with the no-license forces of the town-for under the local option then in vogue each town decided for itself whether or not the sale of alcoholic liquors should be permitted within its borders. And it is an evidence of father's sanity and tact that his attitude on this question did not bring any criticism or lessen his popularity or interfere with the success of his work as a minister. 

Father served for two years as "school visitor," a position which corresponded with what was later known as supervisor or superintendent of schools. And though some delicate situations arose during those years, which necessitated some changes in the teaching staff, father made no enemies by his conduct of the schools. 

For several years, too, he was on the governing board of the State Sunday School Convention (inter-denominational), and one summer was a speaker at numerous conferences held throughout the state. This led to the cementing of friendships, outside the Baptist denomination, which were to endure throughout the years. 

XXXI 

At this time Grandfather and Grandmother Phillips usually spent the winters with us. For some years after 

Grandfather left the farm at Allenton they had kept house in Wickford. But as his lameness, due to rheumatism from which he had suffered for many years, increased they gave up trying to maintain their separate home. During the summers they lived with mother's oldest brother in Allenton, but as I have said were with us in the winter. 

Grandmother Chipman and father's two sisters were often with us. And there was a constant coming and going of visitors from Quidnessett, Walton, and Pawling.

Of course, it fell to our lot to entertain all the missionaries and officials of the various missionary societies who came frequently to speak in the church. Some of these came more than once and became fast friends of father and mother. I recall one district secretary of one of the societies whose friendship and interest were illustrated by a trifling incident which left a deep impression upon me. 

I was attending the Morgan High School in Clinton at the time, as there was no high school in Essex then. 

This necessitated a trip by train to Clinton and return each day. As Essex was on the Valley Branch of the railroad and Clinton was on the Shore Line, it was necessary for me to change cars at Saybrook Junction. 

One day as I stood waiting on the platform at the Junction for the local from New London the Boston express came roaring to a stop. To my surprise the secretary in question rushed down the steps from the train, grabbed my hand, and cried: "Saw you from the window, Charlie. How are your father and mother?" 

I told him they were well. "Give them my love," said he, and darted back to the train just as it started again. 

Another happening which fastened itself in my memory was of entirely different character. A lady missionary from 

Japan was with us over Sunday. After the Sunday evening supper, father came into the kitchen where I was helping mother with the dishes and said: "Did you know the cake was alive with ants to-night?" "No, I didn't," was the answer, as mother inspected the remainder of the cake, only to find that father was right. It was literally covered with the very smallest ants that ever infested a pantry. Mother was chagrined, but her only remark was: "I guess she's had to eat worse things than that in 

Japan," and the incident was closed.

Once one of the ladies of the church remarked to mother: "What a privilege you have, entertaining so many of our missionaries in your home!"

To which mother made the disconcerting reply: "It is a privilege which I am willing to share with you at any time.' 

Once when Grandmother Phillips was visiting us during the summer and Grandfather had remained in Rhode Island, she set the date for her return to Allenton. On the appointed day word was left for the station wagon to call in time to take her to the afternoon train. Grandmother, wrap and bonnet on, sat waiting for the carriage. It did not come. Train time passed, and still Grandmother sat there waiting. Mother said: "You may as well take off your bonnet, mother, it's too late for you to catch the train to-day." "Yes," said Grandmother with a sigh, "but I shall go to-morrow morning." "Oh, you mean you'll go to-morrow, the Lord willing," some one remarked in attempted fun. "No," said Grandmother firmly, "I shall go to-morrow, whether or no." Then, as she realized the possible implications of what she had said, she exclaimed: "No! no! I didn't mean that."

In some localities the Essex parsonage might easily have gained the reputation of being a haunted house, for at times it was subject to unaccountable noises. One evening there came on the kitchen door, which opened on the side porch close by the door to the dining room, a loud bang, as though a stone had been thrown violently against it, followed by the less violent thump of the stone as it dropped to the porch. Some one ran out immediately to investigate, but there was no stone, no mark where it had struck the door, and not a sign of anyone in the vicinity.

Over a period of years another unexplained noise occurred frequently, sometimes when only the family was present, sometimes when there were callers. We would hear steps coming up the gravelled path, they would mount the steps of the porch, and there would be a knock on the dining-room door. But when the door was opened there would be no one there, nor could we ever find any trace of anyone about the yard at such times. 

On one occasion when father and mother were at some gathering and a lady was staying with us children, there

was suddenly a thunderous pounding on the floor of the room in which we were sitting. It sounded just as though some one in the cellar were striking upward at the beams beneath the floor with a sledge hammer. But both outside and inside cellar doors were securely fastened and there was no one in the cellar.

These and numerous other strange noises were never explained. 

XXXII 

The Essex church was most fortunate in having a quartette choir of unusual excellence. The organist was an Englishman, a "younger son," who had come to America and married an American wife. He was a musician of more than ordinary abilities, but had never won the recognition which he deserved until, while still in charge of our choir, he was chosen organist for the Biltmore estate in North Carolina. We were more than sorry to have him go, but rejoiced in the promotion. He remained at Biltmore until his death years later.

The members of the quartette were all Essex folk and three were members of the church. Their voices blended perfectly, each was a capable soloist, and there was no friction between them and the organist or between them individually.

The choir had quite a reputation outside the village and there were frequently out-of-town visitors at church who came solely to hear the choir. They were always satisfied with the music. Let us hope that they profited also from the gospel truths which they heard.

Father had no knowledge of music, could not sing a note, and could not tell one tune from another; perhaps that was why there was never any unpleasantness over the choir in any of his pastorates. But he did in some unexplained way distinguish between good and bad music. 

He was always appreciative of the Essex choir and often referred to it in after years, but never before the members of another choir!

By contrast, mother had a sweet soprano and could whistle like a mockingbird. And until her household cares became so heavy that she could find no time for music

she performed capably if not brilliantly on the piano. 

Many an evening was spent around the piano when guests, old or young, joined in singing hymns or the familiar old songs.

XXXIII 

Eight happy years passed quickly away. In the spring of 1900 father received a call to become pastor of the Baptist church in Damariscotta, Maine. A good part of the membership of the Essex church were aware of the fact before father was. A despatch from Damariscotta announcing the fact that the church there had called to the pastorate the Rev. Dr. Chipman of Essex, Connecticut, appeared in one of the Middletown, Connecticut, evening papers which had a large circulation in our town. However, it was not the evening paper which we took, and so we remained in ignorance until one of the neighbors came in to ask if the report were true. Father replied that he had not seen the paper, that he had had no letter or telegram from Damariscotta himself, but he presumed it was a correct account as he had been in previous correspondence with the Damariscotta church and had been there to preach for them. To a crowded prayer meeting that same evening he made a similar statement.

In due time the official call arrived. I know that none of the family had the least desire to leave Essex, but father believed the call was an indication that it was time for him to close his work with the Essex church, and he accepted the call.

The protests from the church and the community were most energetic and persistent. One of them came from an unexpected quarter. Soon after the call had been received the family were on the train bound for Mystic. The conductor, who was not an Essex man, passing through the car, touched father on the arm and barked out: "Say No,

Say No!" and passed on without further comment. It was not until he returned later to take our tickets that father learned the words were intended as a protest against his accepting the call to the Damariscotta pastorate. 

However, no protests could shake father's conviction that his Lord was calling him to the new field of labor.

It was the first of May when we bade farewell to the dear friends whose love was so warmly returned and started on what seemed to all of us a long journey. 

XXXIV 

Damariscotta and its twin village, Newcastle, are situated at the head of navigation about eighteen miles from the mouth of the Damariscotta river, one of the numerous inlets of the sea which indent the coast of Maine. In those days, of course, the Kennebec river had not been bridged and the trains for Rockland were ferried across from Bath to Woolwich, so that the train leaving Boston shortly after noon did not reach the station on the Newcastle side of the twin villages until half-past seven. 

It was already dark and was raining hard when we arrived, so our trip down the hill, across the bridge, and up the quaint old Main street of Damariscotta gave us no notion of the real attractiveness of the village. But the welcome we received left no doubt of the hospitality and kindness of our new friends. And the years which were to come were only to deepen our conviction of the real worth of the Damariscotta folk.

The Damariscotta church is a beautiful old colonial building with a pillared porch and a steeple containing the town clock. It stands at the head of Main Street at the top of a slight rise which can hardly be dignified by the name of hill. The interior had been newly decorated not many years before our coming and the plain glass windows replaced with handsome colored ones. On the lower floor, entered by the door at the side, were the vestry, ladies parlor, and kitchen. 

The parsonage stands diagonally across the little park at the head of Main Street. It is a commodious dwelling with a small lawn in front and a larger yard in the rear with room for a small garden. It contained in our day eight large rooms in the main house, with a kitchen in the ell which is in the rear, rather than at one side. Above the kitchen were a lumber room and a small bedroom. 

If we were all a bit homesick at first, and I think that we all were, we soon found as kind and loyal friends in Maine as we had left behind in Connecticut. The twin

villages of Damariscotta and Newcastle, on the opposite banks of the river and then joined by a wooden bridge, formed one community though in different townships and each with its separate post office. They had the distinction in our day of being, to quote a well-known advertising slogan, "99 44/100 per cent" pure American (not, however, Indian) stock.

Damariscotta was one of the conservative old Maine villages where ship-building had at one time been a flourishing industry and where sea captains had built their homes. 

Ship-building had long been a thing of the past, but there were still a few retired captains living. Manufacturing there was none, but as a local trading center for the surrounding rural territory it supported some thriving stores and prospered. Damariscotta had two churches, Baptist and Methodist, and Newcastle also two, Congregational and Episcopal. The various congregations managed to exist without the rivalries so often found in village communities.

Social life was more formal than it had been in Essex. 

Both father and mother were drawn into these outside activities to a greater degree than in any previous pastorate, but it was not long before the parsonage was the same center of neighborly and informal visiting it had always been wherever we were living.

In connection with his duties as pastor of the Damariscotta church father also had charge of the small church at Damariscotta Mills, a village a mile or two to the east. 

There he preached every Sunday afternoon. This made 

Sunday a busy day, with morning, afternoon, and evening preaching services, Sunday school, and the meeting of the 

Christian Endeavor Society. 

Mother, of course, had her Sunday School class, her mission band, and the women's societies to make demands upon her time. There was also the Woman's Club with its mingling of women from all the churches. 

The Damariscotta church was the leading church in the Damariscotta Association of Baptist Churches, and father was promptly elected its representative on the board of the Maine State Convention. This required his attendance at the quarterly meetings of the board, and various

responsibilities in connection with its work throughout the state. At one time he was Chairman of the Evangelistic 

Committee of the board, with oversight of the work of the evangelists supported by the Convention. One of these told me in after years that he had never had a more sympathetic and helpful co-worker and that father's frequent letters were a constant source of inspiration to him. 

In spite of his deep interest in such activities outside the immediate parish which called him away from home frequently, and the qualities which made him what is called "a good mixer," father was essentially a home lover and I have known him when absent to rise before day and leave his stopping place without his breakfast in order to catch the earliest possible train, that he might be at home the sooner.

Whenever he was approached on the subject of membership in one of the fraternal orders his invariable reply was: "My time belongs to my Lord and must be devoted to His work. I cannot afford to spend it in lesser concerns.' 

And his attitude never caused offense, even to the most zealous. In fact, I am sure that it increased the esteem in which he was held in every community where he resided. XXXV Large as the parsonage was, all the room was needed that first winter we spent in Damariscotta. Grandfather and Grandmother Phillips were with us and also Grandmother Chipman and father's twin sister-the older sister had died several years before.

And in other years the guest rooms were in constant use, for there was a continuous succession of visitors from Quidnessett, from Walton, and from Essex, to say nothing of the missionaries and others who came to speak in the church.

One of the missionary secretaries who came to stay over Sunday with us one summer was an old friend. He had been at Christmas Cove with the pastor of one of the city churches. They came up to Damariscotta on the morning boat together. The pastor was to take the train back to his

city home. The missionary surprised us by rushing in the front door exclaiming: "Pray! pray hard!" 

Asked what we were to pray for, he said: "That the boy to whom I gave my mileage book may be an honest fellow!" Then out the door he flew and down the street towards the station. 

On his return some time later he explained that he had intended to loan his railroad mileage book to his friend, but had forgotten to give it to him when they parted at the steamer. As soon as he discovered the omission he stopped a passing urchin, gave him the mileage and instructed him to take it to the pastor, who was well-known in the village, at the station. "It was a wrong thing to do," he said, "to place such a temptation in the way of the boy, and I realized it before I reached the parsonage. So I asked you to pray and I hurried to the station. Fortunately the boy was honest, and he had already delivered the book before I got there." 

Sunday evening, after the service was over, we saw another side of our friend's personality. The strain of the two addresses made during the day being over, he sat down and kept us in a gale of laughter for an hour or more with amusing and witty stories of his experiences at home and in foreign lands.

At another time the missionary who was to speak in behalf of one of the mission schools in the South was a negro woman of very considerable ability. She had been travelling for some time and her supply of toilet articles and other necessities needed replenishing. Whether she was too lazy to shop herself, or whether she simply enjoyed having some one wait upon her, we never knew, but she kept father running errands for her the better part of one day. I think I never wondered at father's patience and kindliness more than I did during that day, which was a most trying one for all the family.

At another time we had as our guest over Sunday the representative of one of our Baptist colleges who was endeavoring to secure additions to the college endowment. 

He was a graduate of father's college, and Sunday evening they sat until a late hour discussing the college life of by-

gone days and the problems of the present. Monday morning our guest seemed to be in the best of health and led family devotions before starting for the station. Father wished to accompany him, as he was in the custom of doing with departing guests, but the visitor said: "No. I have one or two calls to make on my way and 

I'd prefer to go alone. I'll start early so as to have plenty of time."

Just about train time a hack drove furiously up to our door. The driver ran up the steps, where father met him, having seen his approach. "Come quick!" the man exclaimed. "Your friend has been taken ill at the station." 

Father ran to the hack, jumped in, and away they went.

But their haste was unnecessary. Our visitor had died almost instantly from apoplexy. A little later the body of the man who had left us in such apparent health was brought back in the undertaker's wagon. 

Of our visitor's family we knew nothing. Father called by long distance telephone the president of the college in behalf of which the man had been soliciting funds, and told him what had happened. The president in turn bore the sad tidings to the widow. That same evening he arrived in Damariscotta and explained to father what was desired. 

A brief service was held at our house, and then father and 

I accompanied the casket to the railroad Junction at

Brunswick, where it was met by the widow and friends and taken to another state for burial. 

Nearly twenty years later father and mother found themselves living in the same town with the widow and there developed a friendship which lasted until the death of the widow.

XXXVI 

From September, 1902, onward I was in college and was at home only during the vacation periods, so that my own observation of the later years of the family sojourn in the Damariscotta parsonage were limited. Grandfather and Grandmother Phillips came to us again in the fall of 1904. For many years Grandfather had been crippled with rheumatism and the long journey from Allenton, Rhode Island, to Damariscotta must have been a trying

experience, but he made no complaint. Most of his time was spent in a chair by one of the living room windows, and only on rare occasions was he able to get out of doors. 

Yet he was always cheerful and never complained. 

He was a great reader of the New Testament and wore out more than one copy by constant use. He also read the local weeklies from Rhode Island from the front page to the last, advertisements included, and so kept in touch with the hometown affairs. In the spring of 1905 his strength gradually lessened and in June he died at the age of eightyfour. Grandmother, who was his junior by nine years, made her home with us almost constantly thereafter. 

Early in 1906 father's health failed. The care of two parishes and the outside work as a member of the State Convention Board proved too heavy a load for his strength. 

His physician felt that a complete rest from the strain of pastoral work and an active outdoor life for a time would be the best medicine. Father resigned his pastorate, although the church was reluctant to have him go and offered to give him a leave of absence with salary for six months and to supply the pulpit while he was away. 

Father and mother had purchased a farm near Talcottville, Connecticut, about thirteen miles east of Hartford. This had been made possible by a small legacy which mother received at the death of Grandfather Phillips together with the cash value of father's life insurance. The location selected was the result of the desire of both father and mother to be near mother's youngest brother, who had been in business in Hartford for many years.

The six years in Damariscotta had so endeared the people of the church and of the community to us all that it was no easy matter to sever the ties which bound us together. As a matter of fact the mere physical separation did not sever them, for they endured as long as father and mother lived.

When the day came for the family to leave Damariscotta, early in May, a large delegation of friends gathered at the station to bid them godspeed, although the train left at six o'clock in the morning and a severe snow storm was in progress.

So another chapter in the family history came to a close.

XXXVII I was in my senior year at college when the family moved to the farm, and was not present on that snowy May morning when the farewell to Damariscotta was said.

My first view of our new home came nearly two months later, after my graduation.

I have often thought since that the last journey which I shall take, when I leave this earthly dwelling for the heavenly one, will resemble in some respects-and I say it with all reverence—the journey from the college town to the farm. For I was going by a way which I had never travelled before to a community which I had never seen and of which my only knowledge came by the testimony of others. When I stepped off the train at the end of the day's ride I felt most decidedly a stranger in a strange land.

But Will was waiting for me at the station and when a few minutes later we drove into the yard there were father, mother and grandmother to welcome me, and though I had never seen the place before, it was home to me from that first moment. So, I think, will it be when the time comes for me to say goodby to the familiar places of this world and go on to the heavenly mansions that have been prepared for those who put their trust in Him who died upon that green hill far away. There will be waiting there not only those whom "I have loved long since and lost awhile," but also the Elder Brother who loved me and gave 

Himself for me, and I am sure that it will indeed be home.

The farm house, a big, roomy dwelling of a type familiar throughout New England, faced the east and stood close by the main highway from Hartford to Rockville, with a narrow strip of lawn in front, shaded by towering elms and maples. The house was two stories in height, with the front door in the center, giving entrance to the regulation front hall, from which the stairway mounted rather steeply to the second floor. On each side of the hall was a large square room. Back of the hall a huge chimney occupied the center of the house. Extending across the rear there were three rooms and a large pantry. An ell which contained the kitchen and summer kitchen ran back to the woodshed. 

On the second floor there were five rooms.

In the kitchen beside the sink stood a cask into which ice-cold spring water was continually coming at the bottom to run out through an overflow pipe near the top. 

The spring from which the water came was located some distance from the house. The view from the windows above the sink was almost sufficient compensation for the hours which mother had to spend there. 

Back of the house there was a cluster of buildingsbarns, icehouse, sheds and corncrib.

It was late in June when I came to the new home, and I had missed the most strenuous days of the transition from parsonage to farmhouse, but I had received some notion from mother's letters of the difficulties encountered, and after my arrival I learned more. It certainly had been a strenuous time for father, mother and Will.

To take over a one-hundred acre farm in the spring, with a herd of sixteen to twenty cows to care for, crops to plant, with haying coming on almost before there was even time to take a long breath, was indeed a venture of faith.

Will was in charge, a heavy responsibility for a twentyone-year-old who had had almost no training which would fit him for the job. Father went to the limit of his strength each day in the lighter work, which nevertheless was a heavy task for a man whose habit of life had been so utterly different. And how mother, with her frail health, was able to shoulder the burden of caring for that large house, washing heavy milk cans, and cooking for men whose appetites had grown tremendously because of their active outdoor life, I am still unable to understand to this day, except upon the belief that God gave the extra strength as it was needed.

We were fortunate in finding-as had always been our lot-good neighbors, who were kind and helpful from the very first day. They may have considered, and doubtless did so consider, the Chipmans somewhat mad to undertake farming on so large a scale. But they did not make their views known; instead they gave what help they could-for they were busy with their spring work-and gave good advice whenever it was needed. Without their cooperation the venture would undoubtedly have proved

133 much more difficult than it actually was. The passage of the years only increased our liking and respect for them. 

The village of Talcottville was about one mile distant, with its village store and post office. There was also a 

Congregational church, where we were to attend service. 

The nearest Baptist Church was in Rockville, four miles away. However, father and mother transferred their membership from Damariscotta to the First Baptist

Church in Hartford, where they were able, for some years, to attend an occasional service. 

Nearer than Talcottville in another direction was the hamlet known as Dobsonville. Here there was a small

Methodist church, almost in sight from our front windows. 

Because it was nearer and was a little flock, in the later years at the farm we attended services at this Methodist church more often than at Talcottville. The pastors of both churches were equally friendly and the members equally cordial. The young people of the Methodist church soon got into the habit of calling at the farm and often had their prayer meetings or social gatherings there. 

The trolley from Hartford to Rockville passed a short distance away across the fields, but in full view from our windows, and it was not a long walk to the nearest trolley stop. There was in those days a half-hourly service in either direction and a twenty-cent fare to Hartford. Later on fares were increased, service was curtailed, and eventually the trolley gave place to the motor bus. But that was far in the future in the summer of 1906. 

To add to the already heavy burdens which rested on father and mother, I had not been home two weeks when I was compelled to take my bed with an attack of typhoid fever, and it was September before I was able to be about again. During all those weeks father and mother cared for me in addition to keeping up with the other work which fell to their lot. Truly God, to whom they looked for strength, heard their cry and enabled them to carry on.

XXXVIII 

I have no intention of giving in detail an account of the management of the farm during the years that it was

the family home. That was the responsibility of my brother, and he acquitted himself well. But this is the story of father and mother and their ministry. Of course, in a sense that story involves the activities of the farm during their residence there, for father did his full share of the farm work, and upon mother fell the household cares until my brother's marriage, but that was years later.

Father had consulted a physician in Hartford upon his arrival and the diagnosis had not been encouraging. A possible six months of life was promised him; but as in the case of mother twenty years earlier the physician had not taken into account the prayers that went up constantly for healing, and there were in fact many years ahead for father, with much further effective service for his Lord. 

The friends in Quidnessett, in Walton, in Essex, in

Damariscotta, were not long in finding their way to the new Chipman domicile, and to them the latchstring was always out.

Father delighted in showing the visitors about the farm and explaining to them its advantages. Mother rejoiced in the abundant supply of milk, cream, eggs, fresh vegetables and fruits, with which to prepare the bountiful meals which she loved to place before her guests. There were times when even her ingenuity was taxed to find a place for all to sleep, large as was the house. 

In spite of the unfavorable opinion of his physician, father's health soon began to improve and as his strength returned opportunity to preach came also. This was a great joy to him, for his heart was in the Master's work and it had been a great cross that he had been obliged, even for a short time, to give up the preaching of the gospel. Soon the Secretary of the Connecticut Baptist

Convention began to call upon him to act as a supply for pastorless churches. As time went on father became a sort of interim pastor for such churches until they could locate the right man to assume a permanent pastorate. 

In a number of cases these periods of service lasted for six months and the parishes ranged from small country towns to large city churches. Of course, father was at home on the farm during the week, going to his church on Saturday and returning on Monday.

XXXIX 

In 1911 father was asked to take charge of what appeared to be a forlorn hope, the Olivet Baptist Church in what was then known as Parkville, a suburban section of Hartford. The church, which was in reality little more than a mission, worshipped in a small wooden chapel on a business street. Without a pastor, it was in a run-down condition, and the Secretary of the State Convention believed that father was the man to put it on its feet. 

Accordingly he asked father if he would be willing to undertake the work. Father agreed to see what he could do and to serve at a merely nominal salary. And so the farm-house at Talcottville became another in the series of parsonages which had begun at Quidnessett. 

On his first Sunday morning at Olivet father's congregation numbered six-not a very encouraging beginning. 

But that did not daunt the new minister. The situation was one in which father's qualities as pastor, gospel preacher, and sympathetic friend were again to prove their value. 

Slowly, by painstaking house to house visitation, by tact in dealing with those whose interest had grown cold, and by an unquenchable optimism, he won the support of the discouraged membership, gave them a vision of their opportunities, guided their efforts, and built up a live and devoted congregation.

Mother had her part in the work, even though her duties as a housewife at a distance of fifteen miles from the meeting house made it difficult for her to attend the services of the church. She entertained the Ladies' Circle at the farm, gave a warm welcome to families who came out for the day or for the evening; she made the members of the church feel that even though she was unable to carry on the work of the pastor's wife in the usual way she had a real interest in them and in the success of the church. 

Once each year, in the early fall, when watermelons were ripe, she opened the house to the entire membership of the church and Sunday School, who chartered a trolley car for the trip, descended on the farm in a body, and proceeded to enjoy themselves to the utmost. There was watermelon for everyone, there were other refeshments, and a social time for all. An old fashioned donation party

in reverse, so to speak, it was a gala occasion to which old and young alike looked forward with pleasure. 

It was inevitable that as the church took on new life the little one-room chapel in which it had held its services for so long should be outgrown. A new meeting-house became a necessity. In this crisis a member of the church came forward to offer a site for the building at the corner of Capitol Avenue and Newton Street. The Hartford 

Baptist Union approved the project, provided the church with the assistance of its friends in the city would raise whatever sum was necessary over and above the amount which could be secured by a mortgage on the completed building.

Upon father the active direction of the whole enterprise fell. Actually, he raised by personal solicitation the greater part of the money necessary to meet the terms of the Hartford Baptist Union; he arranged for the mortgage; he guided the committee in the selection of the plans for the structure; and he attended to the thousand one other details involved.

The new meeting house was completed. It was a substantial building of brick, with an attractive auditorium, adequate accomodations for the Sunday School and the social activities of the church. In its new home Olivet continued to grow and prosper. The attendance had increased from the original half dozen to considerably more than one hundred; there were flourishing organizations of men, women, and young people; it was meeting its obligations promptly. In short, from a moribund organization the church had grown into a going concern. 

But the price which was paid for the successful completion of the task was a second breakdown of father's health. 

One Sunday in the summer of 1916 as he was on his way by trolley from the farm to the morning service, he was suddenly stricken. Carried to the home of one of the families of the church, he remained there for a day or two until he was able to be taken back to the farm. His condition made it impossible for him to continue his pastoral work, and a few weeks later he resigned as pastor of Olivet.

XL 

The close of the Olivet pastorate also marked the end of father's work as an active pastor, although not the end of his ministry. But sixty-two years of age, he was nevertheless physically unable to again assume the charge of a church; indeed, for some months it seemed highly improbable that he would ever be able to preach again. 

But once more the prayer of faith in his behalf was answered. It was not long before he was able to resume the lighter work about the farm; later to preach occasionally as a supply; and eventually to act for short periods as interim pastor in some of the churches of the state. 

In 1917 Grandmother Phillips died at the age of eightyseven after a short illness. Following her death mother's health again occasioned her physician some anxiety, and he at length announced that both father and mother would do well to leave the farm.

After some persuasion on my part, it was agreed that they should come to Waterville, Maine, where I was serving as librarian at the college, and make a home for me there. Will and his wife consented to remain at the farm and carry on there. The change was made in the fall of 

1919, and for the next four years my parents were with me in Waterville during the months the college was in session, but returned to Talcottville to spend each summer at the farm. 

In Waterville father found many friends made during the years of the Damariscotta pastorate, while he and mother were not slow in making a host of new friends among the membership of the First Baptist Church, to which they transferred their membership.

Into the work of that church both entered with zest. 

It was a novel experience to be able to participate in the various activities of a large city church without that sense of responsibility which had heretofore rested upon them as pastor and pastor's wife in smaller fields. Father taught a large class in the Sunday School and mother found a place for herself in the numerous women's organizations.

Father was able to give me most valuable assistance as an unpaid helper at the college library. It was congenial

work which he thoroughly enjoyed and he promptly made friends among the college students. 

His wide acquaintance among the Baptist churches of the state led to frequent calls upon him as a pulpit supply, and during one year he served as a very efficient church visitor for our own church in Waterville. 

While our home could no longer be classed as a parsonage, it was nevertheless a center of influence to such an extent that those years in Waterville were fruitful ones in the Master's service.

From the fall of 1920 until the summer of 1923 we were living in a small suite in a large apartment house, where there were a number of children. These all fell in love with father and mother and there were few days when one or more of them did not find a way to our apartment to spend happy hours with "Grandpa and Grandma" Chipman.

Having no grandchildren of their own, my parents were quite willing to accept these little folk as foster grandchildren and gave them a deep affection in return.

Nor were the children the only ones who knocked at our door. Among their elders there were a goodly number who found father and mother a comfort in sorrow, a refuge in trouble, strength in time of need.

Those brief years in Waterville were among the busiest and happiest that father and mother knew, rich in opportunities for service, in privileges enjoyed, in the lasting friendships entered into. To those years they both looked back afterwards with the utmost satisfaction and, perhaps, with longing. 

But in the winter of 1922-1923 father's health again caused us great anxiety. We were so far from my brother and his wife, and from all the other relatives, that it seemed to us to be wise to come nearer to them. The farm at 

Talcottville had been sold in the fall of 1922, as Will felt that, under the increasingly difficult conditions which farmers were called to face, he was unable to carry on alone. He and his wife moved to Manchester, four miles nearer to Hartford, that fall. In June, 1923, father, mother and I said a reluctant farewell to the many friends in

Waterville and established ourselves also in Manchester, not far from my brother's home.

XLI 

In Manchester we remained for seven years. During that period father's health did not permit him to preach, except as an occasional supply, but he was still able to do some writing, and his last serial, published in 1930, was in many ways the best of all his stories.

At first father and mother were able to attend church services regularly and as there was no Baptist church in Manchester, we went to the North Congregational church and there they made many friends. Our membership, however, was in the Central Baptist Church of Hartford. 

In the later years of residence in Manchester they were unable to go to church as frequently but, like many another shutin, took great pleasure in the services made available by the radio. 

To our home in Manchester came friends from Quidnessett and Essex, and Waterville, keeping bright the memories of other days. Among those former members of the Quidnessett church who came was an elderly man, about father's own age, who felt that he must have one more talk with father before he was called to the heavenly home. Another was the wife of one of the deacons, who had visited in nearly every place where we had lived since the 

Quidnessett days. She came on her eightieth birthday for a last call upon her friends of half a century. And then there were the man and wife who had been among the very first whom father had married. They brought their married daughter and grandson to see father and mother.

There were now more frequent visits, too, from the relatives, who had found the trip to Maine too long to permit them to come often. So the latchstring continued to remain out, as it had through all the more active years of father's and mother's ministry.

The children of the neighborhood soon learned that they were welcome at the Chipman home. They were not slow to make use of their opportunities, so that father and mother knew the almost daily joy of ministering to the little ones. 

On January 23, 1927, my parents came to the fiftieth anniversary of their marriage. Just at that time neither

of them was strong enough to receive many callers, so that the celebration at our home was limited to members of the family. But there were messages of congratulation from friends in all parts of the country, and the Manchester Evening Herald devoted two columns to an account of their long years of united service for the Master. 

My work took me to Hartford for ten hours of each day, during which father and mother were alone with the household cares resting upon them. It became apparent that the burden was too great for their strength, and our physician recommended that we secure a heated apartment, with rooms all on one floor, where father and mother would not have the care of a furnace during my absence and other of the heavier housekeeping duties. 

My uncle and aunt were desirous that we should come to Hartford, where we could be near them; and, after considerable deliberation and searching for a suitable apartment, we decided to make the change. On August 

31, 1930, we moved to the city.

XLII 

Our new home was within five minutes' walk of the office where I was employed, for which we were increasingly thankful as the years went by. It was also only a few steps from one of the main thoroughfares with its frequent trolley service. My brother passed within sight of our windows each morning and evening on his way to and from his work, so that he was able to stop frequently, usually to have supper with us before continuing on his way to Manchester.

We were able to go often to my uncle's in another part of the city, and he and my aunt were almost weekly visitors. 

As always, we found kind and friendly neighbors, and it was not long before the children and young folks were running in to see father and mother.

There were a number of former members of the Essex church, friends of the long ago, living in the city or its suburbs, and these were frequent callers, as were members of the Olivet church. Then there were many others who found the way to our doors from Essex, Damariscotta, and Waterville.

Father and mother were never able to attend services at our own church after our arrival in Hartford, but members of that church, many of whom were already known to us, came often to bring their greetings and to receive inspiration in return. The radiance of those two devoted lives was making itself felt in these quiet days of retirement as well as in the days of their active service. 

Each summer there was a vacation of one or two weeks spent with the relatives in Rhode Island, days packed with the pleasure of meeting nieces and nephews, great nieces and nephews, and great-great nieces and nephews. These vacation trips, which otherwise would have proved too tiring for the waning strength of father and mother, were possible because some of the relatives came to our door with their automobiles for my parents and brought them home when the two weeks were over. 

One lady, who had been a member of the Pawling church when father was pastor there but was now living in Hartford, not only came frequently with her husband, but brought with her on one occasion some friends of hers who were not Baptists but were members of another denomination. And that was the beginning of one of the happiest and most blessed of a long line of enduring friendships.

For these new friends formed the habit of coming often on Sunday afternoons and bringing with them other members of their own congregation, to hold a brief service of praise and prayer in our living room. Thus the sunset years of father and mother were brightened by participation in a "cottage prayer meeting" of the same sort which they had carried to other homes in the earlier days. 

Until they reached the age of eighty years father and mother were able, with such help as I could give after office hours, to carry on all the household duties in spite of the increasing infirmities of age. But at length the time came when it was necessary to secure the services of a housekeeper who could relieve them of the burden. Again the Lord showed his goodness and we were fortunate in securing a member of our own church, a very capable and kindly lady, who remained with us until the final break-up of our home.

It became increasingly evident that the shadows were closing in, though both parents retained their lively interest in all that was going on in the world about them and especially in the work of the Kingdom to which they had devoted so many years of service. As the fall of 1936 drew to an end, father and mother began to look forward hopefully to reaching together the sixtieth anniversary of their marriage. And on January 23, 1937, the great day came. Its observance, for which we ourselves had made no elaborate plans, was taken out of our hands by some loyal friends who felt that despite the frail health of my parents the occasion was too important to pass without some celebration.

All day long there was a continuous stream of callers. 

The living room became a veritable bower of flowers. 

Many other gifts came from near and far, and a deluge of congratulatory messages poured in from every point of the compass. It was a fitting close to six decades of unusual married happiness and devotion to a beloved Master.

For it was the close. Only a month later, on Wednesday, February 24, 1937, as father sat down at the table for the evening meal the dark angel laid his hand upon him. A blood clot on the brain brought paralysis of his entire right side, and although he lingered until the morning of Sunday, the twenty-eighth, he never rallied from the stroke.

Funeral services were held in Hartford on March second, and on the following afternoon the body was laid to rest beside my sister Jennie in the cemetery at Allenton, in the town where sixty years before he had been ordained and married, and for which he had retained through all the years an abiding affection.

Father's death brought tributes from a very large number of those to whom he had ministered during his long life. Perhaps the most touching of them all came from 

Waterville, Maine. There at a prayer meeting a score or more of his friends joined in a round robin letter of sympathy to mother.

Mother's courage in the face of her loss was a marvel to all of us. That it was due to her nearness to her Savior

and to her absolute assurance of a reunion in the Father's house, there was no doubt. Bravely she faced the days and nights which must have been unutterably lonely, in spite of all the comfort we who were left could give to her. 

My brother and his wife, my uncle and aunt, were towers of strength. But in July, 1938, my aunt, to whom expecially mother had turned in those dark days, died very suddenly. Before the month was over mother suffered a fall, from the effects of which she never recovered, though she was able to get about the house with assistance, and occasionally went to spend a day at my uncle's. On January 14, 1939, she suffered another loss in the death of the brother next younger than herself. From that time her decline was more rapid, and in the following summer it became necessary for us to go to a nursing home where we could still be together and where she could have proper care. 

For a time the change seemed beneficial. She was able to sit up during the greater part of the day and even to walk with assistance. She still kept her interest in her reading, her friends-who were frequent callers and in the work of the Kingdom. But from early in October she was confined to her bed. Her patience and courage in those weeks of helplessness and suffering were marvellous. 

When we had our evening period of devotions her prayers, which were almost wholly for others, were until she became so weak that I could no longer distinguish the words -a remarkable evidence of her continuing faith and love. 

On Sunday, January 14, 1940, she fell asleep in Jesus.

Prayers were said in Hartford on the evening of the sixteenth and the funeral was held in Wakefield, Rhode 

Island, the following afternoon. And then the body was laid at rest between father and my sister in the family plot at Allenton.

XLIII 

As I come to this final chapter I cannot help wondering whether any reader will get from these pages the picture which has been in my mind as I have written: the picture of two quite ordinary persons who were worthy of note because they were devoted to each other to an unusual degree and were united in a common loyalty to their Lord

and Master which sought no material gain but only the advancement of His kingdom on earth.

I think I must have made it plain to all who read that to them the religion of Jesus Christ was no killjoy which consisted solely in thundering "Thou shall not's". No such pseudo-Puritans would ever have won, as did they, the love of countless little children and the confidence and affection of hundreds of young folks and persons of mature years. Theirs was a faith which flowered in love and joy and kindness to all without regard to age, race, or creed.

Little has been said of the articles of faith to which they subscribed. I am no theologian, and if I were, it would be, perhaps, dangerous for me to attempt a formal statement of another person's creed. Yet I believe I can give simply the basis of the faith which was theirs through all their years of service.

They believed that God is and that he is a rewarder of them that diligently seek him; that all have sinned; that 

God so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in Him should not perish, but have everlasting life; that neither is there salvation in any other; for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved; that if we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness; that 

God commendeth His love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us; that the blood of 

Jesus Christ His Son cleanseth us from all sin; that Jesus 

Christ is the resurrection and the life, and whosoever liveth and believeth in Him shall never die; that because 

He lives we shall live also; that He has gone to prepare a place for us and that He will come again to receive us unto 

Himself. "Even so, come, Lord Jesus."

It was to make known this old fashioned gospel of salvation through faith in the atoning death of the Son of God that they gave themselves through sixty years of common ministry. Like their Lord and Master they went about doing good, and wherever they went there were those who "took knowledge of them that they had been with Jesus."
