PREFACE 

The material in this book has been arranged so that it may be used with any chemistry text. It is the only attempt which has been made to correlate major understandings based on subject matter with scientific methods of thinking, and scientific attitudes. The author has attempted to take a middle of the road point of view drawing heavily from the 31st Yearbook and the Thayer Reports. General objectives are talked of, but most courses of study in chemistry are still based entirely on subject matter and are taught in a most socially useless way. The claim is made that this course actually meets, in terms of specific classroom situations, the general objectives set forth in the 31st Yearbook. In cases where in organization of materials the subject matter understandings or concepts conflicted with scientific method understandings, the scientific method was given the preference. 

The pattern retains the good features of the daily assignment plan and at the same time enables a pupil to be challenged by the full block of material in an area. 

The pupil with the teacher may organize the material in the area. This tends to establish a feeling of ownership in the pupil toward the working assignment. By organizing his own material and time budgeting the work, the pupil is also given a sense of direction and is stimulated to develop systematic study habits. The work has been disorganized in some areas to enable the pupil to fit the pieces together and see the whole pattern. This is not too difficult for high school pupils. Before preparing lectures or using the book, teachers are urged to read Downing, E.R. "An Introduction to the Teaching of Science", 1934, University of Chicago Press, Chicago, Illinois.

The experiments have been written to stimulate the pupil to use consciously the scientific method with safeguards. It is a difficult thing to get pupils to read an experiment through before they try to perform it. The "cook book" chemist of hazy ideas is too frequently found in beginning classes. The pupil must therefore be trained to write his own objective after reading the experiment through. This should be checked by the teacher in a short conference before the pupil performs the experiment.

This is one of the few attempts which has been made to train pupils directly through experimentation and class exercises, consciously to use the scientific method. 1

The 

The subject matter has been used as a tool or carrier for the scientific method. literature being quite confused on the subject of the scientific attitude, for the purposes of this work, it is suggested that teachers think of an attitude as being a separate element as distinguished from scientific method, perhaps dealing more with the emotional side of the pupils' experience. The attitude may then be used as a mechanism for carrying the scientific method from the classroom to the socio-economic situation. It would be a mechanism to bring about transfer of training which might not otherwise occur.

Understandings, concepts and directions for teachers may be obtained from Martin McGill, Lorain High School, Lorain, Ohio. A laboratory manual with detailed experimental directions is used with this book. The sketchy experiments are to aid pupils in making their time budget plan and will suggest to the teacher the scientific method to be stressed in that particular experiment.

INTRODUCTION 

One morning Becquerel, a French scientist, walked into a dark room which he used for developing X-ray and photographic plates. By accident he had placed a piece of uranium ore upon a sensitive photographic plate which had been lying on a table. On removing the ore, he was surprised to find that the plate had been changed exactly where the ore had rested. Becquerel could not understand how this could have happened. His scientific curiosity was aroused. Why had the plate been changed? With all the precision of a trained investigator, he set about the job of discovering the cause for such an effect. 

Sample after sample of uranium ore was tested. In every case a spot was left on the plate. What was in the ore to produce such an effect? Becquerel, his interest now at a high pitch, analyzed the ore and found a mineral which was much more powerful than its uranium content could account for. 

He said that to affect a photographic plate, there must be another element with power many times greater than that of uranium itself. This element was obtained from the mineral pitchblende. Becquerel needed help. This work had possibilities. 

He searched his laboratories for a student who would be worthy of such a task. He chose. Marie Curie, a Polish girl. Marie was eager for an opportunity to do this experimental work. Marie worked for days over tons of ore and steaming cauldrons. 

Then, in all humbleness, came the great report to the world of the discovery of radium. Imagine the joy that came to Becquerel and to Marie!

Chemical Language

The chemist has a language of his own. The elements, as you have discovered, have names. You might look in the back of your text for a list of the elements. 

When two atoms join to form a molecule or compound, the name of the compound generally ends in "ide". Thus common salt, NaCl, is sodium chloride; KBr, potassium bromide ends the same way; NaNO3 sodium nitrate differs. This compound ends in "ate" for it has a full quota of oxygen atoms present. Sodium nitrite, NaNO2, has just two oxygen atoms one less than sodium nitrate. Consider another example Na2SO4, sodium sulfate.

The ending is "ate".

Sodium sulfite, Na₂SO, ends in "ite" and has one less oxygen than the "ate" ending.

Types of compounds called oxides are formed when some elements join with oxygen to form new substances. Carbon monoxide has the formula CO. Notice the prefix "mono" meaning one. Carbon dioxide, CO₂ has two oxygen atoms present. The prefix is "di" meaning two. Sulfur trioxide, SO3, means that there are three atoms of oxygen present. If things always happened that way, the situation would be simplified but like most things in nature there are exceptions. For instance Fe203 is not commonly called ferric trioxide but ferric oxide. A relative of this compound is ferrous oxide Fe0. Compounds ending in "ic" have more oxygen atoms present than those ending in "ous", but here again there are exceptions like cuprous oxide Cu₂0 and cupric oxide CuO. Study these examples and see if you can work out an explanation for these facts. 

You might also consider the examples of ferrous chloride FeCl2 and ferric chloride FeCl3.
