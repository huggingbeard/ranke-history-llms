THE FIRST LANDOWNERS OF FRANKFORT, KENTUCKY-1774-1790 

A number of unique incidents and a group of interesting, resourceful men are identified with the earliest ownership of the land upon which Frankfort, the Capital of Kentucky, is situated. The story of the bold and aggressive enterprise which resulted in the selection in 1792 of this town-then but a sprawling log-cabin, frontier village-as the seat of State government is a familiar one, but the narrative of the acquisition of the land which made this possible has never been told. The little that is now known suggests that could all or most of the varying detail be recovered from the encroaching oblivion of nearly a century and three quarters of time, an account equal in human interest, if not excelling that of most cities of the American mid-West might be the result.

While Daniel Boone and other early white explorers, whose names and exploits in the wilderness have mostly been forgotten, undoubtedly traversed at various times the townsite area and vicinity of Frankfort during the third quarter of the eighteenth century, it was not until July 16, 1773-about a year before the accepted date of the founding of Harrodsburg, "the mother town of Kentucky"-that man looked upon the broad undulating bottoms of the Kentucky river here with a definite view to possession. On this date, as their separate journals recite, James and Robert McAfee coming up the old buffalo trace, Alanant-o-Wamiowee, from Big Bone Lick and Drennon's Lick arrived at "the great crossing" a place that later became known as the Leestown Shoal. 

About this time James McAfee wrote in his journal: "The 16th [of July, 1773]. 

In five miles we crossed the Cantucky river to the east side along the path; .. in a piece of black old oak timber land; we stopped and surveyed one tract of land for Robert McAfee containing 600 acres, about 100 of that meadow ground."

This survey, the first to involve any portion of the townsite of Frankfort, was made by Hancock Taylor a deputy of Col. William Preston, surveyor of 

Fincastle county, Virginia. Taylor, at the time, was accompanying the brothers McAfee in the Kentucky land explorations. His beginning corner, near the Kentucky river, was at the time marked by "two white oaks. These forest trees have long since been removed, but the point is marked today by a metal plate at "Number 205" in the sidewalk on the east side of Ann Street in the block south of Main Street. This marker recites that: "Hancock Taylor began his land survey here in Fincastle County, Virginia, July 16, 1773." A gracefully carved stone monument with appropriate lines engraved to com-

memorate this event was set up at this location during Frankfort's Centennial in October, 1886, but as it somewhat interferred with traffic has been removed to the stone wall at the dead-end of the street. On a near-by brick building at the southeast corner of Main and Ann Streets, a bronze tablet unveiled during the Frankfort sesqui-Centennial exercises in 1936 bears the following

October 6, 1936 1936 Their survey completed in the high bottoms of the Kentucky river, the McAfee party moved along the old buffalo trail into what is today southeastern Franklin County, thence into Woodford County, crossed the river about two miles above Glenn's Creek and within a few days came upon that beautiful sweep of level upland in Mercer County now marked by the McAfee settlement and old Providence Church. Extensive surveys were made forthwith in this new and inviting area; and this situation finally proving more attractive to 

Robert McAfee, he made settlement here along with his brother James and others and allowed his earlier survey to the 600 acres in the great meadow at the bend of the river, where Frankfort now is, to fail of record and thus go by default. Today the plat of this first land survey of the Frankfort townsite is unknown and its existence may be doubted. 

Nearly a year later-June 17, 1774—Hancock Taylor again appeared on the future townsite of Frankfort commissioned to execute a number of land surveys. Among these was one to be done for his father, Zachary Taylor, grandfather of President Zachary Taylor. Recalling the plats surveyed for

Robert McAfee, and being unaware of his former employer's intent to abandon them, he proceeded to lay off 200 acres for Taylor in accordance with the lines

of the McAfee surveys. Hancock Taylor's original plat of this survey of 1774 may be seen in the archives of the Land Office in the New Capitol Building in Frankfort, and a copy appears in the Virginia Survey, Book I, page 34. 

On the twenty-second day of June, 1780, Governor Thomas Jefferson of

Virginia, at Richmond, signed the grant covering this 200 acres and by this act of the chief executive of the Commonwealth, Zachary Taylor became the first owner of land which six years later was to become a part of the town of 

Frankfort. The grant to Sergeant Taylor follows:

William Haydon, like the McAfee brothers, was an early land seeker in what is now Franklin County. Exploring this part of the Kentucky valley he selected the upland area immediately east of the great bend of the river and the previous surveys of Robert McAfee and Zachary Taylor. Here he built a cabin and planted corn in 1776. Four years later on January 3rd, 1780, he appeared personally and obtained from the Kentucky Land Court seated at Bryant's Station on Elkhorn, pre-emption rights to 1,000 acres "on the Ken- * Virginia Grants, Book 6, p. 46.

-- tucky river situated about two miles above Leestown.' Three years later this large tract was surveyed by John Williams, deputy surveyor of Fayette County, Virginia. A small portion of the western part of the William Haydon Survey on the upper terrace of East Main Street hill is now within the Frankfort corporation. The original survey of this large tract, laid out nearly in the form of a square and without the call of other adjoining property line, may be seen today in the archives of the Kentucky Land Office in the New Capitol at Frankfort.

Neither among the first nor the last to take up land in the great bend of the Kentucky river where Frankfort now stands, Humphrey Marshall, fifth in the line, was easily the most important. Not only did he contribute larger acreage to the town as originally laid out than any other individual, but unshakable legend has it that he was the one who inspired General Wilkinson with the idea of the new town and aided otherwise in bringing about its establishment. Possessed of an unusual mentality, he was at once a lawyer and an adventurer of the first order. While engaged in the legal service of Francis 

McConnell, he discovered that the survey made by Hancock Taylor for Robert

McAfee on July 16, 1773, had not been put to record and that no grant had been issued by Virginia to McAfee because of this default. On May 20, 1784, indicating a prospective boundary of 400 acres on the Kentucky river, Marshall made entry for this important tract of unclaimed Fayette County land in the great bottom just above Leestown-the old stockaded settlement. 

About a year later on June 23, 1785-Humphrey Marshall himself as deputy surveyor under Thomas Marshall, surveyor of Fayette County, completed the survey of the vacant land. William Ballard, a man named Brent and R. Cole were his chain carriers and markers. It fell into two tracts and totaled 260 acres. The largest parcel comprised nearly all of old "North" 

Frankfort the original town of 1786. The remainder extended northeastwardly from the Kentucky river cliffs across the present bounds of the Frankfort 10

cemetery, the Feeble Minded Institute, the upper part of East Main Street and on into the southern part of the Thorn Hill bottoms. 

Humphrey Marshall used a part of Virginia Treasury Warrant No. 19,892 for the purchase of the 260 acres that later became the principal part of Frankfort as established in 1786. This warrant is today in the indexed steel files of the Kentucky Land Office in the New Capitol at Frankfort. It was originally issued "for Aaron Darnold, his heirs or Assigns, the quantity of Five Hundred Fifty-five and a half acres of land in consideration of the sum of nine hundred pounds current money paid into the Public Treasury" on the 15th day of October, 1783.* On the reverse side of the warrant Aaron Darnold assigned it to John Bradford, the celebrated first printer and newspaper editor of Lexington, Kentucky, together with all his "right and title on July 10, 1783;* on February 28, 1784, John Bradford assigned this warrant to William

Marshall, Junior, and on May 20, 1784, William Marshall, Junior, assigned it to Humphrey Marshall, who used it with the survey of June 23, 1785, to secure the grant to the desired 260 acres. This grant, issued by Governor 

Patrick Henry on August 14, 1786, follows:

Anticipating well in advance that a grant would be issued to him from the Commonwealth of Virginia for the desired 260 acres of land in the great bend of the Kentucky river, Marshall approached his neighbor on upper South Elkhorn and laid before him his thoughts as to establishing a town there. No doubt he pointed out that it was on the main road from Lexington to Louisville; stood at the natural headwaters of navigation of the river and the high bottoms of valley at this point were broad enough to permit the growth of the place in years to come. General Wilkinson, alert and actively engaged in various commercial enterprises in the new county, probably needed very little in the way of prompting. He saw the validity of Humphrey Marshall's contentions, likely enough realized that the project of erecting the town and following through with its sub-division was really cut to his measure and afforded him a short and long course if properly exploited to added wealth, prestige and power.

A contract providing for the sale of the land by Marshall and others to Wilkinson was drawn and signed; the Legislature of Virginia was requested to give assistance in the matter and promptly in October, 1786, established by statute the town of Frankfort on the lands of General Wilkinson on the Kentucky river in Fayette County. His grant from the Commonwealth in hand early in the Fall of 1786, on October 25, Humphrey Marshall executed his deed to the 260 acres in the great bend of the river to James Wilkinson and took as consideration in cash one hundred and thirty pounds-the equal at that time of about $433.00. This instrument of land transfer was acknowledged by 

Marshall before the April, 1787 term of the Fayette County, Virginia court in Lexington and its recordation bears the name of the then county clerk, Levi Todd, and his deputy, Thomas Bodley. Though the original of this interesting and important deed was destroyed in the fire which consumed the Fayette County Clerk's office in 1893, it survives as a certified transcript in the voluminous MS record of a suit brought by Wilkinson against Marshall in October, 12

1793, in Lexington and now filed in complete record, Record Book 1-B of the Kentucky Court of Appeals in the New Capitol at Frankfort. 

As the result of a survey completed December 14, 1784, by J. Hite, deputy under William May, surveyor of Jefferson County, Virginia, a tract of 400 acres comprising all of the land between lower Benson Creek and the Kentucky river became the property of Edmund Lyne. This boundary lay immediately west of Hancock Lee's 500 acre survey and the western or larger parcel of Humphrey Marshall's 260 acre survey. It came down to the mouth of Benson 

Creek where the three original counties, Fayette, Lincoln and Jefferson were set to corner by Virginia statute in 1780. This grant of 400 acres to Edmund 

Lyne was executed by Patrick Henry, Governor of Virginia, at Richmond on August 20, 1786. Its issue, preceding by about two months the establishment of Frankfort, by the Legislature of Virginia, made Lyne the sixth landowner of record in the Frankfort area, the southeastern part of his survey being today that part of the Capital known as Belle Point. A warmly contested suit in Chancery in which the heirs of James McConnell, deceased, heir of Francis McConnell, sought and obtained a division of the profits accruing to Humphrey Marshall as a result of his sale of land to General James Wilkinson to form the townsite of "north" Frankfort. This case was begun at the May term in 1804 and was completed May 17, 1806. It involved the survey and invalidated grant of Francis McConnell, and cited the previous survey of Zachary Taylor. The land entry of Francis McConnell was made in 1780 but the survey was not executed until June 24, 1794, and the grant was delayed in issue until June 9, 1796.
