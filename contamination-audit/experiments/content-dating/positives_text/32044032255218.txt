THE JUSTICE OF THE PEACE COURT (Sections from statutes refer to Minn. Statutes 1941) 

I. 

In Minnesota the office of justice of the peace has its roots in our constitution. Article 6, Section 8 of the con stitution of our state provides: "The legislature shall provide for the election of a sufficient number of justices of the peace in each county, whose term of office shall be two years and whose duties and compensation shall be prescribed by law; provided, that no justice of the peace shall have jurisdiction of any civil cause where the amount in controversy shall exceed one hundred dollars nor in a criminal cause where the punishment shall exceed three months' imprisonment or a fine of over one hundred dollars nor in any cause involving the title to real estate."

The provisions embodied in this constitutional mandate date back to our country's early history, and some of them were embodied in the Organic Act of the Territory, and the action was passed when the Organic Act was the fundamental law. John H. Goenen v. John N. 

Schroeder, 8 Minn. 344, 347.

II. 

JURISDICTION IN CRIMINAL CASES Section 633.01 determines the jurisdiction of justices of the peace in criminal matters. It reads as follows: "Justices of the peace have power and jurisdiction, throughout their respective counties, as follows:

(1) To cause to be kept all laws made for the preservation of the peace; (2) To cause to come before them, or any of them, persons who break the peace, and commit them to jail, or bail them, as the case may require; (3) To arrest and cause to come before them, persons who attempt to break the peace, persons who keep houses of ill-fame, or frequenters of the same, or common prostitutes, and compel them to give security for their good behavior, and to keep the peace; (4) To cause to come before them persons who are charged with committing any criminal offense and commit them to jail, or bail them, as the case may require; provided that no justices of the peace shall have jurisdiction of any offenses committed within the limits of any city or village wherein a municipal court is organized and existing, but such offenses, otherwise cognizable by justices of the peace, and those arising under the charter, ordinances, or by-laws of the city or village shall be examined or tried by the municipal court therein existing; provided that this section shall not apply to any cities or villages having justice of the peace courts established by home rule charter, nor to territory within one-half mile of the outer limits of the state fair grounds."

The jurisdiction of justices of the peace is co-extensive with the limits of the county in which they reside. Section 530.01. There are exceptions in civil matters. Section 530.01.

Section 530.02 determines the place where a justice of the peace may hold court. It reads as follows:

"Every justice of the peace shall keep his office in the town, village, city, or ward for which he is elected; but he may issue process in any place in the county, and, in his discretion, for the convenience of parties, may make any civil or criminal process issued my him returnable, and may hold his court, at any place which he shall appoint in the town, village, or ward within his county adjoining the town or ward in which he resides, or in any village located within his town." 

Section 530.03 provides: "No justice of the peace shall hold his office or court in any saloon, nor in any room adjacent to a saloon, or connecting therewith by door or otherwise. Nor shall he hold his office in the same room with a practicing attorney unless such attorney is his law partner, and in that case such partner shall not appear or act as an attorney in any case before such justice."

Section 530.04 declares: 

"A justice of the peace may hold a court for the trial of all actions enumerated in Section 530.05, and hear, try, and determine the same; and for that purpose, where no special provision is otherwise made by law, such court shall have all the powers possessed by courts of record, and all laws of a general nature shall apply to such justice's court, so far as the same are applicable and not inconsistent with the provisions of this chapter; but no justice of the peace shall charge the jury."

The actions enumerated in Section 530.05 are civil in nature, as follows:

"(1) An action arising on contract, for the recovery of money only, if the sum claimed does not exceed $100.00; (2) An action for damages for an injury to the person or to real property, or for taking, detaining, or injuring personal property, if the damages claimed, or, in replevin, the value of the property in controversy, does not exceed $100.00; (3) An action for a penalty given by statute, not exceeding $100.00; (4) An action upon a bond conditioned for the payment of money not exceeding $100.00, though the penalty exceeds that sum; the judgment to be given for the sum actually due. When the payments are to be made by instalments, an action may be brought for each as it becomes due; (5), An action upon an official bond, or bond taken by him, if the penalty does not exceed $100.00; (6) To take and enter judgment on the confession of a defendant, when the amount does not exceed $100.00." 

Section 530.06 denies jurisdiction in justice courts in the following cases: "(1) In a cause involving the title to real estate; (2) Nor for false imprisonment, libel, slander, malicious prosecution, criminal conversation, or seduction, or upon a promise to marry; (3) Nor for an action against an executor, administrator, or guardian, as such."

Section 633.02 determines the geographic limitations of a justice court's jurisdiction in criminal cases as follows:

"Justices have power to hold a court, subject to the provisions hereinafter contained, to hear, try, and determine all charges for offenses arising within their respective counties where the punishment prescribed by law does not exceed a fine of $100.00 or imprisonment for three months."

From the foregoing we determine that a justice of the peace has jurisdiction over any and all misdemeanors committed within the county of his residence and election except cities and villages wherein a municipal court is organized and existing and cities and villages having justice of the peace courts established by home rule charter nor to territory within one-half mile of the outer limits of the state fair grounds. This does not mean that criminal proceedings should be brought indiscriminately before one justice court where the facts show that offenses were committed in townships or villages not adjoining.

III. 

CRIMINAL PROCEDURE IN THE JUSTICE COURT Section 633.03 provides: 

"Upon complaint made to any justice that any such offense has been committed within the county, he shall examine the complainant on oath, and the witnesses produced by him, and reduce the complaint to writing, and cause the same to be subscribed by the complainant; and, if it appears that such offense has been committed, the justice shall issue his warrant, reciting the substance of the complaint, and require the officer to whom it is directed forthwith to arrest the accused and bring him be-

fore such justice, or some other justice of the same county, to be dealt with according to law, and in the same warrant may require the officer to summon such witnesses as shall be named therein to appear and give evidence at the trial."

We note that the section above referred to directs that "if it appears that such offense has been committed, the justice shall issue his warrant, reciting the substance of the complaint, and require the officer to whom it is directed forthwith to arrest the accused * * *." A justice of the peace should not issue warrants indiscriminately. 

The words "if it appears" do not mean that a warrant should be issued upon suspicion, gossip or hearsay. They mean that if legal evidence brought to the justice shows that an offense has been committed within his county then, and only then, should he permit the signing of a criminal complaint. In the application for a warrant for someone's arrest, the fact that a person appears before a justice of the peace and declares that an offense has been committed does not prove such claim. The statute directs the justice to examine the complainant on oath, and the witnesses produced by him. 

In every community there are persons who run to law enforcement officers with both imaginary and factual complaints and there are families here and there who cannot and will not get along with other families or neighbors on a friendly basis. Sometimes difficulties arise from a neighbor's barking dog, the presence of chickens, the break down of fences, the imaginary or factual mistakes in boundary lines, drainage problems, and many other circumstances.

On other occasions difficulties arise from the quarreling and bickering of women over their backyard fences over school teachers and report cards, etc. Many of these quarrels could and would be avoided if they were checked in time in a spirit of tolerance and reasonable understanding.

This clearly indicates that every justice of the peace has two important functions to perform: one, to conserve the peace, and two, to try offenders in cases involving misdemeanors.

Every person resents an unauthorized arrest, and a justice of the peace should be very careful to ascertain from the complainant and his witnesses all of the facts available before a complaint is authorized and a warrant is issued. In plain words, he should refuse to issue criminal complaints and warrants in all cases where there is a lack of legal evidence.

The opportunities to do good are always present with a justice of the peace. Many problems come to his office which can be solved by consultation and advice. When hot-headed persons come to his door with a demand for action and for someone's arrest, a tactful and calm consideration by a justice of the peace will, in most instances, dampen the temper of such persons and bring about a different viewpoint. A calm, tolerant and conscientious justice of the peace can help in such cases. He should not permit himself to be rushed or taken off his feet, as it were. He should remember that he is a judge and that as such he can best serve the people by conserving peace in his community.

Section 625.01 declares: 

"The judges of the several courts of record, in vacation, within their respective districts, as well as in open court, and all justices of the peace, within their respective counties, shall have power to cause all laws made for the preservation of the public peace to be kept and, in the execution of that power, may require persons to give security to keep the peace, or for their good behavior, or both, in the manner provided in this chapter." 

In addition to his function as a court in misdemeanor cases, a justice of the peace has jurisdiction to conduct what is commonly known as preliminary hearings. Whenever a person is charged with the commission of a gross misdemeanor or a felony the accused is entitled to a preliminary hearing before a magistrate to determine whether the evidence is such that the accused ought to be put on trial for the offense charged. The duties of a justice of the peace in preliminary hearings will be discussed under the heading Preliminary Hearings. 

IV. 

THE COMPLAINT AND WARRANT 

Perhaps one of the most difficult items confronting a justice of the peace is the preparation of complaints in criminal proceedings. The complaint should be direct and certain in the following particulars: (1) the party charged; (2) the offense charged; (3) the particular circumstances of the offense charged, when they are necessary to constitute a complete offense.

The material facts constituting the offense must be alleged directly and positively and not inferentially, argumentatively or by way of recital. There must be a direct charge against the accused that he committed the offense. A recital that he is accused of having committed it is not a charge that he has committed it. For instance, if a complaint sets forth that John Doe committed the offense of assault and battery, such a statement would merely be a conclusion and not a statement of material facts and particular circumstances of the offense charged. Our constitution provides that in all criminal prosecutions the accused shall enjoy the right to be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation. Constitution, Article I, Section 6. This principle is not original with the constitution, but is as old as the common law itself. The information required by the constitution must be contained in the complaint. It is a general rule that the offense charged should be described with reasonable certainty in order that the accused may know to what offense he is required to answer, and so that the court may render a proper judgment and that the conviction or acquittal may be pleaded in bar of another prosecution for the same offense. Under these circumstances and mandates, it behooves every justice of the peace to determine three things before any criminal proceeding is commenced in his court: (1) has an offense been committed by the accused; (2) do the facts alleged to have been committed by the accused constitute a misdemeanor, a gross misdemeanor, or a felony under our Minnesota law; and

(3) are the recitals in the complaint direct and certain as it regards the party charged, the offense charged, and the particular circumstances of the offense charged; and do the recitals in the complaint constitute more than one offense? 

Here is one example: in State ex rel. John D. McDonald v. T. T. Riley, 116 Minn. 1, 133 N. W. 86, the defendant was tried, convicted and sentenced to imprisonment in the county jail for 90 days in justice court in the county of Itasca. The complaint charged that he did, at the time and place stated, within the county, "wilfully, unlawfully, and wrongfully use vile and obscene language in the presence of women, against the form of statute in such case made and provided, and against the peace and dignity of the state of Minnesota."

The court, in commenting upon the allegations contained in the complaint, said: "It must be conceded that this complaint does not, as against a direct attack, state a public offense, in that it does not state the name (if known) of the person in reference to and in whose presence the language was used, or the language itself and its natural tendency; and, further, if this were an appeal from the judgment, it would have to be reversed. State v. Clark, 31 Minn. 207, 17 N. W. 344.”

Every justice of the peace should make special efforts to inform himself on the subject of criminal pleadings. 

Most crimes are misdemeanors and therefore come within the jurisdiction of the justice courts. Here is a partial list of the offenses over which the justice of the peace may exercise jurisdiction: assault in the third degree, petit larceny, false statements, slander, keeping of public

places where minors should be excluded, gambling by minors, cruelty to children, regulation of dance halls, fornication, public indecency, betting money at games, public nuisance, permitting buildings to be used as a nuisance, sales of unwholesome poultry and meat, unlawful assembly, written or verbal threats, false statement in advertising, false weights and measures, injury to highways, injury to gardens, wilful destruction of property, violation of game and fish laws, disturbing public meetings, and dozens of other misdemeanors. In other words, his jurisdiction extends into most phases of human conduct. He can do good; he can do evil, all depending on temperament, learning and viewpoint.

VI. 

THE DOCKET 

Let us assume that a justice of the peace has authorized a complaint and issued warrant for the arrest of John Noone upon a charge of assault and battery. What is the next step? The statute provides that a justice must keep a docket and, in the case of John Noone, the law requires that the justice shall enter the action in his docket, in which the State of Minnesota shall be plaintiff and the accused (John Noone) defendant. In addition to this entry, the justice is required to make such other appropriate entries as are required in civil actions. These entries are enumerated in Section 530.08 as follows: 

"Every justice of the peace shall keep a docket in which he shall enter:

(1) The title of all causes commenced before him; (2) The time when the process issued, the nature thereof, when returnable, and the return of the officer; (3) The time when the parties appeared before him; (4) A brief statement of the nature of the plaintiff's demand, and the amount claimed, and, if any setoff was pleaded, a similar statement thereof; (5) Every adjournment, stating at whose request, and to what time and place; (6) The time when the trial was had, and whether the same was by jury or by the justice; (7) The verdict of the jury, and when rendered; (8) The judgment, the time of issuing execution, the name of the officer to whom delivered, an account of the debt, damages, and costs, and the fees due to each person, separately; (9) (10) (11) The fact that an appeal was taken and allowed, and the time thereof; 

Satisfaction of judgment, when made;

All questions of law raised by either party, the order made by the court thereon, any exception taken to such order, and all other matters that are material."

The keeping of a docket is not difficult. All a justice of the peace needs to do is to put down in his docket a record of what he is actually doing. He should be careful about dates, names, motions, rulings, place, trial and sentences.

VII. 

THE ARRAIGNMENT, TRIAL AND SENTENCE 

Let us pursue the case against John Noone further.

The warrant issued by the justice is handed to a constable, who proceeds to arrest John Noone and bring him before the justice. Now what is the justice to do? 

Section 633.05 provides that "* * * the justice shall proceed to hear, try, and determine the action within one day, unless continued for cause." 

Section 633.08 declares that: "The charge made against the accused, as stated in the warrant of arrest, shall be distinctly read to him, and he shall be required to plead thereto, which plea the justice shall enter in his docket. If the accused refuses to plead, the justice shall enter the fact in his docket, with a plea of not guilty in his behalf." 

633.09. "On Plea of Guilty. If the accused pleads guilty to such charge, the court shall thereupon convict him of the offense charged, and render judgment thereon." 

633.10. "Plea of Not Guilty. If the plea is not guilty, and a jury is waived by the accused, the justice shall proceed to try such issue, and determine the same according to the evidence produced against and in behalf of the accused."

It is important that every person brought before a justice shall learn from the justice the charge made against him. Each defendant should be asked what his plea is in respect to the charge made against him: "What is your plea, guilty or not guilty?" No prejudice should be registered in the court's mind against the accused for pleading not guilty. That is his right. However, most persons brought before a justice court do enter pleas of guilty.

Let us assume, however, that in the case against John Noone a plea of not guilty is entered and a demand is made for a jury trial. The word "demand" as used here means that the defendant did not waive jury trial. Under our law, every defendant is entitled to a jury trial unless the accused expressly waives this right.

The court, before proceeding to an investigation of the merits of the action, shall direct the sheriff or any constable of the county to make a list in writing of the names of 24 inhabitants of the county qualified to serve as jurors in the district court. From such list the complainant and accused (the attorneys representing the State and the attorney representing the defendant) may each strike six names and, if either party refuses so to strike, the justice shall direct some suitable disinterested person to strike such names for either or both of the parties so neglecting. When such names are stricken from the list the justice shall issue a venire, directed to the sheriff or any constable of the county, requiring him to summon the 12 persons whose names remain to appear before such justice at the time and place to be named therein, as a jury for the trial of such cause. Section 

633.12. The officer to whom such venire is delivered shall summon such jury personally. He shall make a list of the persons summoned, which he shall certify and annex to the venire, and return the same with such venire to the justice within the time therein specified. Section 633.13.

If any such jurors fail to attend, or if there is a legal objection to any that appear, the justice shall direct the sheriff or any constable who may be present and disinterested to summon bystanders or others who are competent, and against whom no cause of challenge

appears, to supply the deficiency. Either party may challenge any juror for cause.

Section 631.28 explains challenge for cause as follows: 

"A challenge for cause is an objection to a particular juror, and is either: (1) General, that the juror is disqualified from serving in any case; or (2) 

Particular, that he is disqualified from serving in the case on trial.

Such challenge may be taken either by the State or by the defendant."

Section 631.29 determines the general causes of challenge as follows: "(1) A conviction for a felony; (2) A want of any of the qualifications prescribed by law to render a person a competent juror; and (3) Unsoundness of mind, or such defect in the faculties of the mind, or organs of the body, as renders him incapable of performing the duties of a juror." 

Section 631.30 sets forth the particular causes of challenge: "(1) For such bias as, when the existence of the facts is ascertained, in judgment of law disqualifies the juror, and known as implied bias; and (2) For the existence of a state of mind on the part of the juror, in reference to the case or to either party, which satisfies the triers, in the exercise of a sound dis cretion, that he cannot try the issue impartially, and without prejudice to the substantial rights of the party challenging, and known as actual bias."

The provisions with respect to the sentence may, of course, be changed to conform to the actual sentence pronounced. The wording of the sentence should be recorded in the docket. There are several reasons for this. 

Sometimes a justice of the peace pronounces a sentence of 30 days, or a fine of $60, the fine to be paid in instalments of $5 per month. In other words, the court gives the defendant an opportunity to pay his fine in instalments. Now, suppose the defendant pays $5 a month for three or four months and omits or neglects to pay other instalments, what is the justice going to do? In the first place, he should consult his docket and his record so

as to find out what the actual sentence was and the actual wording of the provisions for the payment of the fine because once having pronounced this sentence a justice is without power to change it. If his docket and record show that he has imposed a fine of $60 which the defendant may pay in 12 equal monthly instalments, the justice may commit defendant to the county jail for failure to comply with the provisions for payment of the fine.

Perhaps the justice would like to place John Noone on probation for a period of not to exceed one year. This should be properly recorded in his docket so that if any question concerning the violation of probation comes to the court's attention the conditions of probation may be readily referred to.

If the accused is acquitted, he shall immediately be discharged, and if the justice before whom the trial is had shall certify in his docket that the complaint was wilful and malicious and without probable cause, he shall enter judgment against the complainant for all costs that shall have accrued in the proceedings had upon the complaint. The complainant may stay such judgment for 30 days by giving a bond to the State with sufficient sureties, conditioned to pay the same at the expiration of such time, but if the complainant shall neglect to pay such judgment or give such bond execution may issue as in other cases. The defendant in such judgment may appeal therefrom as in civil cases tried before a justice, and the cause shall be tried by the appellate court on the records and evidence in the case duly certified and returned by the justice. It is difficult to understand the enactment of the section above referred to. We maintain that a Justice of the

VIII. 

APPEAL 

Section 633.20 outlines the steps to be taken in case of an appeal from a conviction of a criminal offense in a justice court. It reads as follows: 

"Any person convicted of a criminal offense by a justice may appeal to the district court upon complying with the following requisites within ten days thereafter: (1) The person so appealing shall enter into a recognizance with sufficient surety, to be approved by the justice, conditioned to appear before the district court on the first day of the general term thereof next to be held in and for the same county, and abide the judgment of such court therein, and in the meantime to keep the peace and be of good behavior; (2) He shall serve a notice upon the county attorney, or, in case there be no county attorney, or he is absent from the county, on the clerk of the district court, specifying the grounds of his appeal, as follows: That the appeal is taken upon questions of law alone, or upon questions of law and fact."

When a defendant has complied with the provisions of Section 633.20, the justice shall allow the appeal and make an entry of such allowance in his docket. All further proceedings on the judgment before the justice shall be suspended by such allowance. If the defendant has been committed to jail the justice shall thereupon cause his certificate that an appeal has been perfected to be served upon the keeper of such jail, who shall forthwith release the defendant from custody. The justice shall thereupon make a return of all the proceedings had before him and cause the complaint and warrant, recognizance, notice of appeal with proof of service thereof, and all other papers relating to such cause filed with him to be filed in the district court of the same county on or before the first day of the general term thereof next to be held therein, and the complainant and witnesses may also be required to enter into a recognizance with or without sureties in the discretion of the justice to appear at such term of court and abide the order of the court therein. Section 633.21.

It is necessary that a defendant, in case of an appeal to the district court, comply strictly with all the requirements of Sections 633.20 and 633.21. He does not comply with the law if he files a cash bond. The bond herein referred to must be in writing and must be approved by the justice who tried the case. The filing of a proper bond in case of an appeal from a justice court to a district court is a jurisdictional matter. If the bond is defective the district court acquires no jurisdiction. If the bond is proper and approved by the justice the district court acquires jurisdiction to hear the appeal.

DE jo

In the case of State v. Johanson, 169 Minn. 272, 211

N. W. 5, our supreme court held that the statute requires, in connection with an appeal from justice court to the district court, a recognizance with sufficient surety to be approved by the justice. Such a recognizance so approved is jurisdictional.

The appellant shall not be required to advance any fees in claiming or prosecuting his appeal, but if convicted in the district court, or if sentenced for failing to prosecute his appeal may be required as a part of the sentence to pay the whole or any part of the costs of prosecution in both courts. Section 633.23. If the appellant fails to enter and prosecute his appeal he shall be defaulted on his recognizance and the district court may award sentence against him for the offense whereof he was convicted in like manner as if the conviction had been in that court, and if he is not then in custody, process may be issued to bring him into court to receive sentence.

IX.

VILLAGE JUSTICES 

Section 412.

14 determines the powers and duties of a village justice. It reads as follows: 

"Village justices of the peace shall possess all the powers of those elected by the towns and be governed in the exercise thereof by the same laws in all respects, except that their official bonds shall run to village and be approved by the council. They may also hear and determine accusations made against persons for the violation of any ordinance, by-law, or regulation of the village; and, upon conviction, may impose the penalties prescribed. They shall have

such other jurisdiction and authority as is by this chapter conferred or implied and receive for their services the fees allowed by law to justices of the town; provided, that in all cases where a village is situated in more than one county, the justices of the peace and constables of such village shall have and possess all powers and jurisdiction conferred on justices and constables of the towns in each of the counties in which such village is situated and may issue and serve processes in each of such counties and file their bonds in each of the counties."

In the past there has been some confusion concerning the powers and duties of justices elected in towns or townships and those elected to serve in villages. There is a difference. The village justice has the power to hear and determine cases involving violations of village ordinances. While these proceedings in respect to pleadings, arrests and trials take on the character and nature of criminal actions and proceedings they nevertheless are not classified as criminal actions or proceedings, and while the violation of a village ordinance may be an offense it does not necessarily constitute a crime. The violation of a village ordinance may be an offense against the village and the government of the village, while the violation of a state law is an offense against the people of the state and its peace and dignity. 

All prosecutions for violation of ordinances, rules and by-laws of any village shall be brought in the name of the village upon complaint and warrant, as in other criminal cases. If the accused be arrested without a warrant, a written complaint shall thereafter be made to which he shall be required to plead, and a warrant shall issue

thereon. The warrant and all other process in such cases shall be directed to the village marshal or the sheriff or any constable of the county or village, but the marshal shall serve no such process except within the village. 

Section 412.15. 

Criminal cases are brought in the name of the state. 

Violations of village ordinances are brought in the name of the village. Village ordinances and by-laws have the same effect as general laws within the village and need not be given in evidence upon the trial of civil or criminal actions. Judgment shall be given, if for the plaintiff, for the amount of fine, penalty or forfeiture imposed, with the costs, and the judgment shall direct that in default of payment the defendant may be committed to the common jail of the county for such time, not exceeding 90 days, as the court shall see fit. The commitment shall state the amount of judgment, the costs, and the period of confinement. Every person so committed shall be received by the keeper of the jail and kept at the expense of the county until lawfully discharged. The committing court may release the defendant at any time upon payment of such fine and costs. Section 412.16. 

There are some distinctions between pleadings and trials in cases involving violations of statutes and those involving violations of city or village ordinances. Let us again refer to the case of John Noone. He was accused of having committed an offense known as assault and battery, a violation of our state laws. He is entitled to a jury trial. The state must prove its case against him by evidence beyond a reasonable doubt. The verdict of the jury must be unanimous, while in cases involving a violation

of a village or municipal ordinance the accused is not entitled to a jury trial. The offense need not be proven against him by evidence beyond a reasonable doubt and the presiding judge renders the verdict, not the jury. 

There is also a difference in the law applying to ap peals. Section 412.17 provides: 

"Appeals may be taken to the district court in the same manner as from judgments of justices of the peace in civil actions; but, if taken by the defendant, he shall give bond to the village, to be approved by the court, conditioned that, if the judgment be affirmed in whole or in part, he will pay the same, and all costs and damages awarded against him on such appeal. In case of such affirmance, execution may issue against both defendant and his sureties. 

Upon perfection of such appeal, defendant shall be discharged from custody."

Thus we see that the appellant gives bond to the village, not to the state as in criminal cases. This section also provides that appeals may be taken to the district court in the same manner as from judgments of justices of the peace in civil actions. This proceeding then becomes civil in nature and in village cases no notice of appeal should be served upon the county attorney. The county attorney has nothing whatever to do with proceedings before village justices and appeals therefrom, The following opinion given by the Attorney General on 

November 20, 1935, helps to clarify this question: "***In your letter you ask: 

1. 'Is it not the duty of the Village attorneys in their respective villages to conduct all prosecutions for violation of the ordinance, rules or by-laws of any village.'

We answer your question in the affirmative. Mason's Minnesota Statutes of 1927, Section 233, pro vides as follows: 

'Misdemeanors and violations of ordinances or bylaws shall be prosecuted by the city or village attorney, and all other offenses by the county attorney.' 

2. 'Is it not also the duty of the village attorneys in their respective villages to prosecute and try appeals taken to the District Court from convictions of violations of a village ordinance, etc.'

We answer this question in the affirmative. Violations of a village ordinance are offenses against the village and not against the state. Section 233, above quoted, contemplates the prosecution of a violation of an ordinance by the city or village attorney on appeal as well as in the original court. State v. Sexton, 42 Minn. 154.

Section 9093. We see no reason why one appealing from a conviction for the violation of a village ordinance should not pay said fee. For villages organized under Chapter 145, Laws of 1885, see Section 37 of said chapter. For villages organized under Revised 

Laws of 1905 see Mason's Minnesota Statutes of 

1927, Section 1184.

District Court.' 

We answer your question in the negative."

Every justice should remember that all fines, forfeitures and penalties recovered for the violation of any ordinance, rule or by-law of the village, and all moneys paid for licenses and permits, shall be paid into the village treasury, and that every court or officer receiving the same shall within 30 days thereafter make return thereof under oath, and be entitled to duplicate receipts therefor, one of which shall be filed with the village clerk. Section 412.18.

In respect to violations of state laws, Section 633.36 provides: "All fines imposed by a justice, paid before the defendant is committed, shall be received by the justice. After commitment, payment thereof shall be made to the sheriff. In either case the officer receiving such fine shall pay the same over to the county treasurer within 20 days after he receives it. Any justice or other officer receiving any such fine who shall fail to pay the same to the county treasurer within the time aforesaid shall be guilty of a misdemeanor."

*9 'Do those sections of law apply to Justices in Villages and prosecutions under village ordinances, that is, is it mandatory upon Justices of the Peace in cases of prosecutions under Village Ordinances to comply with and do the things enumerated in the above mentioned sections of law?' Every justice of the peace should be careful in observing the mandates of our statutes. Our law requires that a justice of the peace shall make a report within 20 days after any conviction had before him, to the clerk of the district court. Likewise, the law requires that every justice shall within 10 days after the conclusion of any criminal case prosecuted before him make a report thereof to the county attorney.

We find from actual experience that a good many justices of the peace neglect to make any of these reports. 

We assume that the reason for such neglect is occasioned by lack of information as to the existence of these laws. 

XI. 

THE PRELIMINARY EXAMINATION 

Section 628.31 provides that: "No information shall be filed against any person for any offense until such person shall have had a preliminary examination as provided by law, before a justice of the peace or other examining magistrate or officer, unless such person shall waive his right to such preliminary examination."

This section deals with those proceedings which involve felonies and gross misdemeanors. The actual practice in Hennepin County has been and is: after a felony has been committed within the city of Minneapolis and the county attorney is informed and has been shown evidence which to his mind proves the guilt of the accused, a complaint is prepared accusing the guilty party of having committed a crime. This complaint is taken to the

clerk of the municipal court and is sworn to and signed by the complaining witness or other person before said clerk or judge of said court. This means that the complaint originates from the municipal court of the city of Minneapolis and, for all practical purposes, this court is in all instances of this nature acting as a magistrate. 

Pursuant to this complaint, a warrant is issued for the arrest of the accused. After the accused has been arrested, docketed and fingerprinted, he is brought before the judge of said court and is then asked whether or not he wants a preliminary hearing.

Likewise, whenever the county attorney prepares a complaint, as herein before indicated, and brings the same before a justice of the peace, because the facts therein alleged indicate that the offense was committed outside the geographical limits of the city of Minneapolis, such justice of the peace can and will act as a magistrate in like manner as the municipal court of the city of Minneapolis, and in such case a justice of the peace has jurisdiction to conduct a preliminary hearing.

A preliminary examination before an examining magistrate constitutes the commencement of a criminal prosecution. It is a judicial proceeding, but it is not an action or trial. The word "trial,” which means the judicial hearing upon the issues in a cause for the purpose of determining it, cannot properly be applied to such an examination, which is a mere preliminary inquiry to ascertain if the evidence is such that the accused ought to be put upon trial for the offense charged. If he is discharged, new proceedings may be at once commenced against him for the same offense; if he is held, that fact can have no influence on his guilt when he is put on his trial to have

it determined. The proceeding is of a very ancient origin. 

The statutes relating to the preliminary examination of offenders are applicable to all criminal offenses, whether felonies or misdemeanors. Though not necessary, an examination may be had for offenses punishable by a justice of the peace. 

Waiver-An accused person may waive a preliminary examination. 

Sections 2429, 2430, 2431, Vol. 2, Dunnell's Minne sota Digest. 

Sometimes a justice of the peace takes it upon himself to issue a complaint accusing someone of the commission of a felony or gross misdemeanor without the knowledge or help of the county attorney. The justice has the power but for all practical purposes he should not do so. He is not acquainted with criminal proceedings involving felonies and gross misdemeanors. He has no jurisdiction to try and determine facts or issues of law relative thereto. He should consult the county attorney and refer all such matters to him.

PROBLEMS AND THEIR ANSWERS 

The following questions and answers deal with some of the difficult problems confronting any justice of the peace: 

Q. Where may a justice of the peace hold court?

A. Every justice of the peace shall keep his office in the town, village, city or ward for which he is elected, but he may issue process in any place in the county, and, in his discretion, for the convenience of parties, may make any civil or criminal process issued by him returnable, and may hold his own court at any place which he shall appoint in the town, village or ward within his county adjoining the town or ward in which he resides, or in any village located within his town. 

Q. May a justice of the peace hold court in the city of Minneapolis?

A. If the offense is one over which a justice of the peace has jurisdiction, and the defendant makes no objection at the time of the trial to such justice holding his court in the city of Minneapolis, we believe that he waives any objection thereto. 

See Holmes v. Igo, 110 Minn. 133. 

Q. Does a justice lose jurisdiction by failure to decide the matter within three days from the day of hearing?

A. Yes. The law requires that on the return of the warrant with the accused the justice shall proceed to hear, try and determine the action within one day, unless continued for cause. A justice has no authority to adjourn a criminal case from time to time simply for the purpose of delaying the actual imposing of sentence. 

Does the defendant, by failure to object, consent to the taking of the matter under advisement, for a longer period than required by law?

A. No. The court is presumed to know its powers and duties, and the defendant is under no obligation to remind a court thereof.

Q. Are jurors serving in justice court entitled to mileage?

A. No. 

Q. Has a justice of the peace power to suspend a sentence?

A. Yes. A justice has power in his discretion to suspend sentence or place the defendant on probation for a period not exceeding one year, or, where a fine has been imposed, to order such fine to be paid in instalments over a period not exceeding one year.

Q. Is it required of a justice of the peace to report to the clerk of the district court and the county attorney convictions under village ordinances?

A. No. A justice is required to report only convictions for violations of state laws. 

Q. In a case where a prisoner has been fined by the justice upon conviction by the jury without a guilty plea and, în default of the payment of the fine, has been committed to the county jail, can the justice thereafter suspend the sentence and free the prisoner upon his good behavior?

A. 

No. 

Q. In a like case, after a prisoner has been committed to the county jail, can the justice suspend the sentence upon payment of part of the fine as determined by the justice on a pro rata basis by days, or upon payment of the costs, and free the prisoner upon his good behavior? 

A. No. Because after a sentence has been imposed and a commitment has been issued the justice's jurisdiction is terminated and he cannot revise the fine or the jail sentence imposed. In other words, the power of the justice of the peace to suspend the sentence or provide for the payment of a fine in instalments must be exercised at the time of the imposition of the sentence and if not done so at that time cannot be done at a later date.

Q. Suppose a defendant convicted in justice court pays his fine or costs imposed in a prosecution, what effect has such payment upon his right to appeal? 

A. He waives that right. We believe that the matter has been definitely settled in Minnesota and that a payment of the fine following conviction, whether on a plea of guilty or not guilty, constitutes a waiver of the right to appeal from judgment on conviction in misdemeanor cases tried in justice or municipal courts. 

See State ex rel. Weich v. City of Red Wing, 175 Minn. 222. 

Q. Does a justice of the peace have the power to issue a bench warrant?

A. No. There is no provision for the justice of the peace to issue a bench warrant. 

Section 630.03 and 630.04 refer only to district courts.

Also, a justice of the peace has no right to stay the execution of sentence, but in any event he still has the right to enforce the judgment and sentence by issuing an order of commitment. Under such order the sheriff can take defendant into custody and compel him to serve his time unless the fine and costs are paid. 

See Atty. Gen. opinion 21, July 12, 1941, 266-b-27.

Q. In the event a justice of the peace dies, has the justice appointed or elected to succeed him the authority to enforce collection of the fines remaining unpaid upon the docket of his predecessor?

A. Yes. Justices of the peace occupy constitutional offices. The authority and jurisdiction of such justices are vested in the office and the individual occupying it merely fulfills the functions of the office while holding tenure. 

Atty. Gen. opinion 22, July 11, 1942, 266-B-11. 

Q. Suppose a game warden desires a change of venue in cases where the warden feels that the court will not,

for some reason, cooperate in the prosecution of game violation cases, what can he do? 

A. He may withdraw his complaint and bring his case before another justice, but since the justice of the peace has criminal jurisdiction throughout his county, except over offenses committed within the limits of any city or village wherein a municipal court is organized and existing, if a warden feels that for any reason a fair trial will not be held, it has been the approved practice for the warden to withdraw his complaint and then obtain the issue of a new complaint in another court. Care must be taken that the complaint be withdrawn rather than dismissed. This is the only procedure by which the state can secure a change of place of trial before the trial has once commenced. Prior to the commencement of the trial the provisions of Section 531.11 apply, and a transfer of the action may be procured by filing with the justice of the peace an affidavit of prejudice. 

Atty. Gen. opinion 14, August 28, 1939, 208 (g). 

Q. Are jurors' fees proper items of cost to be charged against the county, also, may they be paid on auditor's warrants without being first allowed by the county board?

A. Since the question of the payment of jurors' fees of costs in criminal proceedings before a justice where they have not been paid by the defendant has been before the Attorney General on a number of occasions, and since the statutes are not clear on this subject, we can only say that it is the present opinion that justices may present to the county board bills for fees and costs in criminal cases instituted before them, where such costs have not been paid by the defendant, and if the claim be not allowed they may appeal to the district court from the disallowance thereof. Section 633.27. 

Q. May a defendant who appeals from a justice court in a criminal action file a cash bond for such sum as the justice may specify in lieu of a bond with sureties? 

A. No.

Q. If a justice accepts a personal check in payment of a fine, where is the responsibility in the event the check is not good?

A. The justice is responsible for this loss. 

Atty. Gen. opinion 277, dated Sept. 5, 1934, 266h-9. 

Q. Is a justice of the peace entitled to 15c per folio for transcribing the evidence taken at a preliminary hearing and transmitting the same to the clerk of the district court?

A. Yes. In such case, however, it is his duty, if he does not personally make the transcription, to pay the expenses of the stenographer or reporter taking down such testimony and transcribing it. If the justice merely filed the transcript made by the reporter he should file his claim and then pay his reporter. 

The law does not require the justice of the peace to enter testimony in full in his docket and therefore he is not entitled to the folio rate in case he does so. 

Atty. Gen. opinion 278, Nov. 2, 1933, 266b-8. 

Q. Has a justice of the peace authority to order a commissioner of game and fish to return articles used in violation of game and fish laws and seized by the wardens when making arrests?

A. No. It is suggested that in the case of property of substantial value it would be advisable to institute proceedings in the district court for the confiscation thereof, notwithstanding the fact that the statute authorizes confiscation and sale without making any mention of such proceeding. 

Atty. Gen. opinion 164, dated Sept. 23, 1931.

Q. Sometimes when a constable is required to appear as a witness in a number of criminal proceedings before a given justice on the same day, in such case will he be entitled to either constable's fees or witness' fees in each case, or are his fees limited to $1 for the entire day? 

A. The constable is entitled to a fee of only $1 per

day regardless of the number of cases called before the justice and regardless of whether or not the constable testifies therein. 

Atty. Gen. opinion 108, Dec. 19, 1931.

Q. Suppose that a person enters a plea of guilty to a charge before a justice of the peace and is duly sentenced, may he appeal therefrom?

A. Yes, but only on questions of law. By his plea of guilty he has admitted that he did the act complained of, but the district court may review his case on the following questions of law: 

1. that the court had no jurisdiction of the case; 

2. that the statute upon which the accusation was imposed was invalid; 

3. that the complaint did not state a public offense; 

4. that the plea of guilty was not voluntarily made and properly entered; that it was entered through mistake or misunderstanding on the part of the defendant as to the nature of the charge on account of the court's failure to read the complaint to him, or to explain the charge to him. 

17 C. J. p. 32, Sec. 3295 and cases cited. 

State v. Eckert, 123 Wash. 403, 212 Pac. 551. 

Also Atty. Gen. opinion 177, Dec. 9, 1930. 

Q. Has a justice of the peace authority to issue a subpoena requiring the appearance of witnesses before the complaint has been signed and an action is pending before him?

A. No. 

Atty. Gen. opinion 169, Aug. 5, 1930.

Q. May a defendant convicted before a justice, in addition to the punishment prescribed and as a part of the sentence, be adjudged to pay the disbursements of the prosecution, including the amount paid to jurors? 

A. Yes.

Section 631.48 and Atty. Gen. opinion 111, November 

23, 1928.

Q. Is a justice required to maintain an office in the ward, township or municipality for which he was elected? 

A. Yes. 

Section 530.02. Bessessen v. Weber, 53 Minn. 174; 

Atty. Gen. opinion 112, April 24, 1928.

Q. Has the state the right to demand a jury trial in a misdemeanor case where a jury has been waived by the defendant?

A. No. 

Atty. Gen. opinion 129, December 13, 1928. 

Q. Has a justice of the peace jurisdiction to issue a search warrant to search premises within his county for stolen goods brought in from another county? 

A. Yes.

Section 622.12. Atty. Gen. opinion 134, Dec. 14, 1928. 

Q. I am the village justice of the peace for X Village. 

Do I have jurisdiction to issue process and try cases the same as a township justice?

A. Yes. As a village justice of the peace your powers and jurisdiction are equal to that of a township justice, and your jurisdiction extends over the entire county. It follows, therefore, that you have jurisdiction to try and determine, for instance, an assault case if the same comes within the definition of assault in the third degree, or assault and battery which was committed in a township adjoining your village. 

Atty. Gen. opinion 514, Jan. 26, 1909. 

Q. Has a defendant a right to trial by jury when tried for an offense against the ordinance of a city or village? 

A. No. 

Q. Suppose I, as a justice of the peace, in a criminal action impose a fine of $100 which the accused does not pay, the sentence contains no direction for the enforcement of payment of the fine by imprisonment, have I any authority to enforce the payment of the fine by that method?

A. Yes. In all cases where a fine is imposed pursuant to statute as punishment for any offense the offender may be committed until the same is paid if he is otherwise discharged according to law. 

State v. McDonnah, 117 Minn. 173; Atty. Gen. opinion, July 10, 1918.

Q. Do I, as a justice of the peace, have jurisdiction to hear and determine criminal cases involving minors under 18 years of age?

A. 

No. 

Section 260.22 provides: 

"TRANSFER OF CASES. Subdivision 1. Trans fer from municipal or justice court. When any minor is arraigned upon a criminal charge before a judge of the municipal court or justice of the peace otherwise than upon an order transferring the case from a juvenile court the judge or justice shall inquire concerning the age of such minor and, if it satisfactorily appears that he is under the age of 18 years, the case shall forthwith be transferred to the juvenile court of the county. Such transfer shall be effected by filing with the judge or clerk of the juvenile court a certificate showing the name, age, and residence of the child, the names and addresses of his parents or guardian, if known, the specific charge upon which he has been arraigned, and the name and residence of the complainant.

The certificate shall have the effect of a petition filed in the juvenile court; but the judge of the court may in his discretion direct the filing of a new petition, which shall supersede such certificate. The judge of the municipal court or the justice shall have power to commit such child to appropriate custody, when deemed advisable, for a period of not more than one week and to fix reasonable bail, upon furnishing which the child shall be returned to the

custody of his parents or guardian to respond to such proceedings as shall be had in the juvenile court.

Subdivision 2. Transfer to another county. The judge in charge of the juvenile court in any county may transfer any case to another county when it appears that the child resides in such other county and the convenience of witnesses or the interest of such child will be best served by such transfer. Such transfer shall be made by forwarding to the clerk of the juvenile court of the county to which the case is transferred a certified copy of all papers filed, together with an order of transfer." 

Q. To what cases does Section 260.22 apply?

A. The section applies only to minor crimes triable by a magistrate and it is only in such cases it is contemplated that the jurisdiction of a juvenile court shall succeed that of a magistrate and where juvenile court has assumed jurisdiction in a case of an assault with intent to commit a felony and fails or refuses to direct institution of criminal proceedings the County Attorney may cause accused to be rearrested and proceeded against criminally. 

Atty. Gen. opinion 268f, April 10, 1942.

Q. Has a justice of the peace authority to remit a fine once it has been imposed?

A. 

No. 

Q. May a person be held in contempt by a justice of the peace? 

A. Yes.

Sections 530.10 and 530.11 provide as follows: 

"530.10. Contempts; Proceedings; Punishment. No person shall be punished for contempt before a justice of the peace until given an opportunity to be heard in his defense. If present, the offender may be

summarily arraigned without a warrant. If not, the justice may issue his warrant to bring such offender before him. Punishment for a contempt may be by fine not exceeding $20.00, or imprisonment in the county jail not exceeding two days." "530.11. Contempt; Record of Conviction, Where

Filed; Commitment. Upon a conviction for contempt, the justice shall make up a record of the proceeding, stating the particular circumstances of the offense, and the judgment rendered thereon, and shall enter the same in his docket as in civil cases, and file the same with the clerk of the district court. 

Every warrant of commitment for contempt shall set forth the particular circumstances of the offense, or it shall be void."
