CHAPTER ONE 

AS FAR as they could see, lying on their bellies, their heads in cupped hands, the clouds lay heavily to the shore and sea. Up to their right, the clouds were darker, almost blue, sinking into the olive green hills around Topango canyon. 

To their left, the clouds receded a bit, and the curving shore line of Santa Monica stretched out into the mingling greyness of sea and sand.

Paul sniffed the air, pungent with sea-weed and damp with the tangy salt surf, and turning to Lissi, who lay beside him, said: "Smells good." "Lovely," she said, gazing at him as though he were the one who made the sea, the clouds, the faintly curving shore line. She smiled, her grey, oblique eyes crinkling at the corners, then, reaching out, she laid a well manicured hand on his tan shoulder. "Love me?" she asked, sure of his answer. "You're sweet," he said, turning to look back out to the water.

It was a poor day to come to the beach, he thought. And yet, as long as Lissi wanted to come, it seemed that there was nothing else to do. Sentimental, that's what she was. 

Queer that he had never noticed that in her before. He dis-

covered suddenly that sentiment in others could be very annoying when it came at a time like this. He remembered the day, two years or so ago, when he had first seen her on the beach. Lissi remembered the day, too; that was why they were here now. It was Lissi's idea to start all over from where they had first met. 

But Paul couldn't tell her now; he'd have to wait. Time and time again, he felt the words forming in his throat, but when he opened his mouth he said entirely unintelligible things, or something that mattered not the slightest. 

When he was fourteen years old, Miss Pindeck, his spinster piano teacher, admitted that she could teach him no more. "You'll go far, Paul," she said dramatically. 

Miss Pindeck was an old fool, but Paul remembered her with a slightly nostalgic feeling. After all, she was the first person to see something in him. He smiled to himself, half in memory of the last lesson with Miss Pindeck, and half in anticipation of the brilliant future that lay awaiting his challenge.

Lissi, seeing the faint smile on his dark face, patted Paul on the back, naked above his swimming trunks, and said, "I'm happy, too, Paul. I always knew it would be like this-calm and sweet and all kind of funny away down deep inside." "It's too bad people can't feel exactly the same thing at the same time." "Maybe other people can't," Lissi murmured, "but we can. I'd like to freeze this moment and keep it forever.” 

Paul looked away. After a while, he muttered, "Oh, nuts."

"Shush saying those things." "What things?" "Things like 'Oh, nuts,"" she said. "Is something troubling you?" "Yes." "What, Lamb?" she asked, her near-sighted grey eyes becoming softer and darker than usual. 

Now was his chance. He could say it all right now. It would be simple and easy. Lissi would know and everything would be fine and uncomplicated again. But Paul didn't say it. Instead, somewhat irritably, he answered: "Oh, nothing. I'll tell you later." 

Lissi smiled. "I'll go in swimming now and when I come out, we'll go. All right?" 

Paul nodded absentmindedly and watched the pale, pink back of Lissi diminish toward the surf. He watched her push straight blonde hair beneath the shining black of her bathing cap and he watched her hips swing as she walked down to the water, where she placed first one foot and then the other testingly into the foam. She waved with a gesture, childlike and sad, it seemed to Paul, before she began her old-fashioned side-stroke out into the deeper water. He waved back, looking at the swimming body intently.

He let a little pile of sand dribble through his fingers. 

Just wait until Pioneer was finished! He'd work on it tonight. He could work right now if he had a piano with him; he could do that sad, minor movement that came in the middle. He picked up another handful of sand

and looked out to see Lissi. A little, shining black cap was all alone in the green gelatine sea. 

Paul watched the black cap grow larger, squinting his dark, bright, narrow-slitted eyes against the afternoon sun that had begun to come through the clouds. Lissi was in water shallow enough for standing now and she judged the time to walk in to the beach so that she wouldn't be hit by a breaker.

Coming up the beach from the water, her sea-wet body glistening with the sun that lay almost directly in back of her, it was as though her angularly graceful figure had a coating of minute and iridescent pearls.

When she was quite close, Paul saw that she shivered. 

He stood up, placing his flannel robe about her shoulders, surprisingly broad for her slim body, and dried her hands so that she could smoke a cigarette.

They sat down on the blanket together, and though there was another robe lying next to them, Lissi threw the one she was wearing over Paul's shoulders, so that they both shared it. They smiled like children playing that they were in a tepee. "Warmer now?" Paul asked. "Lots," she said. "The sun's out now. That'll help." 

Lissi wrinkled her nose horizonward. "There'll be a lovely sunset if those crazy clouds will stay out."

Looking out, her thoughts wandered, then found the deeply carved groove in her mind that led always to Paul. "What's your place like now? I'd like to do it all over

Lissi shivered slightly again and pulled the robe tighter about her. Paul said that perhaps they ought to go, but 

Lissi wanted to wait for the sunset. "Still cold?" Paul asked.

Lissi shook her head negatively. "Then I can do your place all over again?" she asked as though they had been talking of it all along. 1 

In a sudden jerk, Paul brought his head downward, turning his face from the woman-redolence of her shoulder. It was as though she were a large slab of black slate thrown suddenly before him, shutting out the light. 

He felt that he had to batter and claw his way through now, immediately. Christ! I have to tell her. I have to tell her now.

Lissi laid her slim, cool hand on his dark, broad, spatulate fingered hand. “Well, may I, Paul?” "But why? It's all right as it is.' 99 "Well, it would give me something to do, for one thing."

There! He had said it. It slipped out just as simply as if he had said that it was a fine day. All that worrying for nothing. It had been easy to say. He had said it and Lissi was looking up to him, her grey eyes questioning. For a moment it seemed that they both were going to say something important, vital. Lissi breathed heavily, looked away, and picked up a handful of sand. Both of them looked at it trickling through her fingers as though it were a complicated and tremendously interesting process.

Lissi stood up suddenly. She made no move to help him as she used to do when he would hold his weight against the ground so that she had to tug with all her might and he would laugh at her weakness. She stood alone and looked down at Paul who was still lying. "Let's go. I want to go home."

His jaw was lean and hard, slightly aggressive, and his closely cropped hair curled tightly. Only his eyes, deepset and softly dark, were boy's eyes. 

As he faced straight ahead, Lissi could see his mouth, in profile. Though it was large and full, it looked as though it would be even larger and more full if he did not hold it under control. It was as if his mouth would spread all over his face if he ever really let it go.

The light changed to green and they started up again.

that happen to a person. Suddenly, he felt a sharp hand on his shoulder. He turned quickly. "Take me home. Quick."

For a moment he was speechless, then managed to say, "I ain't done nothing, have I, Miss Pruett? I didn't mean to be fresh." "No. No. You're all right. Just take me home, quickly.”

Uncomprehending, he turned the cab about in the middle of the road and drove back to the hotel. He watched her walk into the building. No one would ever believe that she had sung for him, for him alone. 

Joanna thought about Paul as she undressed, as she showered, as she put on her silk nightgown. For a long time she sat at the window looking at the lights of Kings 

Highway and Lindell Boulevard, feeling the warm night breeze from Forest Park. She knew now that she had wanted him all along, had burned for his fierce heat, his quiet peace. She would send him a wire in the morning.

He could come as her accompanist. They could go-oh, any place! Any place he wanted, Europe, anywhere. 

Now that she was going to see him again, she couldn't stand the hours that separated them. She lay wide-eyed in bed, her lovely dark body above the sheets, unable to sleep. She wished now that she had been kinder to him, had shown more how she cared for him. She remembered being afraid of love because it stood in the way of money. 

She would send him the wire in the morning and he could take the train to Chicago and meet her there. She thought about that. No. She'd telephone him now and he could fly back.

Laughing at herself, she reached across the bed to the telephone on the desk. She began to hum, not with the tone that she used in the morning at rehearsals, but in the same happy minor key that she sang in when she was a little girl lying deep in the grass of her own special meadow.

CHAPTER TWENTY-FOUR NELLIE WETHERBY, twenty-five and pretty in a worn out fashion, listened in on the long distance telephone call from St. Louis to Los Angeles. Nellie had very little life of her own, not because she wasn't attractive, but because most of the people who knew her thought her to be conceited. She tried continually to better herself and took culture like medicine. She plodded her way through all the books that her favorite critic recommended, and once she spent half of all the money she had to buy a season ticket for a course of lectures on "Literature from Chaucer to Dickens".

The other telephone operators tried to be friendly to Nellie, but she never seemed to know about the latest moving picture star or dance and none of the fellows at the party knew how to talk with her.

The Story: 

Set in California, the action ranges from the sun-baked beaches of La Jolla to the snow-capped range of the Sierra Madre. The story presents four young people who work out their destinies in accordance with the code of 1933. 

The Characters: 

PAUL EMERSON. Not a dance orchestra on the Coast has a piano player who can touch him, and he is as popular as he is talented. When the story opens, however, he is having an attack of ambition. He wants to compose a great tone poem. Immensely attractive to women, Paul feels that he will have to renounce love for art and lead a monastic life. Certainly the pleasant affair with the fascinating Lissi will have to come to an end. 

LISSI ARNO is pretty and blonde and by no means a fool. The affair with Paul -whatever it may be for him-is very important to her. She's not afraid of his music, but when Joanna turns up, Lissi realizes that things are beginning to look grim.

JOANNA PRUETT comes to sing at the night club where Paul plays. Paul has never seen anyone like Jo. Beneath her lovely softness is a shrewd, fighting will. She has a fine voice and nothing is going to interfere with her career. 

Love is all very well, but Jo has a young brother to support, and her eye on a future which doesn't include a dance orchestra pianist.

PAT CLEARY, the assistant art director in a big movie studio. He comes into the picture just about the time that Lissi needs someone badly. Pat has real talent, but he's been tied to his mother's apron strings for so long that he doesn't know his way around.

MRS. CLEARY, Pat's mother, has scraped and saved to send him to art school. 

Now, she is brightly determined to be his best friend and keep up with all his interests even Lissi-although that presents difficulties. You can laugh at Mrs. Cleary, but the fact remains that she is the only one thoroughly satisfied with the way things turn out in the end.
