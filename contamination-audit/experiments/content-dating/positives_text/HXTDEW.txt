The past calls for our attention and consideration. It is an instructor and guide to the present. It's disasters and triumphs, it's victories and defeats, are directories to human conduct. It's solemn voice is that of a hoary father, uttering costly experience and paternal counsels to beloved children. It is the great storehouse and repository of knowledge. It contains theories, doctrines, principles and conduct, all again and again tested by experience, and all labelled as true or false, safe or unsafe, practicable or impracticable. It invites the living to open it's archives and learn from its accumulated files. It invites us today to review God's Providence in the Past, and learn wisdom there to guide us in the future. 

When in old age, shall we be called to duty so emphatically as we are at the present time? We are convened to dedicate this new edifice to the worship and service of Almighty God. We enter upon a new and important era in the history of this church and society. Let the occasion then be improved by a consideration of the solemn counsels of God as disclosed in the past. The selections of the religious history of Westchester, that I am now to present, have been gathered from various Mss. and records, from traditions, inscriptions and from conversation with our aged people. May their contemplation excite our faith, hepe and love. Settlement of Colchester, when? by whom? The Town of Colchester was originally very extensive. Tradition says that the first white that settled within its limits was 

Andrew Carrier, not far from 1660. His name still has existence.

This part of the town was settled about 1700. In the spring of 1728 the inhabitants of Westchester forwarded to the legislature at Hartford a petition that they might be made a distinct Society. A committee was appointed by the Legislature, to investigate the case and report at the fall session. That committee came upon the ground and bounded this Ecclesiastical Society much as it now remains. They reported favorably and the request of the petitioners was granted. one of the principle arguments urged by the petitioners for a Society was the distance from the center, and that they sustained worship here on the Sabbath, and "that Mr. Lewis was preaching with them and would probably be settled". Nov. 12th, 1728, was the first time the new Ecclesiastical Society assembled. After organizing, it was voted that Thomas Day, Sargt. Carrier, and Sargt. Crippen be a Committee to treat with Mr. Lewis for settlement, and take his propositions and report at the next meeting. Mr. Judah Lewis was ordained first pastor of this church, Dec. 17th, 1729. He had preached here the year previous. His salary was 85 pounds, (about $425) annually, with 200 pounds settlement. On the same day, previous to the ordination, this church was formed. A solemn confession of faith and covenant was then adopted by the following 16 persons: Judah Lewis, pastor, George Sexton, Nathaniel Skinner, Benjamin Lewis, Jonathan Lord, John 

Rowley, David Chamberlain, Sarah Sexton, Mary Skinner, Hannah Lewis, Ruth Adams, Deborah Rowley, Deborah Hungerford, Mary Carrier and Mary

Skinner. In twe months after the ordination of Mr. Lewis, few and feeble as was the society, they selected a spot on which to build a house for the Lord. Mr. Timothy Carrier freely gave the half acre selected to the society. The first house of worship was raised in 

May following, 28th day, 1730. That house of worship was 40 feet in length, 32 feet in width and 20 ft. high. It stood west a few rods of the great hill between this house and the burying ground. It was annually seated according to age and meeting house rates. 

It was

not painted nor very fully furnished, yet there for thirty years the gospel was preached, there the people assembled during the cold of winter and the heat of summer, devoutly and solemnly to worship God. 

Those north of the river and from each extremity of the society came to the holy place on the Holy Day. Did the dark unbroken forest resound with the howling of savages and wild beasts, was the wilderness trackless, except of the woodsman's ax to mark the trees holy zeal for God's house surmounted those dangers, families came in company armed to the place of worship. At the appointed hour on 

Sabbath morn, they might be seen emerging from the forests, some on foot and some on well trained millioned steed. They come bringing their children to be consecrated to God, and their own souls saved. 

They greet each other as friends and pioneers in the wilderness, and together enter the house of the Lord. Rev. Mr. Lewis is represented as an able and faithful minister of the gospel. He was the son of 

Benj. Lewis, a native of this society. His ministry was short. He died of comsumption, leaving a young family, and a kind people to deplore his death. His remains lie in the burying ground, surrounded by his flock. A large and durable monument that has survived the corrosion of more than a hundred years marks the spot. On that monument is the following inscription: "Here lies the body of Rev. 

Mr. Judah Lewis, a faithful and successful minister of the gospel, 1st pastor of the 2d church of Christ in Colchester, who was born Jan. 6th, 1703, and having served his generation according to the will of God, fell asleep April 15th, 1739, in the 36th year of his age, and in the 10th of his ministry." Mr. Lewis graduated at Yale 

College, 1726, at the age of 23. He was 26 at the time of his settlement. The results of his brief ministry cannot be known until the disclosures are made on the Great Day. But that there were only 16 members of this church at it's organization, and at the time of Mr. Lewis' settlement, while at the time of his successor's there were 198 members so that it appears notwithstanding the deaths that might have occurred during the time there were 182 members more at his death than at his settlement. His descendants of the third, fourth and fifth generation are numerous among us, tho none bearing the name. The death of Mr. Lewis was a very great trial to this church and Society. But God did not long bereave them of a faithful minister. The Rev. Thomas Skinner was ordained pastor of this flock Apr. 16th, 1740, just one year and one day from the death of

Mr. Lewis. The ministers present who took part in the ordination were Rev. Ashbel Woodbridge of Glastonbury, Rev. Ephraim Little of 

Colchester, Rev. Stephen Hosmer of E. Haddam, Rev. Wm. Russell of 

Middletown and Rev, Edw. Eells of Middletown. Mr. Skinner was 32 years of age at the time of his settlement. The church at this time, as I have said, numbered 198 members, a greater number than we find at any subsequent time. They were united in their pastor who was in every respect worthy of their confidence and affection. He was a native of Billerica, Mass., born 1708, graduated at Harvard 

University 1738, and died Oct. 10th, 1762, aged 54 years. Sixty persons were added. Mr. Skinner baptized 353 souls and administered the Sacrament of the Lord's Supper 124 times. Altho he was an efficient minister, yet the church and society became much enfeebled during his ministry. The causes most active that tended to this diminution of strength and numbers, were, first, emigration: several colonies went out from this church and Society to found settlements

and churches in the wilderness. What number of members of the Eccl. 

Society removed I have not the means of definite knowledge. Crippen, Micah Mudge, Cornelius Hamlin, Alex Spencer and Jokiah Skinne ner. Besides these fifteen there were shortly after dismissed others, so that the whole number of church members to Sharon exceeds forty.

Others subsequently followed and enfeebled this church and society. 

The second cause of decline during the ministry of Mr. Skinner is found in the evils and excesses that followed in the train of the great revival which occurred in N.E. about 1743 and 4. Whitfield, Edwards and their tenets were under God made the instruments of turning thousands of souls to God. There was a great awakening thruoat the land under their preaching. But the great work of the Lord was soon marred by the ardent ones that believed the Millenium had come and in the excess of their zeal flew from place to place, parish to parish, self constituted heralds to proclaim the day of the Lord. 

Their fanaticism and fiery zeal could brook no restraint. Those that opposed their measures were all unconverted worldlings opposing the work of the Lord, and must be run over and broken down by public sentiment. Headed by the (famous) notorious Davenport, New London 

Co. was inundated by these ardent ones. In most societies where the ministers would not yield to their dictation a new church was formed by the zealots called Separates, or New Lights. Tho this influence was great and this wild fire raged all around Westchester, it never gained any favor within the parish. Mr. Skinner was a man that acted from reason and judgment and not from the impulses of passion. 

He was surrounded by men in his church and society of a like temperament, consequently this church as a body and it's minixter set their faces as a flint not against the revival, but against the new measures of the blind and noisy reformers. Had the church and sooiety been unanimous in this resistance, they would have experienced no detriment, but some were more credulous and ardent than the majority, and forsook their own pastor and wandered off to disorderly meetings held upon their borders. The church strongly disapproved of this course. These ardent ones were subjects of discipline, and some of them made humble confessions of their disorder and covenant breaking and were restored to charity but most of them under their censure or exclusion of the church permanently removed from the society to locations where they could find more congenial spirits. 

Such are some of the causes that diminished the Church during the ministry of Mr. Skinner. The fiery, passionate and fickle ones went to other settlements so that whatever may have been the character of

10:

Westchester men previously they are the descendants of a firm, cool and intellectual race of men, men not to be swept away by novelties, not to be driven from their ground by noisy and violent harangue, men whose religion must be sense as well as sound, sound reason rather than passion. Mr. Skinner continued his efficient and useful labors until Dec. 1761 when his health failed. The Soc. procured the Rev. Joseph Huntington to aid him till his recovery, but all hopes of recovery failed. He lingered until Oct. 10, 1762, when he ceased from his labors by death. Mr. Skinner resided on what is still called the Skinner place. There stand the trees planted and nurtured by the hands that have for four score years mouldered in the dust. Mr. Skinner left a family of seven children: Thomas aged 22, then Mary, Alice, Joseph T., Benj., Mehitabel and Rachel the youngest aged four. Thomas was a respectable physician in Colchester where his remains now sleep. He had a son that learned the trade of a printer and is supposed to be now residing in Auburn, N.Y. being not far from 60 years of age. Mr. Skinner sleeps in the burying ground here with his congregation. With them he will rise and with them be judged at the great and last day. This inscription is still legible on his tomb stone. It's young men were called away to fight along with Washington and his generals for freedom. The people at home bore heavy and grievous burdens, Provisions were sent from Westchester to the valley forge to supply Washington and his suffering men. This 

Eccles. Society voted in 1778 to hire two men to enlist into the

Thus not only bearing their part in public taxation but as a religious Society assuming the responsibility to send and supply two soldiers. These burdens were heavy upon the Society and heavy upon their minister. His support became small so that like many of the ministers of the day he was compelled to resort for his support in part to agricultural employment. But during the greatest darkness when the Society was enfeebled and oppressed and all things were most discouraging, God caused light to arise in the midst of darkness. It was during the year 1775 that all things seemed to be against the Society. The war was raging, the able bodied youth were called away to Boston, all faces gathered paleness. It was then 

God let loose the pestilence and the destroying angel went from house to house until near forty of the people were carried to the grave. 

One of the first victims struck down by the distemper was Dea. John 

Gates, a pillar in the temple of God. He had long borne the vessels of the Lord beside his venerable and pious colleague, Dea. John Day.

His light was extinguished, his prayers were silent. Soon that good man Mr. David Day was called to follow. His death was a heavy blow to the Society and church. They had now lost one of their most able and willing supporters. But soon it is found that this man tho dead had liberally remembered the Society in his last will and testament. 

Here commences the era of light and the future independence of this society. Is it not appropriate for us here to pay a passing tribute of gratitude to the memory of the benefactor of Westchester? Is not such a tribute becoming those whose burdens have been removed by his munificence? Is not such a tribute becoming him who with his children eats the bread of his toil? Mr. David Day the donor of a large estate was a native of this parish. He was born 1709. He was 

19 years of age at the time of the organization of the Society. He made a profession of religion during the ministry of Rev. Mr. Lewis and remained a member probably for 40 yrs. until his death. God in his own wise providence prospered him in an unusual manner in the work of his hands. He appears to have been a man of great piety and tho his body has slept in the dust for more than three score years and ten yet his godly walk and conversation are remembered with respect by the aged. His piety is seen as he sits down before God for the solemn purpose of disposing of his property. He had been greatly prospered and shall he not remember the author of all good? These are the words introductory to his will: "In the name of God amen, this day of Feb. 1774 I David Day of Colchester in the Co, of Hartford and Colony of Conn., being at present thru the goodness of God in health of body and sound and disposing mind, and memory, praised be God therefor. But calling to mind the uncertainty of life, and desirous to make a settlement of my affairs and first and principally whensoever my great change shall come, would resign my soul unto God that gave it, hoping that I shall receive the remission of my

Society assembled and voted thankfully to accept the donation made in the last will and testament of Lt. David Day, late of Colchester to the parish. God thus relieved the necessities of the Society. The school was soon established supported by the donation. For 72 years this Eccl. Society has drawn its principle support from the funds given by David Day. The Society has endeavored to be faithful to their trust; they have taken good care of these sacred funds. They have never wanted for wise financiers to manage on their behalf. 

Man that have long since gone to the grave who were skilfull to control their own property, have served the Society successfully and profitably as treasurers and committees of this donation fund. And there are men among the living, present today, whose epitaph for their fidenity has not yet been written. How many such houses as this has that worthy benefactor built for this society how many souls have been elevated and fitted for higher spheres of usefulness by his teachers! - how many sinners have been led to the Saviour and heaven by the Gospel supplied by his bounty: - how many onward in ⠀⠀⠀ the distant future shall be sanctified to meet him in heaven, - will not be known in time; these results are to be revealed in eternity. 

One thing is probably true, no population of equal number in the State can be found more amply provided with means to support education and religion than this. Its benefactor has long reposed in the dust, with the men of his day. A liberal stone marks his resting place with this inscription: "Beneath this stone in hope of a happy resurection are deposited the remains of Lt. David Day, who on the 

15th Day of Sept. A. D., 1775 and in the 66th year of his age departed this vain transitory life. By the smiles of heaven while alive, he acquired a large estate in lands, and at his death with a noble spirit of benevolence, bequeathed the entire revenue of them to the support of science, virtue and religion." But this Society and this Church need not that stone as a memorial. Their benefactor is seen in their permanent institutions, educational and religious. 

Being dead he yet speaketh. When the church come to the table of the Lord to commemorate their Saviours death, and the consecrated vessels of the Lord's house are exhibited, we are reminded of those pious departed ones, David and Hannah Day, whose initials they bear. 

Those costly vessels of pure silver, with fine workmanship, the fire could not burn. All things else connected with the sanctuary where the fathers worshipped was consumed. These vessels far more precious and costly than all, escaped the conflagration and remain among the memorials of the pious donors, The utility of Eccles. funds is sometimes questioned and very few donations of this kind

-- are made. It is said that they depress benevolence and enterprise and render the burdens of the people too light for their own good. 

Large societies situated in the centres of business generally find no difficulty in the support of the gospel. The fund of a costly house of worship transmitted can be made a sufficient revenue. It only requires shrewdness and policy on the part of a few to appeal to the pride, envy, rivalship and interest of the multitude, the funds for them are forthwith promised upon the seats, tho even here the ministry are liable to the extremes of plethory and emaciation, and the society to the inroads of error. But no reasonable man can doubt the utility of eccles. funds in a scattered farming population. 

The youth of enterprise are leaving for the cities and mfg. villages, our soil is hard, and various interests, secterian and political, are striving to break down order. An eccles. fund is a bond of union. It is an insuperable barrier to the inroads of sectarianism, and a strong inducement for all to connect themselves with the society. It gives a permanence to society which it would not otherwise possess. Those that preach against the evil of funds are not tax payers but tax receivers and such preachers as far as I can learn, have never made any practical disciples. I have never heard of any society refusing to accept such bequests, nor of any society that have been so far convinced of their evil nature, as to throw away their funds. Preaching should be enforced by practise to be useful. Let it be understood that we are not advocating funds like those which support the Bishops of England, but such as ease the people from discouraging burdens. David Day raised up b" Providence to be a benefactor in time of need, has placed this people and their children in circumstances of great favor, as it regards provision for education, and religion. Let his name be held in grateful remembrance, not only by the people of this society, but by all who honor the good and the great. Let his name go down to posterity on the same catalogue with that of Bacon, Yale, Dartmouth, Bowdoin, Phillips, Brown and Harvard. Church in So. Orange, N.J. Such are the shepherds that God in times past has sent to feed this flock in this place. They have not been left to labor without evidence of the presence and favor of the great head of the Church. And as the pillars of the Church and

Society have fallen by death, others have been raised up to take their places. If there have been periods of darkness and discouragement, God has been good and led his people safely thru their discouragements to the land of safety. Since the installation of the present incumbent of the Pastoral office, which occurred May 1st, 1844 the God of our fathers has blessed his people with peace, and as death has invaded their ranks, his grace hath brought forward others to stand in their places, and sustain their responsibilities. Two important events, however, have occurred during this period, events which will render it memorable long after we and our children are all in the grave. The first of these events is the destruction of the house of God which lately occupied this site. On the night of the 

Sabbath of the 28th of March last, near midnight, it was discovered by some of the neighbors that the Meeting house was on fire. The alarm ran thru the neighborhood. Those who first arrived state that the fire was then upon the west end of the house, that being urged by a strong western breeze, it was soon driven upon the body of the house. And so rapid and furious were the flames, and all was so enveloped, that the house could not even be entered. The people could do nothing but stand around and witness the sad spectacle of the burning of the beautiful house, where their fathers worshipped, with all its contents. The morning light revealed a melancholy scene. 

There were its dust and its stones, but the Sacred Edifice was sumed. As to the mode by which the fire was communicated to that house, darkness and uncertainty still shroud it. Some suppose it accidental others the work of an incendiary. No investigation by competent authority has been made. The mystery will not be probably disclosed until the revelation of the great day. That house was well built, well proportioned, with a steeple and bell. It was sufficiently large. It had been well kept, well painted, and in good repair. 

Its interior might have been modernized and it would have remained a commodious and inviting structure for half a century to come. The other important event to which I referred and which will mark our day and era in the history of this Church and Society, is the erection of this house for Gods worship, Did Goa suffer the former sanctuary to be consumed to test the faith of the people, to prove and try them, that it might be known whether the attachment to the institutions of the gospel was sufficiently strong to induce the self denial necessary to meet the emergency. The problem is solved. I am happy to be able to affirm that I have never heard expressed but one sentiment, thats has always been: "let us go forward, let us build the house for the 

Lord". This has been the spontaneous sentiment of all this people. 

True, there has been difference of opinion in reference to this house. 

Issues have been made from step to step of this nature: Shall the funds be raised by this or that mode? Shall the new house stand on the old site or on another? Shall there be Basement room or not? 

Shall the bell weigh more or less? etc. And there has been the unyielding attachment to personal opinion so fatal to peace and harmony. Carrier forbids him. The neighbor continues to ply his ax. Seeing which way the tree would fall, Father Carrier places himself in its range and straightens up and says: "I shall stand here, and if you fell the tree, you will kill me, and you will be hung. ?? The tree

was felled, but Father Carrier being a stout man was not seriously injured by the limbs.) All were desirous for a house in which to worship God on the Sabbath. This house was raised on the 8th and 9th of 

Sept., and by the energy and perseverance of the contractor, (Gen. 

Baldwin) it has been thus speedily completed. It has been carried through, I am told, on the principles of temperance, and no material accident from first to last has occurred. The young gentlemen that came among us, strangers, and have labored as carpenters, have, by their industry and exemplary deportment, made themselves many friends in this community. No portion of this Society have manifested greater interest, or been more ready to make sacrifices in the work, than the females. Its furniture is the avails of their industry. May this to them be indeed the house of God, and the gate to heaven. 

This food and comely book was the Bible given for the use of the pulpit by an aged widow of almost fourscore; may its truth support her in the decline of life, and her reward be found in heaven. The Society have honorably met the emergency. They have reared a house of the Lord. The errand for which we have assembled here today is to dedicate this new house to the service of almighty God. here from our closets, friends, realizing the solemnity of the errand on which we have come? We would that it be dedicated to no imaginary diety, but to the Triune God, the Father, Son and Holy Ghost. Do we not find this mystery in the Gospel? Was it not recognized in the commission of the Apostles? "Go ye, therefore, and teach all nations, babtizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost." Is it not found on the first and last pages of the 

Bible? Does it not give to the prophecies their principle significance and explain what otherwise would be inexplicable in the ceremonial worship of the ancient church? Is it not seen in the fate of 

Annanias and Sapphira? Is it not proclaimed in the last prayer of dying Stephen? Is it not the truth which God has owned and blessed with precious revivals of religion in all the pilgrim churches? This house if suffered to remain until it shall decay with age will probably stand when the present generation and the succeeding one shall have passed to the grave. We now dedicate this house to God: this pulpit, these seats, this gallery for the singers, these walls, this whole house with its furniture. We dedicate all solemnly and sincerely to God, the Father, God the Son, and God the Holy Ghost. Hence forth let it be regarded as the Holy Place and used but for the purposes of religion. Abler ministers and devouter Christians will 

Occupy our places. God grant that their faith, like that of Lewis, Skinner, and Robbins, and the men of their day, may abide in God, the Father, Son and Holy Ghost. This Church and Society today in reference to their conveniences of worship enter a new dispensation. Let us enter it in the fear of God. Have any alienations, animosities, and unfriendliness been created during the erection of this house, I call upon you before God now to suppress them. Banish them from the mind, and enter this house with a clean bosom and a pure heart, Think of the forbearance of Christ toward ourselves, sinners, and like him exercise the same spirit of forgiveness and love. We shall not all come up here many times. The Lord will soon call us away to our final account. Our fathers, where are they? As we review the past, others will soon review us, to learn wisdom from our failures and success in the Christian life. Let the zeal we have manifested in

erecting this temple go with us in the future, to worship God in spirit and in truth, as we enter its sacred courts. Let us unitedly request that God, to whom we have now dedicated this house, will condescend to take it for his resting place, that Jesus Christ, who came to seek and to save that which was lost, will here appear in his grace and glory, and that the Holy Spirit, whose office it is to sanctify the soul, will descend upon us, as he descended on the last day of pentecost, with mighty converting and convincing power.
