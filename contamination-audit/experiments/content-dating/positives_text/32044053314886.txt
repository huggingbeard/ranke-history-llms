Western Peace Plan 

A Western peace plan containing proposals for a 4-phase program to settle the problems of German reunification and European security was presented to the Soviet Union at the Foreign Ministers conference on May 14. The plan also contained some proposals which were in the past dealt with in the framework of general disarmament. 

Secretary Herter, in outlining the objectives of the plan, noted that the reunification of Germany would not begin immediately with free elections but would be preceded by a preparatory period. 

During this period, he explained, a Mixed German Committee would be established which would have as its primary responsibility the drafting of an electoral law providing for election by all the German people of a national assembly. The Committee would also be authorized to make proposals for facilitating contacts and freedom of movement of persons, ideas, and publications between the two parts of Germany and for insuring human rights in both parts of Germany.

The preparatory period would also be utilized to establish the first steps of a system of European security. The Secretary said that positive measures taken in that direction could include (1) arrangements to secure in the remainder of Germany and in other European countries to the East the prohibition of the manufacture of chemical, biological, and nuclear weapons of mass destruction now accepted by the Federal Republic of Germany; (2) overall limitation of armed forces and armaments to agreed levels; and (3) adoption of an inspection system to guard against surprise attack in agreed areas throughout the world.

At the end of the preparatory period, the Secretary explained, free elections would take place, a national assembly would be constituted, and government for the whole of Germany would formed.

THE PRESIDENT 

Following Soviet Premier Khrushchev's acceptance of President Eisenhower's invitation to visit this country, the President announced that he would make a 10-day visit to Bonn, London, and Paris in that order. One of the major purposes of the visit, he said, would be to reiterate U.S. determination "to support Western unity in opposing, by force if necessary, any aggression; and to preserve the defensive strength required for our common security." The President made clear that his talks with the various Heads of Government and with the NATO Secretary General and the President of the NATO Council would serve to notify the Soviet Union that "regardless of pressure or inducement we shall never retreat from our ideals or principles or weaken in our resolution to remain secure as we continue the search for peace."

Western Powers Stress Unity 

The talks with Chancellor Adenauer of the Federal Republic of Germany, Prime Minister Macmillan of Great Britain, and President de Gaulle of France during the period August 26-September 3 covered such topics as disarmament, the problems of Berlin and German unification, European integration, and the effectiveness of the

North Atlantic alliance. In an informal television-radio discussion with Prime Minister Macmillan, President Eisenhower declared that Soviet 

Premier Khrushchev must recognize that "peace is an imperative," if we are to have a profitable summit meeting. The President made clear that he would not be a party to a conference that would "depress and discourage" people.

Such a trip had not hitherto been undertaken by a U.S. President while in office.

TRADE 

In his Economic Report on January 20 the

President asserted that, next to maintaining the mutual security of the free world, "the chief concern of the United States in its foreign economic policy is to promote conditions favorable to the exchange of goods, services, technology, and capital among nations, and to assist in economic growth and development."

Change in Loan Policy The Development Loan Fund (DLF) on Octo ber 20 announced that its lending policies had been reviewed "in view of the growth in the economic strength of the industrialized countries of the free world and their steadily increasing ability to assist the less developed countries." The DLF said the review had also taken into account the changes which had taken place in the world payments situation. "There is now a fair presumption," the announcement stated, "that other industrialized countries which export capital goods to the less developed countries are in a financial position to provide long-term loans on reasonable terms to assist such countries in their development programs." As a result, the DLF announced, the Fund would henceforth stress the financing of goods and services of U.S. origin, particularly with respect to the financing of the foreign exchange costs of development projects and programs. 19

Promoting International Trade 

The deficit in the U.S. balance of payments must be reduced by means which enlarge international trade and do not restrict it, Secretary Herter emphasized before the National Foreign Trade Council in November. Such a reduction must be accomplished by methods "which promote competition and the flow of development capital rather than restrict them," Mr. Herter declared. He observed that, if this approach is to succeed, action is required of other countries, particularly Western Europe and Japan, "to open their doors to American exports and to provide additional capital to the development-hungry nations of Asia, Africa, the Near East, and Latin America."

Role of GATT in World Trade 

"The great principles upon which GATT was established-of freedom of trade, of nondiscrimination, of mutual advantage and fair dealingthese principles provide the only solid foundation for healthy economic relations in an increasingly interdependent world," Under Secretary of State 

Dillon said at a GATT meeting at Tokyo. Mr. 

Dillon said that the Contracting Parties "must continue to strengthen GATT as the primary international instrument for translating these principles into action-applying them effectively in the day-to-day decisions of our governments." 

During 1959 the work of the Contracting Parties to the GATT was of particular significance in preparing the way for a general round of tariff negotiations in 1960-61 and in assisting U.S. efforts to obtain the removal of import restrictions which have been imposed with particular severity against dollar area exports. Most of the schools assisted are in Latin America, and about half of the funds involved are foreign currencies accrued from the sale of surplus agricultural commodities.

Cultural Presentations The President's Special International Program for Cultural Presentations continued to sponsor tours abroad of American performing artists and groups of outstanding caliber in the fields of music, dance, drama, and athletics. Assistance under the program is given in order to demonstrate to other countries of the world the high cultural, artistic, and athletic attainment possible under the free private enterprise concepts operative in the United States. Tours illustrating the quality of our contemporary cultural life help promote and strengthen international understanding and enhance American cultural prestige abroad. 

In 1959 the President's program assisted 19 touring units, including 8 musical groups, 4 individual artists, 3 dance companies, and 3 athletic teams. 

Performances were given in 232 cities of 88 countries in all areas of the world, including the Soviet 

Union and other Eastern European countries. All available information indicates that this program in its 512 years of existence has been instrumental in creating new appreciation of and respect for the cultural maturity of the United States. 

OVERSEAS INFORMATION 

The President's visit to Asia, Africa, and

Europe was one of a succession of official actions which lent emphasis to the overseas information program's central theme in 1959-that this country works unceasingly for peace. 

Through its 200 posts in 80 free-world countries and through its worldwide communications facilities, the U.S. Information Agency made sure that the world noted the contrast between the United States conception of peace, as expressed by President Eisenhower's phrase "peace and friendship in freedom," and Soviet Premier

Khrushchev's oft-repeated theme, simply "peace and friendship."

The Agency explained to the world this country's official viewpoint on the Berlin crisis, on the volatile situation in Cuba, and on other developments of the year affecting foreign policy. 

At the same time the information program carried on its basic, long-range job of helping to develop among people overseas a sympathetic understanding of American life, culture, and national purposes.

More Facts to Eastern Europe 

The Agency took full advantage of unprecedented opportunities to communicate directly with the people of the Soviet Union and with their Eastern European neighbors.

A notable lessening of Soviet jamming of Voice of America broadcasts-beginning on the day 

Premier Khrushchev undertook his U.S. visitmeant that for the first time in 10 years the

Agency's radio reports could be received in the Soviet Union without artificial interference. 

The American Exhibition at Moscow, presented for 6 weeks in the summer of 1959, brought 10 23

acres of this country to the heart of the Soviet capital. USIA, other Government agencies, and numerous private firms and organizations collaborated to provide this face-to-face view of 

American life.

Some Special Information Programs 

To meet special problems or interests, the Agency carried on several programs designed for particular countries or groups of countries. In 

Africa, for example, a new information center was opened at Asmara, Eritrea. Libraries were set up at Dar-es-Salaam, Tanganyika; at Fez, Morocco; and at Enugu, Nigeria; and a reading room was started in Freetown, Sierra Leone. 

The Agency made use of documentary motion pictures to carry the story of America to Africans. It demonstrated American interest in the newly emerging independent African countries by reporting throughout the continent the visits to this country of such notables as Ghana's Prime 

Minister Nkrumah and Guinea's President Sékou Touré. 

USIA made special efforts during the year to reach Latin Americans-especially students, laboring people, and the intellectual elementthrough the educational and cultural programs of the 91 binational centers which the Agency supports in 19 Latin American countries. 

Participation in the planning and the development of the American pavilion at the 3-monthlong agricultural fair in New Delhi was part of an active information program in India. 

Presenting American Culture 

Most of the information program's effort for the year was as it always is directed toward registering indelibly abroad an accurate image of 

America. The following are a few examples of activities that were included in that program. 

The year-long observance of the Abraham Lincoln Sesquicentennial afforded the Agency an opportunity to demonstrate, through the Lincoln story, that this government of the people has not and will not perish.

Many millions of people abroad learned about the United States through books borrowed from the Agency's 160 information libraries located in 65 countries. The book translation program brought out its 5,000th title.

Al Hayat Fi America, a cultural magazine in Arabic, began circulation in the Arab world. It is the newest of the 70 magazines and newspapers which USIA publishes in 37 languages to add to the world's knowledge about this country. 

The Agency increased its use of television, which is rapidly becoming a major medium of communication in many foreign countries. 

As part of its effort to inform people overseas of this country's scientific stature, the Agency made available to them a 30-book library of paperback volumes on basic scientific matters.
