PREAMBLE TO THE CONSTITUTION OF THE AMERICAN LEGION 

For God and Country, we associate ourselves together for the following purposes:

To uphold and defend the Constitution of the United States of America; to maintain law and order; to foster and perpetrate a one hundred per cent Americanism; to preserve the memories and incidents of our association in the great war; to inculcate a sense of individual obligation to the community, state and nation; to combat the autocracy of both the classes and the masses; to make right the master of might; to promote peace and good will on earth; to safeguard and transmit the principles of Justice, Freedom and Democracy, and to consecrate and sanctify our comradeship by devotion to mutual helpfulness.

INTRODUCTION 

The American Legion enters its twenty-first year of service to the community, state and nation. 

Out of the comradeship of those who served in the World war, the idea was conceived to form and organize the wonderful organization known the world over as the American Legion.

Dedicated to the highest ideals of patriotism and good citizenship, the American Legion is carrying on from year to year a program of constructive service and usefulness to community, state and nation. 

By successfully engaging in a multitude of worthwhile endeavors throughout the land, the Legion has gained the confidence of the American public and is today regarded as an organization that exerts tremendous influence for betterment in our national life. Each year as the Legion has grown older, it has grown stronger and its accomplishments have increased in like proportion. 

It was particularly fitting that the American Legion should have its inception on the soil of France; that battleground of ages, where so many Americans were in service for their country. The idea of establishing an all-inclusive organization of veterans occurred to many men but it remained for a group of twenty typical soldiers of the American Expeditionary Forces to deal with the idea in a concrete form and lay the foundation of what was destined to become the greatest organization. of its kind in the world history. 

After the Armistice had been signed and the war was definitely brought to an end, those who made up the American Expeditionary Forces were restless and anxious to come home. The great mass of soldiers, sailors [11]

and marines could not fully realize and appreciate the magnitude of the task of demobilization. The most important thing to them was the desire to be back home and the longer they remained in France, the more restless they became.

General Headquarters thought something should be done to improve the morale of the troops and as a consequence, authorized a meeting of twenty National Guard and reserve officers of field rank in order to obtain recommendations for the veterans to be okayed and relieved from the service at the close of the war. Joseph 

W. Glidden, a New Castle man, was a member of the 

Indiana delegation to the Paris caucus.

THE PARIS CAUCUS 

This group met in Paris on February 16th, and after reviewing the situation, they drafted a series of recommendations which were designed to improve the welfare of the enlisted personnel of the American Expeditionary Forces and make life more attractive while they waited for ships to take them home. With work out of the way, a pleasant evening in the gay capitol of Europe was in prospect when Lieutenant Colonel Theodore Roosevelt, one of the delegates, extended to all an invitation to dinner at the Allied Officers Club. Lt. Col. 

Roosevelt believed the time was propitious to make a tentative and initial stand for crystallizing the widespread sentiment in the American army that some kind of an organization of veterans should be dedicated in peace to the same ideals that had inspired them in war and should grow out of their comradeship in arms. 

The American Legion was chartered under the laws [12]

of the United States by act of Congress on September 16, 1919, and the charter convention was held in Minneapolis on November 10th, 11th and 12th. The convention approved the acts of the temporary organization and adopted a permanent constitution. Franklin D'Olier became the first National Commander and Lemuel Bolles the first National Adjutant of the American Legion. National Headquarters were established at Indianapolis, Indiana.

Helping the disabled man is the Legion's first and foremost work and it will continue to be as long as there is a single disabled ex-service man in need of help. The veteran at death is given an honorary and military funeral wherever his place of burial may be.

HOWARD R. SMITH, LIEUTENANT UNITED STATES ARMY 

After whom was named 

The American Legion Post No. 137, 

New Castle, Indiana 

The Howard R. Smith Post No. 137 was organized

November 11th, 1919, and named in memory and in honor of Howard Rea Smith, Second Lieutenant of the

United States Army, son of Edward and Mary Bouslog 

Smith, born in New Castle, Indiana, on January 9th, 1888.

He was educated in the University of Michigan and enlisted in aviation service of the U. S. Army in June, 1917. He was commissioned and sailed for England in 

January, 1918, with the 28th Aero Squadron. He met death in an accident on May 27th, 1918, at Gervain, [13]

Scotland. His body was returned to the United States and buried on November 20th, 1919, in Southmound. cemetery, New Castle, Indiana, with full military honors after his casket had lain in state in the corridors of the courthouse for forty-eight hours. 

On the evening of May 30th, 1920, during the commandership of Henry Chesick of the Howard R. Smith

Post No. 137, American Legion memorial services were held at the First M. E. church. Post Chaplain John F. 

Edwards was in charge, at which time an enlarged picture of Howard R. Smith, for whom the post was named, was unveiled during the service. 

Howard's father was always spoken of as the "Daddy" of the Legion here. Before his death, he was very actively interested in all Legion affairs; he was very prominent and attentive at all special and state meetings. of the Legion as one of their most beloved and special guests, forever revealing a cheerful attitude as long as he lived in spite of the deep sorrow he had experienced as a result of the World war. 

CHRONICLE OF EVENTS OF THE POST 

The following pages contain a brief record of the events during the year of each Commander from the inception of the Post to the present time. 

This record is as complete as the Legion files make possible and is extremely interesting to all veterans and especilaly to those who are members of our local Post. [14]

COMMANDER HENRY CHESICK 

【 1920 ] 

At the first meeting of the Howard R. Smith Post 

No. 137, the American Legion, in New Castle, Indiana,

Henry Chesick, an ex-Captain of the United States Army, after being elected by an unanimous vote, was given his obligation when the honor was bestowed upɔn him of becoming the first Commander of the American 

Legion Post in New Castle, Indiana.

Mr. Chesick was all dressed up in military attire for this special occasion. He took full charge of the meeting for the evening in command of his duties and the office he filled in like manner throughout the remainder of the year.

Commander Chesick proved to be a very competent and likeable commander, judging from all reports from his fellow comrades. Since he has been Commander of the Legion, this organization has continued with one aim in mind and desire in spirit with which it was begun, to become and continue to be one of the greatest organizations in a valuable service to the veterans of the community.

Commander Chesick has been very active in the ranks of the Legion ever since it was organized in New Castle. He is well thought of as a Legionnaire and a gentlemen by his fellow Legionnaires and the citizens of 

New Castle.

He has made a good impression on the members of the Post as a leader and a comrade. The good work he began in the Legion as its first Commander has been continued by the following Commanders each year. [15]

The great start that Mr. Chesick has given the 

American Legion will be continued as long as there is one member of the American Legion left surviving. 

In the beginning there was a veterans' organization of four hundred eighty-five members in Henry county with Henry Chesick as president. The first meeting of this social club was held in an uptown business office with only three members present. The next meeting was held in the G. A. R. rooms in the basement of the courthouse. When the American Legion was formed in Indiana, the local veterans applied for a charter and converted their organization into what is now known as the 

Howard R. Smith Post No. 137, the American Legion.

The first meeting of the Post was held on November 11th, 1919, in the old Alcazar theatre. After the meeting, the veterans were entertained at a supper given by the War Mothers at the Methodist church. 

Thus passed the first year of the local American Legion Post under the able leadership of its first Commander, Henry Chesick.

COMMANDER CLARENCE A. JACKSON 

[1921] Beginning the administration of Commander Clarence A. Jackson, up-to-date office equipment was procured for the Post records and files for safe-keeping in the future.

The Post was called upon to officiate at the funerals of six ex-service men, among them Howard R. Smith, after whom the Post was named. All except one of these veterans were returned from overseas. [16]

The principal speaker at the Memorial Day services was Honorable Frank McHale, Indiana State Americanization Officer and former Vice-Commander of the Department of Indiana. The Post paid all the expenses for the Memorial Day exercises and the graves of all exservice men in the vicinity were marked with official Legion markers and all graves were decorated with flowers. 

Early in the spring, the War Mothers put on a home talent women's minstrel and gave the proceeds, amounting to nearly $500.00, to the Post. 

Jesse Sparks, as Service Officer, cooperated actively with the Home Service Department of the American Red Cross. Early in the year the Red Cross contemplated on discontinuing this Department of the Red Cross in New Castle but Legion officers were able to show the Red Cross officials that the discontinuance of that service would work a real hardship upon ex-service men and their families. As a consequence, it was believed that this Department of the Red Cross would be maintained. Close to five hundred claims of various kinds during the year were executed through the Red 

Cross office in charge of Miss Lula Redding. 

Comrade Sparks personally visited dozens of exservice men and helped investigate their claims. In every case where ex-service men were ill and the fact called to Legion attention, they were presented with flowers and the comrades received personal visits. 

Athletic activity for the year was baseball. The Legion team played about six games during the season. 

The report of the Finance Officer was very encouraging. The year closed with a fair balance. 

Finance Officer Gilbert Hewit proved a real watchman in charge of the treasury. [17]

At no time were bills contracted for more money than was really in the treasury. It was especially guarded that no money was spent for purposes that were, in the opinion of members and officers, unnecessary for the good of the Post.

The most successful turnout of the Legion during the year was for the parade during the convention of the Indiana G. A. R. In this parade, which consisted of practically all organizations in the city, about two hundred ex-service men were in line of march, most of whom were in uniform.

The press congratulated the Legion Post on its good turnout and the military bearing of the marchers. Immediately after the parade, with the Fort Benjamin Harrison band at the head of the Legion, the Legion marched to Memorial Park, a distance of practically two miles, and assisted in the flag-raising ceremonies. The march was made in a drizzling rain and over sloppy roads but there was only good cheer and excellent attitude in the ranks.

During the year of 1924, the Post was indeed very fortunate in having Mr. Jackson represent the State of 

Indiana on the board of the American Legion Weekly. 

In 1927 Mr. Jackson was State Commander of the 

American Legion. He has also served on the Advisory 

Board of the American Legion Monthly, one of the largest subscription and financial publications of the nation with a large staff of highly educated editors. 

Commander Jackson has always been very active in the Legion for the good he could do for his fellow comrades that they might see and enjoy a brighter day. [18]

COMMANDER CLARENCE THOMPSON 

[1922] Clarence A. Thompson, now deceased, was the third 

Commander of the local Legion Post. A good attendance during the year demonstrated a splendid interest in the meetings.

By adopting a policy of close economy, the administration of Comrade Thompson was practically able to clear up a debt of several hundred dollars made by the administration of two years previous, caused by the heavy expense of starting the Post and developing it. 

After the debt was paid, a small balance was left in the treasury.

Several entertainments were held during the year replenishing the treasury. Other entertainments were held for social and educational reasons. Two benefit shows were given in March and November at the Princess theatre and made the Post a very substantial income to build up its treasury.

The Indiana University Glee Club in April and the 

"Human Fly" in July were two more activities that added to our treasury. 

In March Comrade Johnny Allen, the hero of the battle of Soissons, gave his two-fold lecture on foreign. immigration and his personal experiences in the war. 

This meeting was held at the First M. E. church. In 

June, Comrade Oswald Ryan, National Executive Committeeman, and State Commander Claude E. Gregg, and 

Mrs. George Hewit, Sixth District Committeewoman, appeared before a large audience at the Memorial Park and the First Methodist church. The meetings were to inform the public of the purposes, the ideals and the [19]

aspirations of the American Legion.

Three military funerals were held during the year. 

Before the close of the year, Commander Thompson recommended that the Post lend its assistance toward the organization of a Ladies Auxiliary.

COMMANDER GILBERT K. HEWIT 

[ 1923 ] The officers of the Post for 1923 were installed on 

January 16th, and on that evening the Post members were treated to a very elaborate banquet given by the War Mothers. Everybody present enjoyed a very good time.

The Legion was fortunate in having Senator Ralston, George Barnard and Clarence Jackson as speakers for the occasion.

The Post, at a special meeting, considered increasing its dues from $3.00 to $5.00 per year in hope that the 

Post would be more secure financially and self-supporting in the future. The meetings during the year of Commander Hewit were well attended. 

The Legion considers itself very fortunate in having a Ladies Auxiliary organized during the commandership of Comrade Hewit. They have since given their wholehearted support to the Legion in many ways. The work they are doing is proving a mutual help to both the Legion and the Auxiliary. 

There was one military funeral during the year for a [ 20 ]

comrade sent here from Tennessee for burial at Southmound.

The Post was presented a gift of a Hundred Dollar Liberty Bond from the Progressive Club of New Castle, for which the members of the Legion were very thankful. The bond was placed in trust, not to be used for a period of five years.

In order to give something to the community and to take care of disabled war veterans, which is the vital program of the American Legion, the local Post sponsored the drive for the American Red Cross roll call. 

During the year aid was given to the sick comrades, and many comrades were helped in various ways. 

Sixteen meetings of the Post were held during the year. Five executive committee meetings recorded during the year. An enrollment of two hundred thirtythree members at the close of the year revealed an increase in membership of fifty members according to the number of members in 1921, and an increase in membership of thirty-nine in 1922. The success in membership increase was due in great measure to the efficient work of the membership committee for 1923, for which Commander Hewit commended the committee on their most successful drive.

COMMANDER CLIFFORD PAYNE 

[1924] Everything possible was done this year to build up the Legion. First the increase of dues from $3.00 to $5.00 went into effect. In face of the increase in dues, [21]

the membership increased from two hundred thirty-three to two hundred eighty-four. The meetings were made very interesting during the year by different speakers being obtained for special programs. Many kinds of entertainment were provided throughout the year. Having held the meetings regularly and having made them interesting, brought the Post a good attendance. Fourteen hundred attended the twenty-four regular meetings or an average of over fifty at each meeting. There were ten public meetings, four executive meetings and four special meetings, making a total of forty-one meetings during the year.

At the ten public meetings, there was an attendance of approximately nine thousand people. 

Early in the year the Post sponsored a series of men's meetings in the Coliseum building. Michael Foley, Dr. 

Smith, Professor Graham, Robert Alley, General Everson and Fred Van Nuys were the speakers. This was one of the biggest things the Legion ever attempted and the public gave it hearty approval.

Later in the year, Lt. Corliss Griffiths, who attempted to capture Bergdoll, spoke of his experiences in behalf of the Legion at the Methodist church. 

In the afternoon on Memorial Day, members of the Legion were present at the unveiling of the memorial at the east entrance of the courthouse in honor and to the memory of the veterans of the Civil war. A large wreath was placed at the memorial in honor of the G. A. R. who pledged themselves to carry on their patriotic work. Rev. 

LeCount gave the address for this special occasion. 

Comrade Gurney Gray was the champion membership go-getter. Floyd Fields and Gilbert Hewit were the [ 22 ]

majors of the membership organization. After the membership drive an initiation was held of a large class of new members.

Never before had more than one hundred seventyfour new members been signed up in two days. Since then the membership drive plan has been used all over the state. State Commander Ball, in his final report, mentioned the successful work of the Howard R. Smith Post.

The initiation ceremony was put on by the Hayward Barcers Post of Indianapolis. During the summer months the Post held its regular meetings at the shelter house at the park. They were marked with a good attendance. 

During the year's activity the long work for adjusted service compensation bill was finally passed. 

The local Post cooperated with the State and National Department by communicating with their congressmen and senators. Officials of the Post helped six hundred ex-service men with getting and filling out their applications.

July 4th marked the big day of the Post for the year. 

Fifty men went to Memorial Park and worked for the Legion from early morning until late that night to make the celebration a huge success.

Comrade Earl Johnson as chairman of the park committee, along with Comrade Shultz, is entitled to much credit for the success of the sales at the stand. 

In the afternoon a program was held in honor of our country's independence. Paul Benson gave a wonderful address. The baseball team for the year was managed by 

Comrade Richter and created much interest among the 

Legionnaires. 

[ 23 ]

In behalf of the people of our country, the Legion was present at the naturalization of foreign-born immigrants who were made citizens. Each one was presented with a flag and made welcome by the Legion. 

Attention was called by Miss Melville, city nurse, to the Legion that the Orphans Home at Honey Creek was without playground equipment. A committee was appointed and baseball equipment was given and a large slide was installed. The children of the Home proved real boosters for the Legion.

Legion social activities were attempted first at the Eagles hall. A dance and card party was given and later at Memorial Park shelterhouse another dance was given for the Auxiliary, Legionnaires and their wives and friends. These were well attended and the Auxiliary then entertained the Legion at a picnic dance and card party.

Representatives of the Shenendoah Flag Company came to the Legion with the proposition of a uniform. flag system for New Castle. The proposition was put before the Chamber of Commerce and endorsed.

Under the auspices of the Legion one hundred fifty outfits were purchased by the merchants. Everyone was pleased with the system.

One funeral was held during the year. Whenever the Legion became aware of sickness, they sent flowers to the sick comrades.

The Legion assisted in the proper observance of Defense Day and circulated thousands of copies of flag etiquette among the school children. The Legion, with the cooperation of Roy Valentine, principal of the local high school, was instrumental in having the Knightstown Sol- [24]

diers and Sailors Orphans' Home team admitted to the 

High School Athletic Association. 

As commander of the Post, Mr. Payne gave a splendid business-like administration. He stated that the many activities would have been impossible had it not been for the faithful assistance of his adjutant, Comrade John Hupp, and many others who gave their generous support to the Legion.

Comrade Payne was very thankful for having been 

Commander during the year.

COMMANDER FRED JOYCE 

[ 1925] The activities of the Howard R. Smith Post of the 

American Legion as reflected in the work and the report of the Adjutant has been numerous and varied during the year Fred Joyce was Commander. The Adjusted 

Compensation Act became effective January 1, 1925, and necessitated considerable paper work on the part of many Legionnaires and a great portion of the work was conducted by the Adjutant.

A program was sponsored at each meeting. A letter was mailed to each member urging him to be present at each meeting and was a success to the extent of splendid average attendance which was maintained throughout the year. The success of the meetings was due in great measure to the efforts of the many Legionnaires who contributed their share of the entertainment. 

At the regular stated meeting on Tuesday, February 3, 1925, after some discussion, it was decided to endorse [ 25 ]

the Sunday afternoon men's meetings. Comrade Payne,

John Hupp and Dr. Rawlings were appointed a committee to cooperate with other organizations in town to that good cause. 

On February 17th, 1925, Comrade Price, then state director for the endowment fund drive, was introduced and gave a brief, convincing talk on the necessity of such fund and how the fund was to be provided. This fund is used for assisting unfortunate veterans and their families. The Post was first to raise enough money to reach its quota of $4,100.00 for the endowment fund drive for the relief of poor orphan children and those in need. 

At the meeting on March 17, 1925, Comrade Brammer entertained the members with a number of interesting and humorous sketches of Negro camp life. 

On February 19, 1925, Barney Breedlove, State Service Officer, outlined the rehabilitation work of the Legion in a very interesting discussion. From a financial standpoint, the year was a success. A membership of two hundred five provided enough revenue from their membership dues to care for most of the running expenses of the Post. The Adjutant's books show a good income from this source alone. 

COMMANDER JOHN HUPP 

[1926] At seven o'clock an oyster supper was served to a number of Legionnaires and guests prior to the installation of officers. [26]

Mr. and Mrs. Clase, guests of the evening, entertained those present with cantonment songs and war-time poems. Rev. Sargent gave a very interesting talk on "Are You a Good Loser?"

State Commander-elect Clarence Jackson gave an impressive talk on "Why Are You a Legionnaire?"

For the first time in its history, the Post had the privilege of entertaining a National Commander. On 

December 20, 1926, National Commander McQuigg spoke to an audience of about eight hundred people in the Y. M. C. A. Due to the extremely bad weather, the attendance was not what it should have been. 

The Knightstown Soldiers' and Sailors' Children's

Home Band was provided transportation to New Castle by the Post and furnished the music for the occasion. 

Through the untiring efforts of Comrade Gray, a home talent play entitled "The Black Heifer" with a cast composed mostly of Legionnaires and Auxiliary members, was staged seven different times in and around the city. Although this was not exactly a success from a financial standpoint, yet it was thought that the people in various small towns where performances were given have a clearer and more friendly conception of the American Legion and this venture afforded lots of fun and good fellowship.

One performance was given at the Knightstown Children's Home at which time each child was presented with a bag of peanuts.

Through the untiring efforts of a committee composed of A. L. Leonard, Monroe McMath and Dr. Fred 

W. Leavell, a portion of Southmound cemetery was set aside and given to the Post by the City Council at their [ 27 ]

meeting in October of 1926 for beautification and burial for all soldiers of all wars. 

One phase of work upon which the officers have laid much stress in the year was the rehabilitation and service work within the Post as was made noticeable from the report of the Service Officer. This is a very important office and one whose duties continue to grow. 

On March 2nd the Post entertained members of the

G. A. R. with some entertainment and a lunch. The older comrades were delighted in telling their experiences in the Civil war. 

During the year Mr. Hupp was Commander, one joint meeting with the Ladies Auxiliary was held in the basement of the Y. M. C. A. with the sad fact that too little room was provided for the large number present. 

Another joint meeting with the War Mothers and War Dads and Auxiliary was held in the form of a picnic at 

Memorial Park. 

On Memorial Day, the Post as usual decorated all graves with flowers and a flag. Legion markers were placed on graves not provided with such. 

The large German gun, which was procured by former Commander Fred Joyce, was placed at Memorial

Park at considerable expense to the Post and was dedicated on June 20th. Paul V. McNutt, dean of Indiana

Law University, then State Commander-elect of the Legion, delivered the dedicatory address. Music was furnished by the Chamber of Commerce Band.

On July 4th, in the light of service for the community, the Post, in conjunction with the Chamber of Commerce, raised the necessary funds and provided entertainment at Memorial Park. Games for the children and [ 28 ]

daylight fireworks provided entertainment and amusement in the afternoon with an abundance of fireworks and a dance at night. About sixty-five Legionnaires assisted in handling the program during the day. 

On July 20th the annual community meeting was held in the town hall at Spiceland. Although it was an extremely warm evening, a good crowd attended the meeting. Senator Luther O. Draper and Major William

G. Everson were the principal speakers. 

In the interest of better citizenship and good roads for Henry county, a number of registration booths were maintained by the Post under the direction of Comrade 

George Jeffrey on Saturday, October 2nd; Monday, October 4th, being the last day for voters to register. A number of people were registered who probably would not have had the privilege of voting had it not been for this service.

After a number of preliminary meetings, a Post Drum and Bugle Corps was organized with Comrade Floyd Fields as business manager. The corps was composed of twenty pieces, twelve drums and eight bugles. Each member purchased his own instrument with the understanding that the Post was to reimburse him by March 1, 1927. The method meant a good sacrifice on the part of the Legionnaires in the corps and they deserved much credit for their efforts. At a later date the members were equipped with very attractive uniforms. 

Comrade Taylor Morris, Service Officer, handled his work of that office in a commendable manner. This year was the occasion of a number of funerals of deceased comrades.

Early in the fall of 1926, a meeting of the executive committee was held and it was decided to hold the sec- [29]

ond annual Indoor Fair in order to replenish our treas ury and enlarge the trust fund. Under the direction of 

Comrade Lloyd Diehl, with the assistance of a large number of Legionnaires, the affair was held in the Kahn- Heller building and proved a great success from a financial standpoint. The success of the venture was entirely due to the splendid spirit of cooperation shown by the many Legionnaires who assisted with the fair in many ways. 

On Armistice Day, November 11, 1926, Comrade

Fields and a committee of his entertained members of the Auxiliary, War Mothers and War Dads. Although quite different from previous celebrations, nevertheless, the ladies and War Dads enjoyed the program to a great extent. Uneaten food was turned over to the Salvation 

Army and credit for it was given to the charity department of the Post. Between three hundred fifty and four hundred people enjoyed the evening. 

Twenty-four regular meetings of the Post were held in 1926 and three special meetings and five executive meetings, making a total of thirty-two meetings in all. 

Comrade Carl Geise, Adjutant, was a wonderful help and inspiration during the year, especially with the bulletins which were sent out after each meeting and for which he was entirely responsible. 

The Finance Officer, Clarence Scott, worked diligently at his duties. The finance committee proved a great value by its timely advice and activities. The committee was given special credit for instituting the trust fund which was materially increased during the year. 

At the close of the year, Commander Hupp thanked every member of the Post for the honor they had bestowed upon him and for the good spirit and coopera- [ 30 ]

tion they had given him during the year. He stated the measure of success was due to the untiring efforts of all members working for the vital interests of the Legion. 

In conclusion, he remarked that he trusted the members of the Post would give his successor the same hearty support.

Commander John Hupp presided at the meeting and guests were as follows: L. J. Hinkel, Ed Smith, R. H. McIntyre, then President of the Chamber of Commerce; Robert Hunter, President of the Dynamo Club; E. G. Scotten, President of the Rotary Club;

The following officers from the State and National Department of the American Legion were present: Clarence Jackson, State Commander; Paul McNutt, Commander-elect; John Klinger,

State Adjutant; Phil Stapp, Chairman of the Endowment Fund; Bowman Elder, Chairman of French Travel; Pleas Greenlee, Sixth District Chairman. There were also Le gion members from the following towns in Indiana: 

Knightstown, Richmond and Shelbyville. 

The following were given tokens of appreciation. from the Post: Clarence Jackson, Past Commander's ring; 

John Klinger, Legion plaque; John Hupp, Past Cɔmmander's ring. [31]

The officers were installed by Commander-elect Paul V. McNutt. Interesting talks were given by Mr. Mc- 

Nutt, Clarence Jackson, Bowman Elder, Phil Stapp, John

The year 1927 was a very busy year for the Post. A program of community service culminated in the purchase of twenty acres of ground to be added to Memorial Park. This was the crowning achievement for the year and speaks well for the spirit of cooperation which existed among the membership during 1927.

Considerable credit for the accomplishment was due Comrade Gilbert K. Hewit who negotiated the deal, and 

Comardes John Hupp, Clarence Jackson, George Jeffrey and Mr. Johnson, who willingly assumed the responsibility as trustees for the project. 

The next to have its inception in the spirit of community service was the organization of the Boy Scout troop. The troop was and still is a glowing tribute to the untiring efforts of Comrade Gurney Gray, who organized the promotion of the Boy Scout movement in New Castle. The expenditure and effort put forth to organize this Scout troop has been repaid a thousand-fold with the cooperation of the boys and the good will and satisfaction of boosting a group of youngsters in the right direction.

The boys have proven to be a noble monument to the ideals of community service, more than that which might have been erected of marble or stone. 

In the spring of 1927 a minstrel, "Chuckles," was produced by the Post under considerable handicap and necessitated much work on the part of a large portion of the membership. The show was an artistic and finan- [ 32 ]

cial success. As in many previous cases, this was due to the spirit of teamwork for which the Howard R. Smith 

Post has always been noted in all that it has done. The 

Ladies Auxiliary gave liberally of their time and talent to making the show a success. The Auxiliary won the lasting gratitude of the Post by giving their half of the proceeds to the American Legion. 

Legion Day at the Knightstown Children's Home was 

September 11, 1927.

The third annual Indoor Fair was held the last week in October. It was also a financial success. The success of the venture of the fair depended in a large measure to the financial status of the Post treasury for 1927. 

John Hupp, Charles Bundy, Leonard Craig and Herman

Witt served on the fair and automobile committee. Expression of thanks and appreciation was extended to them and hundreds of other Legionnaires who helped make the event a success.

A public dance was held the following Monday after the fair in celebration of the outstanding success of this fair.

The Halloween dance was another financial success. 

The Legion donated Y. M. C. A. memberships to ten deserving boys for the year 1927. A considerable sum of money was also donated to the Red Cross. 

The Armistice Day celebration and entertainment was sponsored at the Country Club. Everyone present enjoyed a very pleasant and social good time. 

The Drum and Bugle Corps went to Sulphur Springs on May 29th to help in the memorial services sponsored there for the Sons of the American Legion. Memorial services were sponsored in New Castle as usual. [ 33 ]

The Boy Scouts were taken to a football game at

COMMANDER GURNEY GRAY 

[1930] The record report as given by Commander Gray, reveals the Post meetings were held regularly every first and third Tuesday evening during the entire year. One special meeting was called; four executive meetings were held and the Post was host to one district meeting, which meeting had a good attendance and proved of great interest to the Legion.

A public meeting was sponsored in the auditorium of the Y. M. C. A. The principal purpose of this meeting was, if possible, to make an opportunity for the citizens of New Castle and surrounding community to hear one of the greatest heroes of the World war, Sergeant Alvin York, of Jamestown, Tenn., the principal speaker of the evening who, in his extremely interesting address, explained definitely and clearly his many conquering experiences during his services for his country during the war. Everyone left the meeting with a good word picture in his mind of what war and the real courage in war was really like.

The next attempt of the Post was to open the golf course. This was accomplished and proved to be a success. John Hupp was appointed in charge of a dance program. He and his willing committee who assisted him made this program entertaining as well as a paying success. [40]

Another grand feature of entertainment to be held was a fair known as an Indoor-Outdoor Fair. This was rather discouraging because of the industrial conditions. at that time. 

A Junior baseball team was then sponsored. This organization embraced a total of seventy boys playing ball. 

The Legion Boy Scout troop was the next interesting program of the Post. This work created considerable interest from the beginning and more than twenty-five boys were immediately enrolled, the number steadily increasing and creating a greater interest all the time. 

This troop is under the supervision of Commander Gray who is Scoutmaster of the troop at the present time. 

The Boy Scouts built a camp on the farm of Eli Mills and this camp has become very popular and has been steadily improved with each coming year. 

The boys organized a drum corps and attended the state convention at Fort Wayne, Ind. In October they also went to the Boy Scout Day celebration in Bloomington. The boys were very appreciative for the enjoyment they received from these two trips and expressed their thanks as an organization and individually for all the local Post has done for them. 

In connection with being Commander of our Post, Commander Gray was also the Service Officer of the local Post as well as being District Service Officer.

In addition to his duties of Commander and affiliated with many other social welfare activities, to which he has applied a great portion of his time, Mr. Gray has continued his service with great success. At the end of the year he gave a detailed report of his services for the year. [ 41 ]

The ladies of the American Legion Auxiliary presented the Post with the nice sum of fifty dollars to be used in support of the service work of the Legion. 

On October 7, 1930, Mr. Gray sent the Auxiliary a personal letter of appreciation and thanks, which reads. 

as follows: "On behalf of the American Legion and as 

Commander and Service Officer of the Howard R. Smith

Post of the American Legion, I wish to take this opportunity to thank you for the very generous gift of $50.00 for the promotion of our Legion service work. I assure you that this gift will be used in the promotion of our Legion service program. We believe the Legion and

As Commander of our local Post, I wish to thank the Legion Auxiliary and its fine and efficient corps of officers for the fine cooperation and heartfelt interest in all the undertakings of the Legion during this past year. Mr. Gray experienced a very discouraging year as far as finances were concerned. Aside from all this, we find from his record of service in the Legion, he has pushed on with a patriotic spirit in determination to make a good record one way or the other.

Mr. Gray worked with all his might to carry on every part of the Legion program during the year. Two membership drives were held, amounting to a membership of three hundred four members.

An employment bureau was also made a part of the Legion program, placing several men in employment during the year.

When the Indiana state convention was held at Fort 

Wayne, Ind., in August, 1930, Mr. Gray, while Post [ 42 ]

Commander of the American Legion, was elected Sixth District Commander of the old Sixth district, which had eighteen Legion Posts in the following counties: Union, Henry, Hancock, Wayne, Shelby, Franklin, Fayette and

Commander Gray made a splendid record for the Legion around many of the obstacles that confronted. him during the year. He will always be remembered and cherished by his comrades for his wonderful year as Commander.

THE FORTY AND EIGHT 

All mankind is familiar with the great post-war organization-The American Legion. Out of this continent, spanning a flesh and blood memorial of the World war has sprung, about nineteen years ago, La Societe des 40 Hommes et 8 Chevaux. This is an organization in good standing with the American Legion to provide a playground feature within the American Legion and to bind themselves together in even more closer comradeship and fraternalism. All members of La Societe are known as Voyageurs Militaire and all eligible non-members are known as Prisonniers de Gare or P. G.'s.

In order to apply for membership in the Forty and Eight, one must have been a member of the American Legion for not less than the preceding twelve months and have performed some service for the American Legion which in the opinion of the Voiture Locale, should entitle one to membership.

Joseph W. Breen, an officer of the Breen-McCracken 

Post No. 297 in Philadelphia, conceived the idea the Legion should have a way whereby they could relax in the fellowship of other good Legionnaires and recall the happier side of the war service and keep alive the lusty humor that had characterized the fighting forces during the 

World war.

With fifteen members of the Breen-McCracken Post, Breen began to put his idea into concrete form from the boxcar of the French railways with its legend "Forty and 

Eight." From other familiar names on the French wartime railways came the titles of officers and phrases needed for the operation of the organizaiton. 

The little band of Philadelphia Voyageurs with their [67]

big idea went to the second annual convention of the Department of Pennsylvania. On the final night of the 

Legion convention, an organization promenade was called and a wrecking crew inducted several prominent Pennsylvania Legionnaires. A permanent organization was formed at the same time. 

Joseph W. Breen, the founder, a Major of Engineers during the war, was the unanimous choice for Chef de 

Cheurin de Fer.

The New Orleans Promenade made history when it voted a Child Welfare Fund to be raised by an annual assessment of fifty cents per Voyageur. It was also to be used in caring for the orphaned children of World war veterans. This marked the beginning of the child welfare program of the Legion, made possible at the start and aided ever since by the Forty and Eight. At the fourth Promenade Nationale held in San Francisco, plans for child welfare were merged with the Legion and developed into a national program. 

Forty and Eight headquarters were ordered moved from Seattle, Washington, to Indianapolis to be in the same location as the national Legion headquarters. 

The principle of the Forty and Eight is for the good of their country and to have a little amusement. It is sincere with the Forty and Eight that America be one country under one flag. [68]

THE AMERICAN LEGION AUXILIARY 

The American Legion Auxiliary has been a very active and helpful organization ever since it has been organized. This organization has been of great assistance to the Legion in many ways. Too much cannot be said in honor of this wonderful group of enthusiastic workers. They are a great benefit to the comrades of the various posts throughout the entire nation. 

They have been very good to the members of the Legion by gifts and service in many ways for the welfare and comfort of fellow Legionnaires at any and all times. 

They are appreciated for all they have done and are doing for the personal cheer of every Legionnaire. 

The purpose of the American Legion Auxiliary is to participate in and contribute to the accomplishment of the aims and purposes of the American Legion to consecrate and sanctify our associations by devotion to mutual helpfulness.

The Auxiliary stands ready to relieve World war veterans and their families in case of need or any other emergency. The finances of the unit are secured by means of the sale of poppies each year which are made by the disabled hospitalized veterans. 

Additional money is made by other social welfare activities in the community. 

Members of the Auxiliary invite the wives, sisters and daughters of Legionnaires to join the organization helping them by becoming a member of the American 

Legion Auxiliary. 

The ladies of the local Auxiliary are due a great deal of credit in their help and thoughtfulness to deserving families and the American Legion is justly proud of this unit and appreciates their aid to the fullest extent. [ 69 ]

SONS OF THE AMERICAN LEGION 

The Sons of the American Legion has every interest at heart for the future welfare of this organization. They are marching on as time goes on with that interesting and patriotic spirit of a great nation built by the high ideals of our forefathers. 

As Sons of the Legion, they shall forever be a part of this great organization just as long as there is a Legionnaire. [70]

THE WAR MOTHERS 

The War Mothers are exactly as their name implies. 

They are mothers whose sons have been in the World war. On many occasions, veterans have been remembered by mothers of the organization by some mother other than the mother of the veteran. 

Nevertheless, the veteran has had the opportunity to realize that keen sense of spirit in motherly love in either case.

The War Mothers are very generous and thoughtful the way they reveal their kind attitude toward the veterans of the World war.

Many are the sons who have had and enjoyed the care and consideration given them by the War Mothers. 

The veterans whose mothers were living when they returned home looked forward to the welcoming smile of their mothers and these brave mothers continue to warm the hearts of other sons whose mothers have passed away. [71]

GOLD STAR MOTHERS 

A Gold Star Mother is one whose son died or was killed in service during the World war. Among the 

World war mothers of Henry county there are many who lost their sons in the World war. These mothers have suffered a very intense and sad experience because of the loss of one or more sons during the tragedy of war.

Even though we can never fully understand the depth of the heartache they have had to bear, our sympathy is extended to them for the grief and the sorrow they have undergone because of war. 

They can never be repaid for all they have lost. The great joy they once had they have given to their country. The sons they have given, when these boys made the supreme sacrifice, the most that any man could ever give for the sake of his country.

There is no record in the office of the Quartermaster General of the War Department of any Gold Star Mother having made the Gold Star Pilgrimage from Henry county.

MEMORIAL PARK 

Henry county has a beautiful and wonderful park donated to the people of Henry county and to the veterans of all wars.

The county has expressed its appreciation to her veterans of the war in a substantial and lasting way through the establishment of Memorial park. [72]

Members of the Legion feel a personal pride in the park and consider a personal responsibility in its maintenance and upkeep in the future. Through the courtesy of the Park Board, the American Legion was given the refreshment permit at the park.

The establishment of Memorial park was largely due to the efforts of the New Castle Kiwanis Club and the wonderful personal work of Maurice Goodwin, Salem 

L. Shively and Dr. W. C. Van Nuys. 

The people and the veterans of Henry county are very grateful to the members of the Kiwanis club and these citizens of Henry county who were instrumental in the establishment of this great Memorial park in 

Henry county.

They will always be appreciated and remembered for the wonderful achievement they have made in the work of this memorial and their service to the community. 

In the early spring of 1923 when Gilbert Hewit was commander of the Legion, a re-dedication of Memorial park was made to the veterans of all wars for which the Civil war and Spanish-American war veterans were very thankful to the Legion.

The park is supplied with all necessary requirements for sanitary and comfortable quarters for visitors. The playground equipment provides a wonderful pleasure and enjoyment for children.

Memorial park provides large brick ovens with wood for fires, city water, telephone service, toilet facilities, a shelterhouse, a lake equipped with rowboats as well as ducks and swans abiding in the water, and everything needed to make a desirable place of recreation. This park is also police protected. [73]

The park ground was begun with thirty-eight acres, growing from time to time, until the present size of the park amounts to one hundred eighty acres. 

THE DOUGHBOY MEMORIAL 

The War Mothers of Henry county sponsored the dedication of the Doughboy Memorial in Memorial park on Sunday, August 25th, 1929. The services were in charge of Mrs. Lynn C. Boyd, president of the Henry

County Chapter of War Mothers. 

Prior to the dedication, a picnic was held at noon for the members of the War Mothers, Veterans of Foreign. 

Wars, the American Legion and the Legion Auxiliary. 

The program was begun at 2:00 p. m. and music for the occasion was provided by the New Castle Chamber of Commerce Band. The invocation was given by Henry 

Chesick of the Howard R. Smith Post of the American 

Legion. The ceremony was well attended by the public. 

Captain James Tyner, Past Grand Commander of the G. A. R., unveiled the tribute to ex-service men of 

Henry county.

The Doughboy Memorial is a bronze statue of an American soldier in war garb, mounted on a mound of attractive rocks surrounded with the inset of many different kinds of beautiful flowers. The statue stands on the hill south of the residence of the superintendent of the park.

The height of the monument is twelve feet and was erected at a cost of more than fifteen hundred dollars [74]

and is the first memorial to the veterans to be placed in our Henry county Memorial park.

This statue was solely the project of the local chapter of the War Mothers. General William Graham Everson, then pastor of the First Baptist church of Muncie, Ind., delivered the address for the dedication.

General Everson in conclusion said, "It is well that you War Mothers dedicate this statue. Women have always suffered in time of conflict. You paid a price more precious than that of blood. Your hearts were torn and there was no relief. You gave your very life when your boy went away. Thank God for the world's womanhood. "Wars are fought after all by boys. They marched away with brave hearts but most of them were scarcely out of their 'teens. That's the terrible story of every war. The United States military policy is one of defense. We have not ambition for that which belongs to any other nation on top of the earth. "On the other hand, we have vast lands, possessions and glorious opportunities. These we have a right to defend should some nation or combination of nations seek to destroy or take away. May this statue ever stand here in this beautiful park, a gift of mothers' love. May it lead us to more faithful citizenship and to earnest prayer for the drawing near of a day of world peace."

The following benediction was given by Dr. Robert 

O. Levell: "Our kind, precious and Heavenly Father, we want to thank Thee for the opportunity of gathering here with the War Mothers of Henry county, who have made it possible for the erection and dedication of this great [75]

memorial which stands before the eyes of the public today and for the ages to come, in honor of those whose sacrifices have made possible the peace and freedom we enjoy today. "We ask that Thou will continue to bless our War

Mothers in the good work that they have done and are doing in Henry county. "Dismiss us now with all the necessary blessings for life and help us to realize the value of the good fight for peace eternal. Amen."
