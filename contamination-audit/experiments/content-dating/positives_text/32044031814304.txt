THE LAWYER AND THE KING'S ENGLISH. 

BY CATHERINE DRINKER BOWEN. 

Judge Sloane, Ladies and Gentlemen: First, I want to say how deeply complimented I am to be invited here in this capacity. I have attended many of your meetings and heard very learned talks. I enjoyed them all, even the parts 

I was not equipped to understand, being myself on the outer, fringy edges of your profession-an observer of lawyers rather than a student of the law. 

But I am grateful to lawyers, and I welcome a chance to say so. Think of the material your profession has given me! I've written two biographies of lawyers-400 pages about Justice Holmes and 700 pages about John Adams. 

The world likes to grumble because lawyers make their living from the troubles of laymen. Here stands one laywoman who for the past ten years has been making a very satisfactory living from the troubles-and the triumphsof two lawyers.

In the course of my professional and self-rewarding observation of lawyers, I have noted a sometimes dualistic attitude concerning the use and abuse of language. Last month, for instance, a young lawyer wrote me from New York. He said he heard that I was to address this distinguished Society on the subject of the King's English, and he chose to remind me severely that language is for lawyers a tool which should never be used merely to gratify the feelings of a writer. The young man outlined his duties in the large office where he is employed and pointed out the gulf that yawns between the drawing up of leases, the conveyancing of real estate, and the judicial opinions of a Justice (3) 734 145

Oliver Wendell Holmes. 66 'Magic words", the letter said, "cannot cure defects in legal foresight, or cure the failure to think things out thoroughly. 

I distrust magic words", the letter ended, "and I distrust obvious literary devices." 

Who does not distrust obvious literary devices? Yet in the very weight of that seven-page letter was something I mistrusted also. For a young man who held magic words in suspicion, here was a carefully brewed spell. I thought 

I detected some paragraphs that had been copied over once or twice. In the end, of course, my quarrel with the young man was one of definition. To him the "magic word" signified redundancy, mere ornamentation. To me it signifies that word which is most apposite and most quick with meaning.

Yet this earnest and admirable young man was in good company when he made his protest. There is in the genus lawyer something which makes him mistrust the poet, makes him forever deny the muse, even as he tunes his own harp. "The law," said Justice Holmes, "is not the place for the artist or the poet. The law is the calling of thinkers." 

And in almost the next breath, he said, "Life is action and passion. I think it is required of man that he share the passion and action of his time, at peril of being judged not to have lived." 

Is that the statement of a man purely intellectual?

I call it poetry. "Whether a man accepts from Fortune her spade and will look downward and dig, or from Aspiration her axe and cord and will scale the ice, the one and only success which it is his to achieve is to bring to his work a mighty heart."

The very punctuation is poetry! To say that it was inspired is not to say that it was accidently achieved. I think 

Holmes worked over it. I would like to bet that he knew exactly where he put those commas and why, and that he

shifted them more than once before he was satisfied. This was no child of nature. Holmes was in the best sense of the word a self-conscious man. He always knew what he was doing. In our American history, I could run you up a pretty list of poet-lawyers and of their works. "When in the course of human events, it becomes necessary for one people to dissolve the political bonds. 

Or that other, with which your profession makes you even more familiar: "We, the people of the United 

States, in order to form a more perfect union, establish justice, insure domestic tranquillity, provide for the common defence. 

Those are magic verbs, in there. Each one different, each perfect to form establish insure provide promote the general welfare, and secure the blessings of liberty. 

You will remark that the author never falls into the temptation to repeat the infinitive to-a preposition which, I have long noted, is a baited trap for the legal pen. In that preamble is another quality besides eloquence. 

There is a succinctness of phrase that we might call by the name of wit.

Wit is part and parcel of poetry. Last summer in Vermont at a Writers Conference where I teach, Robert Frost told us a poem should be witty. "Each line should be a slogan," he said. "One should be able to use each line as the title of a book."

The Constitution of the United States was not drawn up as poetry but as a working legislative instrument. Yet it contains a number of book titles. The quality of true wit is rare and the legal profession has it in large measure. 

Lawyers have to be quick thinkers. To be truly witty as I define the word, a man's mind must penetrate instantly to the heart of a matter, straight through to the essence. To do this requires intellectual courage, an almost angry honesty of thinking. . . . And it is not to be confused with

6 our proud native accomplishment known as the wisecrack. 

Wisecracks belong on the American scene; they can take you over some rough ground. But just one of them can put a brutal end to any spirited discussion, because a wisecrack is deliberate misrepresentation for the sake of an easy laugh. But wit lights a fire under good talk. The witty man predicts his opponent's move. He is two arguments ahead, and his chief difficulty is in holding back until he can wedge in his first word. . . For myself, I enjoy Holmes's wit almost more than his eloquence-and it is in his judicial opinions that his wit is most evident. I don't mean humor;

I mean again, the sharp turn of phrase that makes a reader almost start with pleasure. You know his phrases: "a horsecar cannot be handled like a rapier." "If it is 

a bad rule, that is no reason for making a bad exception to it" "All values are anticipations of the future. (Holmes surely skipped three arguments that time "All values are anticipations of the future.") "Even if enough can be squeezed from these poor and puny anonymities to turn the color of legal litmus paper.' 

And, on interpreting the Fourteenth Amendment, "The dangers of a delusive exactness." 

Could it be better said?

Few of you here tonight, proportionately speaking, are judges, concerned with the composition of beautifully succinct and witty opinions. But still, you are concerned with utterance, verbal or written, whether you are trial lawyers who must persuade a jury, or tax lawyers, or patent lawyers, or estate lawyers who must persuade the judge. I have sat in court, listening to those tax lawyers. I didn't have to understand what they said to know they were good, and to recognize that they had dug out their facts and had studied how to present their facts . . . . . In my family is a member of the species called corporation lawyer-that

7. species which Sir Edward Coke intimated had no soul-and I know they also are concerned with how to fit one piece of language to another piece on the legal litmus paper. Sometimes I have taken issue with the whereases and the therewiths that I found, therein. And I have been told summarily, therewith, that I am wrong to declare a highly technical brief to be dry. Fitting part to part, I am informed, is for the lawyer an exercise as exciting as is, for the musician, the development of a four-part fugue. 

You and I—the lawyer and the writer-have something in common. If we do not, actually, belong to the same species, at least we can be classified under one genus-I 

call it the genus, ARTICULATE MAN. Well or ill, rightly or wrongly, you and I are born to speak out, by tongue, by script or by print. Between writer and lawyer there is a sameness, a sympathy-and there is also a great and significant difference. I am not belittling my own trade, or the trade of artist-at-large, when I say that lawyers and judges have a more immediate stake than has the professional writer in what is set down on paper. I write a biography and perhaps it touches the emotions of my readers. I hope it does. Yet nothing immediate happens as a result. . . . But for all your written theory, for your rules of law, for the precedents you may make either before the judge's bench or on it, there is direct clinical application. Somebody goes to jail, somebody pays a fine, or forfeits his money, or his reputation. Somebody hangs, or is saved from hanging.

No wonder you are concerned with the King's English, when upon a word or a word's omission, such penalties or prizes may depend. 

I have reason to suspect that Holmes, when he chose his profession, had this in mind. "The place for a man who is complete in all his powers is the fight," he said. "The professor, the man of letters, gives up one-half of life that his protected talent may grow and flower in peace. But to

make up your mind at your peril upon a living question, for purposes of action, calls upon your whole nature."

Dr. Johnson said that "lawyers know life practically. 

A bookish person should always have them to converse with. 

Lawyers have what the writer wants.”

I long ago divined it: writers can learn much by talking with lawyers. Lawyer and writer are interested in the techniques of utterance, and in what lies behind utterance- 

INTENT: the motivations of man. Surely, intent lies at the base of nearly all your legal actions, whether criminal or civil. Why did Mr. Brown murder nice Mrs. Brown? Why did such and such a firm go so unseasonably bankrupt, or why did the respectable Dr. Smith default on his taxes? 

Even when we try to explain these matters by economics and the condition of the stock market, we find in the end a man or a group of men responsible. In my business, INTENT lies at the basis of the entire structure: the motivations of mankind make up the plot of every biography that is written-and for my money, of every chapter of history, though there are those who will take issue with me. 

Dr. Johnson, I am pleased to note, did not choose to apply that overworked phrase, common sense. Some men are as vainglorious about their common sense as some mothers are about their maternity. Yet Katherine Mansfield remarked that mothers of children grow on every bush, and Einstein defined common sense as that set of ineradicable prejudices we acquire before we are eighteen. 

I do not look upon either Justice Holmes or even plain John Adams as men notable especially for common sense. 

Both rendered great service to their country, and became thereby part of history. Both won a place in our American tradition. Yet neither man started out in life to achieve, consciously, any practical social reform. To me there is something inspiring in this very omission. "Have faith," said Holmes, "and pursue the unknown end."

Justice Brandeis liked to say that Holmes was an ivorytower man. He said it to me, in 1941, and we talked about it. Born in his father's library, Brandeis said of Holmes.

A man remote, a true intellectual. Yet a man who knew instinctively what he, Brandeis, confessed he had had to learn by hard climbing in the money-making end of the profession. Holmes understood what it is that men need to live by. It is good, said Brandeis, to have an ivory-tower man on the Supreme Court once in a generation. 

John Adams, too, came at his destiny not head-on but obliquely, through love of study, intellectual curiosity. Intellectual passion, I would call it. (You know who said the civilized man should be passionate as well as reasonable.) 

John Adams had the conviction that a lawyer should be a man of broad historical knowledge, possessing what we call today a sense of history. From the age of twenty, Adams spent his spare time reading legal history, studying the formation of governments. From the viewpoint of common sense, Adams was wasting his time, and he had trouble finding companions willing to share these impractical interests with him. "I know not how it is," he wrote plaintively to a friend, "but mankind have an aversion to the science of government. Is it because the subject is too dry? For me, no romance is more entertaining.”

Yet in the year 1776, when he was forty, John Adamslargely from knowledge gathered on the printed pagewas instrumental in throwing down an outworn government, and on its very substantial ruins, building our new state constitutions to their very liking and most expedient tastes.

Here then were two lawyers, Holmes and Adams, who while still undergraduates at Harvard testified there was something inviting in the very look of a printed page. Yet neither became a professional literary man. With John 

Adams it was easy to see why he chose the law as a profession. He was poor, he had to choose quickly. Living

in a county seat-Worcester, Massachusetts-population 1500, where the courthouse was the center of business, and lawyers all about him, Adams at 22 took what lay at hand. 

But one of my biggest problems as biographer of Holmes was to find out why he chose the law as a profession -and the story of my search is pertinent to our subject tonight. A son of Dr. Oliver Wendell Holmes of Boston could afford to look about him, be a physician, teach literature at Harvard or choose any of a number of other honorable professions. Wendell Holmes received little encouragement from his father-though I believe Mark Howe and I differ on this point. "A lawyer can't be a great man," Dr. 

Holmes told this son of 24, who carried three wounds from Civil War battlefields, and wondered what to do with his life. The doctor liked to quote a British barrister. "If you can eat sawdust without butter, young man, you will be a success in the law." 

Or Dr. Holmes would repeat with relish his own verses: "Come you in the law, who can talk, if you please 

Till the man in the moon will allow it's a cheese." 

I don't think Wendell Holmes was hurt by these barbs. 

I suspect he caught them in full flight and enjoyed the sport. "My father kicked me upstairs into the law," he said, much later, "and for this I am grateful.”

But there was more to it than this, and as biographer it was my business to run that more to earth if I could. 

Holmes left no record of why he chose the law. Even if he had, in diary or letter, I am not sure we could depend on it. I don't have to tell a roomful of lawyers that confessions are not always reliable. Autobiographies are not always reliable. John Adams's Autobiography certainly was not. His memory was too good. Writing in the year 1805,

Adams put into his own mouth and into his friend James 

Otis's mouth, as of 1761, words which neither man could possibly have spoken so early.

From Holmes all I had were his elderly confessions of early doubts. "One saw," he wrote, "that artists and poets shrank from the law as from an alien world. One doubted oneself how it could be worthy the interest of an intelligent mind.”

I was much impressed by this. I made a list of the books Holmes studied in Law School, and I went to the 

Biddle Law Library and looked them up. One or two were readable for the layman, like Walker's Introduction to 

American Law, which Holmes himself said he enjoyed because it gave him a sense of legal historical continuity. 

But most of the books were positively repellent, and I surmised that Holmes at 24 went at them almost as cold, as ignorant of the law, as I was. Take even the ones that were new in Holmes's day: Austin's Jurisprudence for instance. Sir Frederick Pollock, who himself could surely handle the King's English, called Austin's book an expression of "uncouth, excessive learning." "Austin's literary manner is so repulsive," Pollock said, "that even at his most accurate, it is difficult to believe what Austin says. 

That is something for my young legal correspondent to bear in mind-HUMBLY in mind, shall we say. If the literary manner be repulsive enough, the reader will not credit even factual writing at its most accurate...Magic words! . We would do well to court them, cultivate them, learn the spell no matter for what purpose we intend to use it. But, paging over these thick volumes on Pleading, the 

Law Merchant, or something called Future Interests I asked myself what it was that could pull a young man of parts and imagination through all this murk and mud. 

Holmes's young friend Henry James sat through the first year's lectures at Harvard Law School and quit in despair because he could not understand, he said, one word of what the lecturer said or what the books said. There in

the Biddle Library or at the Harvard Law School, I saw the boys at the long tables in their shirt-sleeves, bowed over a whole pile of such books. Was it fortitude that kept them at it? What beyond dismal necessity drew them on, as Holmes would say, to daylight and a worthy end?

What does the biographer do, when the record fails? 

History withholds so much! It's hard enough to obtain the mere chronology of a man's life for ninety yearswhere he was each decade, year, month, week, day, hour -let alone find out how he felt and why he chose his profession! When the record fails I know what one biographer does. She goes out and tests a dead man's actions in what might be called a living context. People react to given circumstance in ways closely related. The reason a physician recognizes the measles is that he has seen dozens of measly children. In matters of emotion, the psychiatrist has his chart of pathology or normality. Why should the biographer not assemble a chart of human behavior? So I went about, inquiring of law students why they had chosen the law as their profession. What was it they liked? What drew them to it? Some gave me practical answers: in ten years' time they would be making ten thousand a year, or they would be judges, or it was a road to politics, or a man had to do something, didn't he, to make a living?

But I was not looking for practical answers. Finally a student said to me--he's here tonight, a full-fledged member of the bar. "I'll tell you," he said "I'll tell you what holds us.

It's a respect for the written word." 

Now, you don't have to agree with him. The point is, he said it. Respect for the word, for the King's English. 

Holmes respected it. I think he was in love with it. So was 

John Adams. I'm in love with it too. And it's a love

affair that can go on forever, and always a courtship. It never outgrows the exciting stage.

Perhaps I have taken you the long way around, telling a biographer's story to prove a point we already knew when we came here tonight. But I don't see that a littlecircumlocution-is out of order in a congregation of lawyers. If lawyers love to read the written word, some of them like even better to write it. And as for the word spoken, it's axiomatic that lawyers love to talk. In the 

Biddle Library there on 34th Street, when I entered the front door and crossed the wide hallway, I used to see the students lounging on the stairs, leaning against the newel posts and talking, talking, loud and free. It made me smile when I went by, and I used to think, "One of you might easily be Wendell Holmes." When Holmes was thirty, his friend John Gray remarked, "Wendell puts a 'but' at the end of every sentence, so he can keep on without a pause.' 

As biographer, it interested me continually to see the means that my two legal heroes employed to exercise their tongues and their pens, to learn the King's English, outside of the office and the courtroom. They went at it instinctively, it seemed-but they also went at it seriously. In the 1870's, Holmes joined a discussion club. Like the

Brandeis Society it was, only much smaller. It met on 

Saturday nights. William James was a member, and

Chauncy Wright, the mathematician, and Charles Sanders Peirce, the tough-minded Harvard philosopher—the man from whom my personal detective work tells me that 

Holmes got his famous phrase, "We must think things, not words." Holmes used to stand on club nights with his arm against the mantel, waving his pipe and booming out his ideas with those 'buts' at the ends of his sentences. 

William James confessed himself irritated by what he called Wendell Holmes's "conceit", and said in a letter

that the only good Holmes's arguments did him was in giving him something to kick away from. 

But kicking away is good, too; it strengthens the muscles. Bright young men are apt to be cocksure, and young lawyers are not the least cocksure among them. 

I speak tonight to gentlemen of maturity, who are seasoned out of their youthful brashness. In this Society I take it you meet to pick up knowledge as well as to impart it. Perhaps the two exercises complement one another; the output doubles the intake, as it were. Do you remember the old lady in E. M. Forster's novel, who was rebuked for not thinking before she spoke? "How can I tell what 

I think," she said, "until I see what I say?”

Taking a little higher ground, we could quote Aristotle, who suggested that to know anything is to be able to state it. 

Justice Holmes and John Adams both ended up as masters of the King's English. They started from very different levels. Holmes was born into a group of men who talked with the tongues of angels: to his father's study came Emerson, Longfellow, Lowell, et alia. Wendell Holmes was born sophisticated, endowed at birth with literary taste. Much later he acquired humility. Taste and humility—an irresistible combination which we find occasionally in the intellectually well-bred. One would think that Wendell Holmes had no need to work over the English language. Yet there is evidence that he did work over it all his life, though he did not talk about it. 

John Adams on the other hand was forever talking about it, sweating over it even at 89. John Adams was a farmer's boy. Not even when he was President of the 

United States did he attain sophistication, let alone being born with it. John Adams was not born with any quality that makes life easy, that I can see, except a good digestion. 

Adams joined a law club too when he was young in Boston. וד

It was called Sodalitas, a Club of Friends, and its avowed purpose was twofold: to read law, and to learn to handle the mother tongue. The club was founded in 1765 by a distinguished lawyer named Jeremiah Gridley, a man 60 years old, a classical scholar, known as The Father of the 

Boston Bar. "It should be part of our plan," Gridley told the Club at its first meeting, "to improve ourselves by writing, by reading carefully the best writers, and by using ourselves to writing."

I like that phrase, so archaic and so charming-"by using ourselves to writing." Adams must have liked it too, because he copied it in his Diary. "It should be part of our plan also,” Gridley said, "to publish pieces now and then. Let us form our style upon the ancients and the best authors. I hope and expect to see at the bar," Gridley finished, "in consequence of this Sodality, a purity, an elegance, and a spirit surpassing anything that ever appeared in America."

It sounds naïve, young, unworldly, for a Jeremiah Gridley, who was making money and had won his place. 

Why should such a man have found it necessary to "use himself to the best writing."

And yet, we might ask ourselves-we of the so-knowledgeable twentieth century-are we born speaking our mother tongue better than our ancestors spoke it? I would think such an assumption a trifle naïve in our turn.

Adams worked hard over his law club meetings. He listed each book they read, from Hurd's Dialogues to Lord Kames, to Coke-Littleton, as Adams called the famous Commentaries—and thence to Blackstone, who in 1765 was very new.

For Adams, something practical came out of these literary meetings, something that spread his name and his fame. 

One night the club was discussing the European feudal system, and the talk came round to problems of

sovereignty, thence to the vitally important doctrine of allegiance to the king, a doctrine that had to be cracked wide open-by lawyers-before America could even begin to institute her own form of government. 

Adams, at thirty, was the youngest man in the Club. 

But he jumped up that night and quoted Rousseau on representative government. Now, representative government was exciting doctrine in 1765, the very stuff of the times. 

And as for Rousseau on the Social Contract, he was newer than Blackstone. Rousseau's Contrat Social had been out only a year in English (published of course in London) and 

John Adams didn't read French.

I think Adams was pleased with himself, popping up with the latest political philosophy from the continent of 

Europe. But Mr. Gridley was not impressed. Gridley slapped John Adams down. "Rousseau's argument proves him a shallow writer," Gridley remarked, and closed the discussion. 

Lawyers, young and old, are not happy sleeping on a defeated argument. Adams went home and proceeded hotly to write out his ideas under the title, An Essay on the Canon and Feudal Law. He read it to the Sodality and Gridley got it published in the Boston Gazette, the organ of the patriot party. The first thing Adams knew, his essay had become one of the most widely read of revolutionary pamphlets. It went even across the ocean, was published in London and much praised by liberals. .

There is great charm to the essay even today. Nor does it lack timeliness for us in 1950, when freedom of speech is daily threatened in our country. The essay ends with a splendid defiance, a call to literary arms. "The true source of all our suffering has been our timidity,” Adams says. "Let us dare to read, think, speak, and write. Let every order and degree among the people arouse. Let the pulpit resound. Let the bar proclaim. Let every sluice of knowledge be opened and set aflowing!"

That is simple English, but I would not call it unskilful. I could count quite a few magic words therein. "Let the bar proclaim!" John Adams said. . . . All very well, but if the bar is going to proclaim, let it watch its wording, as the rest of us must watch ours when we do our public proclaiming in book, magazine, or even in scholarly historical quarterly. 

This impudently hortatory statement stems from an experience I had, shortly after finishing the Adams biography last spring. It concerned Lawyers and the King's 

English. It took place in our City Hall and it was at the same time inspiring and disillusioning.

I went to City Hall for a biographer's reason. After 

I finish a book and it's in the press, I am irresistibly impelled to go back over my ground, test my written scenes in that-living context I mentioned earlier. It's not logical, it can't help the book. Sometimes it's disturbing. Yet the process keeps on for months, until I begin a new biography. 

Often it doesn't stop then. Perhaps all biographers follow this unreasonable schedule. I don't know.

In JOHN ADAMS AND THE AMERICAN REVOLUTION I had written three chapters about a trial scene, a courtroom scene-Adams's defense of the British soldiers indicted for murder after the so-called Boston Massacre in March of 1770.

The Boston Massacre of itself is an historical chestnut. 

Every school child knows about it. The biographer must beware of feeding to his reader a surfeit of historical chestnuts. What every school child does not know, however, is the trial scene six months later, and Adams's courageous and masterly defense of the redcoats involved. In the midst of a civil war, Adams stood up in court and in defiance of his own political party defended nine soldiers of the enemy's army of occupation-giving as his reason the plain

fact that every Englishman has the right to a fair trial in court.

I wanted to include Adams's entire brief if I could induce, persuade, seduce, bludgeon my readers into following along with me, line for line. It was not an easy assignment. John Adams talked for hours. He was at pains to teach his jury the difference between murder and manslaughter, a distinction not nearly so clear in 1770 as it is today. The five judges were not disposed to help-I deplore to say for political reasons—and Adams knew he must do it alone.

So he quoted rules of law in Latin to the jury, then translated them. He quoted Glanvil-Bracton-Sir Matthew Hale everybody down to the Marquis of Beccaria on

Crimes and Punishments, ending up with Algernon Sidney's magnificent lines:-"The law, no passion can disturb. "Tis void of fear, lust and anger. "Tis written reason, retaining some measure of the divine perfection. 

I set it all down. I was satisfied as to my facts and my continuity. But when the chapters were mailed off to 

Boston I felt an overwhelming urge to haunt the courtroom, any courtroom, refresh myself concerning men's conduct therein. Do juries react today as Adams's jury had reacted? Do judges grow weary under legal eloquence?

Do modern lawyers try the court's patience with long, literary quotations? 

These were only details. What had impressed me most about Adams's argument was the way he stuck to the law, always the law, coming back and back to it as if he had a hammer in his hand or a bellclapper. At the outset, he let his jury know he was going to argue this case on the law, not on the political prejudices of John Adams or of the 

Boston patriot party.

We hear much talk about a government of laws and not of men. Until I studied those Massacre trials, I did not know what the phrase meant. Oh, I knew it with my brain,

I suppose. But not with that other organ. Not on my pulses, as Mr. Keats used to say. Writers have to know things on their pulses.

So I put on my hat and went to the office of the Voluntary Defender, on Fifteenth Street, where witnesses were being interrogated. Then I followed Mr. Pollock across to 

City Hall, Court Room Number 453, with Judge Sloane presiding. Next day I went to Civil Court, where Judge 

Bok was sitting in a negligence case. Then I walked down

Chestnut Street, where Judge Goodrich and his brethren were hearing an appeal. Mr. John Rogge, wearing a red necktie, in impassioned accents that morning quoted Lincoln's Second Inaugural at the judges-and I mean AT. 

So I thought, "What's a quotation or two from 

Algernon Sidney, compared with this performance!" And

I gained a new appreciation of the patience, or perhaps the philosophical resignation, of Judges.

But biographically speaking, that was again a side issue. Right away, the first morning in court, I found the historical reassurance I looked for. What I am about to describe is an amateur's reaction, not to be evaluated against the day-to-day courtroom reaction of experienced professionals like yourselves. I'm not even sure I can make clear what happened. The story falls in two parts, and the disillusionment comes in Part II. 

Courtroom Number 453 has a terrific impact for one who never saw it before. It's a big, raw, shabby place where things happen: we started the day with rape and came right through to forgery and homicide. But what moved me was not the well-known human drama of the courtroom, but Judge Sloane's charge to the jury on reasonable doubt. And next day, Judge Bok's charge on the meaning of negligence.

Both judges gave the law to the jury as uncompromisingly as if they had been addressing a graduate seminar. There was no talking down; it was a real exposi-

tion. The judges took it for granted that juries can rise to a required level. And the assumption worked: up came the jury as if hoisted by spiritual wires. . . . I may say, came right up with them. As the judges expounded the law, it was not merely that thing of precedent the layman is taught to dread, an a priori business, to be accepted whether one sees sense in it or not something dry, beyond the blood and the bone. The law as I heard it expounded was based upon a shrewdly perceived, a universal psychology. It was grounded in men's motivations, upon their actions as predicted by centuries of observation. . 

A person felt fine, listening to it. . . So the life of the law really was experience, not logic. What I had heard was in the great tradition, concerned with the eternal quest of both lawyer and biographer-to discover INTENT, the motivations that lie behind the actions of men. 

And now for Part II-my other little reaction, that first day in City Hall. It was a much less high-toned reaction, and it concerned the King's English. 

My biographies had dealt with lawyers who worked hard over the language and mastered it. Therefore I walked into City Hall flushed with the pleasing fancy that every lawyer is ipso facto a master of the word. 

On entering Court Room Number 453 I was grateful to be shown to a seat inside the rail, where I could hear. 

As the clock struck the hour, the prosecuting attorney got up, walked to his place below Judge Sloane, looking confident; snappily dressed and full of legal benzedrine. He threw out an arm and shouted with fine oratorical effect, "This here unfortunate action! . . 

He was not content to say it once. After he said it to the jury, he turned dramatically to where I sat and said it again, "This here unfortunate action!" 

Well, I tried to tell myself, "What's one misplaced adverb, among so many? Maybe this slight mischance

should be laid at the door of our public school system? 

Perhaps it was an accident of heredity-a colloquialism, a provincialism. Provincialisms are good, they enrich the language."

But this was no provincialism, and I knew it. I thought, why didn't he do it up brown while he was about it, and say, "This here TYPE unfortunate action."

The lawyer was careless. He had no right to be careless. I think he had not proper respect for the first, best tool of his trade. Whatever his background or personal history, why hadn't he as good opportunity to learn to handle adverbs as had John Adams, a country boy whose father never went to college? 

I asked myself, "Why can't City Hall lawyers assemble a Sodalitas, to learn to 'use' themselves to better English?" 

And then I reminded myself that I had little right to sit up and scold. How many times had I been tripped by a misplaced adverb, or trapped by prepositions, by the proper verb to use with a collective noun, or by that ubiquitous word, WHо. (Did you see the picture in the Saturday Evening Post of the florist's messenger standing before his boss, a long cardboard box in his arms, asking solemnly, "Who shall I deliver it THOM?'')

I have been writing for publication for thirty years. (It was that long ago I sold my first piece to a magazine). 

I have had every kind of writing job, from a daily newspaper column to short fiction for the women's monthlies, to a novel, to essays, to biography. And writing never grows easier. I used to think it would. It's incredible, the way a sentence can tangle itself, even when you know exactly what you want to say. You become blind, after you have written a paragraph five or six times. Henry Fowler says I'm sure you know his two books, The King's Eng lish, and A Dictionary of Modern English Usage. 

Fowler says that when a sentence is bad, let the writer re-

cast it entirely, not try to patch it up. Patching can land you in a labyrinth worse than the subway at Times Square. 

Every profession has its specialized vocabulary,—some of it superb. I like to borrow from the physician's jargon: "The trigger stimulus that sets off the disease." "An angry wound." The law also has its beautifully descriptive phrases: "the burden of proof . . . to show the anticipatory breach. stale claims cause not the ultimate, but the proximate cause the doctrine of the last clear chance and restraint of princes ahead." perils of the sea the assured clear distance 

Wonderful phrases, for the layman's context! On the other hand, lawyers can be led into a gobbledegook to end all gobbledegook. They defend this by declaring the extra words necessary in order to leave no loophole for erroras witness the well-known phrase in a release "All claims from the beginning of the world to the date of these presents." I like that. There's majesty to it, though I am sure the clause was included for reasons of expediency, not majesty. And it's true the adversary can crawl through very small holes. Nevertheless, I ask myself if it is necessary to carry this quaint style beyond the office and the court. I have had the queerest letters from lawyers! "I shall see you on or about November 21st", one of them wrote to me. The other day I received from a judge what purported to be a pleasant personal note. This is how it opened: "Dear Mrs. Bowen: This letter is being written to you after speaking with your esteemed brother, Henry 

Drinker, who is writing to you simultaneously herewith.”

I had another letter, a week earlier, from a lawyer in the west-"on the far side of the mountains," as John Adams liked to call it. This was an entertaining letter, but it confused me by being peppered with that word, re.

Take this sentence, "One of the most important reforms mentioned in the rescript is the unification of the organization of the judical institutions and the guarantee for all the tribunals of the independence necessary for securing to all classes of the community equality before the law." 

It's a long way round. One asks oneself, was this trip necessary? In that sentence are nine prepositional phrases.

Two prepositional phrases are enough for any normal sentence to be asked to carry. I call that kind of construction a Hiccup Sentence. "One of the most important reforms mentioned in the rescript is the unification of the organizations of the judical institutions and the guarantee for all the tribunals of the independence necessary for securing. 

I won't finish; it's too painful. Fowler has a name for this kind of writing, where one finds oneself trapped by compound prepositions . . in regard to... in the case of with reference to.. Lawyers are peculiarly liable to these phrases; they catch them like whooping cough, like leprosy! Fowler puts a name to the disease; he calls it periphrasis. It's in his Dictionary, under P, and 

Fowler defines it as "the putting of things in a roundabout way. He has a satirical little paragraph about it. First, he warns us to beware of abstract nouns such as case, character, nature. For example, "The accident was caused by the dangerous nature of the spot. 99 

Why not say, "The accident occurred because the spot was dangerous?" "The abstract nouns," says Fowler, "are proof that abstract thought has occurred. Abstract thought is a mark of civilized men. And so it has come about that periphrasis and civilization are by many, held to be inseparable. These good people feel that there is an almost indecent naked-

ness, a reversion to barbarism, in saying, No news is good news, instead of, 'The absence of intelligence is an indication of satisfactory developments.'"' 

You can fall into the same error by using too many commas or by putting commas in the wrong place. How many of you saw the squib in a recent New Yorker, under the caption, "The Legal Mind at War with Itself?"

It quotes from the announcement of an essay contest conducted by the American Bar Association:-"Clearness and brevity of expression and absence of iteration or undue prolixity will be taken into favorable consideration. 

But what a relief it is, and how somehow enchanting, to come upon a sentence in a book or a whole page of sentences that go with the human respiratory tract instead of against it! Witness a letter written in the spring of the year 1776, by a short, plump, balded-headed lawyer in Philadelphia who wanted to tell a New England wife what had been taking place: 'When I consider the great events which are passed, and that I may have been instrumental in touching some Springs and turning some Small Wheels, I feel an awe upon my mind, which is not easily described. Is it not a saying of Moses, 'Who am I, that I should go in and out before this great People.'"'

That was John Adams, and one might explain the beauty of the sentences by saying they derived from deep emotion, the somewhat rare emotion of gratitude to one's 

Maker. Yet, right feeling by no means guarantees right expression. When I was young and green, I often wept real tears while banging out a sad scene on my typewriter.

And I was quite sure my readers would automatically share my emotion. I could not have been more mistaken.

In Yankee from Olympus I killed off eight major characters. I cried over seven of them. The eighth was a wit, and his last words were funny. But before even one reader would cry or laugh with me, I had to write those scenes many times, dry-eyed, conscious, and as canny as I knew how to be.. Shifting nouns to their rightful place, cutting out adjectives, deleting that tempting word, VERY, making the paragraphs of uneven length so the page would tempt the eye, then reading my sentences aloud to see if they contained hiccups. 66 In the world of artistic criticism, much nonsense is talked about rhythm. Yet it would be interesting to discover, from the standpoint of physics or physiology, just why a rhythmical paragraph moves us to tears, while a jerky one only irritates. 

Through our great good fortune"-a lawyer wrote this, and you will recognize it— 'through our great good fortune, in our youth our hearts were touched with fire. It was given us at the outset to learn that life is a profound and passionate thing. While we are permitted to scorn nothing but indifference, and do not pretend to undervalue the worldly rewards of ambition, we have seen with our own eyes, beyond and above the gold fields, the snowy heights of honor, and it is for us to bear the report to those who come after. But above all we have learned, that whether a man accepts from Fortune her spade and will look downward and dig, or from Aspiration her axe and cord and will scale the ice, the one and only success which is his to achieve is to bring to his work a mighty heart.”

But, wisecracks aside-if I write long-winded history I lose my readers. I'm not sure what you lose if you write dull, obscure, long-winded briefs. A skilful trial lawyer told me the other day that in preparing an argument his chief objective is to keep the judge awake. I maintain that even one periphrastic sentence can anesthetize a reader as surely as a speaker anesthetizes when he prefaces his remarks with an er, or an uh, or a stage cough. I sometimes think that writers have not sufficient respect for readers. 

The writer should be afraid of his readers, terrified of losing them. As a biographer I have to be especially conscious of this, because my books are long and filled with fact. How can I slip those facts into the narrative? How can I persuade my reader not to skip them? I have a motto, it hangs over my desk. It says, Will the reader turn the page?

Sometimes the motto seems so awful to me that I take it down and hide it until I am safely across whatever technical literary chasm confronts me at the moment.

Lawyers tell me that precision is a quality they value in their professional writing and speaking. Precision is not lightly achieved. It means economy in the use of materials. 

It means making a choice, the rejection of one word for another word. Choice-and-rejection is the touchstone of any art. "How beautiful, how satisfying," said Benedette

Croce, "is the art of being observant, economical and sincere. 

---

Economy is a banner under which you and I might operate. By economy I do not mean the time-honored adjuration to be brief, be brief. On the contrary, I have seen many instances where over-compression obscures the meaning. Philosophical subjects, legal subjects if you will, often need length, need to be built up slowly, in detail, before their essence will emerge. I found this to be true even in writing straight narrative biography. For my Boston 

Massacre trials, I had originally one chapter, not three.

It didn't work out. The drama emerged as colorful enough; it's hard to keep dramatic effect out of the account of any murder trial. But something more important was smothered by compression:-the intellectual and philosophical implications of Adams's argument in court, which was the whole reason for the presentation.

So I lengthened one chapter to three and deleted ten thousand words elsewhere-cut out, for one thing, the story of Jeremiah Gridley's Sodality which I told tonight. Better to omit something the reader does not know about, even if it's important, than to tell him something halfway, so he doesn't comprehend it, doesn't feel it, experience it-on his pulses.

That's why I am opposed—BELLICOSELY opposed-to literary digests of all varieties. Any author worth his salt will have made every possible cut in his story before he sends it to the publisher. What remains should be intrinsic to the narrative as flesh is to bone. Only after a person dies is he reduced to skeletal proportions. 

Justice Holmes said, "The purpose of an art is to touch the hair-trigger of an emotion." Gertrude Stein once gave a talk before-of all audiences-the boys of Choate School. "It is the business of an artist," she told them, "to be exciting."

It is the business of the biographer to be exciting. A young history student came to see me last year. The ink was not yet dry on his doctoral thesis. He said, "Now that

I have my training, I'm going to write and make money. 

I'm not going to teach. I plan to take a period in history, and hop it up, the way you do, Mrs. Bowen." 

Hopping up is done with morphine, marihuana. But we biographers don't hop up history!-I told the young man. Does one hop up the Rocky Mountains, or an hibiscus flower? On the contrary. We ourselves are moved by the astonishment of height, the very redness of red. We desire to reproduce this astonishment, this good new news granted by earth or sky-or man. Reading the letters of John 

Adams, I had myself been hopped up and I burned to share the experience. 

I don't believe a good lawyer hops up his cases. I think he extracts the essence of each one, isolates the point at issue that place, as Holmes put it, where the boy got his finger caught in the machinery. After he finds it he brings it forward, pounds at it until it yells aloud with its own intrinsic accents and is perforce heard. To achieve this effect takes skill in analysis and art in expression. 

Now I have done. Perhaps I have taken too high a tone tonight about lawyers and the King's English-about the similarity, the essential sympathy between your profession and mine. It's always easy to take a high tone about the other fellow's business. 

As Justice Holmes said, "Every calling is great, when greatly pursued."
