CHARLES CHURCHILL AND A FRAGMENT OF AN EPIC POEM By L. H. BUTTERFIELD 

IN THE Works of John Hall-Stevenson,¹ which were published ten years after the death of this madcap friend of Laurence Sterne, occurs A Fragment of an Epic Poem. Book IV, nearly four hundred lines long, which, it seems highly probable, is the work of Charles Churchill. The repute of the satirist Churchill in his own day is comparable only to the neglect into which he fell soon after his death, and in which he has ever since remained. The facts explaining the presence in Hall-Stevenson's Works of this poem hitherto unnoticed by scholars are curious in themselves, and throw a little new light on Churchill and his associates.2

John Hall-Stevenson, who met Sterne at Cambridge and became his life-long friend, is known as the "Eugenius" of Tristram Shandy and A Sentimental Journey and the "Cosin Antony" of Sternes letters.

It is commonly accepted that the eccentric genius of Sterne was greatly stimulated in the congenial atmosphere of Crazy Castle, the country seat of Hall-Stevenson which lay a short journey north of Shandy Hall in Yorkshire. Hall-Stevenson, who loved above all other things the companionship of fellow-spirits, was a man of some intellectual attainments, but an incurable dilletante in everything to which he turned his hand. He followed the fashion in his delight in the accepted social diversions and vices of the time, in his penchant for writing facetious verses, and in his dabbling in the political controversies of the day. 

It is with this last interest that we are concerned, for through it he became associated with Churchill.

The key to his political sympathies is his attachment to the cause of the demagogue John Wilkes. That amazing man, who was regarded by the populace as a patriot martyr and by George III as a devil incarnate, kept the Government in almost constant turmoil during the first decade of George III's reign. The young king, with the aid of his favorite, John Stuart, Earl of Bute, had arduously built up a strong political organization, the "King's Friends," which favored the extension of royal prerogative and the extinction of party divisions. 

The resignation of William Pitt the Elder had been brought about within a year of George's accession, and the Whig opposition, weakened by successive desertions, dwindled rapidly into near insignificance. 

George had early learned the lesson that the majority will go where the favors are being distributed, and, having taken personal charge of the Civil List and the other sources of royal patronage, he soon destroyed the ascendency of the great Whig families who had had so large a measure of power since the Revolution.¹ John Wilkes, however, a born political adventurer, refused to compromise with Bute's government and struck a blow in the name of patriotism for his own political fortunes. His astounding success must have surprised even himself. His incendiary propaganda both in the anti-ministerial periodical which he founded and wrote, the North Briton, and in the course of his trial for writing it, was so successful that he virtually transformed the struggle from one between the King's Friends and the Whig opposition to one between the King's Friends and the populace.2 Churchill had already become a satellite of the demagogue, having been Wilkes's right-hand man on the North Briton. Now Hall-Stevenson joined the cry of "Wilkes and Liberty!" Early in 1763 he published A Pastoral

Cordial, and followed it in 1764 with A Pastoral Puke, two tedious satires which attack the leaders of the Government and the Opposition alike: Bute for his unfitness to govern, and Newcastle and his fellows for their desertion of Wilkes and general pusillanimity.

But leaving until later conjectures as to how Hall-Stevenson obtained the poem by Churchill which appeared in his posthumous works, let us examine the circumstances of its publication and the poem itself.

In 1776 Hall-Stevenson published a belated reply to Dr. Johnson's 

Taxation no Tyranny entitled An Essay upon the King's Friends. It is a piece of no consequence, not properly a reply at all, but a facetious squib in the learned-ridiculous style which was Hall-Stevenson's pale reflection of Mr. Tristram Shandy's inimitable manner of expression.2 

The author ironically compliments Dr. Johnson on his accomplishments - philological, literary, and political. Johnson, if he saw the piece at all, undoubtedly found it beneath contempt; and the spectacle of Hall-Stevenson's labored nonsense directed against the literary dictator of the day is somewhat like that of Aesop's fly, who rested on an axle of the chariot and exclaimed, "What a dust I do raise!" But the squib is of interest because Hall-Stevenson interpolated in it ninety lines of the satire we have under consideration. He thus introduces them:

I cannot leave you in the Elysian fields, without informing you of an anecdote to which I am confident you are a stranger. The poniard of that assassin, Churchill, luckily gone to his own place, before he could put his malice in execution, was raised to strike the whole party in a mock heroic poem. By the specimen I give you, no matter how I came by it, you will see the irreparable loss they have suffered by his removal to a cure of souls in another kingdom. His hero, our incomparable L[or]d C[hief] J[ustice], is admitted into hell, and amongst his numberless friends and acquaintance there, he recognizes the last princes, and all the eminent partizans, of the house of Stewart. There is a great deal more of it, but this will be sufficient for one dose.

These specimens corroborate Hall-Stevenson's statement that the poem was written by Churchill. Though the former was definitely in sympathy with the point of the satire, he was incapable of writing verse as effective as this. Hall-Stevenson rarely used couplets — never once in a political satire. When he did use them he never achieved the vigorous and telling effects characteristic of Churchill which this poem contains. Moreover, he was incapable of carrying out any sustained design, his longer satires, and usually his shorter pieces as well, being nothing but collections of puns and digressions. His mind worked much in the fashion of Sterne's, which Hall-Stevenson himself has aptly described as "Going to work Just like a bottle-skrew upon a cork." 2 Anyone who has thanklessly waded through the tiresome pages of A Pastoral Cordial and A Pastoral Puke may perceive immediately that the Fragment is not by him.

At any rate, there is no good reason, in my opinion, to impugn Hall- Stevenson's attribution of the piece to Churchill. There is no motive for his concealing his own authorship; he would have been only too proud, I daresay, to claim such an effective satire as his own if he had not been certain that it could not pass as his. I think we may dismiss the possibility of its being by any other author, for there could have been no conceivable reason for Hall-Stevenson's wishing to mystify Dr. Johnson or anyone else by declaring another satirist's work to be Churchill's.

A brief examination of Churchill's political sympathies and a few parallel passages from works known to be his will show that the subjects of attack in the Fragment were favorite butts of his in his known works.3

The bludgeon-like satire, the rugged couplets, the whole conception — all are characteristic. Here were the baleful caverns of the law. 

Blue lightnings issued forth, and from within His ears were harrow'd with terrific din, Chains, lashes, creaking wheels, and crackling bones, Yells, horrid shrieks, and everlasting groans. 

Before the entrance two grim monsters lay, With many monster cubs, in snarling play,

Chicane the dam, and Rapine was their sire, Their mouths foam'd black, their eyes were balls of fire.¹

The considerations here presented, in my opinion, allow us to assume beyond reasonable doubt that the Fragment published in Hall-Stevenson's Works is by Charles Churchill. If this is so, it is not hard to conjecture how it came into Hall-Stevenson's possession. Churchill crowded into the last year of his life a tremendous amount of work. A good half, at least, of his poetic output belongs to the period between 

November, 1763, and November, 1764.2 It was in this productive year, very likely following the conviction of the absent Wilkes by Mansfield in February, that the Fragment was written. Late in October, 1764,

Churchill set out to meet Wilkes in France. The friends celebrated their reunion in Boulogne in proper fashion, but on October 29th 

in this mock epic. The conception is complete as the poem stands, the only parts lacking being the lacunae at the very beginning and these are merely omissions of descriptive matter. That Churchill had in mind no more extensive a design is indicated by the fact that the whole action takes place, as we discover at the end, in "a feverish dream." The Fragment is called "Book IV" quite arbitrarily; the specific number was perhaps supplied by Hall-Stevenson. The latter is certainly responsible for the characteristically facetious footnotes. No one but him could have written a note identifying David Hume as follows: "An Epicurean Philosopher of the North, in whom were united the principles of two sects, seemingly opposite a Pyrrhonist in Revelation; a Dogmatist in Faction, who does not believe God's word, but will take a Tory's word for any thing." (Works, II, 150.) These facetious notes do not occur after 1. 90, and were for the benefit of Dr. Johnson.

Churchill came down with typhus fever. Five days later he was dead.¹

The satirist had declared earlier that when he died he would leave behind no poetry in manuscript:

We are further told that "Churchill, before his death, destroyed all his manuscripts, excepting the Dedication to his Sermons, and the Journey." He may have destroyed all the manuscripts he had with him in France, but is it not possible that some were left in England? 

Churchill may very likely have left the manuscript of the nearly finished mock heroic verses with Hall-Stevenson before he went abroad.

Hall-Stevenson was in London for the winter season in 1763, and probably stayed on until May. During the conclusion of the Parliamentary war over Wilkes and during Mansfield's hearing of the case, then, Hall-Stevenson and Churchill were in London working in the same cause.
