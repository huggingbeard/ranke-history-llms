STATE OF NEW YORK EXECUTIVE CHAMBER ALBANY, February 17, 1956 To the Legislature: 

Among the many consequences of the tension and unrest of the post war world none is more intrinsically terrible than the steady rise in drug addition among the youth of our nation. Four years ago your Honorable Bodies took action to increase the penalties for the illegal sale or possession of narcotic drugs, since then our police have acted with vigor and our schools and churches have spoken out with growing urgency and alarm. And yet in spite of these efforts the drug traffic has continued to spread, the number of addicts has continued to grow. Narcotic arrests in New York 

City alone have risen 600 per cent in the past decade; arrests of persons under 21 have increased 2300 per cent. 

There can be no reasonable doubt as to why our efforts have failed; they have failed because we have not stopped the smuggling of drugs into our country, because we have not hit hard enough at the drug peddlers, and because we have not developed a dependable cure for drug addition.

To date the State has done little or nothing to help in the development of a comprehensive rehabilitation program for addicts with adequate follow-up services. The present situation is tragic. 

As many as six out of seven addicts released after special treatment in special hospital wards become addicted again in a short space of time.

The seventh lives in constant peril of the same fate. As a result, the problem tends not only to perpetuate itself, but constantly to expand, because of the number of addicts who turn to dope peddling in order to obtain the $10 to $20 a day the average addict requires for his own supplies. This is the central problem of drug addiction: one addict recruits another. The number grows and has been growing-by geometrical progression. 

The obstacles in the way of a lasting cure are formidable. It has proved possible to "withdraw" hospitalized addicts from the influence of drugs and release them as "cured", but it is far more difficult to withdraw them from the influences which originally led to addiction.

Our experts in public health and mental hygiene are generally agreed that most cases of drug addiction arise from basic personal maladjustments. Yet no State government has made an intensive effort to find a method of rehabilitating the addict based on this. fundamental fact.

The Departments of Health and Mental Hygiene have accordingly recommended to me the establishment of an experimental treatment center in New York City, which will initiate a fresh approach to the problem of drug addiction. The object of the research at these centers will be discover the social and psychological factors which combine to produce drug addition in the first [3] 95161

instance and perpetuate it afterwards. The center will be attached to a teaching hospital and will provide facilities for treating 120 patients over a year's time. The patients will be selected from all age groups and social classes, with emphasis on young adults. The group will include, but will not be limited, to parolees and addicts with extensive histories of cures and relapses. 

Each patient at the outset of treatment will be hospitalized for a period of approximately one month during which he will undergo intensive psychiatric observation and care along with the usual withdrawal therapy. On the basis of the information built up during this period a rehabilitation program will be worked out for each individual patient. Following discharge the patient will report back regularly to the treatment center and his progress will be closely followed by the staff over a period of several years. In this way it should be possible to discover patterns of drug addiction and successful methods of treating it which can then be applied on a much larger scale with the object of reaching a more effective approach to the problem.

This project will be the first of its kind in the United States. In a way which has never been attempted, it will combine the psychiatric and bio-chemical treatment of mental illness with the skills of the medical epidemiologist, the psychologist and the social worker to attack a problem which has never been solved. In my supplemental budget I will recommend appropriation of approximately $160,000 for the first year of operation. Although the cost per patient will be considerable, it is in fact negligible compared to the estimated $40,000,000 which is spent each year for the illegal purchase of narcotics in New York State-$40,000,000 which for the most part is raised through criminal acts of the most savage and desperate kind.

In my Budget I have already requested funds so that the Division of Parole may assign specially trained parole officers to work intensively with addicts released on parole from State correctional institutions. In New York City these parole officers will of course work closely with the professional personal engaged in the hospitalization, out-patient treatment and follow-up program. 

The object of the two programs which I have outlined is to develop a technique for a lasting cure of drug addiction once it has taken place. It is equally important that we seek to prevent those susceptible to addition from being exposed to it. 

To do this we must first control the illegal smuggling of drugs into our country. This task is immensely complicated by the fact that opium has become a weapon in the cold war. The smuggling of narcotics into the United States has become a deliberate, organized and increasingly successful policy of World Communism. 

In the face of this fact, acknowledged and documented by the Federal officials who are responsible for controlling the traffic, the Federal Government has in recent years steadily decreased the number of port patrol officers assigned to the Port of New York. 

I am urging the Administration and the Congress to make funds available so that the Bureau of Customs force, including especially

the force in New York Harbor, may be restored to its full strength, and I ask your support in that regard. 

The State, on its part, must intensify its effort to stamp out the evil traffic in drugs within our borders. I recommend that legislation be enacted that will sharply increase the penalties for the unlawful sale or possession of narcotics. I urge that local police authorities step up their efforts to apprehend such persons and bring them before the courts where these penalties can be imposed. 

The education of our youth to the physical and moral dangers of drug addiction must be projected on the broadest possible scale. 

Our educational system in every area should be encouraged to apply itself to this task, to the end that the young people of our State become fully aware of the true horror and evil of drug addiction. (Signed)

AVERELL HARRIMAN
