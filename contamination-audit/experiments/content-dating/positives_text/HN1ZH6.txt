ONE UNFAITHFUL YEAR By PAUL GAILLARD 

Mona, trapped in a loveless marriage, is suddenly stirred into a quest for satisfaction by a talk with an old doctor, Roger Wayne. In her search for love, she stumbles into several rather shoddy affairs which bring her more excitement than contentment. Finally, she meets Daniel Farr, an engineer of mature years, and in his love finds the combination of deep satisfaction and thrill which promises to bring her a full and happy life.

ONE UNFAITHFUL YEAR

MONA A GRANGE lay pondering the increasingly patent fact that pleasure was but an elusive component of marriage, and happiness a maiden's dream whose fulfillment was not guaranteed by the two dollar fee paid for an official marriage license.

It was Thursday, but, Mona reflected, as she lay looking up at the ceiling, that it may as well have been any other day of the week. All her days were alike. Her nights, too, she reminded herself, a shade viciously. She slipped her arms from beneath the daffodil yellow satin eiderdown and locked them behind her disheveled, though still shining brown hair.

Across the few feet that separated her from the bed that was twin to her own, Mona could see a daffodil colored mound that was like a tent pitched on the sands. Her husband's spreading and luxurious back was towards her, and his thin hair in moistened strands made her think of a rain-drenched rooster's plumage. She felt unpleasantly critical, but as he continued to breathe heavily, no less aware of her displeasure now than when he was awake, Mona frowned. 

As he moved in the first stirrings of consciousness, 1

Mona looked away, and slipping the pale yellow sheets still farther away from her, let her small, well formed hands glide down her neck and come to rest on her velvet-smooth breasts. Her French crêpe nightgown, so filmy and loaded with lace that it might have been worn by a bride, scarcely concealed them. But what did it matter! Jonathon never noticed lingerie -Jonathon didn't even seem to know that her body was beautiful. Certainly he never mentioned it.

Jonathon was as punctually uxorious as he was punctually business-like. They did not need an alarm clock-she knew perfectly well that presently he would raise himself on his elbow and say huskily, "Time to get up, old girl!" Almost before the thought had formed itself in her mind, Jonathon raised himself up on his elbow and exhaled deeply. "Awake, Mona?" he inquired sleepily. "Time to get up." He slipped out of bed and groped for his leather slippers, hitching his pyjamas higher around his waist. "Jonathon!" Mona said softly with a sudden hunger for admiration and with an undercurrent of irritation that so much loveliness as she represented was going unappreciated. "Jonathon!" "Huh?" Jonathon was shuffling towards his shower.

Mona smiled up at him invitationally and patted the satin covered eiderdown at the edge of the bed. "What's the matter? Don't you feel well?" Jonathon did not sit down, however-it was past time for his shower.

"Yes-no-oh, I don't know what's the matter." "Get up and have some breakfast. You'll feel better.” He reached down and grabbed at her leg, clutching it just below the knee through the soft padding of the eiderdown, and shaking it like a dog nuzzling a bone still embedded in meat. Then he clumped on to the bathroom. Mona watched the purple and green striped pyjamas disappear and her lips trembled. What was the use of being fairly young and goodlooking, and rich—what did it get her? Life was certainly not very thrilling nor interesting. She was sure any little streetwalker had a more exciting life. In disgust she kicked out at the bed clothes and unconsciously shoved them halfway down the bed. 

Presently the noise of the shower ceased and Jonathon came in, his pyjamas changed for green and purple striped shorts. Idly she wondered why Jonathon always stuck to green and purple stripes. His bathrobe hung from his shoulder like side drapes at a club window. 

Quite unexpectedly he plumped himself down on the side of her bed and she became conscious of her dainty lingerie only half covering her exquisite body. 

She looked up at Jonathon. "Jonathon, I had the strangest dream last night. 

I dreamed that I was walking through miles and miles of marble statuary-they were so creamy white and so beautiful. There were athletes and young boys like the ephebi on a Greek vase." Her voice was

dreamy and brooding, her lips suddenly moist and mobile. She became conscious that Jonathon was looking down at her body barely concealed by the sheer silk. She waited hopefully. "You'll catch cold, Mona, with the flimsy rags you wear." Reaching down, he drew up the sheets until they covered her bosom. "Maybe your dream means you're going out to the Metropolitan to kill time today. Well, get up, old girl, and have some breakfast with me. I like to see my wife across the table from me in the morning." "But I wonder what my dream meant?”

Jonathon got to his feet and dropped his bathrobe to the floor. "Oh, dreams don't mean anything. Guess you ate something that didn't agree with you."

With suppressed irritation, Mona threw back the bedclothes and reached for her saffron-colored velvet negligee.

INNER was unusually good and under the influence of the food, the candlelight, and the flowers, Mona's spirits rose as rapidly as the mercury in a thermometer on a midsummer day. Jonathon seemed less plump and certainly less careless about his appearance than usual. He had a sleek, well groomed look that always indicated that he had spent an hour or two at the barber-shop.

Mona glanced at his hands, usually tipped by ragged nails. The trim nails showed a polish that outshone her own. She laughed, but it was pleased laughter.

Jonathon, across the linen-covered table, was more like the Jonathon who had asked her to marry him one night in a sleepy southern town. She remembered, that when he had spoken, she had been silent for a moment, dazed by her good fortune. Until she was married she had never been able to afford the expensive accessories to a woman's beauty, and her charm had been undiscernable to the village youths who could not discover charm when clothed in ugly and badly cut gowns. No-she was not in Hebron-suddenly she was transplanted to the stage itself. They were on a mountainside and he, the hero, was making her understand the meaning of life for her-he was all that gave life any meaning.

IN the weeks of pleasant inactivity that preceded early winter, Mona tried to fill her time with lectures and her mind with stored bits of knowledge that she thought the lecturers would have to offer. It might help her to understand this increasingly puzzling thing called life. Never before had she thought of life as anything but simple and very uncomplex, but in the face of the varied facets that it had lately presented to her, she felt more than a little bewildered. But the lectures bored her, and she usually left the lecture hall with only a faint recollection of second-hand platitudes, delivered in a forceful and aggressive manner. It was as though the man on the rostrum would atone for the weakness of his convictions by the strength of his oratory. 

Now and then, Mona was aware of a note of insincerity that ran through the talks and she concluded that lectures were merely a stupid way of earning a living. On rare occasions there were stray bits of wisdom garnered from experience, but for the most part the lecturer's statements reeked of bored and boring repetitions. She finally concluded that lectures were not for her, and eventually came to the conclusion that since she knew clothes better than she knew anything 208

else, she would take up dress designing. It was not alone that she felt she must have an occupation, but she wanted to be financially independent. She determined to again demand that much of Jonathon: that he permit her to take a course in costume designing and if he refused, as he persistently had up to now, she would find some way of managing, even if she had to get a job as saleswoman in a dress shop. 

She felt certain that at last she was beginning to be master of herself and believed that life could never again bring her anything that she could not endure --that she could not cope with. 

From time to time she saw Rutger Wayne, but he lately had shown signs of lassitude due to encroaching years and he would fall into long silences. She often wondered whether his silence was filled with memories of his colorful past, or speculations about his imminent future. Though he would talk if she urged him, he much preferred to take one of the old hacks that still remained at the Fifty-ninth Street entrance to Central Park and drive with her slowly along the winding roads, now and then holding her hand tightly. Perhaps she would not even wait until Daniel Farr went on his trip. The more she thought of it, the more the idea appealed to her. Perhaps that, more than anything else, would make Jonathon realize she was in earnest in her determination to be free.

The maid brought the afternoon paper. At first wrapped in her thoughts, she ignored it, but from habit she finally picked it up. On the second page she saw an article that pleased her. Briefly it told that Daniel Farr was shortly accompanying a group of capitalists on a trip to Seattle, Washington, to consider the possibilities of a power plant project in hitherto unimproved territory. The article listed several notable achievements of Daniel Farr and Mona's eyes glistened with pride. Daniel Farr was her man. 

She had loved him from the very first and now a proprietary pride was added to her love. The future was wreathed in rose-colored veils.

MONA'S position was intolerable. She found Jonathon's surly silence even more oppressive than she had feared it would be and anxiously waited to hear from Daniel Farr. The only word she had had from him was a telegram saying that they had decided to go West at once and that he would see her as soon as he returned. Everything was so vague and indefinite. She wanted the comfort of Daniel's arms about her-the consolation of his kisses. Though she knew it was just a question of time before they would be together again, she found the waiting almost unendurable.

Impulsively she picked up the receiver and dialed his number. There was no answer and she slammed the receiver back in place. She might have known he would not have waited for her to call him, had he returned. Dressing hurriedly she determined to delay no longer, but take her savings and rent a room as she had planned. She made a list of possible east side hotels and began her search for a temporary shelter. 

She had looked at several without coming to a decision, though one in the east sixties had seemed quite possible, when she had an uncontrolled impulse to go to Rutger Wayne's. She wanted to tell 237

him of the new turn in her affairs-the new road she was about to travel-and make him understand that she was grateful to him for his never-failing understanding. Also, she confessed to herself, she wanted his approval of the step she was about to take. 

Her sudden realization of her need for Rutger 

Wayne hastened her steps and almost before she realized it, she was turning into his block. Before his house she halted, one foot already on the step, and stared incredulously. A heavy bunch of flowers tied with a purple satin ribbon hung at his door. “Dead,” she said under her breath, "dead!" She could scarcely believe it-and she could not bring herself to enter. 

What was alive of Rutger Wayne would always be with her-and she did not care to see the shell. 

On her slow way back to the apartment she reproached herself for having been so absorbed in her own affairs, and latterly, with Daniel Farr, that she had neglected Rutger Wayne. She reproached herself, too, that she had not been with him at the end to hold his hand. Her best friend-indeed her only friend-had died alone. He had been truly her best friend, in that he had brought Daniel Farr into her life, and she sensed that he had not planned their meeting idly.

For Rutger Wayne death was the consoler-for her and for Daniel Farr, the future was to be the consoler, for she knew that she could compensate Daniel for much of his former loneliness and she knew that

he could bring her the happiness she had never known.

Back in the apartment Mona allowed her hitherto restrained tears to fall, and her tears were as much for herself as for the passing of Rutger Wayne. 

She had her trunk and bags packed by the time she heard Jonathon's key in the door, and she braced herself for the inevitable reproaches she knew would follow her announcement that she was taking a small place alone. But he did not give her a chance to tell him. He was looking at her with such acute dislike, combined with cold cruelty, that Mona wondered what barrier he might try to erect to block her way. 

Settling himself heavily in a chair, he said harshly, "I forgot to tell you, Mona, that your new loveryour newest lover-came to my office to see me. 

Helluva nerve he has, if you ask me. He wants me to divorce you so that he can marry you."

He waited to see what effect this statement would have on Mona. 

She said quietly, “Yes, I knew he was going to see you. I told you I loved Daniel Farr and that I intended to marry him.” "Not just yet, you won't. You forget that you already have a husband and I certainly made that solemn looking ass understand that I had no intention of stepping aside just to be accommodating.”

His voice was high pitched with mockery. “You'd better not be too sure about that bird anyway, Mona. 

You may not see so much of him now."

Mona was startled and showed it. 

Jonathon got to his feet. "I told him what sort of a wife you were likely to be if he did marry you. After all, I owed him that much," he said virtuously. "Men have to stick together. A woman nowadays don't care anything about a man except to have him make love to her and give her money.” "Jonathon! What did you tell Daniel Farr?"

Mona's face was burning with restrained anger, and a tiny fear, quickly suppressed flared up in her heart. 

She was sure of Daniel. She had been honest with him. The knowledge calmed her. "It doesn't really matter, Jonathon, what you told him. I have made up my mind that if you refuse to divorce me, I shall just live with him openly as though we were married." "If he doesn't change his mind.” "What do you mean?” "I mean I told him how you had been spending a lot of afternoons in amusements you forgot to tell your husband about, and that he needn't think you'd stop doing it even if the name of your husband was changed from Grange to Farr. Don't think I've been a blind fool. I know a lot of things I haven't let you know I was wise to." 

Mona shrugged her shoulders. "You couldn't have

told Daniel Farr anything he didn't already know. I told him-you had legal grounds for divorce." "Yeah? But I bet you prettied it up so it wouldn't register so plain. Well, I didn't pretty it. I called a spade a spade and his face showed that he was sore as hell even if he didn't say anything. I didn't let you know that I was wise to what you'd been up to when you came home one night with bruises the size of pork chops all over you. I knew you had been pretty badly handled by some man but I kept my mouth shut."

Mona started angrily for the door, furious and disgusted. "Well, I wish you'd keep your mouth shut now." "I certainly won't, and if you ever try to divorce me, what a stink I'll raise! I'll give the tabloids some snappy dope." "Nothing you could say or do would make the least difference to me or to Daniel Farr." 

He threw a sentence at her as she neared the door, as skillfully as a lasso is thrown toward an escaping animal. “How about Rutger Wayne?” He tossed the paper he had been holding into a chair. "I let him know what had been going on here, too. Not that he counts any more. I don't suppose you know the old boy kicked in last night?” 

Mona did not answer him. "I wonder if he left you any money? He certainly owed you that much."

Mona took a few steps toward Jonathon. "Jonathon, I don't know why I even talk to youor let you talk to me. I used to think you were a kind sort of person, even if a bit rough on the surface. Now 

I understand that the kindness itself goes no deeper than the surface. I have the greatest contempt for you, and I'm leaving this apartment at once. I never want to see you again."

She went swiftly from the room with Jonathon's angry voice following her. "The trouble with you is, you've had it too easy. You should have had half a dozen kids to keep you busy."

Mona went into the spare room and gathered her bags together, thrust the few remaining toilet articles into an overnight bag and left the apartment. 

In the small room she had selected at a hotel in 

East Sixty-fourth Street, she breathed deeply, with a sense of freedom she had not known in years. She determined to try again and find some sort of work that would assure her an income, even if small. Never again, she hoped, would she have to depend upon any man, not even Daniel Farr, for money.

It was not until the week was almost over, that she knew it would not be necessary for her to find a job. 

A letter from a prominent lawyer informed her that Rutger Wayne had left his home on Madison Avenue and his comfortable income to her.

DANIEL ANIEL FARR'S re turn to New York seemed unbearably delayed. A brief telegram from time to time was all that Mona received, though she had sent him an air mail letter giving him her new address and telling him of Rutger Wayne's death. She felt so alone that she could not have borne it if she had not been able to go out on little orgies of shopping. She bought extravagantly, telling herself that this was to be her trousseau, and every gown she selected, every bit of lingerie she purchased, was bought with the hope that Daniel 

Farr would like it. 

Impatiently she waited for Daniel's return, and when at last she received his wire, telling her the hour of his arrival, she feverishly arranged a surprise for him. Flowers and candles converted her small living-room into a bower, and she ordered a carefully planned dinner to be served in her new apartment.

When Daniel Farr arrived, and Mona felt his arms around her, his kisses on her mouth, her eyes, her throat, she was delirious with happiness. He seemed more boyish and gay than she had ever seen him, 243

and from time to time, he would interrupt their most serious conversations to draw her from her chair into his arms-to his knees, as though his last reserve had broken down. "Daniel-when you go to Seattle, I'm going with you. At least as far as Reno. It will be near enough so that we can be together part of the time while I am getting my divorce." 

He kissed her warmly and said: "It would be perfect except for the sad fact that I am not going to Seattle. They have decided not to go ahead with that project. That is why we were gone so long. Instead of Seattle, I am going on a job in Mexico." "Mexico!" Mona's eyes opened wide. "But that's so far away." 

He was silent for a moment. "It is farther," he said finally. "But I'm glad I'm going to be there. I'd rather live in Mexico than any other spot on earth 

We'll be together, my dear, at last, under a tropic moon." "Well," Mona said, "Reno is out. It would be too far away from you. How about Tia Juana? Why can't 

I get a divorce there? All the movie stars get their divorces there." "Why you can- I can arrange for you to get one in twenty-four hours.” "Perfect! When do we start?" "Within a week, darling. But I have a great deal to do before we go. We'll go by plane, of course."

Before the week was out they were on their way. 

Mona was impatient to reach Tia Juana and even their swift flight seemed none too swift for her.

She was secretly disappointed with her first view of Tia Juana-it was not so beautiful as she had thought Mexico would be, from the stories Daniel Farr had told her of the exotic shrubbery and flowers -the turquoise skies. But, she reminded herself, they would only be in Tia Juana twenty-four hours.

It was a low-built, tinsel town, of white-washed and remodeled adobe huts, in which Americans choose to live, and more pretentious haciendas occupied by the Spanish inhabitants. Mona had to admit that the town seemed more alive than any place she had ever seen, and told herself that what it lacked in beauty, it made up in color. It was the first taste of the exotic-the exciting life that she would have with 

Daniel Farr.

Indians with impassive faces and dirty blankets glided by and peons sauntered along lazily, as conscious of observation by strangers, as any Broadway actress leaving the stage door. 

Tired and dazzled by the ever shifting tide of color, Mona at last was willing to admit she was hungry and they went back to their hotel to dine. Daniel had taken a room for her adjoining his own and though she had been surprised, she said nothing. Twentyfour hours would soon pass.

Daniel suggested the gambling halls. She had never

seen a gambling room in her life and thrilled in anticipation. Before the door of the first gambling house they entered, a half-Comanche Indian, with utterly impassive face lounged, and just ahead of them a trio of sleek-haired Spaniards were hurrying in to try their luck. Men and women with hot, feverish eyes drove up in luxurious cars with California license plates. Daniel told her that the people of San Diego drove over daily for the gambling and racing. Inside the Casino, men and women, with tense gaze, crowded around the tables, as absorbed as though it were a matter of life and death. The old men and women were haggard and anxious-the younger ones greedy and rapacious. A dissipated young American, with a cigarette hanging from his mouth, rose from the table as Mona watched him and swaggered from the room. He had lost. The others hardly looked at him before they went back to their game.

Fortunes went up and down amid the incessant rattle of chips. 

Mona and Daniel went from one gambling place to another, scarcely realizing the passage of time. Though it was late, the streets were as crowded as though it were early evening. Tia Juana seemed not to need sleep. 

Mona thrilled under a suspicion of danger when she heard, as they passed, a muttered sentence in which the word "gringo" figured prominently. She suspected the peons were talking about Daniel and her-

self, and she was glad of the protection of Daniel's imposing presence.

They returned to their hotel. Mona, intoxicated by the trip down and the kaleidoscope of swiftly changing scene, wanted nothing now but to be with Daniel. They sat hand in hand on the balcony outside their rooms. Mona's heart was bursting with happiness. She had been transplanted into an enchanted land. The mist rose above the valley and in the far distance, peons with torches made their way back to their adobe huts, like belated fireflies. "Daniel," she said gayly, clinging to him, "a tropic moon-the fragrance of flowers-a blue sky-you and I together—" 

She waited. Daniel held her hand tighter and she could feel his eyes upon her in the half-light. "Just think! Tomorrow I'll have my divorce." "Just think!" he added, "tomorrow we'll be married." "Married! You and I!" She wanted to sing, to dance, to shout-but she could only say, inadequately, "Daniel, I love you."

He drew her to his lap and held her there, his lips against her hair. How long they sat, wrapped in one another's arms and their own roseate dreams, she did not know. At last Daniel said softly, “Let's go in." 

Mona wound her arms around his neck for a passionate embrace and then slipped into her room.

She lay quietly in bed, listening to the measured tread of Daniel's feet as they crossed and recrossed the restricted space of his hotel room. She felt a curious content just to know he was near. There was a bond between her and Daniel that walls could not sever. She would love him, she told herself, even if she never saw him again. How inexplicable love was -how impossible of explanation! Her face grew warm when she remembered how she had hoped to find love through physical contact alone. She had failed, of course. Not until she had known Daniel had she realized the depth of Rutger Wayne's wisdom when he had told her that love was not concerned with surface things.

Though she still did not know what this strange force called love was, she knew that she loved, and that no matter what happened to either of themwhether success or failure stretched before themlove would remain: the invisible bond. Then she thought of the marriage ceremony which had seemed so meaningless when she had faced the minister with Jonathon Grange. Just stereotyped words to legalize a relationship. Now as she said them over to herself, they assumed a new significance. Tomorrow she would repeat these same words before another minister, but with what a difference! "... take this man, Daniel Farr, to be my wedded husband, to have and to hold from this day forward, for better or for worse, for richer for poorer,

in sickness and in health, to love and to cherish, till death do us part. Mona breathed a sigh of content, and with a smile of utter peace wreathing her lips, she fell asleep.
