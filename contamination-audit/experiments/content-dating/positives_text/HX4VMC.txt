THREE QUEENS FULL BY MILTON HERBERT GROPPER Author of "Ladies of the Evening” MADAME DUBARRY (born Selina Schultz) dealt in a commodity whose appeal was permanent. She called it companionship.

One of the photographs in her sample album was Kay Martin. And Kay was tired of it all. "I'm sick of being rented. I'd like to be bought," she complained. She wanted a man to buy her groceries instead of garters. 

Then along comes Kenneth Whitney, Third, the poloplaying Ken Whitney, the heir to the Wall Street Whitneys. True, a Whitney befuddled with liquor, but what a find! He remembers very little of his visit to Dubarry's, or the hectic trip to Greenwich where he marries Kay Martin, but the wise little Kay manages to involve him in a very precarious situation. Her adventures move at a fast pace from then on, while her cronies at Dubarry's circle around her; with a surprising climax when the older Whitney loses his money.

This racy tale by a popular author and playwright, depicts vividly and interestingly the seamy side of Broadway and its red-light outer fringe.

JUDGE RAMSAY began his charge. It was short and lucid. 

He pointed out to the jury that the revolving point of the case was the idea of involuntariness. If it could be shown that intoxication was procured by the defendant for the purpose of stifling the reasoning power of the plaintiff and for the further purpose of marriage and if further, it was proved that the marriage was not consummated, they must decide for the plaintiff. It was, however, not enough to prove that the plaintiff was intoxicated. 

A man can be intoxicated and still be in control of his mental faculties. Further, if when intoxicated, with the intoxication not procured by the defendant, the plaintiff insisted on, or acquiesced to the marriage, it was valid, especially after spending the night in the defendant's room, which presumed consummation, and they must decide for the defendant. They must decide the case on the facts. Did the defendant ply the plaintiff with wine in the Club Mentone in order to reduce him to intoxication for the purpose of getting him up to Connecticut and rushing him into a marriage without his will or desire, or did the plaintiff, whether 133

or not under the influence of the wine, desire and insist on the marriage? Was his state of mind, as brought out in the testimony, such as to lead him into a marriage, on the rebound, as it were, and also because he had fallen in love with the defendant? Did the defendant acquiesce to his pleas to marry, because she liked him or did she engineer, manage and procure the marriage thru guile or fraud for mercenary purposes? The jury must decide the case on the facts. 

At the same time, jurors are expected to use their judgment as well as their ears. They must determine the credibility of the witnesses, discriminate between the true and the false, between the probable and the improbable. If the jury believed that any witness wilfully testified falsely to any material fact, they might, if they chose, disregard the whole or any part of his testimony. Finally, the burden of proof as to the alleged procurement by fraud of this marriage, rested with the plaintiff. If they found that the plaintiff had sustained that burden of proof by a fair preponderance of credible evidence, then they must decide for the plaintiff. If they found that the plaintiff had not so sustained the burden of proof, they must find for the defendant.

MRS. FLAHERTY shuffled down the stairs in her carpetslippers. Kay stood at the grimy window, looking out at the blind wall of the garage next door. She hated that wall.

"Free, white and twenty-one," she muttered. "And look at me." Her hair was dull and uncared-for, her face thin, with a flat, lost look about it. "I sure made one swell job of it. Bet there's a bunch of people wish they were in Kay 

Whitney's shoes-only they don't know I got to stick cardboard in 'em, to keep out the sidewalk. Didn't it look great in print, marrying the heir to the Whitney millions? 

And a real honest-to-God marriage that stuck. I won the annulment suit hands down, made 'em pay me alimony,

I'm still a member of the First Family. I won all along the line, on paper, and look at me! It's a riot." She gave a short hard laugh; then glanced nervously at the bed where 

Kennie lay asleep. "Lucky little sucker, wish I could sleep thru it and never wake up." She controlled another laugh, but her lips curved sardonically. "That's a big laugh, too. If I 244

wanted to go for the works, I couldn't-where'd I get a quarter for gas to inhale?" As if the thought was a reminder, she opened the window softly and brought in a milk bottle containing two inches of bluish milk at the bottom. "Better let that stand in here to warm up. Can't heat it without gas, that's sure." For an instant, panic lit her dull eyes. "That's the last. And not a nickel to buy more. Not a damn thing left to hock.-Well, it had to come some time. Tomorrow, that drunken old hag can put me out and I'll take Kennie to the Society. He'll be better off than in this dive. Poor little rat! Back to another asylum with walls." Her chin-the chin that Ken had once admired for its round firmness-set with determination. "Like hell! He's mine! What did my body ever do for me, anyway, that I should wrap it in cellophane? Christ! When

I think of that forty-three bucks I passed up at Clark's party!" She lit the candle and began to comb her hair and make up her face. "Wonder if I can land a pick-up with this pan. I'll hang out near that speak up the block, and nab some lush on his way out. Maybe he won't be fussy about my face." 

Kennie woke and cried. She heated the milk as best she could in a tin-cup over the candle-flame, gave it to him and watched him sink back into solid slumber. Relieved, she put on her hat and coat, and tiptoed out. "He won't budge till morning. Maybe I can land two birds and eat a square meal myself. I'm so sick of oatmeal, I could gag.' وو It was one of those mild, damp February nights of fog, when street lamps blur and the pavement oozes moisture.

Kay walked slowly thru the dark block from Tenth to Ninth. Meeting nobody, she turned and ambled back, keeping her eye on a heavily curtained basement about midway in her patrol. On her fourth trip, a man came out, burly in a huge overcoat. "Hello, there, big boy. Going my way?” "Brush by, tart." "Look, fella—” "Scram, d'you hear?"

Three more trips up and down. The wet street had turned the cardboard in her shoes to pulp, her stockings were rough with moisture. 

Another man came out, jacket-collar turned up, hatbrim tilted. "Hello there, big boy. Going my way?" "Sure. Any way suits me." He had a husky "whiskey" voice. "The fare's two bucks, brother," she said daringly. "Class to you, eh? Come along." "Where you taking me?” "Home sweet home, beautiful. Right up here. Always try to locate near a speak." "Couldn't dodge 'em if you tried, in this town.”

He ran up a stoop ahead of her and unlocked the front door. "Can't be soused," thought Kay, disappointed. "Not the way he handled that key-hole.”

The house was old and drab, but palatial compared to 

Mrs. Flaherty's. They mounted two flights and entered a

fair-sized back room. The man snapped on the electric light and then came toward her. "F.O.B. at the door, big bo-" Kay began. She stopped. 

The man was Ken.

A gaunt, unshaven Ken, with lines pinching in around a sardonic mouth. Kay began to babble senselessly: "You-how come-here-I don't underst― You are 

Ken Whitney-or am I—” "Have a drink, Kay?" he asked with ironic courtesy. "Sure, sure," she said in a high voice. "Damn chilly work on a night like this."

He brought out a whiskey bottle and glasses, while Kay kicked off her sodden pumps. Ken poured out two stiff drinks, raised his glass and toasted satirically: "To marriage, Kay, for better or for worse.' وو Kay lifted the glass to her lips, tried to drink it and couldn't. She had had no food since breakfast. "Rotten booze," she sniffed. "Bootleg's cheaper than the legal stuff." "Guess I could get it down with something to eat." "Nothing here but a few crackers." "That'll do." 

He brought out a box of saltines, Kay dipped into them ravenously. She had finished half the box when she felt his eyes on her. She flushed a furious, burning scarlet. Ken leaned forward. "You poor damn kid,” he said. "Say," she countered defensively. "You don't look like 

Pierpont Morgan yourself."

"That's right. I'm broke to the wide. Got eleven dollars to my name," he admitted fraternally. "But you've got friends-" "Ever count your friends when you're on your uppers?" "But you're one of the Whitneys—' 

"That's a bad name these days after what my father did." "You have a job." "Had." "Lost it?" "That day at the Vandervliet. I went off on a binge. 

The unpardonable sin in aviation. That's when your alimony stopped." "And you haven't had a job since?" "It didn't seem worth while." "Those were lies that day, if you want to know." "The five hundred was for the Irish kid?” "Yeah." "Why did you lie?” "The Whitneys-I hated their guts and you were one of 'em. Anything to get under your skin." "How long you been doing-this?" "You're my first customer since-Greenwich." "Don't bother to lie now, Kay." "It's the God's truth. I tried once-got as far as the bed and ran out on him." "Why?" "How should I know?"

"Want me to tell you?" "You?" "Remember I told you once we'd get together and money wouldn't have a thing to do with it?" "So what?" "You haven't been hating me all this time, Kay." "What do you call it?” "You've been fighting love." "Will you stop it!" she cried fiercely. "Never. Honey, we've both had the knocks separate. 

We'll take the breaks together." 

Kay sat, silent, for a long time. Then she smiled. "Have 

I been dumb!" she said wonderingly. "I should have known there was something the matter with me when I passed up that Park Avenue proposition. I just thought I was hating the Whitneys." "Why not? What a God-awful skunk I was!” "Oh, shut up about that." "Kay, we're a couple of bums. Isn't it swell? Let's start at scratch and play the course in par." "I can make eleven dollars go a long way, Ken." 

An hour later, Kay started up. "Gosh, I forgot all about Kennie!" "Kennie?" "The Irish kid. I still got him." "Great! Know what we'll do? Park him with Emma while I look for a job." "Emma?"

"Sure. She runs a boarding-house in Yorkville. I met her one day. Otto does the heavy work and waits on the table. 

Will she be glad to see you!" "The darned old egg. I'll help her with the housework if she'll take us in." "Will she? She's been looking for you as hard as I have. 

And nuts about the kid, too. Come on. We'll go get him and fix him up on that couch for the night. I've got something to tell him anyway.” "What?" "Going to warn him he'd better watch out for the joint in his nose. The Milk Sea's full of little sisters!"

THE END
