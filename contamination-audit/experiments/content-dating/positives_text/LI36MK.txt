FOREWORD 

Publication of the first "According to Merit and Fitness" a year ago was, quite frankly, an experiment. We felt it was time to issue something in addition to the formal report we are required to submit annually to the Legislature. Replete with tables and statistics, such reports generally contributed little toward public understanding of our work.

The warm response to our first modest effort toward a lively. modern report was heartening. We were pleased that requests for it came, and are still coming, from every corner of the nation, and even from countries in Europe and the Orient. Letters from public officials and employees, from educators and business heads, and from many other people whose interest had been aroused, encouraged us to continue in the same direction. 

Last year, for the sake of clarity, we planned our report to the people along functional lines. We described briefly what we do, how we do it, and what we had accomplished, without resorting to the technical language which discourages the interest of most people. We believed this scheme would help to introduce us to new friends, and it did.

This report does not follow the same pattern as its predecessor. 

We have chosen for emphasis certain features of our work which seemed particularly significant as the year 1949 and the first half of the twentieth century came to a close. Many important advances are reported, and plans are described which presage a fuller measure of accomplishment in the future. On the whole 1949, like 1948, was a very good year.

In the next year, and in the years to come, we will continue with undiminished diligence the development of a well rounded personnel agency, staffed and equipped to handle the challenging, difficult and important job of civil service administration in the 

Empire State.

FIFTY YEARS AGO 

efore we look at the record for 1949, let's glance back over our shoulders at what civil service in New York State was like at the turn of the century. 

To set the scene we had veterans then, too, but the young ones were Spanish-American War veterans... middle-aged folks still talked about the Civil War... the Victorian Age was nearly over... New York State had in Theodore Roosevelt a civil-service-minded governor who had, as an assemblyman, introduced the first Civil Service Law and had headed the Federal Civil Service Commission for several years. . . the United States had joined the ranks of the industrial nations and there was already talk about "big business" and "trust busting". . . some of us, at least in the cities, had telephones and electric lights. . . the few automobiles there were looked as if they should have a horse in front . . . and the first World War was 14 years away.

Folks then were at the end of an era, but civil service was just off to a healthy start. New York, the first of the states to adopt the merit system, had passed its first Civil Service Law in 1883 after a period of dissatisfaction with the spoils system and agitation for reform. Opposition from defenders of the old way of doing things did not die easily, and as late as 1890 certain citizens of Buffalo tried to have the Civil Service Law declared unconstitutional. In 1894 the Constitutional Convention incorporated the merit system in the State Constitution, putting an end to the uneasiness of the previous decade. 

Nineteen hundred was definitely a turning point. The Commission's goal was then, as it is now, an efficient public service and a fairly administered merit system. Some of its methods were different, and others were astonishingly similar to civil service practice as we know it today. A few of the problems uppermost then are still plaguing us. Ideas which then seemed almost revolutionary are accepted now, and many prominent parts of the Department's program in 1950 had their beginnings as far back as 50 years ago. 

It was a very thorny path indeed that civil service traveled over the last years of the nineties. Passage of the "Black Law" in 1897 was a serious blow, tying the hands of the Commission by removing from it complete control of the examining process. The new law, backed by Governor Black for whom it was named, permitted the appointing officer to conduct examinations of the fitness of candidates which had equal weight with civil service examinations. This often had the effect of nullifying the Commission's examinations. 5

Echoes of the old arguments for and against decentralization of the examining function can be heard in our time. It is interesting to note that, at the Federal level, it is strongly urged by many that the work of recruiting, testing and selecting employees be delegated to the appointing agency. 

Many members of the Legislature were violently opposed to the Black Law, the Commission itself was divided and finally eight cities refused to change their rules in accordance with it. This storm of protest caused a complete renaissance of interest in the merit system, and with the election of Theodore Roosevelt as Governor in 1898, it was obvious that a change for the better was in the offing. The Black Law was almost immediately repealed at the instigation of Governor Roosevelt and replaced in 1899 by the "White Law,' which was named after its sponsor. 

The White Law returned the entire examining function to the Commission, repealed all existing legislation, and established a unified and more detailed statement of the powers and duties of the Commission. It also provided for a reclassification of all positions and combined all the existing laws relating to veterans' preference. A great step forward, it laid a firm foundation for the work of the future.

Now, in 1950, it is time once more for a thorough overhauling of the Civil Service Law. Following the Commission's recommendations to Governor 

Dewey, he has asked the Legislature to set up a temporary State commission to revise and modernize it. 

We find in 1900 the introduction of a rule which is now established practice in civil service jurisdictions all over the country. In that year the 

Commission established the "rule of three," which allows an appointing officer to make his choice from the top three persons on the list as it exists at the time of the appointment. This followed a decision of the Court of Appeals that appointing officers should not be required to appoint the highest ranking individual on the list, as the practice had been up to 1900. 

Looking again at the Federal government, we find still more freedom sought by many who advocate abolition of the rule of three and grouping of the eligibles in such a way that a wider choice would be offered to appointing officers. 

There were 9,375 positions under the jurisdiction of the Commission in 1900.

Now the number of employees under the direct jurisdiction of the Commission is 

85,769 of which 66,861 are employees of 

State departments. There are also 243,028 public employees who are indirectly under the Commission's jurisdiction.

Even though the size of the public service was small, inquiries about qualifications, salaries and examination practices were numerous enough to burden the staff.

Fifty years later we are getting ready to issue a new sample examination question booklet, but it won't be so long, so complicated, or so detailed. 

Today it seems wiser to have different kinds of publications to meet different needs. Our most popular one last year was a small illustrated leaflet, "How to Get a New York State Civil Service Job." In four months a first printing of 25,000 was exhausted.

Just as much a problem five decades ago as it is today was the high percentage of eligibles for some positions who declined appointment. Many eligible lists were of little value when they were issued because people had changed their minds since taking the examinations. The same situation holds today in some lower grade positions. 90 The question of salaries. -- often a factor in determining whether a candidate accepts or declines a position provokes some interesting comparisons and contrasts. Today we generally insist that State employees be paid according to a standard system of salary grades. Most people enter the service at the lowest salary grade in their series and are promoted by successive steps within the series after service in the lower grade. That all started back in 1901, with the passage of the Higgins Law, of which many principles were later incorporated in the Feld-Hamilton Law of 1937. 

Some of the salaries paid in 1900 seem extremely low, although we must remember that it took less to live in those days. Clerks and bookkeepers could expect to start at $600 per year and might, if they proved themselves extremely capable, work up to a maximum of $1200. Junior clerks were paid $480, while a clerk in the Regents' office started off at $20 per month. Today's salaries are decidedly better: for clerks of all kinds, the beginning wage is $1840. They can progress to the administrative service with salaries in five figures.

Some titles in common use then have disappeared with the years. Gone, alas, are the coachman and the vinegar expert, but we have introduced many others to take their places; today we have some 2,000 different job titles covering almost the entire range of human occupations. Many changes have also been made in the subjects in which candidates for jobs were examined. Of these, penmanship is the most notable loss. In the days before typewriters were common and before bookkeeping machines were even invented, the ability to write a clear, legible "hand" was of primary importance in many jobs. 

Another development of 1900 was the appointment of a staff member to visit local civil service commissions and inspect their work, practices and accomplishments. Many violations were found, but they seemed to be rather the result of ignorance of proper procedures rather than deliberate transgressions.

In that year the Commission began the helpful service to local governments that is now the function of one of the Department's major divisions. 

It was also in 1900 that the Commission held the first state-conducted examination for a personnel position in the municipal service. Mr. Herbert 

B. Myron ranked highest on this exam and was appointed Chief Examiner for the Municipal Civil Service Commission of Syracuse. Still an active civil servant, Mr. Myron now holds the important post of Executive Director Mr. Herbert B. Myron, left, Executive Director of the Onondaga County Civil Service 

Commission, began a half-century of public personnel work in 1900. He has given longer continuous service than any employee of any Civil Service Commission in the State. of the Onondaga County Civil Service Commission which also administers the 

Syracuse civil service.

Something new was added in 1903. In that year, with the approval of the State Civil Service Commission, the Municipal Commission of New York City promulgated regulations for rating the performance of employees. The qualities on which they were rated do not differ materially from those rated today excellence of performance, quantity of work produced, initiative, punctuality and attendance, character and habits as they affect efficiency or trustworthiness. ..

That's where we were 50 years ago. We've come a long way since then, but we still don't think we're perfect and we keep trying to improve. Fifty years from now our successors will still be trying!

ONE YEAR'S CHANGES 

ooking now through the other end of the telescope, we narrow our field of vision to the 12 eventful months of 1949. Reappointment of Commission President J. Edward Conway to a second six-year term insured continuation of progressive policies instituted during his first term and will permit vigorous pursuit of plans for further development of the Department.

We had grown rapidly in recent years. This was the year for tightening and strengthening our organization, for pulling things together. We joined two established functions and initiated two new ones to round out our program, and the changes were accomplished without materially increasing the staff. 

Fixing job titles and establishing salaries are two basic functions of the personnel agency. For a number of years they had been carried on by two separate divisions of the Department which had been headed by the Classification Board and the Salary Standardization Board. It had been clear for a long time that their work could be accomplished more effectively, with greater dispatch, if the two closely related functions were united within a single division.

During its 1949 session, the Legislature passed a bill merging the for mer Classification and Salary Standardization Divisions under a Director of 

Classification and Compensation. Authority to fix all titles and all salaries was delegated to the Director. A new Classification and Compensation Appeals Board was given the power to hear and decide on all appeals from decisions of the Director on either title or salary matters. These changes became effective July 1.

Some members of the former Salary Board's staff were transferred to the new Division of Classification and Compensation. Others who had worked under the Salary Board, including its Director of Research, formed the core of a new Division of Personnel Research. Organization of this new division on July 1 made it possible to embark upon many projects long delayed for lack of facilities, and to plan others for the future. 

Salary research, which must go on without pause to provide information on which to base salary determinations, will still be done to a considerable extent by the Personnel Research Division. Technicians familiar with tried methods of gathering salary data pursue the endless task of keeping up to date a mass of statistics on pay scales in private employment and in other public jurisdictions so that New York State salaries will be high enough to attract and keep desirable employees, but not higher than they need be, for the taxpayer's sake.

The annual review of employee performance known as service record rating has been assigned to the Personnel Research Division, which is where we believe it belongs. A subject on which there are many divergent theories, it needs thorough study. Service rating is required by law; salary increments

may not be granted to employees with an unsatisfactory rating. It is a valuable tool in measuring the contribution of the individual employee, both for his own sake and for the guidance of those who direct his work. The Research 

Division is hard at work studying ways of making it a more realistic, more vital and more effective process.

Established to serve all branches of the Department and, as far as practicable, other agencies who request help in the field of personnel research, the Research Division will engage in studies of all phases of public personnel work as the need for them emerges. The Statistical Unit keeps current information on 70,000 State positions Establishment of the Training Division in January 1949 permitted the Department to move forward with long-delayed plans for injecting new life into the State service by training the people who do the work of government to do a better job. With the appointment of a director and a small technical and clerical staff, the division was ready to get down to business. Its objectives are to increase employee effectiveness, to prepare employees to assume higher responsibilities, to re-train employees toward greater versatility and adaptability to changing requirements, and to develop coordination, morale and other elements which make for effective working together. 

These major changes rounded out the Department and equipped it to give full service to the agencies of the State and the employees whose welfare, progress and performance are among its principal concerns. 10

OUR WORK IN 1949 

t the end of the year, when reports come in from all parts of our organization, we are in a position to view the year's accomplishments as a whole. Substantial advances all along the line are reported in the following condensed, factual account. Although many details must be omitted from so brief a report, this section will serve to give some idea of the range of our functions and the volume of our work. Activities in the field of public employee training are treated separately in a later section. 

THE COMMISSION 

The three Commission members, who maintain offices in Albany, Buffalo and New York City, are concerned daily with all phases of civil service administration. At least once each month they meet formally, usually for three days. A typical calendar includes some 125 items, most of which deal with 

State and local administrative problems. Such matters as appeals on examinations and requests for continuation of employment beyond the retirement age make up the balance. In 1949 there were also 49 hearings and conferences. 

In January, for the first time, the Commission's calendar was made public. This policy was welcomed especially by the employees and the Press, who now know in advance what matters are to be considered. Another important step toward better employee relations was the Commission's encouragement of free exchange of views on controversial subjects with employee representatives. 

Aiming steadily at finding the best man for the job, the Commission pursued its public information program through exhibits, publications, speeches, participation in career forums, and greater use of facilities offered by radio and newspapers for reaching prospective candidates with news of examinations and general information about the State service. A persistent effort is being made to raise the prestige of the public employee and to stimulate wider interest in government work as a career. 

All legislation introduced during 1949 at the Commission's request became law. Among the more important laws affecting public personnel administration and the rights of employees were those which created the new Classification and Compensation Division and the Classification and Compensation Appeals Board and defined their powers and duties; allowed employees to retain salary increments for temporary service when they are appointed permanently to similar positions; incorporated emergency salary increases in permanent base salaries; and, to avoid the possibility of discrimination on the basis of national origin, eliminated the requirement that applicants for examinations disclose the place of their birth.

STAFFING THE STATE'S PUBLIC SERVICE 

In 1949 emphasis was placed on holding examinations, completing work them promptly, and making appointments as rapidly as possible. The first step was developing a systematic plan for the year. We were in a position to do this as 1948 came to a close. 

Early in 1949 a complete inventory of examinations which had been authorized and examinations which were in progress was distributed to appointing officers and various information offices visited by the public. (Priorities were determined jointly by the operating departments and the Examinations Division.) Each month revisions and changes in plans were reported to keep appointing officers, employees and the general public up to date on our schedule. This was a tremendous advance. 

Examinations are being completed and eligible lists established within three months of the examination date, with few exceptions. The figures show that on the average we're doing even better than the three-months goal. (See chart on opposite page.)

Production figures fail to show how better planning made many examinations unnecessary. Combining examinations for several departments eliminated the need for about 700 separate examinations. This has helped us catch up with the backlog of old orders and build up lists of eligibles. Twelve interdepartmental examinations, which enabled more than 6,000 employees to compete for promotion both in their own and other departments, were held to produce promotion lists equivalent to 582 separate examinations for single promotion units. Intradepartmental promotion examinations permitted 3,400 employees to compete for advancement in parts of their departments other than their own promotion units. Twenty-two intradepartmental examinations held in 1949 were the equivalent of 150 individual promotion unit examinations. 

Intensive recruiting for the third annual Professional and Technical Assistant examination for college seniors, combining examinations in several fields, brought applications from approximately 6,500 candidates although it was generally known that competition would be keen and only a relatively few appointments could be made. There were 4,000 candidates the first year and 4,845 the second year. The 1949 examination has resulted in 227 appointments in 24 different titles during the six months following establishment of eligible lists in June.

DISABLED VETERANS SALARIES AND PAY CHECKS 

Some of the most important personnel services we perform for the employee deal with his salary. We do the research upon which salary determinations are based, determine the salary paid for each title in the State service, and review all State payrolls to make sure that employments are in accord with the Civil Service Law. Prompt processing of payrolls means that the worker gets his checks on time.

Salary research, formerly directed by the Salary Standardization Board, now is done largely by the new Division of Personnel Research. Since July 1, research technicians have been gathering data for use in determining pay rates for more than 50 different titles. The fourth annual Statewide salary survey, comparing salaries of 61,640 employees in 230 leading private agencies with State salaries for similar jobs, was completed. Wage information on 350,000 jobs was collected from the Federal government and seven other states. Overall studies of salary trends in private industry, in other states, and in the 17

Federal Government, have been continued and broadened. 

An annual survey of salaries paid for 1,158 positions in 16 private colleges, for purpose of comparison with positions in the State's higher educational institutions, has been completed. A single salary schedule for all institutions comprising the State 

University was constructed at the request of the director of the Budget. This included a detailed cost study for conversion to the new schedule. 

Decisions as to how much should be paid for a particular job now rest with the Director of Classification and Compensation. Since July 1, when reorganization of the Department combined this function with job classification, 185 appeals for changes in salary and 18 combination title and salary appeals have been acted on. Many salary appeals affected groups of positions, so that the total positions affected numbered several thousand. 

Because a major concern of the employee is whether his check will come on time so he can plan his budget and meet his bills, we are proud that in 1949 even the traditional delays in certifying payrolls for the first payroll period of the new fiscal year were overcome. Generous praise came from department heads, to whom this is also an important matter. 

A particularly difficult assignment performed by the Payroll Section in 

1949 followed the Court of Appeals decision on the DeMarco case in March.

This case affected several thousand State employees whose jobs had been reallocated to a new salary grade retroactively to April 1, 1947. Procedures were set up for the filing and certification of individual claims under the DeMarco case, and the claims began pouring in on May 15. During the following six months all of the 6, 123 claims filed by employees occupying 236 different titles in 118 agencies and institutions were processed by the Payroll Section. Each had to be handled separately, and some involved legal interpretation. Close coordination between the operating departments, the Department of Audit and Control, and this Department was required to get the job done. 

JOB CLASSIFICATION 

Changes in the State's title structure to keep pace with shifting needs in the service require constant work on surveys and resurveys. 

In 1949, projects involving 4,995 positions, on which the field survey work had been accomplished previously, were completed. Maintenance resurveys were made of 4,110 jobs in six Mental Hygiene institutions. A survey of 122 positions in the Labor Department was completed and final determinations were made. 

New classification surveys were started in the Departments of Education and Agriculture and Markets. A total of 5,065 new positions requested by department heads was classified. 18

Following the merging of two divisions to form the Division of Classification and Compensation, new procedures and new forms were developed to deal with classification and salary appeals. During the year 1, 143 reclassification petitions were determined. Hearings totaled 104. 

Work has been continued on occupational studies and cross comparisons, covering several departments, in farm, kitchen and ward services in institutions, and also in institution psychology positions, and in office positions in the fields of business management and personnel administration. 

Improvement and simplification of the title structure has resulted from the elimination in recent months of 45 titles which were found, after much study, to be obsolete, and the standardization of titles for positions which had similar duties but had carried different titles. The words "T.B. Service'' were used in 1949 for the first time in titles of employees whose positions involve special hazard through contact with active tuberculosis patients. Persons in these positions are paid extra salary. 

RESEARCH ON PERSONNEL MATTERS 

Although salary research consumes a large part of the time of the Personnel Research Division, and most of its projects completed since organization of the Division July 1 have been in that field, other studies have been launched and still more have been assigned for the future. 

Primarily intended to act as a staff agency, the Division has one "line operation" function: administration of the service record rating program. 

During the calendar year 35,500 ratings of the performance of permanent employees were processed. Ratings supplied to the Examinations Division for use in computing scores on promotion examinations numbered 4,908. Careful study of past experience is expected to make this program more effective. 

Current information on approximately 70,000 positions in the State service, most of which are under the jurisdiction of the Commission, is maintained on punch cards by the Statistical Unit. Kept up to date by semi-monthly corrections, this information is used to determine recruiting difficulty, turnover rates, costs of salary changes, average salaries and employment trends. 

The subject matter of research studies under way includes recruitment methods, oral examining techniques, certification procedures, reporting methods and validation of examinations. A comprehensive study of personnel practices in 250 private organizations is being made which may have a bearing on determination of personnel policies in the New York State Service. 

SERVICE TO LOCAL GOVERNMENTS 

The merit system is working better each year in local units of government. 

Cooperative work and friendly relationships between local officials and the Department's Municipal Service Division are bringing New York closer to the goal of good public personnel administration everywhere in the State. 

Solutions to day-to-day personnel problems are worked out for government units directly under State administration -five counties, one city, 74 towns and 43 villages. Hamilton, Erie and Westchester Counties, which operate under personnel officers, require assistance mostly with rules and examinations, 19

with some attention to classification matters. 

In 1950 Wyoming will become the fourth county to adopt this type of administration. 

Revisions of civil service rules were achieved in 1949 by Dutchess and Hamilton Counties and the cities of Canandaigua, Middletown, Norwich, Oneonta and Utica.

The same five cities also adopted complete classification plans with specifications for each kind of work in the city's service. A classification plan completed for Herkimer County covering Herkimer Memorial Hospital was adopted. Classification plans for five other cities (Auburn, Dunkirk, Gloversville, Ogdensburg and Poughkeepsie) were submitted to local officials for consideration and approval. Commissions in Amsterdam, Batavia, North Tonawanda, Norwich, Olean, Oneonta, Rochester and Rome were helped in installing classification plans previously adopted. Classification work was begun in the city of Rensselaer, and five reclassification surveys were begun. Work with professional groups on developing statewide specifications for psychiatric social work and health educator positions was completed. Public housing, library, recreation and school lunch program positions are getting the same kind of attention.

A large part of the Department's examination work concerns positions in local governments. In 1949 the Municipal Service Division was asked to prepare 901 examinations. We held 999 local examinations (some requested the previous year) for 8,513 candidates, rated 6,397 papers and established 380 eligible lists. These figures are included in the total examination statistics for the Department.

DISTRICT OFFICES The 

Branch offices in New York and Buffalo are helping the Department carry the tremendous load of detail work involved in personnel operations. 

New York office in 1949 certified 791 county payrolls, recorded 2,348 personnel changes, administered 346 written examinations to 20,208 candidates and 557 medical examinations, received 10,061 applications with fees, submitted 365 reports on investigations, counseled and assisted veterans who made 2,125 visits to the office. The Buffalo office, opened in 1948, processed 

4,225 applications, and gave information to 18,867 visitors. Both offices assisted substantially in recruiting activities. 

THE PERSONNEL COUNCIL 

The outstanding contribution of the Personnel Council during its fourth year was accomplished through committees of operating personnel officers. 

Working toward the general objective of improving personnel policies and establishing uniformity in their interpretation, the committees on procedures, examinations, administrative problems, leave and transfers have already ironed out many problems and currently are progressing toward the solution of others. 

The agenda committee, by sifting through material for group discussion at regular monthly Council meetings, has scheduled speakers and discussion topics of general interest to personnel administrators. Among these were training, service ratings, legislation, retirement, and adjustment of employee personality problems.

The Council's work as an informational clearing house, including the 20

TRAINING: A START IS MADE 

In the short space of a year, 2,925 State employees have participated in training programs conducted, developed or sponsored by our new Training Division. Courses have been held in high school classrooms, offices, boiler rooms, auditoriums, hearing rooms wherever space could be found to accommodate the people who recognized that here, at last, was a long-sought opportunity to learn new skills and polish up old ones. employees are no exceptions to the general urge for self-improvement. State

With temporary and very cramped quarters in the State Office Building in Albany, the Training Director and his staff began their job of finding out what training was most needed, where it was needed and how it could be accomplished, and within a couple of months they moved into high gear. Contacts were established with departmental training units where they existed, and with professional organizations, boards of education and higher educational institutions so the division could move ahead effectively toward its goal of the most training for the most employees where it was most essential, without wasted effort or duplication. An advisory council, on which several departments are represented, was established to provide advice on matters of policy and the scope of training programs to be instituted. 

Pilot programs established first in Albany to try out plans and perfect them before branching out to other localities helped to lay the groundwork. 

Classes have been launched in New York City, Amsterdam, Troy and Rockland County. Employees in Buffalo and other cities where there are concentrations of State employees, and in State institutions, can expect the services of the Training Division before long. 

The great bulk of the division's work during 1949, its first year, fell into the category of in-service training, exclusive of supervisory training. A total of 1,986 persons enrolled in 67 courses, most of which were 40-hour courses in clerical subjects. "Refresher" or brush-up courses for stenographers and typists stepped up speed and accuracy for 280 more employees.

There were courses in administrative analysis, key punch operation, statistics and actuarial science. Tax examiners, game protectors and others in specialized fields had their turn.

Training programs for supervisory personnel brought 249 employees who are responsible for the performance of other workers into 13 courses dealing with fundamentals of organization, administration, conference leadership, job 22 22

analysis, service record rating, human relations and other factors involved in effective supervision.

A program expected to have far-flung effects when it comes to maturity is the induction program started for new employees of the Civil Service Department. There has been a definite need for planned orientation of the new employee who, coming into government service, finds himself in a complex world in which he must learn to function as an effective part. Induction training helps him to find his place, to see his own tasks in relation to the total objective of government work, and to understand his obligations as a public servant. General induction training will be offered to employees of all departments not presently conducting this type of training for their own people. 

The Public Administration Internship Program launched by Governor 

Dewey in 1947, now a well-established pattern for combining training and practical experience, is administered and operated by the Training Division.

e could plug away at our desks till doomsday, and most of our 14 million bosses wouldn't know what we were doing unless we took a little time out to tell them about it. We've made some headway with this problem in previous years in the regular course of our work, but it isn't easy to reach the storekeeper on Main Street, the farmer and his wife, and many of the other wonderfully diverse elements that make up our population. 

There are two stories to tell these people. One is our own story: what the merit system is, how it operates, what it means to them in terms of better government.

The other, equally important to us, is the more dramatic and appealing story of thousands of public employees working all over the State, in hospitals, laboratories, forest preserves, classrooms and dozens of other places as well as offices. We would like their contribution to our progress and well-being to be more generally recognized.

EXAMINATION ANNOUCEMENT THIS IS WHERE THE MONEY GOES 

very year when budget time rolls around, the people responsible for running the Department put their heads together to figure out exactly how much money is needed to do the next year's job. As any household budgeter does, they cut corners here, and they add a bit there in the hope of starting some long-needed new project. The final figures are gone over minutely by the Division of the Budget, which usually trims them a bit. They then become part of the Governor's budget which he submits to the Legislature, and the Legislature has the last word. 

Our appropriation for 1949-50 was $1,775,422. Since this money comes out of the pockets of all the people, they naturally have a lively interest in what happens to it. The opposite page tells how we spend our money. We try to be careful stewards, and we think many taxpayers would be reassured if they could feel the firm hold on our purse-strings or eavesdrop on some of our meetings where expenses come up. Every expenditure is considered carefully. 

Additional checks on spending are provided by the State Finance Law which requires that every item purchased by any State agency must be approved by the 

Division of Standards and Purchase and the Department of Audit and Control. 

Our accounting is split into two parts. One shows how much is spent for personnel, and includes the salaries of everybody on the staff from mailboy to president of the Commission. There are 458 of us on the regular staff, and 

28 public administration interns who are paid out of our funds although they are assigned to other agencies for most of their internship period. Our average salary is $3,076.76. The other part shows what we spend for rented office space, equipment and office supplies, travel, lighting, telephone calls, printing examinations and announcements, and the other items for which any going concern has to pay.

The Department's budget as proposed for 1950-51 is lower than that for 1949-50 to conform with the Administration's policy of paring down government costs to meet a lower State income. 

Examination fees collected from candidates during the year amounted to $115,653. This money was transferred to the State Treasury, except for $1,314 which was turned over to the Unemployment Insurance Administration Fund. The latter figure represents a percentage of the fees for examinations which are given exclusively for positions in the Division of Placement and Unemployment Insurance. The payment of examination fees, which range from one to five dollars according to the salary paid for the job, is required by law. 26
