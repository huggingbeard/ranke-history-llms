INTRODUCTION 

The detection and apprehension of the criminal and the production of evidence against him has long since ceased to be a matter capable of being efficiently handled by the untrained and unskilled peace officer. 

Properly conducted criminal investigation is a profession, dealing not only with those matters peculiar with crime and criminals with which the older officer is familiar but calling to its aid and enlisting all of the sciences, trades and arts so that it might well be said that the sum total of human knowledge should be at the disposal of the criminal investigator. The arrest following the commission of a crime is the mere performance of a physical act, capable of being performed by any officer possessed of sufficient strength and courage but the arrest throws no light upon the guilt or innocence of the prisoner. It is the function of the criminal investigator to locate and produce the evidence tending to show whether the accused or someone else committed the offense in question. The more complete and accurate the investigation the more nearly will this question be solved. The omission to secure available proof or the inaccuracy of the investigation even in a slight degree may defeat the purpose of the investigation, and, if a trial ensues, may result in a miscarriage of justice. A crime mystery remains a mystery only until it is solved. To say that a crime mystery cannot be solved is usually an admission that the investigator is unable to solve it and is no proof that it could not be solved if the proper investigation is conducted.

PREFACE 

The following discussion of some of the phases of criminal investigation and the presentation of evidence in criminal prosecutions are offered, not in a critical spirit or faultfinding with any particular peace officer or group, but with the purpose of suggesting to peace officers generally a means to become more proficient and successful to the end that through the mistakes of others they may avoid similar mistakes. The author also has in mind the fact that there has been very little written upon the important subject of criminal investigation and practically nothing has been written with the idea of assisting the ambitious officer whose experience has been limited. The older officer in service should find herein many hints and ideas for improving his work. The subject is presented in as nearly nontechnical a manner as possible and the various topics discussed are presented from the standpoint of their importance and practicability. 

The author has had an extended experience in the criminal courts as defense counsel, prosecuting attorney and on the bench and many of the suggestions in the following chapters were prompted by actual experience in the court room and personal experiences in making or directing criminal investigations, the many experiences related to him by many of his friends engaged in criminal investigation, the study of many of the cases of the past and research and experiment along lines of value to the investigating officer. out these pages a sincere effort has been made to be of assistance to the officer. If, in some respects, it appears that undue stress or repetition has been indulged in by the author in connection with certain subjects, the explanation lies in the fact that those matters have been found by personal experience to be of such importance that they can hardly be overemphasized. The greatest difficulty has been found in selecting from the vast amount of available information those matters to be included and those to be omitted. Whether this selection has been wise must be left to the reader.

CHAPTER I. 

The Importance of Accurate Detailed Information 

The mere observing and recording of the evidence as it would be done by the everyday citizen will not meet the standards required of real police work. The absolute necessity for the highest degree of accuracy and attention to the minutest details cannot be overlooked in modern criminal investigation. It will no longer do to merely find a revolver, a pistol shell or a handkerchief on the scene of the homicide. In addition the exact position of each object and the respective distances between the objects and from the body must be fixed, accurately measured and a record kept of the findings. The exact distance may be vital and ambiguity or indefiniteness may largely destroy the effect of what would otherwise be a strong circumstance. 

Throughout the course of his investigation the officer must bear in mind that ultimately a jury of twelve people will have to pass upon the evidence. If the evidence presents each fact in an accurate and unmistakable manner the jury will be in a far better position to arrive at a correct conclusion as to the real facts than if the evidence is indefinite and inaccurate. There is never any danger of securing too much accurate and detailed information while, on the other hand, the absence of proof which diligence could have secured is the one big cause for the acquittal of guilty defendants.

One of the everyday experiences in our courts is an almost incredible variance and inaccuracy in the testimony of honest witnesses as to matters of distance, time, color, speed and other matters of description. 

This may be natural and excusable in the case of untrained witnesses but, when this sort of testimony comes from the lips of men composing that department of our government whose duty is criminal investigation, it is inexcusable. The trial of a criminal case should not be a guessing match participated in by the witnesses but should be an accurate and scientific presentation of the facts. Too much emphasis cannot be placed upon the importance of observing, as nearly as is humanly possible, everything which can be observed, not merely in a general way but with regard to the minuest details. As an example:-We know that once in a while, say once in a thousand cases of homicide, the condition of the shoes, whether clean or dirty, is a material circumstance. No one can tell in advance that particular one in a thousand cases in which the condition of the shoes will prove to be of importance. 

Hence, not knowing which case may involve the condition of the shoes, their condition should be observed in every case so that, in that one case out of a thousand, the evidence may not be lost. The same may be said of the multitude of facts which present themselves in every crime. This means real work, more work than officers are accustomed to put upon a case, but it is the only way by which an investigation can be made complete. We expect a judge or a jury to decide our cases in accordance with the facts. To do so they must have the facts. The more facts presented at the trial the more nearly can a just decision be reached. The absence of facts which, if presented, would shed light on the true state of affairs not only deprives the court of information which would assist in the decision but may result in the drawing of an inference not justified by the actual facts. Thus, if an observer states that he did not notice that a particular individual had on any head covering, this is likely to be taken by a jury as an indication that no head covering was worn. This might occur in a case in which the fact that the individual in question had on a cap was of great importance. 

There is another good reason why the investigator should know all the available facts. When he is placed upon the witness stand he is very likely to be asked to describe what he observed and it detracts very much from the weight and value of his testimony if he has to admit that he is unable to give any information as to some of the things he could and should have observed. 

The writer recalls a very prominent murder trial a few years ago in which the officers were unable to state whether there was any liquor in the apartment in which the body was found. After the trial several of the jurors told the writer that they were quite sure that there must have been liquor in the place but that no doubt the officers had consumed it and therefore did not want to tell about finding it; the jurors added that they did not think that those officers were very reliable, an impression not at all justified in fact. One does not have to be a psychologist to appreciate that, if a man is asked ten questions concerning matters relating to his own business and he is unable to answer one or two of them, the reliability of his answers to the other questions is open to question and his ability will not be rated very highly. The man whose statements carry weight is the man who "knows his stuff” and who can give the information expected of him and this is just as true about police work as of any other profession.

CRIMINAL INVESTIGATION Getting All of the Facts 

Too often, unfortunately, is it the case that the officer ends his investigation when he has secured proof sufficient to convince himself that the accused is guilty. 

In doing this he overlooks the fact that much of that which he considers evidence and proof of guilt is not, as a matter of law, evidence which can lawfully be introduced at the trial. We may not always agree with the rules of law governing the admissibility of evidence in court but the cold fact is that those rules of evidence do exist and we must abide by them. And, even though the proof secured is properly receivable in evidence, the degree of proof required to convince a judge, and especially a jury, of the guilt of the defendant beyond all reasonable doubt is far greater than that which will satisfy the investigating officer. The officer must realize that jurors are selected from the citizenry generally and that they have no knowledge of crime or criminals or their methods of operation. With all respect to our judges it must be admitted that some of them know little more about criminology than the jurors and this is especially true in the case of those crimes which are less frequent. A given description of the conduct of two men boarding a street car and working their way forward through the crowd of passengers or their conduct while standing in the aisle may raise not even a suspicion of crime in the mind of the ordinary citizen, but to the mind of the trained officer it would present a typical picture of the operations of a couple of pickpockets. This is but an illustration of the difference of the effect of evidence upon the trained officer and the layman, a difference which must be overcome at the trial by proof so detailed and plain that everyone may understand and realize the true facts of the case.

It is but natural that, when a police officer has made an arrest, he himself should hold the belief that the prisoner is guilty and but little proof is necessary to satisfy him that no reasonable doubt of guilt exists. 

Upon a trial the conditions are very different. The judge, or jury if it be a jury trial, knows nothing of the facts except that the defendant is charged with a particular offense and has no belief as to the guilt of the defendant and will not form such a belief until proof has been produced sufficient to show guilt to a moral certainty. The situation is further complicated by the fact that many defendants present an appearance, especially to a jury, which, at the outset of the trial make them hesitate to believe that so prepossessing an individual could have committed the crime charged and, when such a feeling exists, it also must be overcome by evidence so convincing as to remove all reasonable doubt of guilt. Always remember that no juror is willing to return a verdict sending a man to his death or a term in prison unless that juror is satisfied of guilt beyond all reasonable doubt. At the trial, also, the accused presents his evidence which, to a greater or lesser degree detracts from that of the prosecution and, if such proof raises a reasonable doubt of guilt an acquittal must follow. Before he is justified in expecting a conviction the officer must produce proof so complete and convincing that, notwithstanding the evidence for the defense, no such reasonable doubt can exist in the minds of an impartial jury. It is but natural for an officer to feel aggrieved and even somewhat discouraged when a jury acquits a defendant as to whose guilt the officer can see no room for doubt and he feels justified in blaming the jury although in nine such cases out of ten the acquittal resulted from a failure to produce the degree of proof which would entitle the prosecution to a conviction. It is wholly unimportant so far as the trial is concerned whether the officer believes the defendant guilty; the important thing is that the jury should be forced to the conclusion by the convincing character and quantity of the proof that the prosecution is entitled to a conviction.

No officer, unless he thoroughly knows the law of evidence, can tell in advance what portion of the evidence gathered by him will be permitted to be introduced at the trial nor can he anticipate, in more than a small percentage of his cases, the proof of the defense. Even the district attorney with many years of experience often finds himself suddenly called upon, in the midst of the trial, to meet a line of evidence he had not anticipated and it is not unusual for the district attorney to find that evidence, which he believed legally admissible, cannot be introduced because of some rule of law which he had either overlooked or with which he was unfamiliar. Again, some of the evidence gathered by the officer may miss fire or fall short of proving what it was expected to show or a witness relied upon to give certain important testimony may be unavailable or may fail to come up to expectations by reason of a failure to remember or because of nervousness when called to the witness stand. 

No hunter, desirous of securing three ducks for the family dinner, would, even though a perfect shot, go duck hunting with but three shells. No fisherman seeking a mess of trout would go equipped with but one hook and but one kind of fly or but one kind of bait. Even with several boxes of shells or a complete outfit of hooks, flies and bait the sportsman may come home empty handed but at least he went prepared.

There is only one way of doing a job and that is in a complete and thorough manner and, when going into court with a case, the officer should have with him every single bit of evidence bearing on that case which could by any possibility be secured. The time to get evidence is when the case first breaks, as soon as the crime has been discovered; the more time allowed to lapse between the discovery of the crime and the search for evidence, the greater the chance that some of the evidence will disappear or be unprocurable. It is of the utmost importance, therefore, that the investigator should consider that the arrest and his own belief in

the guilt of his prisoner constitute but the beginning of his real labors. Neither is it sufficient if the investigator secures every bit of available proof tending to show that the accused is guilty. He must go still further and must ascertain, if he can, the possible defenses which the accused may urge at the trial and the evidence which the defense will probably produce at the trial. Such defenses may be based on facts and on the testimony of honest, though perhaps biased witnesses or may be false and based on perjury. With this last mentioned task done the officer must still continue his work until he has proof that such defenses and defense evidence are reliable or that they are unreliable and, if the latter be the case, he must secure the proof necessary to meet and overcome the anticipated defenses. All this may be summed up in a few words, "Get all the facts on both sides of the question and get them at the earliest possible opportunity." The success of the investigator is not determined by the outcome of the trial. Many guilty men have been convicted after the most indifferent and slipshod investigations. The true test of a successful investigation is whether all of the available proof has been secured and placed at the disposal of the prosecuting attorney. 

The most common error of an investigator is to pass up, as immaterial or unnecessary, available evidence which later on, frequently when it is too late to secure that evidence, turns out to be of great importance. This error is particularly likely to happen in the early stages of the investigation and in investigations of the scene of the crime. At the beginning of an investigation it is the usual thing that no one knows exactly just what happened and frequently the identity of the perpetrator is unknown or in doubt. The evident facts may seem clear and demonstrative as to just what has happened, but the trained and experienced officer will refrain from drawing the apparently obvious conclusion and proceed with the same degree of care, with the same regard for every available bit of evidence, as though the entire affair were shrouded with mystery. 

The apparently obvious suicide may turn out to be a murder. A few years ago a man was sentenced to death for a double murder in the state of New York the conviction being based upon what appeared to be satisfactory proof that the fatal bullets had come from a revolver owned by the defendant. The efficient work of Judge C. E. Waite saved the defendant from the penalty and secured his release from prison when he called attention to the fact that the fatal bullets showed that they had been fired from a revolver having only four lands and four grooves while the defend ant's revolver had five lands and five grooves. Every experienced officer can recall cases within his own experience in which mistakes were made in the early stages of an investigation because of a failure to proceed carefully and thoroughly. Sometimes these cases have been ruined by such failures; often such neglect has made the conviction of a guilty defendant un- necessarily difficult. Genius in police work as in every other line of endeavor means simply the infinite capacity for observing and making use of details. Sometimes, it is true, an officer is fortunate in securing conclusive evidence of guilt without special effort but the officer who depends upon good fortune to obtain results has about as much chance to make a success of his profession as the man who believes he is lucky enough to make a success of bucking a roulette wheel. 

No man investigating a case today can tell what evidence will in the future be of importance. He may spend days in finding the person who has committed a homicide only to find that the accused admits the homicide but claims it was in self defense; by that time much of the evidence which would shed light upon the claim of self defense may have disappeared. It is obviously of importance that everything out of the ordinary should be observed and noted but it must also be borne in mind in this connection that a thing which is in fact out of the ordinary may appear very ordinary. Thus a blue bordered handkerchief neatly arranged in the breast pocket of a person found dead may appear ordinary until it is learned that the deceased had never owned or used other than plain white handkerchiefs. What is apparently only a laundry mark on clothing may lead to a lengthy inquiry among laundrymen only to learn later on that the mark was that of a dry cleaner. The author recalls a case in which no suspicion had a pair of kid gloves in his possession. It attention was paid to the fact that a man arrested on was later learned, for the first time while the trial was in progress, that this man never wore gloves and still avoid leaving finger prints on his burglarious expedilater it developed that his sole use for gloves was to tions; prior to that no one had given the gloves more than a passing thought. Again, the apparently extraordinary may turn out to be very ordinary and the seemingly important fact prove to be of no special significance while the apparently unimportant fact may become vital to the ultimate determination of the case. 

All of this brings us to the ultimate conclusion that the true importance of facts, testimony and physical evidence, is not and can not be appreciated until the ultimate decision of the questions of fact and even then they may fail to receive full value because some associated factor has not been proved. Consequently the more facts ascertained and the more evidence secured by the investigator the less likelihood that the trial will disclose that one or more important pieces of evidence were overlooked in the investigation. In a case recently tried in the author's court a defendant was acquitted on a manslaughter charge because there was no way of disproving the claim of the defendant that the automobile with which his automobile collided at night had but one headlight burning. Looking back over a personal experience in court rooms for over a quarter of a century the writer is satisfied that more miscarriages of justice result from insufficient prepar-

ation and presentation of evidence than any other cause. The officer who feels dissatisfied because a defendant, whom he believes guilty, has been acquitted is usually himself to blame for the acquittal. The conviction of a defendant of a crime, especially if it be one which will carry a severe penalty upon conviction, is a serious matter to the mind of the honest and conscientious judge or juror and a conviction cannot be expected unless proof of guilt to a moral certainty and beyond a reasonable doubt is produced at the trial. 

On the other hand no judge or jury, if they be honest, will hesitate to convict if the evidence be so strong that, to an unprejudiced mind, no other conclusion is rational.

The production of evidence at the trial is the function directly of the prosecuting attorney but the gathering and bringing of the evidence into court is the job of the investigator and the proof is complete and produces conviction in an unprejudiced mind only when that job is well done. But, simply because a defendant has been found guilty, this does not prove that the case was thoroughly investigated and prepared; the case may have required but a few facts and there may have been no credible defense or, as not infrequently happens, the credit for the conviction should really be given to the defense counsel. The real test of an investigation is the answer to the question: Is there any evidence which tends to shed light upon any question of fact involved in the case which could with reasonable effort and diligence have been obtained but which was not available at the trial? If the answer is in the affirmative the investigation is, to the extent of the failure to bring such evidence into court, not a success. 

The Effect of Confessions and Admissions on Investigations 

The inexperienced officer very often ceases all further efforts at investigation if he gets a confession or substantial admissions from the prisoner, especially when the prisoner expresses his intention of pleading guilty. 

This is due in part because a peace officer usually very much overrates the value and importance of a confession, in part to the fact that, until he learns that such is not always the case, he is unaware that such statements are not always true in all their details, and in part, at times, to a lack of interest in his work or to downright laziness. The man who would be a success in any line of endeavor is never satisfied to drop his task when it arrives at the stage when he thinks he can "get by" but continues his work until he has done everything within his power. The fellow who says, "It's too much trouble," or "I haven't time,' or 

"That's good enough," can expect a similar response when he asks his superior to transfer him from his old job to a better one.

The experienced officer can readily recall all too many cases which resulted in an acquittal in spite of what had seemed a perfectly good confession. Evidence of the oral declarations of a person do not, ordinarily, carry great weight and are never so convincing to the impartial juror as they are to the police officer. In the case of a person charged with crime the effect of such proof is likely to be further minimized by the effect upon the members of the jury by what they have heard, read or seen portrayed upon the stage or screen relating to what has been called the "third degree." Another complication which may always be expected at the trial is a denial by the accused of his prior declarations thus immediately creating a conflict of testimony; or the defendant may partially admit and partially deny the confession or may offer an explanation which, if believed, will destroy most of the effect of his confession. While not so common an occurrence, it has happened that the confession is false in great part. In such a case if the prisoner denies the confession and the proof shows that the confession is false in many respects, the jury is quite ready to believe the testimony of the prisoner and to suspect that the officer made up the confession. As more fully discussed elsewhere in this volume, the confession is admissible only when shown to have been made freely and voluntarily and after independent proof of the corpus delicti and the instances are not at all rare of cases in which this rule of law excluded a confession from the evidence because someone had made a statement to the prisoner which, though quite harmless to all appearances, under the law rendered it inadmissible in evidence.

The only safe course for the investigating officer is to make the same investigation, with the same degree of care and industry, regardless of whether or not the prisoner has made a statement. Incidentally, also, the statement should be followed up by an examination to either verify or disprove the statements it contains. It must also be remembered that no confession can be received in evidence or even considered by the court unless there is other and independent evidence which, standing alone, proves every single element of the crime charged. In other words, even though a prisoner has confessed to every detail of the crime charged, it is absolutely necessary to have other evidence which will of itself show that the crime charged has been committed.

CHAPTER II. 

Previous Preparation, Equipment, Note Book 

Good work in police work, as in every other line of endeavor, depends upon previous preparation for that work. Life is too short for any of us to personally acquire all of the available experience and we must rely upon books, magazine articles and other sources of information for much of our knowledge. But there is much that the individual can do to prepare himself for the handling of future cases. The officer who does none of these things can expect to fall down on the job. 

The successful officer is the man who is constantly trying to learn something which will be useful to him in his future work.

Much can be learned by observation of daily facts and occurrences having no relation to crime whatever. 

The fact that women button their coats on the left and not on the right as men do, the appearance of automobile tire marks on the road when going straight ahead, when turning, when sliding forward or when sliding sideways, the difference in tire marks of a car going forward from the marks by the same tires when the car goes backwards, the differences in the appearances of footprints of persons walking, running and jumping, of lame people or of a person walking backwards or of a person carrying a heavy burden, the tendencies and characteristics of people of different races or different parts of the country, a familiarity with the more common types of firearms and bullets, customs and habits peculiar to particular trades or business; these are but a few illustrations of the many things the officer may observe in his everyday life any one of which may in the immediate future prove of real value in an investigation. In fact it is difficult to conceive of any bit of information which may not in the future prove itself the means of clearing up a crime. 

To some it may seem too much trouble to be continually observing things a knowledge of which does not at the time seem to be of immediate use, a bad state of mind in any business, profession or employment, but their wiser brothers appreciate that success and the successful grasping of the opportunities which present themselves depends very little upon genius but a great deal upon being prepared.

Every officer should have a general knowledge of criminal law, the necessary elements of each of the various crimes of common occurrence and the evidence necessary to prove their commission. He should also know what facts and evidence to look for, what lines of investigation to follow, when to call for the assistance of scientific experts. A familiarity with the facts and histories of crimes committed in the past is of inestimable value. Criminals are not particularly original and crimes are committed today in much the same manner they were committed years ago; the concealing of evidence of the crime and even the false stories told by prisoners seem to have come from the same mold used by former prisoners charged with similar offenses. Many crimes appear baffling because they are committed in a manner unusual to that particular community but quite usual in some other part of the country or abroad. The very method of operating may point strongly to the place from which the perpetrator of a crime has come or may even point to the actual perpetrator.

Every officer should make diligent effort to familiarize himself with the territory to which his duties are confined. He should know the distances between points within his county, the routes of railroad and street cars, the location of taxi and stage depots, car rental stations, firearms stores, pawnshops and dealers in second hand goods. A wide personal acquaintance is of great value. Store keepers, persons conducting boarding houses and hotels, and other people whose business brings them in contact with many people are often the source of valuable information which they part with most readily if they have an acquaintance and friendly feeling for the officer making the inquiry. Even the young boys must not be overlooked. They are often the source of valuable information due to their natural inquisitiveness and tendencies to observe things which are overlooked by their elders. The small boy usually knows more about the people living in the neighborhood than does the older person. Not only that but boys are more than willing to help the officer dig up information and do so with most remarkable success. 

It certainly is worth while to make a friend of every boy on the beat.

Equipment of the Investigator 

For some reason most investigators seldom carry any equipment to assist them in their work but in the more advanced investigating forces we are finding more and more appreciation of the fact that the investigator needs tools to work with. Much of the poor and inefficient work charged to investigators is due to lack of the necessary equipment.

Of the greatest importance is the officer's notebook of which more will be said elsewhere but which cannot be overemphasized as an absolute requisite to a complete record of the investigation. For preserving exhibits there should be provided envelopes of various sizes, glass jars and bottles, tags for attaching to exhibits, and sealing wax for securely closing all containers of exhibits. A small file for marking exhibits otherwise difficult to mark is sometimes of use but the most preferable course is not to place any mark upon an exhibit but to enclose it in an envelope or other container, securely sealed with a seal in sealing wax and having attached to it a tag giving the date, name of the case and any details which may be of value, among which should be included a brief description of the exhibit itself. A tape line should always be carried. Six foot steel tapes may be purchased for a very nominal figure and are easy to carry. A supply of chalk for marking the position of a body before its removal, the position of objects for future reference or the outlining of sections of a floor or wall to be later removed will be found handy. A pocket magnifying glass has so many practical uses that it should always be carried as should also a flashlight. A few candles should be carried in the general equipment as a source of light in the event of the failure of the flashlight and will be found useful as a supply of wax for preserving certain classes of footprints, taking impressions of keys and other small objects and for marking on glass. Officers are so frequently unable to give the directions of the compass of places involved in their testimony that the writer is prompted to add a compass to the list. 

Plaster of paris, oil and a camels hair brush should be carried to preserve footprints, tire marks on the road, etc. A good pocket knife, a pair of scissors, a saw, a wrench, and a jimmy should be promptly available and, since the automobile is so generally used, a spade and a few other tools will often be found useful. A supply of paper for drawing diagrams and a camera should be provided but the drawing of diagrams and the taking of photographs should, whenever possible, be left to persons trained to those lines of work. Other articles will suggest themselves but those mentioned are so commonly useful that they should be immediately available in all cases.

The Note Book 

Since the ultimate purpose of an investigation is to produce proof in court the officer should bear in mind that when he takes the witness stand he will have to rely upon his memory or some aid to the memory or a combination of both. A good memory depends upon natural ability, acquired ability and practice, training and effort. To remember a thing we must pay attention and observe so that a mental impression is made, and to this we must add the ability to recall the fact in question and to recognize it as the original impression. It is not at all strange that, with the lapse of time between the investigation and the trial and the many other matters occupying his attention, the officer relying upon his memory alone is uncertain and indefinite in his answers from the witness stand and frequently has to admit that he does not know or does not remember. While even the best of memories will fail at times there is no good excuse for an officer finding himself in such a position. Available proof is scarce enough in the average case without losing part of it by relying upon a doubtful ability to remember. 

The officer who would do good work must absolutely keep a note book record of his investigation and the facts ascertained. A prominent police executive once stated that in his opinion the most efficient weapon of the officer against the criminal is his note book, and I am strongly inclined to agree with him. 

Merely carrying along a note book and lead pencil is not sufficient; it must be constantly used. Full, complete and detailed notes should be made of every step in the investigation, every fact ascertained. The investigator should not stop when he has recorded every fact which he considers of importance but should include every fact even though it may seem wholly unimportant since no person can tell until the case is finished that any particular fact is unimportant. Not only the fact but each detail in relation to the fact should be recorded. Thus if a revolver be found on the scene of a homicide the note book should contain a record of the exact place where it was found and the time it was found and the other persons, if any, present. 

The gun should be briefly but accurately described as to its make, model and calibre, its number or absence of number noted, its condition whether wet or dry, cold or warm, new or old, dull or bright, odor and the presence of any hair, blood, etc., all recorded. So important is the keeping of a complete detailed record that on all lengthy and important investigations one person, preferably one who can write shorthand, should be detailed for that purpose. If more than one investigator is working on the case it will be sufficient if one of them keeps a note book so far as the facts ascertained by them in common is concerned but the notes should be read over and initialed by each of the officers after being verified. In some instances, such as where statements of the accused are made, it is not always possible to make immediate note of the incident but wherever and whenever it is possible to keep the notebook out and make an immediate record that course should be followed. No person would attempt to list the articles in his own living room by looking around and then making a record after leaving the room and the task would be even more difficult, in fact impossible, for a person strange to the room and its contents. 

Every object desired as an exhibit should be identified by a mark either attached to the object or placed on the container in which the object has been sealed. 

Such identifying mark should be distinctive and peculiar to the particular case and a record of the mark used in each case should appear in the case record.

CHAPTER III. 

The Scene of the Crime--Reconstructing the Crime 

The commission of a crime injects into the social atmosphere of the place where it was committed a strange element which may, almost with certainty, be expected to leave definite traces in the nature of evidence not only as to the perpetrator but also as to the manner in which the crime was committed and the movements of the perpetrator and other actors in the crime. The investigator who visits the scene of the crime and learns none of these things has simply overlooked that which was ascertainable. It is a fair general criticism of American police practice that the investigators rush through an investigation too rapidly, overlook much valuable evidence and pay all too little attention to details. To determine what is and to recognize evidence of the crime the investigator must first determine what the conditions were at the scene of the crime prior to its commission. If some person familiar with the place where the crime was perpetrated is available, his information will simplify this prelimininary determination but intelligent and careful observation alone, though requiring greater effort, will secure the desired information. The greatest care must be used, however, to avoid jumping to conclusions or developing theories without first securing the available proof. The writer recalls a case in which an officer wasted a lot of precious time and energy following a theory based upon some blood spots on the carpet only to find out eventually that the spots had been there prior to the crime. In another case, somewhat similar, the officer accepted the explanation of a boarding house keeper that certain red spots on the carpet were caused by a boarder spilling some liquid rouge by accident; it was later learned that, while this statement was true, there were present also some blood spots which, however, were not discovered until it was too late to use that circumstance as effective evidence there being no way, when the full facts were known, to prove that the blood was connected with the crime. In observing and noting the customary objects, those which were present before the crime, it must not be assumed that they are in the same position as that which they occupied before the crime or in their accustomed places. 

Objects are frequently moved in the perpetration of a crime, especially a crime of violence, and their positions are often of the utmost significance. Thus, if it appears that one chair has been pulled up close to another, it tends to show that two persons had seated themselves close together; an ash tray, even though not used, placed close to a chair near a table tends to show that the person in the chair was about to smoke or was known to be a smoker or that he had just been seated. These are but elementary illustrations. Furniture on carpets or rugs, especially if they are heavy, leaves the mark of lengthy standing in the pile of the fabric and, even on bare floors, chairs and other articles, particularly if they are of a character to be moved slightly in use, leave marks showing their accustomed position. Small objects on table tops, mantlepieces and bookcases show a less dusty area where they have been standing than the surrounding surface unless that surface has been recently dusted and, not infrequently, articles will mark their positions by reason of long standing or by reason of having been placed on a varnished surface while it was still new. The position of pictures and other objects against the wall is often literally printed on the wall paper by reason of the fact that the shadow has protected the paper from the fading which has taken place in the exposed portions of the paper. If an object is found out of its proper or accustomed place both the new and the former locations should be noted even though, at the time, no significance is attached to the change. Particular care should be devoted to the doors and windows and it should be observed and noted whether they were locked or unlocked, what type of locks they carried, whether any of the locks have been forced or picked and also the condition of the shades, curtains or other window hangings.

Having determined tentatively which are the customary objects, the investigator may assume, for the time being, that all other objects have been recently placed on the scene and that there is great likelihood that they are connected with the crime or at least with the movements of persons shortly before or after the crime. Glasses or cups used recently for drinking, the condition and contents of fireplaces and waste baskets, injuries to furniture, walls or other objects, signs of spilled liquids, blood or other spots, cigar and cigarette stubs or ashes and burnt matches may, if carefully noted and considered, throw much light on what has recently happened especially if the investigator has developed the habit of constantly observing the actions and conduct of people and the physical signs left by them of their actions. If it is noted that some person using an ash tray has deposited the ashes of his cigar partly in the tray and partly has missed the tray causing some of the ashes to fall on the table that circumstance not only indicates that he was not careful in depositing his ashes, which may be a characteristic piece of carelessness with him or may have been the result of abstraction or emotion, but also tends to indicate that the person reached toward the tray from the side on which the ashes were spilled on the table. Facts such as this may be learned by observation during the daily life of the student of crime detection and by such observation he will become acquainted with the manner in which people customarily perform the various actions and the causes which will lead them to perform acts in other than the usual manner, and, as well, will learn that, even though they may conform to the general manner of performing similar acts, each individual develops characteristic methods and manners of acting. 

Ten men each sharpening a lead pencil with the same pocket knife may go through the same general motions but the ten pencils, when sharpened, may be distinguished from one another. In the prosecution of Kid 

McCoy for the murder of the woman with whom he was living one of the strong circumstances pointing to a violent altercation in the kitchen prior to the shooting was an overturned coffee cup and a salt cellar on the floor; the fact that these articles were found as they had fallen clearly indicated that their overturning was but an incident to a more powerful action in which the parties had participated and distinctly contradicted the defendant's story that no quarrel or altercation of a violent character had taken place in the kitchen. It may be incidentally added that one of the surest means of overcoming a defense is to prove by physical facts that the defense story is false at least in part. Merely observing the position of an occasional object is of little value unless it fits into the picture and is properly accounted for in the reconstruction of the crime. A cigar butt found near the hand of a murdered man may indicate a number of things. It may have been the cigar of the deceased or of his slayer, and a knowledge of the kind of cigars used by the deceased or the killer or both may help to tell which one had smoked it or at least who had furnished it; the teeth marks on the stub er on a cigar holder or pipe may establish the identity of the smoker beyond possibility of doubt. Its condition whether wet or dry may indicate whether it is connected with the crime and, if still warm, will narrow down the possible time of the offense.

A proper study of the scene of a crime requires that it be in the same condition as when the crime was committed or, at least, when it was first discovered and, furthermore, an indiscriminate handling of objects may destroy or confuse the fingerprint evidence. No person can, during the early stages of an investigation, say what fact will later prove to be of importance and the only safe course is to assume that everything is of importance. 

Blood spots, finger and foot prints should be protected from possible injury and, especially out of doors, should be covered up with boxes or other covering to prevent their obliteration until all further reason for their preservation has ceased.

Careful note should also be made of what might be termed negative facts, the absence of what one would expect to find under the circumstances. The absence of any weapon, the absence of blood when blood would from the nature of the wound be expected, the fact that the perpetrator left without washing his hands, absence of fingerprints from places where they would naturally be expected to be found, missing articles, the absence of empty shells on the scene of a shooting, the absence of evidence of a forced entry into a house or room, these are but examples of what have been here termed negative facts which may have strong evidentiary value. 

It is an excellent plan to adopt a general course of procedure in the examination of the scene of a crime to be followed in all cases rather than to rely upon inspiration and a haphazard looking about for evidence.

Scientific investigation requires definite procedure. Thus, a chemist, given a few small crystals for examination and analysis, does not apply tests in a hit and miss fashion but follows a definite plan; he first observes the appearance of the crystals with the naked eye and under the microscope, then a part of the crystals are dissolved and the solution is subjected to various tests, the tests applied being largely determined by the information previously acquired and, even though one or more tests indicate the nature of the crystals, he still continues his tests to verify previous findings. It will be found valuable, when first arriving at the scene of a crime to stop and observe its general appearance as it first comes into view, noting everything in sight before any further and more intimate examination of the individual objects is made. Incidentally this point of view is an excellent one from which to take a photograph, not only for its value as a record but to show the jury just what the place looked like when the officers first arrived. Following this general survey a general course of procedure should be followed rather than that more common procedure of every man dashing about for himself without any definite plan. If the crime was committed in a room it is a good plan to first observe the floor closest at hand and then, following a definite plan to minutely examine the entire floor for any possible marks, stains, objects or other evidence, disregarding for the time being all such larger objects as furniture, a dead body, large pools of blood, etc. Next the larger objects which appear not to have been connected with the crime should be carefully examined to determine whether in fact they have not some bearing on the case. Then the objects on the floor which appear to be connected with the crime should be examined. 

Following this should be an examination of objects appearing on tables, dressers, chairs and other articles of furniture. In all this examination there should be borne in mind what has elsewhere been said about not touching or moving any objects unless it becomes absolutely necessary. Next the walls of the rooms, starting at one point and proceeding carefully about the room from left to right or vice versa. Thus far we have only made a general visual examination in the course of which photographs of valuable discoveries should be made. Next comes the more minute and detailed examination of individual objects. Let us take the examination of a dead body as an example. Its position should be carefully noted, the position of the hands, feet, head and trunk. If this suggestion seems superfluous to the reader the writer responds by referring to a case tried before him in which two of the investigating officers differed as to whether the body was lying upon its back or side and as to the position of the arms and legs, a conflict which never was decided and which introduced much uncertainty into the case. The position of the body with reference to other objects should be carefully noted next, and then should come a more careful examination of the body and its clothing. It is suggested that the examination begin at one extremity and proceed towards the other. Beginning at the head, the covering or, if none, the hair should be observed as to color, whether combed or not, whether it contains blood or dirt. Wounds, even though trivial, appearing to be of recent origin should be most carefully noted. 

Next the face is observed, first as to injuries, dirt, blood, etc., and then as to hair, presence of scars or other distinctive marks of identification, condition of eyes and mouth as to being open, clean or dirty, and the expression on the face to be followed by an observation of as much of the neck and chest as is visible. The hands should come in for careful attention. An entire chapter might be written upon the subject of the examination of hands alone. The investigator should at least observe whether the hands are clean or dirty and, if the latter, the nature and location of the foreign matter, the condition of the hands as indicating whether the person was accustomed to hard work or not, the position of the fingers and the flexion of the wrists, old scars and marks of value for purposes of identification, and marks, scratches and wounds of recent origin. The finger nails should receive special attention. While it is generally known that small particles of oil or any other substance handled have the distinct habit of getting under the finger nails and staying there even in spite of efforts to clean the nails, very little attention has been paid in this country to this source of information. Merely because the amount of foreign matter under the finger nail is small may be the reason for ignoring it, but in fact this is no reason at all, for by the microscope, spectroscope, chemistry, biology and bacteriology scientists are accustomed to the examination of even smaller quantities with accuracy, and the material found under finger nails may tell us not only where the deceased has been, what work he has been doing, but may contain particles of blood, cuticle or other substance connected directly with the crime. What has been said about the finger nails applies also to an examination of the shoes, particularly the soles, the cracks or crevices in the leather, and the space between the upper and the sole, the inside of pockets, and in fact to all places where small particles of foreign matter may gather. 

The clothing on the body should be observed, its arrangement or disarrangement, whether wet or dry, clean or dirty, the presence of visible spots, the color of the various pieces of apparel, the material and its pattern and name of maker, if any, and any observations as to its condition. When more than one investigator takes part in an investigation of the scene of a crime, every important discovery should be communicated to the other investigators so that they may participate in the discovery and be able to give the same testimony as the original discoverer. It is elementary that the testimony of two witnesses to the same fact is stronger than that of but one, and there is always a possibility that, when the trial arrives, the officer who made an important discovery may, by reason of illness, absence or other cause, be unobtainable as a witness. Furthermore there is less likelihood of a fact being forgotten or inaccurately observed if more than one person participates. 

The contents of articles of furniture-desks, dressers, etc.-of waste baskets, cuspidors or any other containers should be carefully noted, letters and memoranda carefully read and all exhibits thus secured carefully preserved.

Above all the investigation must be made as soon after the crime is reported as possible. Not only do conditions change by mere lapse of time but articles may be carelessly or thoughtlessly moved and there have been many cases in which evidence has been destroyed or "planted" by interested persons.

Exhibits 

All objects gathered to be used as evidence upon the trial should be handled with the greatest care and every effort made to deliver them into court in the same condition in which they were found and to keep such trace and record of all exhibits that they may be identified when produced in court. Any article which may by any possibility bear fingerprints or any foreign substance of evidentiary value should never be handled with the naked hands, but should be picked up with a clean cloth or paper and placed in a box or other container which is free from dirt, and so handled and packed that it can not be injured and that the marks or foreign substance cannot rub off. Since the automobile is now so generally used there is no good reason why the cars allotted to investigating officers should not carry a supply of proper containers for exhibits. While boxes and cotton wool in which to pack exhibits have the disadvantage of being somewhat bulky this is more than overcome by the certainty of preservation in the best possible manner. Bullets and other small objects 

Bullets and other small objects can be preserved in this manner but, since such objects will be looked at and passed from hand to hand quite a few times before the trial is concluded, and especially since some jurors have a distinct dislike to handle bullets, pieces of bone, etc., taken from human bodies, it is advisable to place and keep such object in small clean glass vials with a piece of clean cotton under and above the object to keep it from moving about. Exhibits enclosed in such vials may quite easily be examined without removing them and with less chance of their being lost or confused with some other exhibit. It is the writer's personal opinion that the glass container system is superior to any other, but, of course, the glass containers should be protected against breakage. Regardless of what the exhibit may be it should be the rule that it be handled as little as possible and that no person should be allowed to handle an exhibit whose only interest is curiosity. When placed in its container a notation should be made on the package or a tag attached thereto of the nature of the exhibit, the name of the case, where and the date and time when it was found, and this memorandum should be signed by all of the officers present, and the package should then be sealed with sealing wax so that it cannot be opened without breaking the seal. Incidentally the impression in the wax of the wet thumb of one of the officers is a good means of identification. Whenever an exhibit is delivered from one person to another the fact of such transfer should be noted on the container or tag attached to the exhibit and a receipt delivered by the recipient, all receipts to be placed in the files of the case. Each container of an exhibit should bear a distinctive mark of identification which mark should not be the worn out cross or letter "X" but should be distinctive for each case and every exhibit in the same case should be identified by the same mark. Objects which cannot be placed in containers should be wrapped in clean paper, if possible, and properly tagged being first protected by a wrapping of tissue paper or cotton if the article requires such protection. In wrapping and packing hats and clothing it is important to preserve the exhibits so that, when presented in court, they are not a mass of wrinkles and all out of shape as the result of careless packing and preservation. All exhibits should be tagged as above noted. Under no circumstances should a bullet, revolver shell or any other object which may bear marks or foreign substances requiring expert examination, be marked by scratching an identifying mark on its surface. While it may seem an unnecessary caution it is advised that all writing on the tags or containers be legible and in ink or other permanent record. 

Reconstructing the Crime

It is of material assistance to the investigator if he can with reasonable accuracy determine the movements of the criminal and the course followed by him in the perpetration of the crime. In reconstructing the crime the investigator will have poor success if he places himself in the position of the criminal, and then assumes

that the criminal has acted as he, the investigator, would act. The question is not how would someone else have acted but, how did the perpetrator of the crime act? If one knows the perpetrator and the character of man he is, one may with considerable success reason what his actions and conduct were under given circumstances. 

This principle is illustrated by the story of the farm hand who had speedily found a mule which had strayed and who explained his success thusly: "I figured out where I would go if I was a straying mule and I went there and there the mule was." In tracing the probable course and conduct of the perpetrator of a crime his apparent movements must not be rejected merely because they seem foolish or unreasonable. The investigator must be ever mindful that the average criminal is not overburdened with intelligence or good judgment, and that the perpetrator of the crime may be a madman, a degenerate or a fool. In the reconstruction process one must also consider the possibility that the unlawful act may have been committed by more than one person or that one or more persons may have aided the actual perpetrator. The means of arrival and the means and course of departure from the scene of the crime should be considered. In checking the flight of the offender recourse should be had to taxi drivers, railroads, airports and other methods of conveyance. 

If the perpetrator is known, a check of his neighbors and associates, especially his wife or sweetheart, and a close check on the mail or telegrams delivered to them, are usually productive of results. In reconstructing a crime, however, we must not seek to adopt merely a probability but the effort should be to truly reconstruct the crime and in so doing one must not assume as a fact anything which cannot be proven nor can one assume that a fact did not exist unless proof of its absence is available. The reconstruction idea is particular valuable in analyzing the statements of the defendant and other witnesses. Did they, according to their stories, act consistently with the facts as they must have existed at the time; what must their conduct necessarily have been under certain conditions which the proof shows did exist; if the conduct of an individual was out of the ordinary, what caused him to act as he did? 

Thus, the claim of an automobile driver that he did not see the approaching danger until he was just entering the intersection may be tested by viewing the scene to determine the reasonableness of the story and if, as occurred in a recent case, the tire marks on the roadway showed that the car had begun to "burn rubber" from locked brakes over twenty feet from the intersection we know, not only that the story of the driver is not true but that he has either deliberately falsified, itself a proof of guilt, or that his statements as to what occurred cannot be relied upon for some other reason. 

Until we have proof of what occurred our conception cf the crime is mostly a matter of theory but, while this theory should be kept in mind and followed as long as the proof is consistent with it, we must also appreciate that it is but a theory which must be abandoned as soon as the proof shows that it is inconsistent with the facts. 

The ability to judge and appreciate just when a theory should be abandoned is a valuable asset to the investigator and its exercise depends largely upon a willingness to submerge personal pride of opinion once formed and a willingness to concede that all theories are but theories and may not be well founded.

CHAPTER IV. 

Evidence of Specific Crimes--Proof of Motive 

To understand what evidence is necessary to prove a case in a particular instance the officer should be sufficiently acquainted with the law of crimes to know the necessary elements of the crime, known in law as the corpus delicti. Every crime is composed of certain elements or ingredients each and every one of which must be present, and must be proved, before the defendant can be convicted. All crimes must consist of an act or negligence and criminal intent. Criminal intent does not mean an intent to commit crime nor need it even be an evil intent; a person is said to act with criminal intent when he voluntarily and intentionally so conducts himself as to violate a law defining a crime. Many crimes require and contain as an element what is known as specific intent. Specific intent exists when the person not only does the act in question intentionally but does so with a specific object or purpose in mind, such object or purpose being an indispensable element of the crime. Thus, in forgery and obtaining money by false pretenses there must exist an intent to defraud and in burglary the entry must have been made with intent to steal or commit some felony.

Since intent lies within the mind we can prove intent only by circumstantial evidence. The conduct of the accused both before and after the offense, preparation for the commission of the crime, the commission of other similar offenses, declarations by the accused prior or at the time of the offense, the use of particular means to

CRIMINAL INVESTIGATION 

commit the act, all tend to establish the intent. The officer must, however, avoid jumping to conclusions, and ever remember that he must produce evidence which will convince any rational person just what the intent was. If a man enters a dwelling of a stranger in the latter's absence, gathers up all the valuables in sight and carries them away with him, the circumstances, in the absence of other evidence would clearly show an intent to steal and a jury would be justified in concluding that such intent existed at the time the building was entered and that burglary had been committed. If, however, even though he steals something after entering a building, a man enters that building under circumstances which leave in doubt the question as to whether the intent to steal existed at the time of the entry or was formed for the first time after he was inside the building, then further evidence must be secured to prove the burglarious intent at the time of entering the building. This evidence may consist in proof of the use of burglar tools, making of a forced entry, an entry at an unusual hour, other similar entries of buildings accompanied by thefts, the carrying into the building of a suitcase or sack to carry off the loot. proof of prior conduct or declarations evidencing intent or the fact that the thief had no excuse or reason for being in the building or had been ordered to remain out of the building or a false explanation, after his arrest, as to his presence in the building and reason therefor. 

Proof of Motive 

Evidence of motive is never necessary but is oftentimes valuable in determining the intent with which an act was done or in identifying the perpetrator and in murder cases it tends to prove the degree of the crime. 

Motive, like intent, is shown by circumstances and all evidence which can be secured which tends to show the possible reason or cause which might have induced the accused to commit the crime in question should be carefully gathered even though this involves the interviewing of many witnesses. In crimes for profit, such as theft, burglary and robbery, inquiry should be made to determine whether the prisoner has been in need of money, whether he has been spending money more freely than his honest earning power would justify, whether he has been borrowing money or selling articles of personal property to obtain money, whether he owes bills, whether any demands have been made upon him for money. Whenever men, apparently supplied with money are found hanging about pool halls and places of amusement and investigation discloses that they are not engaged in any honest gainful employment, the police are justified in suspecting that they have no honest purpose: it may be that they are in fact honest but an investigation is worth while.

Murder and attempts to commit murder may be prompted by any one of many causes but, speaking generally, they are crimes of emotion, revenge or passion or for the purpose of acquiring property either immediately or by inheritance or eliminating an obstacle to their desires through the death of the intended victim. Careful inquiry should be made as to all persons with whom the deceased may have had any quarrels or prior difficulties, not only those of recent date but during a considerable period of time previous to the affair in question. One of the biggest mistakes is to stop further inquiry along this line when it is found that a particular suspect had a serious grievance against the victim; he may have been the perpetrator but then again he may be wholly innocent. There is another reason why full investigation should be made to determine all the persons who might have had a motive for the felonious attempt on life. Even though the actual culprit has been arrested and placed on trial, it is not at all unlkely that the defense may seek to throw suspicion upon someone else who may also have had a motive for committing the offense and, if the investigation has been complete, the officer will be prepared to meet such a line of evidence by proof that, though motive existed, such other person was in fact free from guilt. It may be here added that it not infrequently happens that in proving the identity of the perpetrator of a crime the best method is by proof that all other possible perpetrators had nothing to do with it. On one occasion the author, while district attorney in the state of Wisconsin, prosecuted a man for murder of a young boy whose body was found floating in a narrow stream with the neck broken some twelve hours after he was last seen alive.

The entire case, except for the evidence furnishd by the body itself was circumstantial. The cause of death, i. e. that it was not due to drowning or by the boy diving into the water and breaking his neck but that he was killed on shore and then thrown into the water, was proved by the process of elimination showing that death on shore was the only reasonable deduction. The crime was tied to the defendant by showing not only that he was the only one who had a motive for killing the boy but by other proof showing that no person other than the accused and his wife was sufficiently close to the possible scene to have committed the crime. This proof was simplified by the fact that the crime was committed in a sparsely settled part of the county but required a large amount of painstaking work which was rewarded by the conviction of the defendant by proof so conclusive that no one has ever doubted his guilt.

Threats, quarrels and prior difficulties with the victim of a felonious assault likely to produce death should be investigated in detail. It is not enough to discover that such incidents have occurred; the investigator must secure all the details of such incidents, the motives back of them and the merits of the contenders. Sometimes the prior difficulty is one involving a person who has nothing to do with the subsequent crime, as for example, crimes of vendetta or revenge where the slayer kills to avenge a wrong done to some one near and dear to him. Murder is often committed to cover up some other crime, to silence a person who may be dangerous

to the life or peace of the killer or who has threatened to expose the killer to the police or to public disgrace. 

Men, becoming entangled with women or mistresses, not infrequently kill them to put them out of the way and men have killed their wives for similar reasons. Wife killers are frequently motivated by a desire for financial benefit, either the acquisition of the property of the wife or the collection of insurance upon her life and, in the latter instance it is quite common to find that the life of the wife was insured at the solicitation of the husband, that the insurance was taken out but a comparatively short time before the death and for an amount in excess of that consistent with the marital and financial circumstances of the parties. 

One class of murders is that in which a police officer is shot by a pedestrian or the occupant of an automobile whom he has stopped for investigation or some minor infraction of the law. In such a case we can assume with assurance that the person doing the shooting is wanted somewhere on a serious felony charge or has committed a felony; in any event the shooting is to prevent an arrest and the reason for fearing an arrest should be definitely fixed so that it can be proved at the trial, for motive is not only a circumstance tending to prove guilt but, where the crime is one divided into degrees, it has a bearing upon the question of degree and has always an influence upon the penalty imposed. 

Arson may be committed for the purpose of collecting insurance upon the property sought to be burned, to cover and hide the traces of some other crime, frequently in an attempt to cover up a murder and destroy the body or at least its identity, to destroy incriminating records contained in the building and not infrequently as a means of revenge or for the purpose of inspiring fear in the victim. It is also committed as a step in the perpetration of another crime. Thus in a famous insurance fraud a building was burned under circumstances leading to the belief that the owner was in the building. When the ashes were searched a charred human body corresponding closely to that of the owner was found. This was followed by a claim on the insurance company carrying life insurance on the supposed deceased owner. A clever investigator proved by a dentist who had done work for the owner of the building that the body was that of another person. 

Motive, as well as intent, can often be proved by evidence that the accused had previously made unsuccessful attempts to commit the crime which he later successfully perpetrated. 

Motive and intent can also be frequently shown by proof that the prisoner had in his possession, or had ready access to, the particular means or instruments used to commit the crime charged. 

In considering all of the possible motives which may have caused a particular crime it is of inestimable value if the investigator is familiar with or has access to the facts of crimes which have occurred in the past. 

Crimes are committed today from similar motives and by similar modus operandi as have figured in similar crimes in the past; in fact it is uncanny how frequently crimes are committed which in most particulars resemble crimes committed many years ago. Just as an instance we may mention that, in cases in which a husband has killed his wife and concealed the body, it is his common explanation that the wife has gone on a visit to a particular place or person and, in almost every instance it will be found not only easy to prove that this statement is a falsehood but, when the defendant is confronted with his barefaced attempt at fraud, he can almost always be counted upon to make further incriminating statements. Witness also that in killings by men who have studied anatomy it seems to be a characteristic to bury the body on the premises; the well known cases of Dr. Webster, Dr. Crippen and Dr. 

Young, though committed years apart, are instances. 

Bunco games, technically known as larceny by trick and device, are merely repetitions of former bunco games with a few new alterations and modern trimmings and often are merely the same old game. The well equipped bureau of investigation should have as complete a case history of past crimes as can possibly be collected. Even detective fiction should not be overlooked for the author is after all merely describing what to him is a good method of committing a crime and, also, the criminal frequently gets his ideas from a story he has read and either follows the modus operandi set forth in the story or modifies it by supposed improvements of his own. By knowing the facts of former cases we can frequently, when we encounter a case which appears puzzling, direct our investigation along the assumption that the criminal has proceeded in the present case as did the perpetrator of some case in the past which it strongly resembles. Thefts

To constitute a larceny it must appear from the circumstances that it was the intent of the thief to permanently deprive the owner of his property and the proof should not only tend to prove this intent but proof should be available to rebut any possible defense that the property was only taken for a temporary purpose with the intent to return the same after such purpose had been served. The value of the property, the manner in which it was taken, and the character of the property must be established at the trial and have an important bearing on the question as to whether the crime was a felony or misdemeanor. Value may be proved by the testimony of the owner but this proof, if controverted as it often is when the value is close to the border line between grand and petit larceny, is not very strong. The investigator should therefore secure independent evidence of value. Value means market value, the price at which similar articles are sold in the open market in the same vicinity. Thus, if the article stolen is a piece of tapestry, it should be shown to dealers in similar tapestries in the same community and a statement secured from them as to its retail selling

In larceny it must be proved that the property belongs to someone other than the accused and that it was taken without permission. Where property is under the control or possession of some person other than the actual owner the evidence of both such person and the owner that the property was taken without the consent of either must be secured. 

In bunco cases every little detail of the manner of operating should be secured. Evidence of other similar offenses by the same person should be gathered with the same regard to detail as in the case on which the accused was arrested. This type of offense is characterized by the fact that falsehood and deception is used. 

The trained officer will see this element in a flash but, to convince a jury, the most conclusive evidence of the falsity of the representations made to the victim should be secured. Every false statement made in the perpetration of the crime should be definitely proven to be false. If a mechanical device, such as a fake money making machine, is used in the crime, the investigator should become so familiar with its operation and demonstration that the working of the fraud can be demonstrated by operating the machine in the court room. It is well to bear in mind that the machine should be in working condition when brought into the court room for this purpose; this caution may seem unnecessary but is given here because of an embarassing experience in this line which was only saved by the fact that, fortunately, the author was able to repair the machine and ultimately make it work in the presence of the jury. 

The falsity of representations can frequently be shown by proof that the statements could not possibly be true, a fact frequently proved by experiments conducted either in or out of court. 

In cases of obtaining property by false pretenses we must likewise be prepared to prove the falsity of the representations. It must be borne in mind that in this crime the false representations must be as to a past or present fact. Expressions of opinion or statements as to the future are not false pretenses. The evidence must also show that the parting by the victim with his property was because of the false representations and not for some other reason. This class of cases generally presents a picture of a crooked trade. 

Embezzlement is the fraudulent conversion of property which has been intrusted by the owner to some other person. The property is intrusted to some person as agent, bailee or trustee to keep for the owner or to handle in a particular way in accordance with the wishes of the owner; the property is honestly received but thereafter the receiver forms the fraudulent intent and appropriates the property to his own use or to some use other than that for which it was given to him. This relation of trust and confidence must be proved. The conversion is easily proved if evidence can be secured showing just what the embezzler did with the money, but frequently such proof can not be secured. In such cases the fraudulent appropriation may be shown by proof that, although property was intrusted to the accused to be used for a specified purpose within a definite time, he did not perform that duty, by proof that, upon his failure to perform such duty a demand was made upon him for a return of the property intrusted and that he failed or refused to return it, that he denied ever receiving it, that he made false entries in the firm books in relation to the transaction, that he made deposits in his own bank account corresponding in time and amount with his receipt of the money in question or any other facts and circumstances tending to prove the fraudulent conversion. Burglary

While a burglary is complete as soon as the burglarious entry has been effected, it is usually followed by a theft or some felony which may be charged as a separate crime but in any event should be investigated thoroughly as it forms a part of the evidence under the burglary charge and is particularly valuable as proof of the intent. To constitute a burglary the entry must be made with the intent to commit a larceny or some felony.

In investigating the entry careful examination should be made to determine the place at which the entry was made and the means used to effect an entrance. If a window or door screen has been cut, the length of the cut, its direction and height from the ground at both ends of the cut should be carefully observed. In cutting a screen with a knife, the method commonly used, it will be noticed that the severed strands of the screen are bent in a direction away from the place where the cut was begun. The foregoing factors tend to prove the movement made in the cutting, whether with the right or left hand and give some idea as to the height of the burglar; these factors can be made clear by experiments conducted by the investigator in an effort to make an identical cut in a similar screen. Normally a person making a cut in a screen begins at a point the height of his eyes and cuts downward and towards the side of the hand used. If an instrument is used as a jimmy to pry open a door or window, marks are usually left on the woodwork which can be compared with similar tools found in the possession of suspects or in places to which a suspect has had access. As a general rule tools used as jimmies have some peculiarities due to prior use and abuse and frequently these peculiarities are impressed in the places they are applied. A careful examination should be made for fingerprints and footprints, and for small articles such as matches and cigarette stubs left by the burglar as they all are valuable as means of identifying the perpetrator. While it is not at all conclusive that a suspect has on his person matches and cigarettes of the same kind as the burnt matches and cigarettes left on the job, it is a circumstance which may swing a jury in an otherwise

doubtful case. In burglary investigations great care should be observed to ascertain in detail the exact method of procedure since the solution of one burglary often solves a series of burglaries. Each burglar has his method of operation which usually differs, though sometimes in only a small detail, from the methods of others. 

Diagrams and photographs of the premises are often of great value in illustrating the oral evidence before the jury. Where an entry is made by use of some tool not ordinarily used in burglaries of similar premises it will be usually discovered that the burglar was more or less of an amateur who used tools of his regular trade. Thus a machinist in drilling a hole in a door is more likely to use one of the drills customarily used by machinists while à carpenter, under similar conditions, would use one of his augurs; a carpenter may be expected to use a sharp chisel while a machinist or unskilled workman is likely to use a dull chisel because that is probably the kind of tool available to him. The point of attack upon a door or window often indicates whether the burglar is skilled or not and, especially when an attack is made on a door to draw a bolt or turn a lock by reaching through a hole in the door, the point at which the hole is made and the accuracy with which it is placed to effect the object throws light upon the question as to whether the burglar was familiar with the location of the lock. Careful record should also be made of the places evidently searched for loot by the burglar and the type of goods stolen and the class of property which the burglar evidently had no use for; these are factors of modus operandi which may be of great value later on. Robbery

Robbery may roughly be defined to be a larceny accomplished by means of force or fear. Here the question of the identification of the robber is of the utmost importance. At the earliest opportunity the victims should be questioned for as detailed a description of the robbers as possible, but the utmost caution must here be used. Some persons hestitate to identify anyone as the person who robbed them while others are ready to identify almost anyone exhibited to them as a suspect. It is rather to be suspected that a description of the robber by the victim will not be accurate or correct due to the excitement or fear incident to the occasion, the poor powers of observation of the average individual and the likelihood of the victim to state his beliefs rather than his recollections. A thirty-two calibre revolver a few inches from a person's face is likely to appear to be at least a forty-five. Convicts attempting an escape have been known to successfully intimidate their guards by displaying dummy pistols cut out of wood and covered with tin foil. Descriptions of the hold-up men by their victims should be received with caution and every effort should be made to determine the extent to which they may be relied upon. If the witness appears to be a person whose descriptions are likely to be inaccurate or misleading it should be impressed upon him that he should give only those matters of description as to which he is positive and that he be requested to indicate those matters as to which he is uncertain. A similar course should be observed even with the witness who seems reliable. Care should be taken to get the latter type of witness in particular to be scrupulously exact in describing the robber. Do not stop with the witness's statement that the robber wore a gray cap; get a detailed description, whether it was a plain gray or a pattern or mixture, whether it was one shade of gray or another, and similar inquiry should be made as to all the items of description. Forgery 

In forgery we must prove the specific intent to defraud and the document or instrument must be one capable on its face of being used to perpetrate a fraud. 

Also the document may be a forgery in whole or in part; a material alteration of a valid and genuine document is not at all uncommon. One subject which is often overlooked is the necessity of proof that the forger was acting without authority. Thus, in the case of the forgery of the signatures to a deed, the prosecution must prove not only that the defendant signed the names but also that he was not authorized to do so. 

This proof is usually furnished by the testimony of the persons whose names were forged that they had given no such authority, but may be proved by circumstantial evidence that it was impossible for them to have given such authority because of absence from the city, or by reason of mental or physical condition, or that the persons and defendant were strangers.

In the case of check forgers who have recently arrived in town, "paperhangers" of the traveler type, it will be usually discovered that they have issued checks over the route they have followed and, if they have left town, a knowledge of the direction from which they came will usually indicate the direction in which they are going. Evidence of other forgeries is admissible to prove intent to defraud and should also be given to the judge to be considered at the time of sentence. Furthermore, a forger who knows that the local police are aware of the trail of checks he has left fully realizes that his best course is to plead guilty to the local charges.

Under a charge of uttering a forgery evidence must be secured to prove that the accused knew that the forgery uttered by him was in fact a forgery. This is comparatively a simple matter if it can be proved that the defendant was the actual forger, but it is often quite difficult to prove knowledge where the check uttered was written by some other person. If other similar checks were uttered, proof of the other transactions, prior to the check involved in the prosecution, is admissible to prove not only the intent to defraud but also the element of knowledge. Any evidence which tends to show this element of knowledge should be gathered for the most common defense to a charge of uttering a forgery is the claim that the defendant received the

Conspiracy may practically be defined as an agreement between two or more persons to do an unlawful act (usually a crime) or a lawful act by unlawful means. While the criminal agreement or partnership can be proved by evidence of a witness or witnesses who heard the conspirators enter into the agreement, it may, and usually must, be proved by circumstantial evidence. This means, to the investigator, that he must gather every available bit of evidence tending to prove that the parties were undoubtedly cooperating in pursuance of a common object and that they were acting by agreement. Frequent meetings of the parties, assisting one another in a manner indicating a prior agreement, the parties each individually doing something or procuring something needed in the accomplishment of the object, and then pooling their efforts so that the total of the contributions of each shall fit in with one another and all tend to accomplish the object in view all tend to prove conspiracy.

The mere agreement to commit, for example, a felony is not punishable. There must be in addition an overt act, an act which shows that the parties to the agreement meant to carry it out and that they have begun to put their plan into effect. The overt act need not be an unlawful act; the only requirement of the law is that it must be an act towards accomplishing the object of the conspiracy. Purchasing revolvers by one of the gang to be used in carrying out a robbery agreed upon would be an overt act; so also would be the purchase of a can of gasoline if the crime agreed upon were arson.

Rape The alleged victim of a rape should be carefully questioned. Her story should be taken down, by a shorthand reporter if possible, with the utmost detail. 

When did she first meet the accused, when did he first make any suggestive advances, the full details of the act charged. The investigator must, even though it be a delicate subject, get every possible detail not only to prepare his case but to guard against the possibility of a false charge. The prosecutrix should be examined physically by a competent physician at the earliest possible moment. If the attack was promptly reported the clothing of the prosecutrix should be submitted to an expert to determine whether there are any evidences of semen, blood or other foreign substance of evidentiary value. In any event the under and outer clothing of the female should be preserved and brought into court without any washing or other change in its condition.

Since the defense in this class of cases so often claims that the charge is a "frame-up" the possibility of such being the case should be carefully looked into. Women and girls not infrequently make such charges prompted by a desire for revenge, blackmail or at the suggestion of some other person using them as a tool. Also when a girl is picked up by juvenile officers and found not to be a virgin the girl is likely, when questioned as to who caused her condition, to protect and conceal the identity of the guilty party who may be a sweetheart or pseudo-sweetheart, to accuse an innocent person. If the suggestion as to taking a complete and detailed statement from the prosecutrix is followed, an investigation of her statements will usually make it a simple matter to determine whether her story is reliable. Evidence should also be secured to meet a false claim of "frame-up." 

While the consent of the female is immaterial in cases of statutory rape, it is a complete defense to a charge of forcible rape. Under the latter charge it must be proved that the female did all that she reasonably could and, often in statutory rape, the law permits the defense do to prevent the sexual act. In cases of forcible rape to introduce evidence that the prosecutrix had a bad is permitted in the theory that it is more likely that an reputation for chastity and that she was unchaste; this unchaste woman would consent to an act of sexual intercourse. Since such evidence will have to be met the investigator should include this subject in his investigations, not only for the information of the prosecuting attorney, but that false evidence on this question may be met and rebutted. Arson

In every case of arson the investigator should ascertain who is the owner of the property, who occupied the property at the time of the fire and for a short time prior thereto, who has any interest in the property and who, if anyone was in the building at the time of the fire. These matters are not only important and necessary as a matter of law, but tend to shed light on the possible perpetrator and his motive. The time of the fire should be fixed as definitely as possible as the degree of the crime may depend on this factor. Many incendiary fires are set by devices and contrivances which permit the incendiary to start them and get far away before the fire breaks out. Careful and painstaking examinations should be made to determine just how the fire was set, how many separate fires were started, what materials were used and where they were obtained and particular note taken of anything which seems unusual even though it may appear to have nothing to do with the fire. A careful check should be made for evidence to prove that the fire did not owe its origin to natural or accidental causes; every possible imaginable cause for the fire should be investigated so that any suggestion made at the trial that the fire was caused by defective wiring or other accidental cause can be promptly disproved. The financial condition of the suspect and

1 other possible reasons for destroying the property, amount of insurance on the property and when it was secured and the nature and value of the property insured are all important factors in arson cases. 

Receiving Stolen Property 

This crime has as its element: (1) that the property in question was stolen or embezzled, (2) that it was received by the accused, (3) that at the time he received it he knew that it was stolen or embezzled, and (4) the intent on the part of the accused to convert the property to his own use or to keep it from the owner. 

Proof of the stolen character of the property may be furnished by proof of the theft by the testimony of the owner or by that of the thieves who, in a prosecution for this offense, are not accomplices. The receipt of the property may be proved by the testimony of the thieves or the evidence that the property was found in the possession of the accused. Care must be taken, when relying upon possession as proof of the element of receipt, that it is truly possession of the accused that is proved. Thus, if a man occupy a room alone, property found in that room is prima facie in his possession even though concealed but, if the room be occupied by more than one person, the mere finding of property in the room amounts to proof only that it is in the possession of one of the occupants and no legal inference can be drawn that it was in the possession of any particular one of the occupants. In the latter case it would be necessary to prove which of the occupants was the possessor, either by securing the testimony of all of the other occupants and thus by process of elimination, proving the fact, or by other evidence, as for example, finger prints of the accused on the property, significant circumstances such as finding it contained in luggage of the accused or proof that the property had at one time been seen in the possession of the accused, showing him to be the possessor. Knowledge of the stolen character may be proved by the testimony of the thieves that, at the time the accused received the property he was directly or indirectly informed that the property had been obtained by theft. Knowledge may also be proved, circumstantially, by proof that the property was bought at a ridiculously low figure, that it was bought from known thieves or irresponsible parties or that it was not bought at a regular place of business or that it was bought from a person who could not reasonably be taken as a person having a legitimate right to sell the same or who could not reasonably be believed in his statements as to how he came to be the seller of the property or that the circumstances of the receiving or buying the property tend to prove the illegal character of the transaction. The intent to convert the property or to keep it from the owner is usually provable only by the circumstances under which the property was kept or hidden and, in some cases, by evidence of efforts to dispose of the same. The crime of receiving stolen property is one of the most difficult crimes of which to convict an accused. The testimony of the thieves, if produced, does not carry much weight with a jury, the accused generally has a legitimate business and produces a fairly plausible explanation of how he came by the property or denies absolutely all knowledge of how it came to be where it was found tial evidence. Consequently all possible effort should and the case is usually won or lost by the circumstanbe made to secure every bit of available proof, going into the history and activities of the accused with great detail and securing from him and all possible witnesses the most complete statements possible. In this class of case the accused usually protests his innocence quite vigorously and a lengthy statement can usually be secured from him by careful questioning and requests for details. False statements by persons accused of this crime contribute much toward their ultimate conviction. Murder

Felonious homicides consist of two elements-death of a human being and the criminal agency causing death-constituting the corpus delicti. Of the death and its causes more will be said under another heading. 

The investigation should be directed to finding the instrument causing death or any other means used to that end. This is of especial importance when the cause of death is not immediately apparent, but we cannot stop to determine this point and let every other line of investigation wait. If the investigation at the scene of the homicide is as complete as it should be the investigator will have at his command every fact which appears on the scene and which will have a bearing upon his individual problems. The location and an accurate description should be recorded of every wound or injury, however slight, appearing on the body. The position of the body, the location of customary objects such as furniture and the location of occasional objects such as cigar ashes, burnt cigars and cigarettes, drinking glasses, papers, handkerchiefs, weapons, etc., are worthy of the most careful attention. 

As the body is frequently removed before the investigation is complete it is usually valuable to mark the outline of the body in the position in which it was found with chalk or other material so that its position may be beyond question during the remainder of the investigation. 

Before anything is touched by anyone photographs should be taken showing the position of the body, the wounds on the body and the objects in its immediate vicinity and any additional photographs which might by any possibility be of value in the future should also be taken. A draftsman or surveyor should then make a complete set of measurements so that he can prepare a diagram for the trial showing the location of the body and any and all surrounding objects. The fingerprint expert should next make his examination but, under no circumstance should any object be picked up or moved until a careful record has been made of its exact

Pools of blood, their size, shape, depth, location and condition as to whether wet or dry, congealed or coagulated should be noted and the same applies to rops and spatters. This examination should be made not only about the body, but at any place in the vicinity which might possibly have such traces. Thus, if the homicide occurred in a house, important evidence might be overlooked if every room, its walls and floors were not examined.

If any one thing is more important than any other it is that of visible marks connected with the crime.

As once stated by a famous investigator, an investigation of the scene of the homicide should be so complete that, were another investigator to go over the same scene immediately after he would not find anything relevant to the homicide which had not been discovered by his predecessor. This high ideal, which might as well be applied to all crimes, may be impossible of attainment, but the closer the investigator approaches it the more certain will be the result of the trial. Manslaughter

Voluntary manslaughter, the killing in the heat of passion or upon a sudden quarrel, differs from murder principally in that the elements of malice and premeditation are absent, and the course of the investigation is much the same as murder. The cause of the anger and the quarrel, if any, should be carefully looked into. Instances have been known wherein a person, desiring to kill another and at the same time avoid the consequences of murder, has forced a quarrel to create the appearance of killing by necessity. A check-up of the prior relations of the parties, possible motive, premeditation and preparation should not be overlooked.

Involuntary manslaughter, the killing of a human being by an unlawful act not amounting to a felony or in the commission of a lawful act likely to cause death either by unlawful means or through negligence may be committed in so many different ways that no general suggestions can be offered other than that the same care be used to secure every available bit of evidence.

A large percentage of homicide cases is chargeable to automobile collisions with pedestrians or other vehicles and sometimes the death of passengers riding in automobiles is involved. The car or cars involved in the accident should be examined with the greatest care to determine the condition of the steering gear, whether the car was in gear after the accident and in which gear if any, and whether the emergency brake was set.

If the car can be driven, immediate tests should be made to determine whether any functioning part of the car was out of condition prior to the accident. Many cars are habitually driven with defective brakes or defective operating or control devices. The defendant can always be relied upon to produce some reason for the collision and an examination of the car or cars involved will often prove or disprove the story of the accused. The lights on the car should be examined to determine whether they are burning, whether they will burn and, if any will not burn, it should be determined whether the failure to burn is due to injuries to the lighting system due to the accident. In one case the accused was acquitted because the jury believed the story of the defendant that the automobile with which he collided had but one headlight burning and that he thought it was a motorcycle and believed that the course he was taking would carry him safely by. An investigation by the officers might have corroborated or disproved this story. If the officer arrives on the scene of a night-time collision within a few minutes after it occurs, the heat of the lamp may still be sufficiently noticeable to prove that it was burning at the time of the accident. Particular notice should be paid to every injury to the automobile involved. The time to do this is before it is hauled or driven away from the scene and before repairs can be made or incriminating evidence destroyed. The writer recalls a case in which conclusive evidence of the innocence of a driver charged with manslaughter was nearly lost because some injuries to one of the wheels was covered up with paint after the accident by a garage employee; fortunately the injuries had been carefully observed by a reliable witness immediately after the accident. 

The tires should be examined to determine the kind of tires, kind of tread, condition of the tread and whether they bear any marks chargeable to the accident. Most collisions are preceded by the driver putting on his brakes and the most significant and important evidence can be gathered from the marks on the road. A diagram of the vicinity should be made showing all such marks and all other objects which might have any bearing on the questions involved. The length of the marks and distances on the scene should be accurately measured with a tape line. The location of broken glass from a car with reference to the spot at which the collision occurred frequently sheds much light on the circumstances of the accident. Where glass is broken in more than one car involved in the collision and where different kinds of glass come from even one car each different exhibit should be separately marked and its exact position noted. In collision cases we find the most honest witnesses differing as to what occurred and it is the general rule that, to determine the actual facts we must rely on circumstantial evidence and physical facts. In the case of a night-

time collision carefully observe the number, location and intensity of all artificial lighting including the height and spacing of lighting posts. 

Connecting Links 

The prime requisite for the admissibility of evidence is that the proffered evidence be relevant and material, that it have such a connection with the offense charged as to contribute toward proving either guilt or innocence. This connection must appear affirmatively, mere probabilities or possibilities are not sufficient. 

The investigator, perfectly aware that a piece of evidence has a direct bearing upon the case, frequently overlooks the necessity of producing evidence to prove its connection with the crime charged. Thus, for example, it is perfectly evident to the officer that the findings of the autopsy surgeon are not only admissible evidence but of great importance. The officer knows that the body autopsied was that of the person whose death is the basis of the prosecution. Still the law provides that the testimony of the autopsy surgeon is admissible only when it is shown that the body examined by him was that of the person for whose killing the defendant is being prosecuted. It is necessary that evidence be introduced at the trial tracing the body continuously from where it was found to the autopsy surgeon's examination coupled with proof that the body was that of the person named as the deceased in the indictment or information. Proof of the identity of the deceased must come from some person who knew the deceased while he was alive and who saw him after death. The connecting evidence must follow the body from where it was found to the autopsy table unless proof is available from a person present at the finding of the body that he knows of his own knowledge that the body examined by the autopsy surgeon was the same body. Such connecting testimony is of especial importance where death was caused by means not evident from a mere view of the body as found, such as internal injuries and poison, disclosed only by the autopsy in which cases it may be anticipated that the defense will insist upon positive identification and connection. If the reader thinks that so obvious a matter did not require mention, let him bear in mind that convictions have been reversed for the sole reason that this connection had not been established. 

A similar condition exists with reference to the means used to commit a crime. Before such means can be received in evidence there must be proof that they are the same as those which were found during the investigation and, in addition, it must appear that they did in fact have a connection with the crime charged. The mere fact that a revolver is found in the possession of the accused is not ordinarily sufficient to permit its introduction at the trial. There must be some proof tending to show that it was used in the perpetration of the crime. The law does not require positive identification, but it does require proof sufficient to sustain a finding that the gun was so used. 

Thus the revolver would be admissible in a robbery case if the victim, though not able to swear that it was the identical weapon, should testify that it looked like the weapon used. In all cases where a witness is expected to identify a weapon or other object he should be shown the object before going into court and his statement obtained as to the identification if he is able to identify.

Where a homicide or assault is committed by means of blows from a blunt instrument or club the weapon may be found on the scene, but the mere fact that an object, capable of inflicting the injuries in question, is found near the place where the crime was committed will not permit its being introduced in evidence. There must be, in addition, some proof that it was the precise weapon used. This connection is frequently established by finding blood or hair of the victim on the instrument. In other cases the connection may be made by proof that the peculiarities of the injuries correspond to similar peculiarities in the instrument found and in some cases splinters found in a wound have been proven to have come from a club found near the body. In burglary cases, and other crimes in which tools or instruments are used in connection with the commission of the crime, while the tools found in the possession of the defendant, or traced to him, are admissible in evidence, their admissibility depends upon preliminary proof that they were the tools used in the crime for which he is being tried. Here it is well to bear in mind that old tools develop characteristics, rough spots, nicks and other defects, which are distinctive and peculiar and these characteristics are quite likely to leave their "fingerprints" on the "job." Thus a jimmy with a nick in the edge will leave its identifying mark on the window frame upon which it is used. Sometimes a tool may be connected with the crime by reason of a small bit of material being transferred from the place to which it is applied to the tool itself. Thus paint from a window frame may be found on the jimmy and the expert can identify the paint. A saw frequently retains in its teeth minute particles of the object on which it was used. Also the tool may have been injured while being used and a little piece of the metal broken from an augur bit, for example, may be the means of identifying the bit used. 

In theft cases, particularly of the larceny type, and other crimes involving the taking of property, in which the property is recovered, it is extremely important that the proof should show that the property recovered was the property which had been taken. Such cases may be divided into two classes: Those in which the owner can go no further than to say that the property recovered "appears" to be the same, or is similar to, that which was taken, and those in which the owner can positively identify the property. In the first class of cases the owner should be carefully questioned and his reasons for declaring the property to be similar

noted. Where the property has identifying marks or peculiarities the owner should be asked to point them out to the investigator and the identification should be made as positive as possible. While it may satisfy the officer or the owner if identification is made of an automobile by the license plates, this is far from satisfactory to the court as, in fact, it only identifies the plates and amounts to an identification of the automobile only if we assume that the plates were on the same car when it was taken. No such assumption is warranted, especially since the practice of thieves to "switch" license plates is so well known. Every owner of an automobile should be able to point out marks, scratches, dents or additions or other peculiarities which will conclusively identify his automobile if the officer on the case will only take the pains to go over the question of identification with the owner with the car before them, but in spite of the importance of the identification the writer recalls quite a few cases in which the process of identification, left until the owner was on the witness stand and without the automobile before him, was quite difficult and painful.

Identification of Perpetrator 

Those crimes, such as robbery, burglary, forgery, bunco and homicide, in which the offense or part thereof is committed in the presence of one or more witnesses, usually involve at the trial the question of the identification of the perpetrator. Regardless of the fact that there are always chances that a person may be mistaken in making an identification, the fact remains that an identification of the defendant as the perpetrator, made by a witness whose statement carries conviction to the mind of the jury, will do more to bring about a conviction than the most perfect fingerprint evidence connecting the defendant with the job. 

On the other hand, a perfectly good case is often ruined by the testimony of a witness, who to the minds of the jury should be able to identify the culprit, that he cannot say whether the defendant is the guilty party. Also, where the identification was apparently influenced by some conduct or suggestion on the part of the officers, the doubt cast upon the validity of the identification spreads to the entire case. 

The ability of a witness to make an identification and the weight which will be given to his testimony depends primarily upon two factors-the personal ability of the individual to make an identification and the manner in which the matter of identification was handled by the investigating officers. The attention which witnesses pay to the personal appearance of the criminal and their abilities to observe vary as much as the witnesses vary from one another and 1 these factors are further affected by the circumstances of the transaction. In bunco and forgery cases, for example, where the action is slow and ample opportunity is afforded for calm observation we have a right to expect a good identification. In the case of a crime by sudden violence, such as robbery or homicide, where the action is rapid and the elements of excitement, fear, anxiety and nervousness are likely to be present, we must consider the possibility of either a defective identification, an erroneous identification or no identification at all. It does not follow, however, that excitement or nervousness necessarily interferes with or prevents an accurate and positive observation, for, under such conditions the senses frequently become more acute and the mind more active. Reliable instances have been recorded of almost unbelievable and accurate identification. The author once tried a murder case arising out of a quarrel on a street car lasting only a minute or two and still one of the passengers was able to state positively and correctly that one of the defendants was one of the participants and that he wore a brown suit with very fine stripes of red and green. The remarkable feature of the identification was that the green stripe was so fine and nearly invisible that, prior to the testimony of this witness, and in spite of the fact that they had handled the suit several times, neither counsel had noticed the minute green stripe. There are also several authenticated instances wherein, upon a dark night, a person was identified by no light other than that coming from the flash of a pistol. On the other hand there have been instances where bandits entered a bank without masks or disguise, were there as long as five minutes and the bank employees identified entirely innocent persons as the robbers. 

Intelligence, education and training tend to make for more accurate observation and still we find men of this class whose identifications are wholly unreliable and, on the other hand, we find witnesses of a low grade of intelligence and little or no education whose identifications are positive and reliable. There is no rule or standard by which we can measure a witness in advance and say whether or not he will be able to make a reliable identification. The only course for the investigator to follow is to use the highest degree of care in the matter of identification by witnesses and then, by analysis of the statements of each witness and a check-up as to any possibility of his being mistaken, misled or influenced by emotion or interest, weighing his evidence of identity just as a juror would weigh it. 

It is a natural tendency for the investigating officer to desire an identification of the person whom he believes to be the guilty party. This tendency, however, must under no circumstances be allowed to influence any of the witnesses. Every identification should be so conducted that, when made, it is wholly and solely the product of the identifying witness and absolutely

uninfluenced by any hint or suggestion of any other person. The practice still followed by some officers of telling the victim that they have the man who committed the crime or that they believe they have him and will the complainant please come down to the station and identify him, followed by bringing in the one suspect and calling upon the witness to make an identification, never had its place in scientific criminal investigation. The sooner such methods are abandoned the better.

The public, represented by the jury, resents any such forced attempts at identification and, even if the witness is so certain of identity that he could, had he been given the opportunity, have picked the culprit out of half a hundred men, juries generally give little weight to such leading methods of securing identification.

All witnesses who may by any possibility be able to identify the person whom they saw commit a crime should be carefully interviewed and their statements reduced to writing. Effort should be made to secure as clear and definite a word description of the criminal as possible. It must, however, be borne in mind that the ability to describe another person with any degree of accuracy of detail belongs to but few human beings and this is true regardless of how intimately such person is known. In securing the witness' statement the investigator should seek particularly for identifying details-scars, moles, peculiarity of features, deformities, type of voice, manner of speech and action, etc., -those things which are peculiar to the individual. 

Even where such details are given they must be accepted with a reservation even in the case of the accurate witness. A mole or a scar may be faked, a limp simulated and the wearing of dark glasses by a criminal is more likely to evidence a disguise than poor eyesight. The investigator should also ascertain the state of mind of the witness at the time of the crime and whether he made a special effort to observe the criminal so as to be later able to identify him. 

Inquiry should also be made to ascertain whether the eyewitnesses have gotten together to talk it over and have compared their impressions. Such getting together tends to reflect doubt on their subsequent identifications, especially if they agreed after talking it over although they differed previously, but, on the other hand such a conference may merely have the result of convincing one or more of the witnesses that they were mistaken. We have here illustrated another reason for promptly interviewing all witnesses before they have had an opportunity to talk it over. The statements of all identifying witnesses, after being reduced to writing, should be de- Ilivered to the prosecuting attorney as such information is most valuable, not only in the examination of such witnesses as are called to the witness stand but as a means of eliminating the witness who is probably mistaken and the witness who is not reliable. 

When there is no suspect under arrest the word description of the criminal is rarely of any value. This is more likely to be due to the inherent inability of the victim to describe the person than to an inability to identify. In such cases it is advisable to have the victim look through the photographs of the local identification bureau, not solely because the culprit's picture may be in the collection but also because, in so large a collection of photographs, even though it does not include the picture of the man wanted, it is likely that one or more will be found which strongly resemble him or at least will give some idea of his appearance. appearance. When such photographs are found the witness can usually point out wherein the pictures resemble and wherein they differ from the individual desired. Where a picture shows a man with a moustache, this facial adornment may mislead the witness if the criminal was clean shaven. Laying a match or other small object across the moustache in the photograph will usually eliminate its effect on the mind of the witness. Even though the criminal's picture is not found in the collection such an examination of photographs will at least indicate the general appearance photograph which partially resembles the perpetrator and type of man who committed the offense and a of a crime, especially where the witness can tell the officer the details in which the picture resembles or differs from him, is better than no picture at all and narrows down the search. If there be no identification bureau at the disposal of the investigator he can still have the witness look over newspaper and magazine pictures, or any collection of photographs, as a means of getting the criminal's type and general appearance. 

Where a suspect is under arrest it is extremely bad practice to bring him before the witness by himself. 

If the witness can really identify the perpetrator of the crime, he can do so if the suspect is one of six or a dozen prisoners shown to him at the same time. 

If a witness cannot do this his identification, made in an improper manner, is, to say the least, unreliable. 

Where a group of prisoners is brought out for inspection with the purpose of ascertaining whether a witness can identify one of them, they should not differ radically in size, color, race and build from one another. If the suspect is five feet tall and the witness has described the criminal as a little fellow, it is unfair to bring him out in the company of six footers. Make the test a real test and the identification made will satisfy any reasonable juror. 

In such identification by the viewing of groups of prisoners it is advisable to inform the witness merely that he is going to be shown a number of persons, that the person sought may or may not be one of them and that he should carefully look them all over. 

The witness should also be instructed to say nothing and make no indication as to whether or not he identifies anyone while the group is in the room. There is no reason why, at this stage of the investigation,

the criminal should know that he has been identified. 

If a shadow box is used, while the prisoners cannot see the witnesses, they can still hear what is said. All conversation between the officers and witnesses should be out of the hearing of the prisoners. The fact that victims of crimes often fail to identify, at times even declaring that the guilty person is not the man, is sufficient reason for the rule. It is most embarrassing to have such a complaining witness asked on crossexamination whether it is not true that he was shown the defendant at the police station and at that time declared that he was not the one who committed the crime. 

Where a mannerism, speech characteristic, deformity or identifying mark has previously been mentioned by the witness, the exhibition of the prisoners should be such as to display the same if it exists. Sometimes the witness desires that a suspect walk or talk or put on different clothing or a cap. Such requests must be anticipated and an arrangement previously made with the witness so that they may be communicated to the officers without the prisoners overhearing. As a general rule the room or shadow box in which prisoners are displayed should be well illuminated so as to light up the entire figure. It sometimes happens, however, that the victim of a crime saw the criminal only under an unusual lighting condition such as, for example, by a single light overhead. The effect of such overhead lighting is vastly different than a broad scheme of lighting and the latter type of lighting may fail to produce an identification while, with the lighting arranged to imitate the lighting at the time of the crime, the identification may be positive and easily made.

Some victims are prepared to identify almost anyone shown them. Others, though actually identifying in their own minds one of the prisoners as the guilty man, will, either by reason of fear, friendship, personal implication in the crime or some other personal interest, declare that they cannot identify anyone. These factors can be eliminated or at least discounted if the witnesses have been properly interviewed ahead of time by the officers and inquiries have been made from them and other sources as to any personal interest or motive for failing to declare the truth. The use of the shadow box or other system whereby the prisoners may be viewed without their being able to see the witness has a great tendency to remove the fear which some witnesses have of identifying.

Where a witness has made a positive identification it is frequently advisable on a later occasion to confront the identified prisoner with the witness and have the latter directly state in his presence that he is the guilty man. Such identification has a natural tendency to shock the morale of the prisoner and increases the chances that he will thereafter confess his guilt, plead guilty to the charge or make statements which can be used against him. 

CHAPTER V. 

Self Defense, Wounds, Cause of Death 

In all homicides by shooting, stabbing or involving the use of a weapon the investigator must anticipate the claim of self-defense and this is true even though the claim of self-defense does not seem to be tenable. 

This defense is frequently fabricated and the surest method of meeting such a false defense is thorough investigation as soon after the homicide as possible for the circumstances surrounding the killing. 

Great care and caution must be exercised in recording and checking the testimony of eyewitnesses. Excitement, fear, anger or other emotion are extremely likely to produce inaccurate, exaggerated or biased statements. If others than the deceased and the person who did the killing participated in the affray we must expect that the stories of such others will align themselves as for or against the slayer as such others favored or opposed the slayer in the incidents leading to the killing. This may be due to deliberate falsehood or may be due to the influence of bias but, regardless of the cause, these stories must be checked with the facts ascertainable from other sources and diligent inquiry must be made to learn the possible interest of each witness, his position as bearing upon his opportunity to observe what was going on at the time of the affray and his possible participation therein. 

The law of self-defense is predicated upon the existence of an actual or apparent necessity for killing. 

Hence the inquiry should be directed to a determination of all the circumstances of the difficulty and how those circumstances must have appeared to the mind of the slayer. Particular attention should be given to the question of whether the deceased was armed or whether he had in his hand or on his person anything which could reasonably have been mistaken for a weapon. It is not sufficient to examine the body of the deceased for it frequently occurs that a weapon

The movements and actions of the participants in the fatal encounter should be ascertained from all of the available witnesses with the greatest attention to detail. All distances involved in the story of the affair should be accurately measured and, if greater attention be paid to certain measurements than to others, the greatest accuracy should be given to measurements of the distances between the deceased and the slayer and from them to prominent physical objects, the position of the body and the distance, if any, the deceased ran, walked or staggered after receiving his injury. The exact words used during the encounter, whether uttered by the participants or by bystanders are often of importance. The condition of the ground or floor of the place where the affray took place should be carefully examined for footprints or other marks demonstrating the movements of the parties. A diagram of the scene, accurately depicting everything which by any possible stretch of the imagination could be of any significance, should be prepared as soon as possible. 

A reenactment of the affray, experiments and observations to check the stories of the witnesses may well be conducted on a later occasion and frequently will prove of real value.

Dying Declarations 

One of the exceptions to the rule that hearsay evidence cannot be received in court is that relating to dying declarations. To be admissible such declara- 

To be admissible such declarations can only be used upon a trial for homicide, they must be made by the deceased under a sense of impending death and must be confined to the circumstances immediately attending the infliction of the fatal injury. The requirement "under a sense of impending death" means that the declarant must, at the time of making the statement, believe that he will die as the result of his injuries. It is not necessary that he believe that his death will immediately or speedily follow, but hope of a recovery must be absent.

This state of mind is often provable by declarations of the injured person but may be proved by the circumstances. It is advisable, however, to secure a direct expression from the injured person as to his belief on the subject of his possible recovery. The dying declaration, setting forth the circumstances surrounding the injury should, as far as possible, be taken down in writing and signed by the declarant. Even though the injured person has such a hope of recovery as to make the statement inadmissible as a dying declaration, it should be reduced to writing and signed for if, at a later time when all hope of recovery is gone, the dying person should reaffirm the statement or declare that the statement previously signed by him is true, such reaffirmance would render the statement admissible as a legal dying declaration. If possible the statement should be secured by one of the investigators who is reasonably familiar with the facts of the case so that, if the patient's condition permits, questions may be asked of him to clear up doubtful matters. It is also well to bear in mind that if the statement is made in the presence of the person who caused the fatal injuries it may be admissible as an accusatory statement in connection with proof of the statement or conduct of the accused tending to prove guilt or a failure to make denial even though the injured person may still have a hope of recovery.

Cause of Death 

Usually the appearance of the wound tells the character of the instrument which caused it, but it happens sufficiently often that first impressions as to the character of the weapon are incorrect that this subject should receive careful attention. Thus, what appears to be a cut with a sharp instrument may in fact have been caused not by a knife or other sharp edged tool, but by a piece of glass, a sharp edged piece of wood or metal or by any sharp edged object. Even a comparatively dull edge may cause the appearance of a cut with a little muscle or other tissue between the skin and the sharp edged instrument. For example, where there is bone, as on the skull, at the knee, shin or elbow, a blow with the edge of a board is very likely to cut through the skin in such a manner as to give the appearance of a cut with a sharp instrument. The area about the wound is of as much significance and often of more importance than the opening of the wound. In gunshot wounds the area about the hole will indicate whether it is an entrance or exit wound and the area about the entrance wound should be carefully observed, noted and photographed for it may establish with practical accuracy the distance between the muzzle of the gun and the wound.

Every wound on the body of the victim of a violent assault should be carefully observed and, in homicide cases, minor and apparently insignificant wounds, scratches and bruises may be of far greater evidentiary value than the wound causing death. This is particularly true when we have the story of the accused as to the transaction in which the injuries occurred in which instance the physical injuries on the respective parties are very likely to either corroborate or disprove the story. There is no excuse for carelessness such as appeared in the trial of Kid McCoy for murder. In that case the defendant gave as the explanation of a cut on the upper lip of the deceased passing entirely through the lip and injuring the gums, that the deceased had accidentally struck her lip with the butt of the handle of a butcher knife for the possession of which they were struggling. Not a single witness, not even the autopsy surgeon, was able to state whether the cut in the lip was caused by the handle or edge of the blade of the knife. Incidentally, in the same case some of the investigating officers were unable to describe the

If such things occur in as prominent a case as that mentioned, the writer feels justified in urging the highest degree of care and attention to detail in all investigations. Again it must be borne in mind that the cause of death may not be evident to the eye of the investigator. Many instances can be cited where a person was buried without any suspicion that death was caused by criminal agency wherein it developed later that murder had been committed, and this is a possibility and has happened within the writer's personal experience even where a post mortem examination was performed by the official autopsy surgeon. The investigator is justified in assuming, until the cause of death is definitely determined, that it was probably due to criminal agency.

It sometimes happens that a body will bear wounds. caused by more than one kind of instrument or more than one calibre of bullet. This may mean that the assailant used more than one weapon, but more commonly indicates that more than one person participated in the assault or that one assault followed the other. In this connection it should be borne in mind that a person inflicting a fatal injury upon another is just as guilty where that person is already dying from a prior fatal injury or disease as if he were uninjured and in perfect health.

The obvious cause of death may not be the real cause. 

A man burned to death in a fire may have been shot or poisoned and the house fired to conceal the homicide. 

A body found in the water may be that of a person thrown into the water after death or after infliction of fatal injuries or the administration of poison or some narcotic. A body found hanging may in fact be that of the victim of a murder by strangling or other method, the murderer hanging up the body to create the idea of suicide. Careful investigation, proceeding slowly but surely, noting all signs and marks, and the assistance of the autopsy will bring forth the real facts. The autopsy surgeon should also be able to determine whether or not any of the wounds found on the body were inflicted before or after death. 

Stab Wounds

Stab wounds are most always longer than the width of the blade causing them since the blade does not usually go straight in and straight out and, in addition to the puncture, there is a cutting action by the edge of the blade. However, in rare cases, the stab wound, if caused by a weapon with a blunt point and where the wound is not very deep, may be shorter than the width of the instrument due to the stretching of the skin by the blunt point. Knife wounds usually appear as though made with a two edged blade regardless of whether the knife blade was two edged or not. depth of the wound cannot be determined by its size or the amount or lack of bleeding and care should be taken not to draw conclusions on this point until the report of the medical expert has been received. Gunshot Wounds

Gunshot wounds at short range often have particles of clothing, specks of unburnt powder or shotgun wads carried into the wound and the area about the wound may be burnt and discolored by the hot explosion gases. At certain distances the specks of unburnt powder are found around the entrance wound imbedded in the skin by the force of the explosion, in the clothing or merely on the surface of the skin and, while actual burning may be absent, the discoloration by powder gases will be seen. The position of the bullet hole, whether in the centre of the powder-marked circular area or nearer one of its edges, taken in connection with the course of the bullet, is a strong circumstance tending to prove the position in which the weapon was held.

All wounds caused by pistol or rifle bullets do not necessarily come from weapons of that class; the instance has been known of shotgun shells being loaded with bullets of the type used in ammunition for rifled firearms. This fact may be determined by an absence of rifling marks on the bullets and the fact that such bullets do not, except in rare exceptions, enter point first but "keyhole" and tear their way into the body. 

But a "keyhole" wound may come from a bullet fired from a rifle or revolver, particularly if reloaded ammunition has been used or if the bullet has passed through some other object before striking the body. 

The number of wounds may mislead the investigator as to the number of bullets causing the injuries. A bullet entering the body may split into two or more pieces and cause more than one exit wound or the same bullet may pass through the body after passing through one or both arms. Some years ago the writer saw the wounds suffered by a hunter who was shot as a result of stumbling and accidentally discharging his rifle; the bullet passed through one lower arm, one upper arm, the breast muscle and then went through the shoulder.

CHAPTER VI. 

Blood Spots and Stains, Fingerprints, Foot Prints 

Spots or stains of blood may offer far more proof than the mere fact that some animal has been injured, in fact the blood may turn out to be that of a bird or fish. Modern science has not yet developed the perfect test for blood, but it has advanced to such a degree that various kinds of blood may be distinguished from one another and, while the expert can go no further in his testimony to show that certain blood was human blood than to say that a particular specimen is either that of a human being or that of an anthropoid ape, the ape possibility is so easily eliminated that we have a test which will make practically certain that human blood will be proven to be such. While not generally true, it is scientifically possible in some cases, due to blood peculiarities, for the expert to say whether certain blood came from a particular person. Science can also distinguish menstrual blood from other blood and blood mixed with mucus, as from the nose, may often be so identified.

Blood found on the scene of a crime, shown to be human blood, may have come from the criminal, his victim or some third person and the effort of the investigator should be to determine which of these possible theories is correct. In the case of some crimes this is quite a simple matter. If traces of blood are found on the scene of a burglary, we may quite safely assume that the prowler injured himself and the quantity of blood will tend to indicate whether the injury was a serious one. The physical facts may show how the injury was occasioned and whether it was an injury to a hand and even which hand has been hurt. In all cases where blood is found, especially in cases of assault and homicide the greatest care should be exercised to find not only the larger pools and spots of blood but all signs of blood. This data may be of the greatest value in proving the case of the prosecution or in disproving the defense offered. All articles bearing traces of what appear to be blood should be carefully preserved. It should be borne in mind that blood marks, even when quite fresh are not always red. Exposure to the sun, contact with dyed fabrics or other chemical laden surfaces may cause blood spots to look very different from what would be expected. On the other hand spots not due at all to blood may have all the appearance of blood and no positive opinions should be formed until the spots have been examined scientifically. Where a person has been wounded and blood drips from his wound while he is walkng the drops falling on the ground or floor will generally be pear shaped with the smaller end towards the direction in which the party was walking, but this is not the invariable rule. Thus, if the hand be injured, the backward swinging of the arm in walking will throw some of the drops away from the direction in which the party is walking causing the smaller end of the pear-shaped spot to be in the direction toward the body. Drops of a generally round appearance are usually caused when the injured person is not in motion, but if the injured part is moved we can expect the pear-shaped splash. Bloody hand or finger prints do not necessarily indicate that the hand or even the arm was injured; a serious injury to the face of a person while in a conflict or while under the stress of excitement or other emotion will involuntarily cause that person to wipe the blood away with his hand and also, there is always the possibility that a person's hand became bloody by touching some other person who was injured.

Even though a person whose hands have been bloody washes them to the point when he believes that all traces of blood have been removed, we have still the most excellent chance of finding enough blood under the finger nails, between the cuticle and nail or in creases in the skin to enable the laboratory expert to identify it. Brushing and even washing of clothing is quite likely to fail to remove recognizable traces of blood and, for this reason, all clothing which may bear evidence of value as to blood stains should be carefully protected from contamination and promptly sent to the laboratory. The washing and scrubbing of floors to remove blood is usually unsuccessful for there is always the big chance that blood has gotten into the cracks in and the spaces between the boards or into the wood itself in quantities sufficient to show positive in the laboratory. Blood is often found on knives and other weapons even after the owner has cleaned them to his own satisfaction although it may be necessary to take the knife or weapon apart to find the blood.

Bloody objects should receive no more handling than is absolutely necessary, not only because of the danger of injuring the evidence of the blood itself, but because tiny particles of bone, hair or other tissue or foreign matter may be adhering to or covered up by the blood which are of greater evidentiary value than the blood itself. In handling bloody exhibits these possibilities should always be kept in mind. If the 25

Finger Prints 

The palmar surface of the hand, but more particularly of the distal or nail joints of the thumbs and fingers, displays a mass of tiny ridges forming patterns which do not change from the birth to the death of the individual. Still more important is the fact that these ridge patterns differ so distinctly from one another that for all practical purposes we may say that no two persons have the same ridge characteristics upon their respective thumbs or other fingers and this is true even through the individuals are as closely related as twins or parent and child. These ridges, arranged in patterns which are denominated Arches, Loops, Whorls and Accidentals, permit the classification and filing of finger prints by systems which enable the person familiar with the system to identify a finger print found on the scene of a crime as that of a finger print in the identification files. As the national, state and municipal governments, as well as sheriff's and other offices, now commonly carry the fingerprints of at least the felony prisoners it is reasonably certain that, if an identifiable fingerprint is found, it can, if that of a person having a felony record, be definitely identified as that of a particular person. Furthermore, by communicating with other bureaus, it is possible to obtain the entire criminal record of any prisoner whose finger prints are sent to such bureaus and there is no more accurate way of determining whether a fugitive from justice is in fact the person wanted in another jurisdiction. Incidentally, also, the fingerprint of the accused on the scene of the crime is most conclusive evidence of his presence there.

The value of fingerprints as evidence can hardly be overestimated. The most iron-clad alibi must fail if the fingerprint of the accused is found "on the job." 

It is regrettable that this powerful class of evidence is so seldom discovered and preserved. The materials for taking the fingerprints of prisoners are so inexpensive and so simple to use that there is no excuse for even the smallest force of peace officers to be without its assistance. The developing of latent, invisible or semi-invisible, prints upon objects connected with the crime is more difficult and frequently demands exceeding skill and experience but, even here, the small department will find that, even though it cannot afford the services of an expert of its own, there is usually available some nearby fingerprint expert whose abilities can be secured without any great expense. 

When the palmar side of a finger is touched to another object we get much the same effect as when an ordinary rubber stamp is touched to paper-if finger and stamp are coated with ink, an impression of the pattern is made. But a finger need not be rubbed in ink or covered with blood or other foreign substance to leave a print. The tiny pores of the skin normally exude a moist oily substance which coats the ridges sufficiently to leave a print of the ridges upon the object touched. Such a print is, however, so delicate that it is nearly or actually invisible to the eye and can be seen and photographed only after it has been developed by chemical means or dusted with white, black or aluminum powder used for that purpose, this dusting process resulting in the powder sticking to the moist oily lines left by the ridges thus making them visible. It is, however, most inadvisable for any person not thoroughly trained in the work to attempt this developing of latent fingerprints because of the danger of improper technique destroying the print. 

Furthermore, once the print has been dusted, the slightest friction, such as wrapping the object in paper, will destroy the print. The better practice, if the fingerprint expert cannot be taken to the print, is to carefully wrap the object suspected of bearing a fingerprint in such manner that no part of a possible finger print can be rubbed off. No object which may by any possibility bear the fingerprint of the criminal should ever be handled by anyone as the mere act of touching the object will not only destroy any delicate print which may be thereon, but may leave the print of the handler superimposed on the obliterated print. 

Rough and absorbent surfaces rarely display fingerprints unless the finger was coated with something such as blood, ink or grease. Smooth non-absorbent surfaces, particularly glass, chinaware, polished metals, varnished woodwork or furniture, enameled surfaces, are most likely places for finger prints. Custom, habit and tendencies of the individual cause him to leave his prints in characteristic places. Thus, since, for example, the automobile thief finds that the rear vision mirror is not adjusted to his stature and driving posture and, since he is particularly interested in being able to observe whether he is being followed, he has the natural tendency to adjust the mirror and, in doing so, leaves his finger print on its surface. 

While paper is hardly an ideal surface for picking up finger prints, still it is not at all unusual for the fingerprint expert to develop prints left on the smoother types of paper. It will be remembered that the finger prints of the murderer Hickman, found on the notes sent to the father of the girl whom he kidnapped and killed, were perhaps the largest factor in his capture.

The photographing of finger prints is best done with a camera expressly manufactured for this purpose. In taking the picture there should be included an identifying note or memorandum written on a card or paper and placed below the print at the time the picture is taken. Such note should state the date, case, initials of the operator and a key number. Along with this record the photographer should, in his note book, make a full record of all these details and, also, the exact location of the place where the print was found.

Footprints

Footprint evidence has received very little attention in this country and, when used, is usually presented to the court and jury in such an inaccurate and unscientific manner that it lacks convincing force. This is due no doubt to the fact that many officers have had no knowledge as to how to handle the matter of footprints. Footprint evidence is not confined to a comparison of the print with the print of the suspect. Much can be learned by observing the footprints of people on the highways and other places where footprints are readily recorded. It will be found that in walking some people toe in and others toe out and still others walk with the feet parallel to the direction in which they are going. Not only this but some persons walk with the feet fairly far apart, others with the feet close together and some cross the advanced foot over the other. Physical defects such as lameness, shortness of one leg, etc., also evidence themselves in the footprints left behind. Then we have the peculiarities of footwear. Even though shoes start their existence fairly regular in shape the personal peculiarities of walking and physical defects of the owner soon cause the shoes to become characteristic; the heels wear down in a characteristic manner as do also the soles. some nails become more prominent than others and if the sole wears partly through or has a patch placed upon it we have strong evidence which may later result in the identification of the wearer or the owner of the shoes. Usually it is the owner who wears the shoes, but there is always a possibility that someone else may have worn the shoes either with or without the owner's permission and knowledge and the instance has even been known of a person wearing shoes of the opposite sex. The length of the stride in walking is a general indication of the size of the individual so that it may quite safely be assumed if footprints of two people walking together are observed that the person with the longer stride was the taller of the two. Where a person takes a longer stride than is natural and customary this will be evidenced by the fact that the marks made by the heels in striking the ground will be more pronounced by reason of their hitting the ground with greater force. Some European criminologists have even claimed that it is possible to draw conclusions as to the occupation of the owner of footprints from the prints alone and while it is true that occupation may develop a characteristic type of walking and wear upon the shoes we not only have insufficient data for this country but most of the occupations which our foreign cousins seem to have identified do not exist here their places being taken largely by motor transported workers. The idea is not, however, entirely fantastic for it must be remembered that the American Indian was able to tell the tribe from which came the maker of discovered footprints. Tracks of persons walking and the subject of investigation may lead directly to the travelers or furnish sufficient description from their manner of walking and the character of the prints to enable the officers to telephone or wire ahead and have the travelers stopped. The investigator should be prepared by past experiences, observation and experiment to tell from the footprints whether the pedestrian was walking or running. Sometimes a criminal will walk backwards to conceal his movements, a trick easily exposed by any person who has observed the obvious difference in footprints made while walking backwards as compared with those while walking forward, for the foot strikes the dirt slightly before it becomes firmly planted and the print is a little ahead of the point at which the foot first came into contact with the ground with reference to the direction in which the foot is moving. Footprints are often found indoors as well as outdoors but are much less evident and must be more carefully looked for unless the shoes left imprints by reason of being wet, muddy or covered with some other substance which has come off in walking. In every case the question of footprints should be considered and traces thereof looked for since, if discovered, they may not only be the means of identification but will frequently disclose the movements of the maker of the prints. When footprints have been discovered on the scene of a crime the average officer seems wholly at a loss as to the proper method of determining whether they were made by the suspect whom he has arrested. The writer recalls one murder case in which the officer

broke a stick until it was the length of the footprint and marked on it the width of the print and then compared the stick with the shoes of the prisoner. Not quite so ineffective but wholly unworthy of good police work is the more common practice of fitting the prisoner's shoes into the footprints. Even where such a test is made by the identical shoe which made the print it is almost certain to damage the footprint and if made by a different shoe the print will be destroyed. 

The investigator who has experimented with footprints in various soils soon discovers also that under some conditions a shoe will make a larger print than the shoe itself and that some soil conditions result in a contraction of the footprint, especially if it has dried somewhat, so that it is distinctly smaller than the shoe which made it and that comparisons for size are not very reliable. The real means of identification lie in the peculiarities and characteristics of the print and their comparison with the foot or shoe which is suspected of making the print. 

The first step in preserving footprint evidence should be to photograph the print as a record. the photograph being so taken as to disclose the peculiarities and this should be supplemented by a visual examination of the print. Preserving the print comes next. Many methods of preserving footprints have been used and suggested, but there is a large field for improvement. 

When the print is upon damp or firm ground the most practical method is to take a plaster of paris cast. The first step should be to remove from the print any small particles of soil, leaves, etc., which may have fallen into the print if this can safely be done. 

This must be done carefully and a pair of tweezers and a camel's hair brush will be found useful for this purpose. If conditions permit, a slight coating of oil, just enough to coat but not enough to fill up any of the minute depressions in the print such as made by shoe nails, cracks in the sole, etc., can be applied, as this makes the removal of the cast easier after it has hardened. If this is not done the earth may adhere to the cast when it is removed but as such earth is easily removed from the cast the oiling process is really not necessary and in damp soils is better omitted. If the print is shallow, it is advisable to build up around, but a little distance from, the footprint a rim of earth or a circle of cardboard so that the top level of the plaster when poured will be well above the print and that the cast will include the edges of the soil about the print. A word as to the plaster of paris. The best plaster is that used by sculptors or dentists. It should be thoroughly dry and should be kept in a sealed container to keep it so. In mixing never pour water into the dry plaster. The proper procedure is to fill a bowl about half full of water and then slowly sift the plaster into the water, allowing the plaster to sink before more is added. This is continued until the mound of wet plaster in the bowl reaches the water level. The plaster should then be quickly stirred and should have a heavy creamy consistency, thin enough to pour readily and free from all lumps. In pouring the plaster into the mould there should be no delay as it hardens quite rapidly, but the plaster should be poured in a small stream and not just dumped into the print. Good plaster will be quite hard in about fifteen minutes but, unless speed is absolutely necessary, it is best to allow more time before the cast is removed. Even when fully hardened the cast should not be subjected to any rough handling and it should be removed from the print very carefully. Most persons underestimate the amount of plaster required to cast a print and it is advisable, upon the first trial to mix enough as it is not practical to supplement the amount already poured by enough to fill up the print. The investigator should not wait for his first actual case before making plaster casts of footprints but should practice and experiment in advance so that he may proceed with assurance when an actual case presents itself. 

The cast when dry and all adhering soil removed will display all of the imprinted characteristics of the corded in the print provided that the work has been foot or shoe which made the print and which were reproperly done. The cast may be compared directly with the foot or shoe suspected of making the print or a print may be made of the suspected foot or shoe in soil similar to that in which the original print was found, a plaster cast made of this test print and then both the casts compared.

In sand or dry soil we meet a real difficulty in preserving the print and especial care should be exercised to secure a good photograph for the effort to preserve the print may fail and the print be destroyed in the effort. One method in such a case is to scrape minutely fine particles of paraffine into the print and then hold a hot iron plate over the print until the wax has melted and soaked into the print. In some instances this method will permit the print to be raised from the surrounding soil after the wax has hardened and under other circumstances, and perhaps the best method, a plaster cast should be made of the wax coated print. 

Such casts will not display the detail of casts taken from footprints in damp soil but will still be of great practical value.

The foregoing remarks apply to impressions by automobile tires, a cane, marks of a ladder, or any other marks on the ground which should be preserved for their evidentiary value. It will be found by observation that automobile tires which have seen some use leave prints distinctly characteristic to themselves. 

Where casts cannot be made the prints should be accurately measured and photographed and outlined in chalk or pencil to preserve a record on the scene of their discovery.

29 formation as to further details of the crime or the identity of the perpetrator. Stains, dirt, grease, paint, etc., on weapons or other objects are a record of their history and past uses and may be so recent as to have been caused in the commission of the offense. Foreign matter, such as paint, grease, etc., in the hands of the chemist or under the microscope, displays characteristics which may easily connect the instrument with its owner. In a murder case tried before the author some of the grease on a pipe used as the lethal weapon was found on the head of the deceased thus identifying the pipe as the weapon used, thereby connecting the owner of the pipe with the homicide. Not only the foreign matter appearing prominently on the outer surface should be considered but also the "dirt" found in the crevices and cracks. Not so long ago a man accused of a stabbing was arrested shortly after the alleged offense. In his possession was found a pocket knife such as the victim claimed had been used but an examination of the knife by the officer failed to disclose any evidence of blood. A more experienced officer called attention to the fact that the knife had the appearance of being washed and then proceeded to take the knife apart and after scraping out all the dirt and submitting the accumulated material to a bacteriological laboratory received positive proof that the knife bore distinct traces of quite fresh blood from either a human being or an anthropoid ape. The accused in his statement insisted that he had never gotten any blood on the knife although he had had it a long time. When told that blood had been found on the inside of the knife the prisoner recalled that he had used the knife to clean some fish on a recent fishing trip, but when shown the laboratory report he admitted the stabbing. 

Nicks and scratches in edged tools often leave their marks on the object upon which they are used and distinctive markings are sometimes found which are traceable to the distinctive manner in which they are sharpened. The shape and wear and the manner in which edged tools, saws and other tools have been sharpened may indicate the particular purpose for which they have been customarily used and may indicate the line of inquiry to be followed to locate the owner. 

Knives, for example, are shaped and ground differently for the particular uses for which they are intended or to which they have been put and it is not usual for craftsmen in the same line of trade to sharpen their tools in the same manner and frequently a mechanic can identify his own tools positively by the manner in which they have been sharpened. While the professional skilled burglar is likely to have a special high grade kit of tools the ordinary burglar uses any old tool he happens to have around or which he can get hold of and these tools may be his own or those of someone else, in some instances the proceeds of some previous burglary.

Weapons used to commit assaults, particularly clubs and objects used as clubs, should be carefully handled and examined as they may be expected to have clinging to them hair, blood or cuticle of evidentiary value. Odd objects such as clubs, iron bars, etc., are frequently thrown away by the perpetrator of an assault and to permit their being received in evidence it is necessary that some proof be offered tending to show that the particular object offered in evidence was connected with the commission of the offense. It is also possible that some grease or other identifying material may have been transferred from the hand of the assailant to the weapon used. Here, especially, should be observed the rule that no exhibit should be handled unnecessarily lest valuable evidence be destroyed. 

CHAPTER VII. 

Experiments, Searches, Expert Testimony 

Evidence of experiments conducted to prove or disprove a fact or the statements of the defendant or any of the witnesses is often a valuable means of arriving at the truth. Questions of the ability to see from one place to another, the character and audibility of sound, the length of time it takes to go from one place to another, the actual distance traveled in going to a particular place, the results of pistol, shotgun or rifle fire, the length of time a candle will burn, whether a particular firearm loaded with particular ammunition will produce smoke or a visible flash and the distance and direction in which an automatic pistol will throw the empty shells are all example of experiments which have proven of value in actual cases of importance. To render evidence of an experiment admissible in court the experiment must be conducted under substantially the same conditions as those which existed at the time of the incident which the experiment is intended to prove, disprove or illustrate. Thus, if visibility at night is to be tested, the experiment should be conducted with conditions, so far as artificial and natural light and condition of atmosphere are con-

cerned, practically the same as at the time of the incident in question. Tests with firearms should be made with the identical firearm if possible or at least with a duplicate and the same applies to ammunition. The writer recalls a case in which a lengthy and expensive set of tests to determine the distance at which powder and smoke marks would be made by a discharged shotgun were entirely excluded from evidence because the expert (?) making the tests used ammunition of a different kind than that which had been used in the homicide. It is always advisable to have experiments made by persons not connected with the police or other law enforcing departments as evidence by disinterested witnesses carries greater weight and is not open to the attack that the experiments were not fairly conducted. 

Searches of Person or Property 

The same principle which applies to other phases of criminal investigation thoroughness applies to searches. It would seem too self evident to justify its mention to suggest that the search of a prisoner for weapons immediately after the arrest should be most carefully conducted but the cold fact remains that the list of officers who have been assaulted with deadly weapons and seriously injured or murdered by prisoners in custody is not a short one and has had recent additions. A complete search is also of the utmost importance as a means of disclosing stolen property, means used to commit crime and leads to further investigation. No distinction should be made between cases in which the arrest is for a minor offense and those arrests for major crimes. Men arrested for petty misdemeanors have been known to kill the arresting officers and there is always a fair possibility that every person arrested, regardless of the charge, may have committed a serious crime in which his participation is not suspected. Some years ago a negro arrested merely as a vagrant because he was loitering on the streets after midnight was discovered to have committed a murder. 

Regardless of the reason for his arrest every prisoner is naturally anxious to rid himself of anything of an incriminatory character which may be on his person and will do so, if afforded an opportunity, unless he flatters himself that it is so well hidden that the search of his person will not bring it to light. It is obvious that an absolutely complete search is rarely possible immediately following the arrest but the search should be sufficiently thorough to disclose deadly weapons and obvious evidence. Such a search is not complete if it goes only to the extent of "patting" the hip pockets and looking for a shoulder holster but should always include a search of the pockets, sleeves, coat lapels, all around the trouser waistband and the lower legs to the shoe tops, all of these places being likely locations of weapons and articles easily reached by the prisoner. Articles kept in these locations are likely to be disposed of on the way to the station. In some cases, particularly those of narcotic peddlers, the possibility of an arrest at any time is considered and the contraband is kept so that, upon the first sign of danger, it may be thrown away and not found on the person. 

Such tactics may also be expected from the criminal to whom, for the purpose of entrapment, marked money has been passed.

Upon arrival at the police station all excuse for an incomplete search ceases. The inside of hatbands, neckties, back of coat lapels, trouser cuffs, shoes and all clothing including the space between cloth and lining should be painstakingly gone over. Jailors whe know their business have adopted the rule to compel every prisoner to leave all of his clothing, even though it has been carefully searched, and make a change to jail clothing before being locked up. This practise leaves the clothing available for any subsequent search which may be required and permits the garments to be examined for blood, stains, etc., if there be any or if the case is of a character in which miscroscopic, chemical or bacteriological investigation may be of value. The evidentiary value of discoveries through such scientific investigation is often reduced where it is not conducted until a later occasion and after the prisoner has worn his clothing for some time in the jail. The writer recalls such a case the presence of human blood found on a prisoner's clothing was significant and would have had a telling effect but for the fact that the prisoner claimed that the blood came from a cut finger he had suffered in his cell, a statement which was apparently corroborated by the fact that he had actually cut his finger on a piece of sharp metal on his bunk for which injury he was treated by the jail doctor. While there are cases in which there is no occasion for such a detailed search and degree of care, one can never tell with certainty that a particular prisoner is one who should not be thus handled and the only safe rule is to exercise extreme care in every case. 

The greatest care should be exercised to remove and search and examine every article found in or on the clothing of the prisoner. The first step should be to make a detailed itemized list of every single article. It is not sufficient, for example, to list the total sum of money found on the prisoner. The list should disclose the particular pocket or place, and container, if any, where each piece of money is found and each individual bill and coin should be listed. In a recent case it became important to know the reason why a driver, who had knocked down a pedestrian, had continued to drive on and went into a drug store. The police theory was that he had run away to avoid detection.

The proof showed that when found by an officer the driver was seated at the soda fountain with a silver dollar on the counter before him. The driver claimed that he had laid the dollar on the counter to get five cents in change to telephone for an ambulance. Had the prosecution been able to prove that this story was of doubtful credibility, by showing that the defendant when searched at the station had one or more five

31 cent pieces on his person, the defendant would very likely have been convicted, but the officer who made the search could not remember what coins were on the prisoner and the only record on the property slip was the total of all the money found on his person. On the other hand there have been many cases in which a conviction was made possible or assisted because the prosecution was able to prove exactly and in detail what was found on the defendant at the time of his arrest. 

The officer must not accept articles taken from the prisoner at their apparent face value. Thus we do not ordinarily look upon a fountain pen as a container, unless we consider it as a container of ink, but the experienced officer never overlooks the possibility that it may contain poison, a narcotic, small jeweler's saws or other contraband. Innocent looking pencils or cigarettes may be similarly loaded and packages of tobacco and tobacco pouches are natural hiding places. 

The ordinary citizen would never think of depriving a newly arrested prisoner of his corn cob pipe but the officer should know that the stem of such a pipe is a choice place for hiding a couple of jeweler's saws. 

Even where a careful search has been made it is most inadvisable to allow the prisoner to take any of his possessions to his cell. Even though an article may be entirely innocent and so proven after a most careful examination and may have absolutely nothing to do with the charge on which the prisoner is arrested we must always consider the possibility that the prisoner has committed other crimes and that the article which seems so innocent may be connected with some other crime and that, if the article be of an incriminating character, its return to the prisoner affords him an opportunity of disposing of or destroying it. Letters, notes and memoranda found on a prisoner may, at the time of search, appear perfectly innocent but there is always the possibility that they may be in code or may be of evidentiary value. The only safe rule, if there must be an assumption, is to assume that every article. found on a prisoner may be of value as evidence. Cases are not lost because the investigator has gathered unnecessary evidence but are frequently lost or conviction made more difficult because evidence was overlooked because of lack of effort or because, at the time, it seemed insignificant. 

It should also be the invariable rule to require every prisoner to strip and leave all of his clothing with the property custodian and to examine the person of the prisoner thoroughly for all recent injuries or concealed articles before putting him in jail clothes. Articles and narcotics may be concealed in the hair, ears, nose or under adhesive tape on the soles of the feet. 

Expert Testimony 

Expert and scientific proof has progressed to such an extent that the investigator who does not avail himself of this valuable aid is hopelessly out of date. This does not mean that the investigator should be an expert on ballistics, microscopy, bacteriology, biology, handwriting or fingerprints, but he should know the nature of the assistance the sciences and expert knowledge can give him, what to expect of an expert and what not to expect and what to present to an expert for the latter's examination. As he acquires experience, especially if he is sufficiently interested in his work to study and experiment to increase his knowledge, the investigator will himself become an expert to a degree, but under no circumstance should he permit himself to act as the expert, but should call in the expert even though it only be to confirm and check his conclusions. 

Reference has been made in another part of this work to a case in New York of a man convicted of murder and sentenced to be electrocuted who was later exonerated when it was found that the purported firearms expert upon whose testimony he was largely convicted was not only in error in his conclusion that the fatal bullet had come from the defendant's revolver but, had the expert been only half efficient he would have discovered that the bullet and the rifling in the defendant's gun were radically dissimilar. The writer is personally familiar with another murder case handled by him while in the district attorney's office in which the investigating officers spent days trying to connect a man with the crime as the one who furnished the thirty-two calibre pistol to one of the slayers. When their attention was called to the fact that the fatal bullet showed that the rifling had a left twist and that it could not have come from the gun of the suspect, it having a right twist, they were very much disappointed. 

In another well known case the officers proceeded on the theory that a supply of chemicals in the possession of a man suspected of complicity in a bombing outrage was a part of the ingredients of an explosive, only to find out later that it was nothing but epsom salts. In this latter case it could hardly be expected that the officers would be sufficiently familiar with chemistry to tell the difference, but in the former case a careful examination and observation of the fatal bullet and the suspect's gun would have disclosed the true facts and a little knowledge of firearms would have enabled them to eliminate the suspect's gun once its make was known even without a comparison. One of the best means of improving the preparation of cases for presentation to a jury is the more common use of experts and it is to be hoped that, especially in the larger communities, arrangements will be made whereby the investigating officers can have such service available to assist them. 

All articles intended to be submitted to experts should be most carefully preserved and protected from all possible injury or contamination. What may seem to be mere dirt may be, and usually is, found by the expert to be capable of identification as a specific substance or substances which have a definite relationship to the crime and care should be taken that this "dirt” is not removed and, since it may require chemistry to decide its character, nothing should be brought into contact with it which might contaminate it. The Microscope 

The ability of the microscope to magnify small objects to almost unbelievable size is of inestimable value. 

Under its glass the hair of one person may be distinguished from that of another or identified with hair found in connection with an investigation. Hair found in the hand of a murder victim may be the means of identifying and convicting the guilty person as may also the hair found in a cap dropped by a burglar or, as in the "torso murder case, may be the means of identifying the body of a murder victim. Examination of the hair found will disclose whether it has been recently cut or singed, dyed or bleached, and, to a certain extent, will indicate the age of the person from whom it came. Dust picked up from the floor of the room. under investigation may tell much for, after all, dust or dirt in a room has either been blown into the room or carried there and, in the latter instance, the dust consists largely of tiny particles detached from the shoes, clothing and persons of the occupants or articles brought into the room. Brushing a grey woolen suit will cause tiny particles of grey wool to fall to the floor, opening a package packed in excelsior will be almost certain to leave tiny particles of excelsior on the floor even after sweeping with a broom. Dirt found on the shoes displays its peculiar characteristics, and, in many instances, will disclose where the wearer has been, and the same may be said of the dust and dirt on an automobile. In these days when automobiles are so frequently involved in crimes there should be many opportunities of proof through the dust and dirt in and out side of the car. While the microscope cannot make all of the possible scientific distinctions between different kinds of blood it can often distinguish between some of the kinds of blood and a very tiny speck of foreign substance may, by the microscope, be known to be blood and probably human blood. An examination of weapons and instruments believed to be connected with a crime may show easily identifiable matter; in the nicks in a knife blade, may show whether the nick is a recent one and will reveal much that is hidden to the naked eye. 

Under the microscope tiny fibres are easily recognizable as hemp, cotton, silk or wool, and a few fibres left by a criminal in his passage over a back fence may be identified as having come from his suit of clothes. Dust on clothing will often indicate the occupation of the owner since constant association with a particular kind of material will almost certainly result in tiny particles becoming detached and embedded in the clothing. Thus the working clothes of a manufacturing jeweler will carry tiny particles of gold, those of the chemist will bear signs of chemicals, and the carpenter will pick up in his clothing tiny particles of wood. Dust in the pockets of clothing will contain particles of the articles carried-tobacco, wood from loose matches, metallic dust from coins carried-and may also carry particles of blood and other foreign material which became detached from the hand when inserted therein. In sexual cases the clothing of the accused and the complainant as well as any towels, cloths, furniture, etc., which might possibly bear spermatozoa or blood should also be submitted to the expert.

Observations with the microscope require the services of an expert who is not only able to operate a microscope and take full advantage of its possibilities, but who is also able to distinguish what he sees. Thus the examination of suspected blood, spermatozoa, hair and other substances connected with the body, should be left to a man with medical training while the examination for metals and chemicals should be conducted by a man specially qualified in chemistry. Chemistry

Chemistry finds its most common use in the analysis of suspected poisons and the contents of the stomach and of other organs of the body to determine whether poison is present. But, as heretofore indicated, the field of the chemist includes the examination of a multiple variety of spots, stains and materials. If the identity or composition of a substance, suspected to have some connection with the crime, is in doubt the chemist will, in most instances, be enabled to determine its character.

Other Sciences 

There is hardly a science which will not at times be of real assistance to the investigator, Questions involving light, speed, speed of falling bodies, heat and electrical phenomena, are often solved by the laws of physics. Astronomy, and particularly the service which can be obtained from the weather bureaus will tell us when the sun and moon rose and set on a particular

33 day to the very minute, whether there were clouds in the sky or not, the condition of the weather and the length of shadows cast at a particular place at a particular time of day. In a rather famous murder case a witness claimed to have seen and identified a person standing in a particular position in the middle of the night. The story was effectively attacked by proof of the position of the moon at the time in question and that, at that time, the place where the person alleged to have been identified was standing, was in a heavy shadow and experiments conducted with this information showed that it would have been physically impossible for the witness to have seen what she claimed. 

Medical science, though the most commonly used aid of the investigator in cases involving attacks upon persons, is not called upon as often as it should be. 

Prisoners frequently carry evidences of wounds and other recent injuries, apparently of no value as evidence and possibly wholly disconnected from the charge upon which the arrest was made, but which, had they been discovered at the time, would prove to be very important. Some years ago a man was arrested by Los Angeles police officers for no definite reason other than that he was acting suspiciously. One of the detectives noticed a cut on his hand and the prisoner explained it by saying that he was a barber, which he was, and that he had cut his hand honing a razor. When the officer, noticing that the man was right handed, called attention to the fact that the explanation of the cut was physically impossible the prisoner stated that he did not know how he received the cut. No significance was attached to the incident other than that it was apparent that the man had lied but shortly thereafter the prisoner was turned over to the sheriff, who had connected him with the burglary of a fur store in which the entry had been made by smashing a large window. The tiny cut on the hand and the prisoner's false statements regarding it were a big factor in his ultimate conviction. The defense of insanity could be largely eliminated if all prisoners were examined by a competent psychiatrist, especially those whose crimes were of a character in which insanity might be pleaded, particularly cases of homicide, felonious assaults, crimes by ex-convicts, forgers and check writers whose activities are of an unusual or childish or illogical character. Handwriting 

The handwriting expert is useful not only in cases of forgery but in all cases where there is any writing to be traced to its writer. A written memorandum or letter dropped on the scene of a crime or found on the person of the victim may lead to the apprehension of the guilty party. Inks, papers and pencils have distinguishing characteristics which enables the expert to identify them and distinguish them from what appears to the casual observer an identical specimen. The expert may be able to tell from a writing whether it was written recently or some time ago, whether it was all written by the same person or two different persons, whether it was all written at the same time or at different times. The instance is not rare of a party producing a diary which the witness claims to have kept from day to day only to have the expert prove that it was all written at the same time. Books of account may appear perfectly regular, setting forth transactions over a period of time or a hotel register may be produced to support an alibi; the expert can prove whether this evidence is manufactured or not. 

In securing specimens or exemplars of handwritings it is rather a common occurrence for the person to be asked to write the words of the document under investigation. While this is a good idea, it should be done at the close of the interview with the individual and not at its beginning. If the individual is the guilty author of the questioned document he will immediately be put on his guard when asked to write the very words of the incriminating document. Then, also, it is easier for a person to simulate or disguise handwriting when he knows and is familiar with the words he is to write and can devote more of his attention to the effort of writing than if he had to think of the substance of what he is writing. It is always a good idea to have the person write all of the letters of the alphabet, both capital and small letters, and the numbers from one to ten, but, instead of allowing the writer to place them down in their natural order, better results will be obtained if they are dictated to him in irregular order, or he is asked to write them in reverse of their usual order. It is also a good idea to dictate and have the individual write what is dictated and to incorporate in the dictation words similar to those in the questioned document, similar word endings and beginnings and the capital letters appearing in that paper. If it can be done it will also help to have the person being tested write a statement or some description of an incident with which he is familiar or to ask him to write a hundred words about just anything. The object of these methods is to secure the individual's handwriting characteristics and to take his mind off of the thought of how he is writing. If the questioned document is written in ink the exemplars should be written in ink and with a pen of the same general kind as appears to have been used in that document; if the questioned document is in pencil it is well to secure specimens both in pencil and in ink.

Contributed by James Clark Sellers, Examiner of Questioned Documents There is probably no act that an adult performs which is more identifying than his writing; and inasmuch as it is such a common act there are few cases indeed which do not possess some handwriting feature. Unfortunately those who are investigating a matter often do not realize this and overlook the very evidence which may solve the case.

A handwriting expert's services are often thought of as being necessary only where it is suspected a document has been forged, whereas the expert's evidence may solve many other types of cases. Notations in a memorandum book, the handwriting on an automobile ownership certificate, an ink offset on a blotter, a piece of paper found at the scene of a crime, or a host of other documents which at first may appear of little importance, later may prove to be the most important evidence in the case.

A scrap of paper found in the watch pocket of an unidentified murder victim, overlooked in two previous searches through his clothing, established his identity and led to the apprehension and conviction of the murderer. 

A blotter found in the home of a murder suspect bore an ink offset from a woman's signature which had been forged to an assignment of stock. The motive for her murder was to obtain her valuable stock in a wellknown public utility. The forger blotted the forged signature and kept the blotter in his desk for several months, where it was found during an investigation of the case. It proved to be highly incriminating evidence. 

A paper tablet found in the room of a kidnap suspect contained on the first sheet indentations which corresponded exactly with an extortion letter demanding ransom money. The indentations on the tablet were at first overlooked, but when discovered became important evidence.

The subject of questioned documents is by no means restricted to the identification of handwriting, but also embraces the identification of typewriting, inks, paper, writing instruments, the sequence of crossed strokes, the alteration of documents, and other kindred questions which may arise about a document. 

Due to the increased use of the typewriter numerous identification questions arise about typewritten documents. On account of use and misuse type becomes de- 34 fective on a typewriter, making it possible to identify typewriting as being written on a particular machine.

Some spurious documents are unwittingly dated back beyond the date when the watermark was manufactured, or even before certain constituents of the paper were used as paper-making materials. 

The sequence of two crossed lines may enable the determination of whether or not a fraudulent addition has been made to a document. 

An erased area on a document may contain valuable evidence that can be made readable by ultra-violet rays or by infra-red ray photography, or by other aids in deciphering obliterated writings.

In the search for facts nothing should be overlooked, and all documents likely to become evidence should be handled with extreme care and protected against alteration, careless or otherwise, in their physical condition. 

Protection of Documents 

A document which may become important evidence should be enclosed between two pieces of glass, or better, enclosed unfolded in a cellophane envelope. A document protected in this way can be read on both sides and handled without soiling. It is a wise precaution to have it photographed actual size as soon as possible. 

A questioned document should be protected against putting any new evidence on it or removing any evidence already there. It is bad practice to touch a questioned document with a pencil or pen or with any sharp instrument. Making underscores or circles around words to call attention to them is amateurish, is very poor procedure, and may result in the document being excluded from evidence. No new folds should be placed in an important document, and it should be handled the minimum amount necessary.

The indiscriminate use of fingerprint powder on a document may obscure other evidence, and the use of chemicals in an attempt to develop latent fingerprints may remove ink writing on the paper. A microscope should not be placed directly on a document, but an intervening layer of thick celluloid with a suitable aperture cut in it should be placed over the document. 

This enables one to examine it with the microscope and yet prevents the base of the microscope from rubbing and marring the document.

Handwriting Identification

It is comparatively simple to state a rule by which handwriting and all other things are identified. However, it is not always a simple task to identify handwriting or to answer the numerous questions which may arise respecting a disputed document. Experience, study, research, and a developed ability to observe accurately and reason correctly are background necessities if one is to avoid the numerous pitfalls which ensnare the careless and the unqualified. 

A person is identified by handwriting in precisely the same manner that a man is identified by his fingerprints, or by an accurate personal description including scars and moles in addition to general characteristics such as the color of the eyes and color of the hair, or in the same manner as an automobile or a horse is identified; that is by a sufficient combination of identifying characteristics.

Handwriting is developed by practice in doing the same thing repeatedly until it becomes easier to write in a certain manner and difficult to write in any other way. Thus handwriting habits are formed; in fact a mature handwriting is a succession of habits. Many of these become deeply ingrained. The writer of an anonymous letter is confronted with the very difficult task of eliminating these lifelong habits in an attempt to avoid identification. The forger is confronted with a still more difficult undertaking, that of throwing off all his own characteristics and at the same time taking on all the characteristics of the genuine writer. 

Handwriting identification consists of comparing the handwriting characteristics in the exemplar handwriting with those in the questioned writing. These characteristics are not alone the design of the letters, but include the writing movement, the line quality, the shading, the slant, the proportionate size of one letter to another, the amount of natural variation, and the writing skill.

If a sufficient number of similarities are found to exist in the known genuine handwriting and in the questioned, with no difference which cannot be reasonably accounted for, a conclusion is reached that the person who wrote the exemplars wrote the questioned handwriting.

It is in evaluating the weight to be given to the various features and qualities of the handwriting that difficulty is encountered, and distinguishes the qualified from the unqualified observer.

The evaluation, or the judging of the weight that should be given each characteristic is far from being a mere counting of the similarities and dissimilarities. 

One fundamental difference may outweigh numerous similarities, or a few inconspicuous characteristics may outweigh obvious general characteristics. Proper appraisement requires not only careful observation but also an ability to reason correctly about the thing observed. The ability to appraise handwriting characteristics correctly is gained partly through actual experience in examining hundreds of questioned handwritings. In writing about the process of comparison A. S. 

Osborn in his work Questioned Documents, Second Edition, states: "The qualified and experienced student of the subject brings to this performance of comparison, not only the document under examination and the exemplars or standard genuine documents in hand, but all of his knowledge of other forged signatures and documents and of genuine documents that have been attacked as forgeries. This phase of comparison is inevitable unless one has had no experience and it is this special knowledge that makes of the act of comparison something more than the shallow performance of the novice who has never seen a forgery or a fraudulent document. He does not know what to look for, nor what to look at; he is as helpless as he would be in a laboratory, or in a library, without knowledge and without experience."

The principal causes of error in identifying handwriting are: 

1. Mistaking general characteristics for individual characteristics. 

2. An inability to observe differences or to evaluate the weight which should be given each characteristic. 

3. Mistaking disguise or natural variation for fundamental differences. 

4. Mistaking similarities in form as indicating genuineness and overlooking significant indications of forgery such as a slowly drawn, hesitant line quality. 

5. Centering attention on one phase only of the problem and overlooking other important evidence on the document. 

6. A lack of technical equipment to examine the document properly. 7. 8. 

A lack of technical knowledge.

A biased state of mind that prevents an impartial consideration of the problem. 

9. Arriving at a dogmatic opinion based on an insufficient amount of questioned writing. 

10. Arriving at an opinion based on an insufficient amount of exemplar specimens.

Specimen Exemplars 

Since identification of a person by his handwriting is accomplished by a comparison of his known genuine handwriting with the handwriting in question, it is

There is no rule that can be laid down in advance as to what kind of specimens of handwriting will be most helpful in arriving at a correct conclusion regarding the identity of the writer. In some cases handwriting written in the regular course of business is better, while in others handwriting written by request is more valuable. For these reasons it is a wise precaution to get all the specimens of handwriting possible at the first opportunity. A suspect may be willing to give extended specimens of his handwriting at the first interview but may refuse to give any at a subsequent time. 

If more than a signature is in question it is advisable to obtain some extended specimens of a suspect's handwriting. In that case a text should be dictated to the suspect with which he is unfamiliar and which will focus his attention largely on the matter to be written; thus a more natural writing is secured than if disjointed letters or words are dictated to him. The following text includes all of the letters of the alphabet, the numerals, and most of the punctuation marks and abbreviations in common use:

If such a letter is dictated three times, faster at each dictation, it is quite likely most of the real handwriting characteristics of the writer will be present. As each sheet is written by the suspect that sheet should be taken from his view, so that he cannot copy the forms previously made. By this test it usually becomes evident whether or not the suspect is trying to disguise his writing. However, even his disguised writing may prove valuable, especially in anonymous letter cases. 

A guilty suspect may use the same disguise that he used to write the anonymous communication. That occurred in the Lindbergh Kidnaping Case. When first asked to write Bruno Richard Hauptmann attempted to disguise his handwriting and in so doing he wrote almost a facsimile of the disguised writing in the extortion letters to Colonel Charles A. If the document in question is written with pen and ink, then the suspect should be asked to write with the same type of pen and ink; that is, if a flexible pen and blue ink has been used in writing the questioned document the suspect should be provided with a pen of similar flexibility and blue ink of the same type with which to write the exemplars. Also paper should be used which is similar in quality, color, and size to that of the questioned document. If it is claimed the document was written while the writer was standing at a counter, or with the paper against the wall, or on a book, or on the knee, or in any other peculiar or irregular writing position, then have the suspect do similar writing under as nearly as possible the same conditions and writing position. If a hard pencil, or a colored pencil, or an indelible pencil was used in writing the questioned document, then the suspect should use the same kind of a writing instrument.

It is advisable to have the suspect write the exact wording contained in the questioned document, after other suitable specimens have been obtained. Contrary to general belief the first writing written by the guilty suspect of the exact wording in the questioned document is frequently the most identifying. If it is known in advance that only a limited amount of request writing can be obtained, it is advisable to obtain all or at least some of the same words as were used in the questioned writing, including all misspelled words. 

If the questioned document is a forged check the suspect should be asked to write several specimen checks for the same amount. It is best to use a check form that is similar to the one in question. Many persons acquire a distinct individuality in writing out checks which would be lost if he were merely asked to write on an ordinary piece of paper. 

It should be borne in mind that sometimes an innocent person cannot write a fair specimen of his handwriting when he knows it is to be used to compare with some disputed handwriting. It may be well to supplement writing obtained by request with writing written in the regular course of business. Cancelled checks, memorandum books, and letters; and signatures to legal documents, hotel registers, voting registrations, gas and telephone service applications, and bank signature cards furnish useful exemplars for comparison purposes.

The three general types of ink in common use today are (1) carbon ink, which writes black and remains black; (2) commercial iron-nutgall ink, which writes blue and turns black; and (3) aniline inks, such as nigrosine which writes black and remains black and the colored aniline inks which remain more or less constantly their original color.

The iron-nutgall inks, which write blue and gradually turn black, are more widely used than any other. 

This ink is a chemical solution which filters into the fibers of the paper and oxidizes there. It is this type of ink that furnishes the most definite basis for an estimate of its age. If an allegedly old document is presented bearing this type of ink and the color is still blue, it was written recently.

Much misinformation has been written respecting what can be told from an examination of inks. It is difficult to tell the age of a document from the ink alone except under very favorable circumstances. 

Carbon ink, sometimes called India or China ink, is the oldest ink known, and was in use for centuries. 

Lucas reports that he examined writing written with carbon ink on documents in Egypt dating back to 1580 B. C. A good grade of carbon ink is very permanent but does not penetrate the fibers of the paper as deeply as does the iron-nutgall type of ink. Writings with carbon ink cannot be bleached or erased by chemical eradicators, but may be erased by abrasion or by washing. 

Aniline colored inks such as blue, violet, green, red, and nigrosine (black) are in common use. These inks maintain their original color more or less constantly and are distinguishable by their color or by chemical tests from the iron-nutgall and carbon inks. Some aniline inks fade in sunlight and this fading may be mistaken for age.

Chemical reagents should always be used with great care and should be applied to writing in very minute quantities. The sharpened end of a wooden toothpick or a wooden applicator such as a doctor uses for swabbing a patient's throat makes a good applicator for applying chemical reagents to ink strokes. The chemical reaction should be watched under a microscope and the color reaction observed both while wet and after it has dried. A skillfully applied chemical reagent cannot be detected easily afterward with the naked eye. There is no justification for obliterating or smudging a large area of a writing in order to test the ink properly. Iron-nutgall ink turns a bluish color when a

There are many other types of inks, such as logwood, which are not now in common use. Secret or invisible inks were used quite extensively during war times and are occasionally used by criminals to send secret messages. Writing Instruments 

Steel pens have been in common use only during the Under past century, being preceded by quill pens. favorable circumstances the pen used in writing a document may be identified, if the point is defective or some other identifying feature is present. The angle at which the pen is held, the speed and pressure exerted, and the viscosity of the ink are all factors that must be considered. It may be much easier to say a certain pen was not used than to identify the pen that was actually used. 

Black lead pencils made from graphite date from the 16th Century, but before that pencils were in use made from metallic lead. It is frequently difficult to say two writings were written with the same identical lead pencil. Here again it may be easier to say that two writings were not written with the same pencil. A.

Paper as a background for writing has been in use. for more than a thousand years. During practically all of that time linen and cotton have provided the fibers principally used for paper-making. However, wood fibers have today extensively displaced linen and cotton fibers. But it is principally during the past fifty years that this change has taken place. This fact has provided the means for not only exposing allegedly old handwritten documents as being spurious, but also for proving whole alleged first editions of printed books to be fabrications. Thus a "first" edition bearing the date 1842 was printed on sulphite wood paper. The process for making this paper was not patented until 1866.

Watermarks in paper have long been known to have identifying value. Occasionally a careless fabricator will write a document on a paper bearing a watermark that is much younger than the date written on the document. Some watermarks carry the date in them, either in code or plainly written out. Others carry no date information, but frequently the date can be ascertained by tracing the date of manufacture of the "dandy roll" which impressed the watermark in the paper.

Paper may be differentiated in a number of ways, among them by its color, size of sheet, weight, thickness, opaqueness, direction of grain, finish, watermark, or fiber content. 

Fraudulent Alterations

Interlineations, additions, erasures, discoloration, or mutilation of the paper may be clues which lead to the exposure of a fraud. Some investigators are "signature-conscious" and think if the signature is genuine that that is all that is necessary, whereas many frauds are perpetrated by altering a document above a genuine signature. 

In one noted case a woman obtained a signature of a well-to-do old man to a memorandum. He died soon after and she filed a will leaving her all his property. 

She had erased the memorandum and had written in the terms of a will above his genuine signature. It was at first thought by his heirs that the will was genuine because the signature appeared genuine. However an examiner of questioned documents was consulted by the lawyers for the estate. The expert discovered erasures above the signature and an ink test disclosed the ink in the body of the will differed from the ink in the signature, although it was alleged by the claimant that the will was all written and signed with the same pen and ink. As a result of these discoveries the woman was charged with forgery. One of the "witnesses" to the will disappeared but when apprehended he admitted his part in the attempted fraud and pleaded guilty. The woman was convicted of forgery, as was a second "witness" to the spurious will.

In another case a typewritten power of attorney was converted into an alleged will by adding on the same typewriter the words "and I also request at my death my estate goes to her." This very significant clause did not align with the typewriting preceding and following it, and the ribbon ink impression in the added clause did not match the color of the other typewriting on the document. Also it had been necessary to make an erasure to make room for the added clause. The erasure partly obliterated one stroke of the signature, which established that the document was already signed when the alteration was made. The will was declared a forgery and denied probate even though two alleged witnesses testified to the genuineness of the will. 

In another case a property owner was induced to sign some options for the rental of property, and unwittingly was tricked into signing each of four papers

Each paper was later cut off just below the writing, leaving a blank space above the genuine signatures. 

The terms of a promissory note were then typed in above each signature, and subsequently suit was instituted to collect the amount of the notes, amounting to over $20,000. An examiner of questioned documents pointed out to the court that the document had been trimmed and that there was other evidence present indicating that the document was not then in the condition it was in when signed. Nine months later the man who had attempted the fraud made a confession and explained how he had obtained the genuine signatures and that he had subsequently trimmed the papers and typed in the alleged promissory notes. 

Mutilations of a questioned document should always arouse an inquiring curiosity, if not actual suspicion. A part of a paper bearing a spurious writing is sometimes torn or cut to get rid of a date, or to remove printing on a letterhead, or to eliminate some other tell-tale evidence that would incriminate the perpetrator. Sometimes fabricated documents are artificially discolored to give them the appearance of age or to hide incriminating evidence.

The sequence of crossed strokes sometimes becomes an issue. This is particularly true on check endorsements, where the question arises as to whether or not "Without recourse" or "Paid in full" or some other like notation was written before the endorsement of the payee. Although this question can often be definitely answered, sometimes it is a very perplexing problem. The heavier line usually appears to be on top, especially to the inexperienced observer. One sure indication of which line is on top is the spreading of an ink when it intersects an ink line already there. If the ink first written has not thoroughly dried, another line crossing it will usually run in one or both directions along the first line.

An ink line is often repelled by a freshly typewritten line. The oil in the ribbon ink occasions this repulsion of the ink from the pen. Under the microscope this phenomenon sometimes appears to those unfamiliar with it as though the writer had lifted the pen on approaching the typewritten line and replaced it on the other side.

The Document Expert 

No man can be a master of all trades successfully. 

Even the field of identification is far too broad for one person to master. Recognizing that fact has resulted in a growing tendency toward specialization in one branch of the identification field. Thus, there are now specialists who devote all their time to ballistics, others to fingerprints, some to toolmarks, others to the chemical aspects of criminal investigations, and still others to questioned documents. While it is not expected that the investigating officer should be an expert himself in the many fields of identification, nevertheless he should know something about the subject, know what to look for, how to preserve the evidence found, and when a qualified specialist should be consulted.

The field of usefulness of the document expert is growing. This is due partly to the scientific advancements made by the experts and partly to a better reception by judges and jurors of expert testimony respecting questioned document problems. This is likewise true of other experts dealing with physical evidence. A document, a fingerprint, a bullet are all physical objects that can be taken into court. They can be seen and they can be examined by the jurors or judge. Such evidence is frequently its own witness. 

As the Supreme Court of Nebraska in the O'Connor 

Estate case (179 N. W. 401-1920) has so well stated: "Silent circumstances, without power to change their attitude, or to make explanations, or to commit perjury, may speak as truthfully in court as animated witnesses." or as the Supreme Court of Oregon stated in the case of Lauderbach v. Multnomah County (226 Pac. 6971924): "The exhibit is dumb, but as evidence it is most effectual."

There has been a great advancement in scientific equipment which has added immeasurably to the skill of the examiner of questioned documents, both as aids in the discovery of the facts and in demonstrating in court the correctness of a conclusion. The wide-field binocular microscope with special lighting equipment. color filters, the camera, ultra-violet light, infra-red sensitive photographic plates, polarized light screens, precise measuring instruments, systematized and extensive collections of reference materials such as specimens of handwriting written by persons of all ages, and specimens of typewriting from each model of the various typewriters, and of paper and inks, combined with a scientific attitude, have done much toward increasing the usefulness of the expert specializing in the examination of questioned documents.

CHAPTER IX. 

Firearms and Bullets, Ballistics

Having so much to do with firearms it would be supposed that the police, sheriffs and constables would be fairly familiar with this type of weapon, but the writer's experience as a prosecutor and on the bench leads him to the conclusion that such knowledge on the part of peace officers is the exception and not the rule. 

Every officer should be reasonably familiar with the commonly used types of revolvers and pistols; he should know the difference between a pistol and a revolver, how each type of arm functions, and should be able to distinguish between the commoner makes of the American manufacturer and also be able to distinguish them from their Spanish imitations, He should know that certain kinds of revolvers cannot be discharged by dropping or hitting them on the hammer and why it is that they cannot be so discharged. Thus the Iver- 

Johnson has a separate firing pin attached to a small piece of steel and the hammer is cut away in such a manner that it cannot strike the firing pin until, by the pulling back of the trigger, a lever is pushed between the firing pin and the hammer so that, when the hammer falls, its force is communicated through this lever to the firing pin causing it to hit the cartridge. Smith & Wesson have in some of their guns, but not in the .44 Military or .45 model of 1917, a block of steel placed below the hammer in such a manner and so controlled that, unless the trigger is pulled back, the lower part of the hammer rests on this block and cannot possibly go down far enough to permit the firing pin on the top of the hammer to get anywhere near the cartridge.

The Colt has a lever attached to the trigger in such a manner that when the trigger is in its normal position a piece of steel is between the frame of the gun and the face of the hammer, thereby preventing the firing pin from hitting the cartridge but which piece of steel is pulled out of the way when the trigger is pulled back. 

Such knowledge will promptly dispose of the claim of a defendant that the gun dropped on the floor and was accidentally discharged. It is also well to know that cartridges loaded with black powder are very rare in revolver ammunition, and that pistol and revolver ammunition is almost all loaded with Bulls-Eye or Du 

Pont Pistol Powder No. 5, but .22 calibre ammunition is loaded with semi-smokeless, although it too is coming to be loaded with smokeless powder. Given merely a bullet found in a body the expert can, in almost every case, determine and prove not only the calibre, but also the manufacturer of the ammunition, and the kind of weapon from which it was fired, and whether or not it was fired from a particular weapon; similar information may often be given if, instead of the bullet, the discharged shell alone is submitted to the expert. Truly scientific identification of bullets and firearms requires not only a thorough knowledge of the various kinds of guns, bullets and ammunition, but the ability to efficiently use a microscope, to know what to look for, and to be able to recognize it when it is present. In addition to the microscope other expensive apparatus is required. While this is not possible to the investigator because of its being a profession by itself, every officer should be familiar with the principles of such identifications, and should also have some knowledge of the subject to be utilized in the course of the investigation.

As the bullet progresses through the barrel the little scratches acquired are rubbed or burnished off, but the tiny marks left by the last few millimeters of the barrel near the muzzle remain on the bullets unless destroyed by its contact with some hard object. It sometimes happens that, earlier in its travels through the barrel, the bullet passes over a large defect or imperfection the effect of which is not removed in its passage through the balance of the barrel. Every other bullet fired through the weapon while it is in the same condition will bear the same tiny characteristic scratches, the "finger prints" of the barrel. Thus a bullet found in the victim of a shooting may be compared by the expert with a test bullet fired from the weapon suspected to have been used. This comparison is made under a special microscope, the fatal and test bullets being held by a special device, and should only be made by a qualified expert. The test bullets are fired into a bale of cotton which effectively stops the bullets without destroying any of the tiny marks on their sides. To the naked eye the test bullet will display many facts, particularly whether the twist of the rifling is right or left, the number of lands and grooves and their comparative size, all information which, as will be noted a little later herein, will declare whether as to these details the test bullet is like the fatal bullet and, incidentally a visual examination of the test bullet will often put the officer on the right track as to the kind of weapon from which the bullet was fired. The investigator should not, however, handle the bullet connected with the crime any more than is necessary and, if a bullet is removed from a body the surgeon should be cautioned to use all possible care to avoid injuring or effacing any of the tiny marks on its sides. 

As there is no necessity that firing pins be absolutely identical or perfect in shape there is a very distinct difference between the shapes of the striking ends of firing pins even in two guns of the same make consecutively turned out. The powerful blow of the firing pin on the shell or primer stamps the imprint of the firing pin, with all its defects of shape, imperfections and possible file marks, on the shell or primer and these same identifying marks may be expected to be found on any shell fired in the same gun. It is sometimes claimed that the position on the primer or shell at which the firing pin or hammer strikes is the same on all shells fired in the same gun. While this is to be expected in rifles and automatic pistols and most revolvers of high quality, there are individual revolvers in which, by reason of defect in the crane, cylinder or cylinder mechanism, the firing pin or hammer will strike different shells in slightly different relative places and, consequently, the fact that the depression in the shell or primer is off center should not be too greatly relied upon as being a characteristic which will be found on all shells from the same revolver. 

When we consider the terrific pressure generated when a cartridge explodes it is easily seen that this pressure has a strong tendency to force the sides of the shell against the sides of the chamber and the base of the shell against the breech thereby imprinting defects in the chamber and breech upon the metal. 

This imprinting is especially noticeable on copper shells and copper primers because of their comparative softness. The finish on the breech of a gun is not so perfect but what there are left tiny scratch or file marks of characteristic shape and arrangement which, when impressed upon a discharged shell, are a valuable means of identification. 

The value of the information which can be obtained from bullets and shells lies not only in identifying them with a suspected weapon but in preventing their being connected with such a weapon when in fact they came from an entirely different gun. Unless he really knows, the investigator should not jump to conclusions in the matter of guns and ammunition. The finding of a discharged .32 calibre automatic shell does not necessarily prove that it was fired in an automatic pistol. There are some revolvers of .32 calibre which will take and discharge ammunition intended for the automatic. Neither is it certain that a metal jacketed bullet has come from an automatic pistol as there are cartridges carrying bullets of .32-20, .32, .38, .38-40 and .44-40 calibre made for revolvers which are metal jacketed. Incidentally the witness who describes a bullet as being "steel jacketed" is justifiably suspected of knowing very little about firearms and ammunition as there are no steel jacketed bullets made in this country though a few are made abroad. A test with a magnet will disclose whether a bullet is jacketed with steel and, if it is attracted by a magnet, we can say with assurance that it is of European make. The 

American made jacketed bullet has a plated copper or copper alloy jacket which, it may be added, is soft enough to carry the "fingerprints" of the rifling.

Most factories carry and will furnish to police offi cers, if given the serial number of one of their guns, the dealer to whom that particular gun was sold and, in those jurisdictions where dealers are required to keep records of sales of guns, these records furnish at least the initial steps in tracing a gun to its owner. The fact that it appears that all of the identifying numbers have been filed off of a gun does not necessarily prevent that number from being ascertained. By the secret process quite generally known to the police obliterated num bers may often be "developed," the process being based upon the fact that where numbers have been stamped into metal, the metal under the impression is denser and the numbers may be made visible by the process which, for obvious reasons, cannot be set out here. 

Even if this fails, there is still a chance to learn the number of the gun for it is quite possible that it is one of the guns carrying a secret number in a place the location of which will be known to the expert.

The investigator should know the outstanding char acteristics of the more commonly used guns. The Rem ington automatic pistol, .32 or .380 calibre, is the only pistol the rifling of which contains seven narrow lands and the rifling has a twist to the right. Colt's automatic pistols and revolvers have six grooves, the grooves being nearly twice the width of the lands and are the only pistols and revolvers made in the United States in which the rifling twists to the left. It is generally a safe guess to say that a bullet bearing the marks of left twist rifling came from a Colt's, but it must be borne in mind that there is always a possibility that a left twist bullet may have come from a foreign gun; the question can, however, be absolutely answered by the real expert. Smith & Wesson, Iver-Johnson, Harrington and Richardson revolvers are very similar in their rifling, each having five lands and grooves, all having a right twist but differing in degree of twist and, microscopically, in the width of the lands and grooves. As to Smith & Wesson guns, however, it should be remembered that the .32 D. A. and Safety, the .45 calibre made for the United States during the late war, the .22 calibre, and the automatics have six grooves. The Savage .32 calibre automatic pistol has six grooves and lands, but is easily distinguishable from the Colt's .32 calibre automatic which, though it uses the same ammunition, has a left twist while the Savage has a right twist to the rifling.

The weight of a bullet is an important factor in its identification and, to the expert, who has at his disposal the technical data and records, it is an important factor in determining the calibre of the bullet and the identity of the manufacturer. Not infrequently a bullet is injured in its flight by striking some intermediate object or injured in striking the body in which it is found and, as a result, the bullet may be broken into two or more pieces. If such appears to have been possible the surgeon should be urged to secure all of the pieces of the bullet so that its weight may be known. 

The particular shape of the bullet, the character of its jacket or other coating and the chemical composition of the bullet metal are all factors from which the real expert will be enabled to draw accurate conclusions of great assistance in the making of identifications. 

CHAPTER X. 

Witnesses--Honest, Dishonest Witness 

Under our American practice the task of securing the necessary witnesses is left to the officer handling the case. These witnesses are sometimes supplemented by other witnesses suggested by the prosecuting attorney or the complaining witness. The writer has gained the impression that it seems to be the idea of the average officer to secure only the minimum of witnesses to prove the people's case; the question of witnesses to meet the possible defense receives little or no attention and is left to be taken care of by the witnesses by whom the case in chief is to be proved. The witnesses secured are interviewed briefly by the officer, but rarely does the prosecutor receive more than the sketchiest outline of what these witnesses will say when placed on the witness stand. As a result of this loose practice not only are many valuable witnesses left uncalled but the witnesses who are called do not give all of the knowledge they possess because the prosecutor has no means of knowing all that the witnesses know. 

Every available witness should be interviewed, his entire testimony in detail reduced to writing and, if possible, signed by the witness and turned over to the prosecutor, leaving to him the question of dispensing with the testimony of such witnesses as may be unnecessary. Only a good trial lawyer is competent to determine whether or not a particular witness should be placed upon the stand and even he is liable to make a mistake of judgment in this regard and it is almost impossible for an investigator to make such a determination without making a mistake.

The Defects of Honest Witnesses 

What has previously been said with reference to memory and the ability to relate a past occurrence is more strongly applicable to the layman than to the police officer. Nearly every witness finds it difficult to describe accurately what he remembers, partly because of imperfect observation but also because of his inability to express himself. The latter defect may be largely overcome by going over the story with the witness not by leading or suggestive questioning by an inquisitive method calling for the relation of details and exact information. It is quite commonly known that a person observes only about ten per cent of that which he could have observed; he can fix his attention only upon a part of what he sees and he observes only that which has an apparent interest to him. 

Witnesses are also frequently handicapped by inaccuracy or defect of one or more of the senses. Some people are wholly and some are partially color blind, others have other defects of vision, others have defects.

It is the duty of the investigator to ascertain if any such defects exist, conducting his inquiries along this line as tactfully as possible, ever bearing in mind that the average person suffering from any such defect may be wholly unaware of such defect himself and, even if he is aware of such condition, is reluctant or unwilling to admit it. 

The facts must, however, be ascertained for the value of the witness's testimony depends largely upon his ability to observe. The witness should also be questioned to determine his accuracy of observation independent of the manner in which it may have been affected by physical defect. Witnesses vary as much in accuracy of observation as they do in physical appearance or any other characteristics, and are quite likely to overcome the omissions due to defects of observation by inserting in their testimony those things which they believe must have occurred or must have existed. Careful inquiry should also be made to ascer tain whether the witness is speaking of his own knowledge or whether he is expressing an opinion or is basing his statements on hearsay. Just as we have people who confess to crimes which they have never committed, usually crimes which have received much newspaper publicity, we have witnesses, usually psychopaths, who never had the experience they purport to relate. 

Specific inquiry for accurate information should be directed to the witnesses as to time, whether it was before or after sunset, whether there was moonlight, climatic conditions, the hour, the accuracy of the timepiece by which the time was judged, colors and shades of colors, quantities, amounts, speeds, distances. The accuracy of a witness' statements as to these matters should be checked by comparisons as well as inquiry. 

Thus, if a witness says that two minutes elapsed, test him for his judgment as to the length of two minutes by the watch; if he describes a distance as being seventy-five feet have him point out some object a similar distance away and then measure it. When the question of a specific date is involved the investigator should ascertain how the witness fixes the date, whether other incidents occurred on the same day in question and by a check of the date of such other incidents the date may be verified or fixed; inquiry as to the day of the week of the date in question is another manner of checking. It may seem a great deal of trouble to check up all these matters but the investigator must bear in mind that in no other way can accuracy be assured and that, if he does not check his information, the chances are that the defendant's attorney will do so with telling effect at the trial. Some years ago a bunco man was tried in Los Angeles for defrauding a banker of that city out of a large sum of money. The defense was an alibi, the witnesses placing the defendant in San Diego on the date of the theft. 

Unfortunately for the defendant the witnesses when questioned on cross-examination as to other incidents on the same day described various activities, such as going to church, which fixed the day they were referring to as Sunday; as the crime was committed on 

Saturday and more than ample time was available for the defendant to have been in San Diego on the following day the defense was completely overcome. Such a situation could not have arisen had the defendant's counsel or investigator carefully checked the testimony of these alleged alibi witnesses. Witnesses should also be checked with one another and where a variance in their stories exists it must be determined which witnesses are mistaken. The mere fact that four witnesses agree but differ with a fifth witness does not by any means compel the conclusion that the four are right; the fifth witness may be the only one who is reliable. Inquiry should also be made to ascertain which witnesses have been talking the matter over, for there is always a likelihood that in such a process, even among honest witnesses, such discussion may have the effect of eliminating differences in their recollections and, in adopting another version, the chances are that the adopted version may be wrong. 

Where witnesses testify to physical facts and occurrences they may often be checked by experiment. If a witness claims to have heard a shot at a distance the firing of a similar shot may be observed and the fact determined whether or not it could be heard. If the witness claims to have seen something at a distance this may be checked by experiment as may also the statement of a witness as to the length of time required to go from one place to another. In conducting experiments, however, and as elsewhere more fully discussed herein, the conditions under which the experiments are performed should be as nearly identical with those existing at the time of the occurrence to be checked as possible. 

Nervousness, pain, anger, excitement, fear, or other emotion may cause a witness, particularly an eye witness, to form such an inaccurate impression of a transaction as to render his testimony worse than useless. On the other hand, it is not at all unusual for a person confronted with a sudden peril, such as being held up by a highwayman, to become a more acute observer than he would be under normal conditions. The intelligence of the witness must also be taken into consideration and his familiarity or unfamiliarity with the matters regarding which he is relating his experiences. 

Ordinarily we can rely more fully upon a woman's description of a woman's dress or the precise color of clothing or other object, but when she comes to a description of a firearm the chances are that she will give an inaccurate description. Descriptions by witnesses are often the products, at least in part, of their impressions and beliefs rather than of actual observation. The Dishonest Witness 

Much of what has been said about the honest witness and the checking of his story and the urging of him to give all of the minute details applies to the dishonest witness; in fact it may be that by such a course only may we be able to disclose the dishonest witness. Dishonest witnesses, witnesses who knowingly are falsifying, range all the way from the witness who has made up his story out of whole cloth to the most dangerous witness of all, the one whose testimony is ninety-nine per cent true with the one per cent of perjury which changes the version of the occurrence and which still is so difficult to detect. Perhaps the most common incident of false testimony is that in which the defense is that of an alibi. If such testimony first becomes known at the trial it is difficult to meet and overcome but in many cases such a surprise could have been obviated by a more thorough inquiry before the trial. 

Alibi testimony may be wholly false or the incidents related by the witness may have occurred on a day other than the day of the crime or the witness may relate, as having occurred on the day of the crime, exactly what did occur but adding thereto the statement that the accused was present. The Thor- ough questioning in detail and a careful checking of the story and securing from the witness a later statement of the same transaction, and again asking for details will often disclose discrepancies and a basis for an attack upon the testimony of the witness when given at the trial.

Even the complaining witness must be checked up. 

The layman would easily be taken in by a person making a complaint but the police officer in time learns that the making of a complaint may be but a smoke screen. Dr. Young, who killed his wife and then concealed the body under a pouring of concrete at the bottom of a cistern, personally went to the central police station and insisted that members of the wife's family must have caused the disappearance of his wife and that the police do something about it. A woman losing money at bridge and pawning her jewelry to pay her gambling debts rather than disclose the facts to friend husband may make complaint to the police of having been robbed, or of the jewels having been stolen in a burglary. The instance has been known of the woman going so far to make her story stand up as to bind herself to a chair and place a gag in her own mouth. 

An embezzler may accuse an innocent coworker and a bank messenger may himself have stolen the money and have gone even to the extent of inflicting serious wounds upon himself in order to lend truth to his story of being attacked by bandits. A girl charging rape may be telling the story out of a spirit of revenge or jealousy or because of some other grievance. In the case of girls taken to juvenile halls and there found to have had a sexual experience it is not uncommon for them to conceal the fact that they have had sexual relations with a certain boy of whom they are very fond and to make the charge against some other boy and where the girl is pregnant it is rather remarkable how often the pregnancy is charged to some man whom she wants to marry and who, oftentimes, is wholly innocent. Also, in the case of children as complainants, it sometimes happens that they have been coached and induced to accuse a person of crime by some person who has a motive or grievance against the accused. 

In cases of false stories we can expect to find an automatic or phonographic type of recital by the witness, the story is told the same, with the same mention of details whenever it is called for; if the witness is interrupted he will resume at the place he left off and his weak point is the inability of supplying details which he should know if the transaction actually did occur and the invention of details to fill in and which are overlooked or changed when the story is repeated. 

The more clever the witness the less are these earmarks visible and, on the other hand, many of them may be present in an honest truthful witness who has had to repeat his story on a number of different occasions until, by reason of oft repetition, the telling has become mechanical. In the latter instance, however, a request for details not mentioned will bring the same answer whenever called for, not as to exact phraseology but as to substance. A dishonest witness will often, not trusting to his ability to deceive the inquisitor, answer a request for further detail by stating that he has forgotten and does not remember. This deception can frequently be detected and exposed by later on asking the same question in another form, the questioner adopting the attitude that the witness has previously given the information. The statements of witnesses should be taken down in writing whenever possible and signed by the witnesses. A written statement provides a record of what the witness stated at the time when the incidents covered were fresh in his mind and may be used by him at any future time to refresh his memory. If in the course of the questioning it becomes necessary to refer to certain objects which are expected to be exhibits in evidence in the case, such objects should, if possible, be exhibited to the witness and identified by him and a particular description of the exhibit read into the statement. Thus, if the exhibit is a revolver, its color, calibre, length of barrel, make and number should find their place in the statement; if it be a check or letter its date, character and essential identifying characteristics should be recorded. It is not at all unusual for a witness to hesitate while on the witness stand to identify an exhibit which he has not seen for some weeks or months but, if the exhibit was distinctly identified by him at the time of making his statement and an accurate description inserted therein, he has a means of refreshing his memory and making certain of the identity of the exhibit, especially if it is mentioned to him that he will no doubt be required to identify the exhibit at the trial and it be suggested that he carefully observe it so as to be able to make such identification.

Where a witness, questioned on the scene of the crime, points out just where a person stood it is a good idea to have him stand in that position and note the position of his feet on the floor or ground as a record. 

In going over the story of witnesses the examiner must be careful to guard against inaccuracies arising from illusions. A pistol pointed at the victim of a robbery is likely to appear much larger than it really is, and the reflection of light upon the barrel of a blued revolver may cause it to look to the victim like a nickel plated gun. People in light clothing, especially in conditions of comparative darkness appear taller than they usually are and the converse is likely to be true if the person wears dark clothing. The hair of a person wearing a hat standing under an overhead light may, by reason of the heavy cast shadow appear much darker than it really is, the shadow being mistaken for the color of the hair. A person wearing a tan cap may quite easily be mistaken by an inexperienced observer under conditions of poor visibility to be a person without any head covering and having blond hair. Given a little imagination a witness to an affray is likely to mistake a pipe with a nickeled band, held by one of the parties, for a knife; even the flash of a finger ring in the light may be so mistaken. The direction from which sounds came, their resemblance to certain familiar sounds, their comparative loudness, and the time intervals between successive sounds, such as pistol shots, frequently produce in the mind of a witness an impression not justified by the facts. These illustrations, taken from actual cases, should suggest to the investigator the care which must be used in taking the statements of witnesses and the line of questioning to be followed to secure as accurate information as possible. A questioning of the witness as to all the details and circumstances may reveal the possibilities of erroneous impressions of the witness and further questioning may help to clear up the story of the witness and bring forth the true facts. Such a course may, however, bring the witness to a realization that his impressions were incorrect, and he may be unable thereafter to state accurately what he observed and will admit that he can not give the desired information. 

While this is a loss of evidence as far as this particular witness is concerned it is far better that this happen and the true state of the witness's knowledge be discovered when his statement is taken than to have the situation disclosed at the trial under cross-examination of the attorney of the adverse party. 

A phenomenon not very well known may exist in the case of a witness who has been rendered unconscious by physical violence. Not only will the witness be unable to relate anything which occurred from the time of his injury to his recovery of consciousness but the incidents for a period of time preceding his injury may be absolutely wiped from his memory. This additional period of forgotten events may be as long as half an hour or may cover only a few minutes. A similar condition may exist where the witness has suffered a psychic instead of a physical shock. Great fright, horror or similar emotion' may produce a state of unconsciousness during which the individual may, though apparently normal, be acting automatically under the direction of his subjective or subconscious mind, the period being an entire blank so far as memory of what occurred is concerned when he recovers consciousness. 

Especially in the last class of cases is the uninformed officer likely to assume that the witness is concealing facts and feigning a lack of memory when, in fact, the witness truly can not remember. 

Mere improbability, especially if not glaring, does not necessarily justify the conclusion that a witness is either falsifying or that he is mistaken for the improbable may have happened. The witness should be carefully and considerately questioned and his story taken down as he gives it, and an effort made thereafter to verify or disprove it by investigation, experiment or the statements of other witnesses.

Regardless of who the witness may be, his bias or interest in the case or unwillingness to make a statement, it is a good general rule with only the rarest exception that courtesy, kindliness and consideration will produce more cooperation from a witness than harshness, discourtesy or other offensive conduct. If the questioner disbelieves the witness it is better to proceed without permitting the witness to observe that his statements are being doubted. If the witness is falsifying and believes that his deception is successful he is encouraged by his apparent success to complete his prepared story, supplementing it with the details called for by his kindly and apparently credulous inquisitor whereas, if the suggestion is made that he is not telling the truth or that he is withholding something, he is immediately placed on his guard and his statements thereafter will be few. Witnesses may be reluctant to tell what they know from a desire to protect themselves from being implicated and being charged with an offense or from a desire to protect the accused or some other person or merely from a desire to avoid going to court to give testimony. If possible, the investigator should ascertain the reason for the witness's reluctance to give information. Here again kindness will produce greater results than a threatening attitude. On the other hand we frequently find a witness who is fairly at the bursting point in his desire to give information. 

This may be due to a sincere desire to help the officer but it may be actuated by a desire to help himself or someone else, to carry out a private revenge, to mislead the officer or merely to occupy the limelight and read his name in the paper. The safe rule in such a case is to let the witness make all of the statements he desires, secure his signature thereon and investigate his possible motives and ascertain whether he is reliable. 

The best course in getting the statement of a witness is to ask him to tell his story in his own way and to permit him to finish his story as he wishes to tell it without interruption and then to ask such questions as have occurred to the examiner as necessary to make the statement more complete and understandable. It is extremely dangerous to ask leading questions or any questions which may convey to the witness any information which he may not already possess; such a course will assist the falsifying witness and may mislead the honest witness or at least confuse him. There are witnesses of such defective mentality that they are likely to accept the statements of fact conveyed by the questions put to them and add them to their story as if they were matters of which they had always had the knowledge. It is also advisable not to convey to the witness any particular piece of information desired until a general line of questions has failed to produce the desired information. Some witnesses are so easily influenced that, in their desire to be obliging and helpful, they may draw upon their imaginations or beliefs to supply the required information. 

Every statement taken should recite the names of all the persons present, the shorthand reporter who took the statement and should contain the name, address and telephone address, if any, of the witness, and his signature to the statement should be witnessed by several persons who have seen him sign the same. 

Statements of the Accused 

Most of what has been said on the subject of the statements of witnesses applies equally well to statements by a person accused of crime. The questioning of the accused should be conducted calmly and thoroughly with a desire to get the accused's version of the facts. The officer who loses his head or gets angry loses his keenness and judgment and, if the prisoner confesses, the confession may be excluded from evidence by reason of the officer's statements or conduct. Guilty persons will either confess, refuse to talk, deny everything or tell a story partly true and partly false. It may be stated that, generally, a person charged with crime wants to talk and he refrains from doing so only from fear that he will injure his cause by speaking. Since this fear does not exist in the case of the prisoner who is satisfied that the officers have already ample evidence to secure his conviction the importance of thorough investigation becomes at once apparent; it not only lessens the need of a statement from the accused but enables the officer to show him that there is no occasion for his refusing to talk about matters of which the officer has ample proof. An evident anxiety and overanxiousness to secure a statement is practically a notice to the prisoner that the police have not sufficient evidence against him and he very naturally will decline to give much information. 

Whether the prisoner tells the truth in whole or in part or is entirely false in his statements, everything stated by him should be carefully noted and, if possible, taken down by a shorthand reporter. The common practice of having the shorthand reporter take down only what the investigating officer tells him to record not only leaves unrecorded many statements which will later prove of value but detracts strongly from the weight of the statement in the eyes of the jury. The jury naturally wonders why a certain part of a statement was not taken down, why it was that, as a result of an hour's conversation, the statement resulting occupies only a page and a half of typewriting and, in most cases, the jury is not inclined to believe the officer's explanation for the omission of the balance of the conversation and there is always a feeling that the officer has recorded only the damaging portions of the statement, a conclusion that is generally justified. Inconsistencies and falsehoods are best shown where the entire statement has been taken down and, if the officer has heeded the suggestion so often repeated in these pages, to secure all the details possible, the statement will open avenues of investigation of extreme value. A confession or admission is much more easily explained or minimized by the testimony of a defendant than a series of falsehoods clearly shown to be false. The old saying about giving a calf enough rope and he will hang himself might well be paraphrased into the statement that, if one will only let the prisoner talk enough, his statements will either prove his guilt or innocence or furnish the basis for the

solution of the case. In most instances it will be found to be of benefit to take several statements, not only to thereby develop inconsistencies but to check the truthfulness of the story and it will usually be found that the subsequent statement or statements will disclose matters either concealed or overlooked on the former occasion. In their desire for brief statements it is the practice of many officers to talk with the prisoner a while on a particular line of inquiry and then crystallize the subject by a few questions and answers which are usually so worded that it is obvious to almost anyone that the answers are those prepared by the officer. 

This detracts from the weight of the statement as evidence and tends to support the defendant's testimony should he deny on the witness stand that he made such answers. If a defendant has signed his statement, especially where each page of the statement is signed or initialed by him it is less likely that he can successfully deny having made it. If, in addition, the prisoner, after reading the statement, makes corrections thereon in his own handwriting there is even less likelihood of a denial and it is well to secure corrections or additions in the handwriting of the prisoner as this is not only proof of it being his statement but also that he has read it before he signed it. 

When a prisoner is accused by a witness or COdefendant or other person, his conduct and statements, if tending to show guilt are admissible in conjunction with the accusation but, if he denies the accusation, the entire conversation becomes inadmissible in evidence. Silence under accusation, if there is no good reason why the prisoner can not speak, is admissible as a tacit admission of the truth of the accusation. 

Confessions and Admissions

An admission is any act or statement of a person accused of crime which tends toward proving his guilt. 

If such act or statement, standing alone and by itself, without other essential fact, shows him to be guilty of the crime with which he is charged, the statement or act is a confession. While the rule has no application to admissions, it is a settled rule of law that, before a confession can be received in evidence, it must be shown that it was made freely and voluntarily. So strict is this rule that any suggestions by an officer that the accused might benefit by making a statement have been held to render the confession inadmissible. Thus if an officer were to tell a prisoner that it would be "better to tell the truth" such language has been held, as a matter of law, to imply that the accused would be better off, that he would benefit, by making a statement and a confession following such a statement by the officer would not be received in evidence, and the statement of the accused, constituting a confession, would be inadmissible even though such confession was made, not to the officer who made the suggestion that it would be better to tell the truth but to an entirely different person. If a prisoner has once been subjected to treatment or statements have been made to him which will render a confession inadmissible in evidence any statement made by him thereafter which amounts to a confession will be inadmissible even though made on an entirely different and later occasion unless it clearly appears that at the time of making his confession the prisoner did so freely and voluntarily, without hope of immunity or reward or expectation of any gain or benefit from the making of such statement. Since there is always a possibility that some prior officer may have been guilty of conduct or may have made statements which would vitiate a confession, the officer securing a statement from the prisoner should inquire whether he has made any prior statements; the manner in which he has been treated by other officers and whether they have made any promises to him or held out any hope of immunity or reward or mitigation of punishment and should secure from the prisoner a statement of his reasons for telling his story and, if such be the fact, a statement from him that his making of the statement is a purely voluntary act on his part, prompted merely by a desire to tell the facts and that he does not expect to gain any special consideration by reason of having made a statement.

The Investigator in the Court Room, on Witness Stand 

While not strictly a part of the subject of criminal investigation, the trial is the culmination of the in-, vestigator's work and, as the conscientious officer is desirous of having his case presented in the best possible manner and not prejudiced by any unconscious action on his part, the writer has decided to offer a few suggestions for the assistance of the officer while in the court room. Such

The matter of personal appearance is of far greater importance than we are ordinarily inclined to believe although we are aware of the favorable impressions formed when meeting a man who is neatly dressed and gentlemanly in his conduct. Audible or energetic gum chewing or toothpick gymnastics may amuse the jury but do not raise the performer in their estimation. 

Above all things the investigator should not make himself conspicuous in the court room or by any action. convey the impression that he is interested or overzealous in his desire to see a conviction. If he has done his work as it should have been done and has furnished the prosecutor with all of the information acquired during the investigation there will be no occasion to jump up from his chair and tell the prosecutor some bit of information he has just thought of or to suggest the asking of some question which he thinks should be asked of the witness on the stand. conduct distracts the attention of the prosecuting attorney from what he has in mind and the testimony of the witness and the chances are that he will not be able to absorb the suggestion of the officer. While it may be irritating to an officer not to have the case tried as he thinks it should be tried and questions asked which he thinks should be put to the witnesses, let him remember that the prosecuting attorney is responsible for the conduct of the trial, that he probably knows best how to perform his own duties and that, after all, each attorney has to try his cases as his best judgment and knowledge dictates. If, as may occasionally happen, it is really important to convey information to the trial counsel it should be done as unostentatiously as possible and without interfering with conduct of the case. Usually the best method of doing this is to write out the suggestion and have it conveyed to the attorney. The prosecutor always appreciates the benefit of having by his side, during the course of the trial, an investigator who is really familiar with the case who can give him information and assistance when called for and who will not inject himself into the case except when absolutely necessary. If occasion arises to move about in the court room or to leave the room this should be done without attracting any attention, if possible. If two or more officers are sitting together in the court room they should avoid whispering or talking together in such a manner as to attract attention to themselves. Nor should they by any conduct exhibit their reactions to testimony given from the stand by indication of approval, pleasure or derision or other exhibitions of their feelings. That officer creates the best impression in the court room who tends to his own business and by his acts, conduct and testimony justifies the conclusion that he is there in performance of his duty, that he has no other interest other than that the cause be justly decided and that he is reliable. Exhibits should not be exposed in the court room by the officer unless called for by counsel; the unwarranted display of an exhibit may introduce reversible error into an otherwise perfect case. 

The Officer on the Witness Stand 

When he takes the witness stand the officer should have no difficulty if he knows his case but, if he is asked for information which he does not possess or does not remember, he should frankly say so. His notebook, if made the kind of a record of his investigation that it should be, will prove an excellent memory refresher before he takes the stand and, should he while on the stand desire to use it to refresh his memory in general or as to matters of detail this will be permitted him. The witness on the stand is a salesman, he is trying to convey to the jury information which they will accept, and his success depends not only upon his knowledge but the manner in which he gives his testimony and his conduct on the stand. It is not at all unusual for the officer in his first experience as a witness to feel nervous but this feeling will very promptly disappear if he has nothing to be nervous about but, if his investigation has been conducted in a haphazard and incomplete manner and he is uncertain as to the facts with reference to which he is called to testify, he need not expect a comfortable time on the witness stand. Above all things he should be natural and avoid acting or speaking in other than his usual manner. His manner should be quiet and reserved, he should sit erect and avoid slouching in his chair. Since his testimony can reach the jury only by his word of mouth he should talk distinctly, just loud enough that he may be distinctly heard by all of the jurors and 49

If assailed by a vigorous or vicious cross-examiner, the witness should refrain from fighting back or attempting to match wits with his questioner but should in a quiet, definite and dignified manner answer the questions just as fairly and frankly as if they had been asked by a friend in a friendly manner. Legal Photography--Photographs, Close Ups 

It is truly unfortunate that the subject of legal photography has been so sadly neglected. True, we often have pictures of the scene of the crime or some place or building involved in the case on trial and now and then a post mortem photograph of the deceased in a homicide case, but the instances are so rare as to be almost curiosities wherein the photographic proof is up to the standard of which legal photography is capable. When it becomes necessary to familiarize the jury with the appearance of an object which cannot be brought into court, the scene of the crime, etc., a few well taken photographs can produce more definite and actual results than an hour of description from the lips of a master of the English language. Furthermore a photograph, once in evidence, can at any time be referred to, used during the argument and taken by the jury to the jury room while the oral testimony is likely to be forgotten or faultily recalled.

The idea that the ordinary knowledge of how to take a photograph and the use of any old available box that answers to the name of a camera is sufficient to take a photograph for use in court belongs to the age of the muzzle loader. Even the technical knowledge of the everyday professional photographer is but a part of the equipment of the legal photographer. While the original draft of this chapter was in course of preparation the defense in a murder case being tried before the writer had six photographs taken by a commercial photographer and under the direction of defense counsel. All six were offered in evidence and all but one were excluded from the evidence in the case because they violated the rules of law relating to posed photographs.

The technical operative side of legal photography requires the knowledge of the expert commercial photographer, a knowledge of the abilities, values and uses of lenses of various focal lengths, orthochromatic and panchromatic plates and films, filters, the use of a camera equipped with rising and falling and sliding front, swing back and the uses of various types of plates, films and paper and the personal ability to get results. We have been satisfied in our court trials with any old kind of a picture and, even when we have appreciated that a particular photograph was not entirely satisfactory, we have generally assumed that that was the best that could be done when as a matter of fact it is an example of the most ordinary kind of photography. We have only to recall the many photographs we have seen of Niagara Falls and the other much photographed scenic beauties of our country and the photographs taken of our friends to realize that there is a great deal of difference in the manner in which a picture is taken. The trained photographer knows that it is easily possible by a change of the height and "shooting angle" of the camera and the use of different lenses, plates or films and color filters to produce half a dozen or more photographs of the same subject which are radically different from each other.

51 1 The garden variety of "press the button" artist will photograph a bloody fingerprint on dark wood and get nothing; the skilled operator will produce a perfect picture of the fingerprint. Among our own and our friends' snapshots we see repeated examples of the distortion of which the ordinary camera is capable. 

Such distortion is common where the camera is close to the object and the lens used is one of comparatively short focal length. Except when forced to use a wide angle lens by reason of the lack of room its use should be studiously avoided. Even the lens of average focal length has a tendency to give false impressions as to distances and sizes, and, excellent as is the ordinary hand camera for the purpose for which was intended, it is wholly unfitted for the requirements of legal photography. 

The practice of sending a professional photographer out with directions to "shoot" this, that or the other thing is bad. You will get pictures but their evidentiary value is a matter of accident. An investigator familiar with the case and appreciative of what the photograph is to demonstrate, just what shall be photographed and from what point of view should accompany the photographer. If the scene of the crime is to be photographed it is worth while to have one or more of the eyewitnesses accompany the investigator and photographer; their suggestions and comments while at the scene are quite likely to be of real value. The photographer should be informed of the nature of the case and the facts sought to be proved or disproved by the photographic evidence for his technical knowledge will often suggest photographs of a demonstrative character which would not occur to the investigator unless he also has a practical knowledge of the possibilities of photography. If any doubt exists as to the number and variety of pictures to be taken, the only safe course is to take all the photographs that suggest themselves as of any possible value. No one can tell with certainty just how valuable a particular photograph will be until the time of the trial and the time to secure photographs is at the earliest possible moment after the investigation has started. The actual expense involved, especially where a photographer is attached as a member of the police department, is so small that the difference between the cost of taking one photograph and of taking a dozen is more than compensated by the advantage of the additional pictures. Compared to the cost of a trial, the result of which depends on the jury's understanding of the facts, the cost of photographs becomes negligible. And, even though there may be no occasion for using every photograph as evidence, every picture taken is a wonderful means of refreshing the memory as to those details appearing in the photograph and which one is so likely to forget and at times even to overlook when viewing the scene. Since detail is important the photographer should not only use every means for securing a sharp negative, such as a good anastigmat lens, stopping down and careful focusing, to produce detail, but the finished print should be made upon glossy paper. Prints of large size, incidentally, can be kept from tearing by mounting them on muslin, such mounting being not only less bulky than mounting the prints on cards but the danger of breakage where prints are mounted on cards is eliminated. Notes should be made of every photograph setting forth a description of what is included in the picture, the name of the case, the day, hour and light conditions, plate, film, lens and camera used, length of exposure and stop used, and a note of the particular object of taking the picture, also the location of the camera, compass directions and particular reference to the important objects included. 

Any one of these facts may be of great importance at the trial especially should the photograph be in any manner questioned or attacked. The practice of placing any such data upon the back of the photograph intended for use in court is objectionable as it not only constitutes inadmissible hearsay evidence but the data naturally furnishes even the unskilled and uninformed cross-examiner with material for questions which, even though not intelligent, may cause trouble. There is, however, no objection to placing an identifying number on each picture and such practice is recommended. It is suggested that the number be placed directly on the negative, preferably by scratching or marking it on the extreme upper right hand corner, provided, of course that such number does not cover up or destroy a material part of the picture. 

But photography is not limited merely to the production of pictures of scenes, buildings and objects figuring in the evidence. Occasion frequently arises to prove, as a definite physical fact, whether or not a witness who stood at a particular spot on a specified occasion was able to see another person at another point. This fact may be of great importance, sufficient to demand corroboration of the statement of the witness. Ordinarily this corroboration is furnished by the making of an experiment as herein before discussed. 

Still stronger would be such proof if backed up by a photograph, particularly if taken by an unbiased witness. The ordinary routine photography is naturally handled by the department photographer but, when a picture is taken to demonstrate a particular fact, or an experiment is conducted, it is advisable to have such services performed by some person not in any manner connected with the department. To take such a picture a person of the same general height and build, and similarly clothed, should be placed where the witness claims to have seen the person in question and the person so placed should be caused to assume the same position as to trunk, arms, legs, hands and head as that occupied by such person at the time the witness claims to have seen him. The camera should then be placed in such a manner that the lens will be at the same height and in the same position as were the eyes of the witness at the time he made the observation. By all

By this placing of the camera so that the lens shall be in the position occupied by the eyes of the witness, the eye of the camera sees exactly what the eyes of the witness, in the same position would see. 

In a recent murder case the question arose as to whether a police officer, riding by a cafe at night, could see the drawer of the cash register in the cafe. This could have been demonstrated by placing the camera in the car in such a manner that the lens would be in the same position as the eyes of the officer at the time he made the observation. In this case a photograph was taken but the photographer stood in the street, the picture was taken in the daytime and not at night and, not knowing how to take such a picture, the photograph when finished showed a perfect reflection of the street on the windows of the cafe and not a thing inside the cafe was visible in the photograph. The photographer made the mistake not only of failing to place the camera properly, of taking the picture in the daytime. when the incident to be proved took place in the nighttime but evidently had no knowledge of the technique of taking a picture in such a manner as to avoid reflection and to show the inside of the cafe. But we cannot place all the blame upon the photographer in this case for he was accompanied by the investigating officer in charge of the case and one of the district attorneys to whom the trial of the case had been assigned. The incident is cited, not from any desire to criticise but as an illustration of the importance of a knowledge of legal photography.

In taking a photograph from the street looking into a store with the object of portraying the interior as a part of the view it is absolutely essential that such interior be well lighted, preferably from the side and with no lights showing in the picture unless there is a reason for their being shown. A convenient means of lighting is by the ordinary photographer's flash-light. 

Reflections on the glass can be avoided by arranging a black or non-actinic curtain just in front of the camera with a hole for the lens to look through or by placing such a curtain between the glass and the objects whose reflections appear. The use of a polaroid filter is of great value in killing reflections. This procedure means effort but effort brings results. 

When fatal or other injuries to the person are involved their appearance should in every case be preserved by photography and, since color rendering with the ordinary plate or film is not correct, this work is preferably done by the use of color sensitive plates or films and the use of ray filters. We are generally so accustomed to the ordinary photographs that we accept as natural a picture in which red looks black and yellow looks like dark grey in spite of its visual brightness and in which a light blue appears as white. That sort of thing may do for the casual snapshot but, when we take pictures for use in court, we should insist in the utmost fidelity and accuracy of portrayal. Unless there is no other person available with the necessary knowledge none of the photographic work in connection with a criminal case should ever be intrusted to any person who has not a sound technical knowledge of the subject of photography and its possibilities. 

With the continued perfecting of photography in natural colors the use of this medium is worthy of serious consideration especially where color of the subject photographed is significant. Consider the effect of a photograph in natural colors depicting the appearance of the victim of a brutal beating. 

In photographing footprints, tire tracks, bullet scars on woodwork, and other objects taken "close up" it is always advisable to include in the picture, close to the principal object, another object to show the size of the object photographed and the best object for this purpose is a rule or measuring tape. The rule or tape used should itself be marked so that it may be positively identified and put in evidence should occasion ever require. It is suggested that such mark be placed on the tape or rule before the picture is taken, that it be a permanent mark and of such a character that the mark itself will show in the photograph.

In the photography of objects which can not be moved special attention must be given to the subject of lighting. The lighting on the imprint of an automobile tire left in the dust may be so "flat" that the photograph may fail to accomplish its purpose. The use of reflectors and shades, flood or flash lights, even waiting until the light has changed, may be necessary to the production of the best photograph. Strong sunlight falling upon an object tends to create great contrast between the lighted portions of an object and the shadows and usually results in a poor picture but there are occasions when this very contrast is valuable. In a case where the object to be photographed should be shielded from the direct rays of the sun it is seldom advisable to interpose an opaque shade between it and the sun as this creates generally too flat a lighting; a better procedure is to partially shade the object by using a shade of tissue paper or thin white cloth. Sometimes a "tent" of white cloth or tissue paper completely surrounding the object except where the lens "looks in" will be the only means of satisfactorily photographing a silver or other highly polished article.

Photographs improperly taken, whether as the result of ignorance or deliberate intent may present so distorted a view of the object or scene photographed as to amount to misleading falsehood. Such a condition is often evident from the photograph itself but often a knowledge of the true appearance of the subject is the only means of detecting such a deception. 

As the defense is likely to take photographs to support their theory of the case it is well worth while to have properly taken photographs illustrating the case as a means of checking the accuracy of the photographs for the defense. In any event all photographs offered

To assist the jury the testimony of a photographic expert is necessary, a man so versed in the subject that he can explain the subjects of distortion, focal plane, focal lengths of lenses, and the results of the use of the various lenses, particularly in a manner which the jury can understand. Most everyone knows the old trick of the fisherman photographed with his fish held closer to the lens than he himself is standing and the resulting exaggerated size of the fish in the picture. The same principle is frequently evidenced in photographs offered in evidence. Then again the position of the camera may cause the resulting picture to be misleading. One photograph of a railroad track may make it appear barely above the level of the ground while another picture of the same track makes it appear nearly half a foot higher than the ground. Even the light falling upon the object may render a picture misleading as may also the angle at which the camera is tilted. The whys and wherefores and the proof of the existence of such distortion are simple to the real photographic expert. But-and this is important-the expert witness, no matter what his specialty may be, must never lose sight of the all important fact that his testimony is worthless unless made so clear and given in such simple language that every single member of the jury fully understands it. The value of checking a defense photograph is well illustrated by a case in which there appeared, alongside of the tire track photographed, what appeared to be an ordinary lead pencil. 

The photograph tended to show that the tire mark was larger than contended for by the prosecution. Whether by accident or design the pencil included in the picture was actually thinner than the ordinary pencil. Fortunately the prosecution had photographed the same tire track with a foot rule by its side and when this photograph was shown to defense counsel and the suggestion made that something was wrong with their picture the deceptive photograph was returned to counsel's brief case and never offered in evidence. 

It is not as well known as it should be that, if a photograph is taken showing people and objects posed in a position which will be declared by a witness to the original transaction as the position of such persons and objects, or similar persons and objects, at the time when he saw them, such photograph is admissible in evidence for the purpose of illustration. The rules of evidence, as we know, permit a witness to draw a diagram or picture of what he has seen or to identify and verify as correct a diagram or drawing made by someone else. There is no difference in principle of law where the drawing or picture, instead of being made by hand is a drawing which is the product of actinic and chemical powers as employed in photography. 

To produce a proper posed photograph, the eye-witness or witnesses should be taken to the scene and there asked to place the objects and persons in the positions they occupied at the time of the transaction in question. 

If the original persons and objects are available so much the better; if not, the persons and objects selected to be posed should be as like the originals as possible. 

As an illustration, suppose we had a case of murder in which self defense is an issue. The position of the deceased and defendant just before and at the time of the infliction of the fatal injury are most important.

Persons may, in accordance with the rule stated, be posed as seen by the eyewitnesses during the various steps of the affray, care being taken to accurately place the feet, legs, arms, hands, trunk and head, weapons or other objects held in the hands and surrounding persons and objects. It is advisable to take a number of these posed photographs, reposing the persons and objects, and, where there is a difference of recollection between the eyewitnesses, photographs should be taken according to the positions as recalled by each witness. Such photographs, identified by the eyewitnesses and declared by them as correctly portraying the positions of the parties and objects as they saw them are most powerful proof of the facts and most easily appreciated and understood by a jury for no amount of oral testimony will equal such a photograph in enabling the jurors to visualize the scene.

Similar in principle to posed photographs are photographs including objects as markers or pointers to illustrate facts not evident from the mere photograph itself. Thus, even though the body has been removed from the place where it was found, an outline of the body in chalk or other visible marking material may be included in a photograph of the scene provided, of course, that the outline correctly shows where the body lay. Skid marks and tire marks on the pavement are frequently too indistinct to be visible in a photograph of the scene of an accident. After taking a photograph of the scene just as it was found it is a good idea to paint in white the tracks and tire marks, making the painting conform accurately as to length and width of the marks as found, and then take another picture. 

Where bullet holes, finger prints or other marks appear they should be photographed by a close up view and also included in a general view, and, to make their location more visible their positions may be indicated by circles drawn around or arrows pointing at them. 

The direction of bullet holes in and through buildings, doors, walls and furniture is usually very important. 

This may be shown on a photograph in several ways. 

One method is to insert a white maple dowel rod about a yard long, or other similar object, of a diameter

slightly less than that of the hole, into and through the bullet hole allowing the ends to project. Taken from the proper angle and position the picture will clearly show the course of the bullet. Where a bullet has passed through two or more objects such as, for example, a door and then through the opposite wall after passing through the back of a chair, dowel rods may be inserted through each of the three holes in the door, chair and wall and a white string stretched between the ends of the dowel rods, thus forming a visible straight line. 

When photographed the rods and string will vividly show the course of the bullet. If, perchance the chair has been moved but it can be shown that it was punctured by the bullet, its position at the time of the shot can be fixed by inserting the dowel rods and attaching the strings as before and moving the chair about until, with the string taut, it and the dowels form a perfectly straight line. If, instead of the chair, the bullet passed through the deceased, his position at the time of the shot may be shown by the use of a dummy of the same size and shape as the deceased and through which a hole has been made in exactly the same position and taking the same course as that in the body of the deceased. It is not advisable to omit the dowel rods in the type of pictures last referred to because, if string only is used it is entirely too easy to unwittingly cause it to deviate from a straight line as it passes through an object. The course of a deflected bullet may similarly be illustrated by stretching a string from the point of origin of the bullet, then tacking the string to the place where the bullet struck next and was deflected and so on as far as the bullet's course can be traced. The string used for the purposes indicated should be considerably thicker than ordinary store string and should be about an eighth of an inch in diameter so as to be clearly visible.

When taking photographs for the purpose of illustrating the case every effort should be made to take the pictures under the same conditions as those which obtained at the time of the transaction they are to illustrate. 

Thus, if it be desired to demonstrate that a danger sign was visible to the driver of an automobile at night, the picture should be taken at night. It may require a long exposure to get picture where the sole source of light is that coming from the headlights of an automobile a hundred feet away from such a sign but the picture when taken will be a true portrayal and demonstration of the visibility of the sign. A similar photograph, taken, however, in the daytime might create an entirely false impression in the minds of the jury and would always leave an opening for the argument that, while such a sign would show in the daytime, it would not be visible at night.

The use of flashlights is of great value in legal photography and the recently invented glass flash bulbs are particularly valuable because of the absence of smoke, freedom from the danger attending the ordinary flash powder. Investigations at night frequently call for photographs on the spot and the use of flashlights not only permits the taking of pictures before any object is moved but provides a more practical lighting of the subject. Even in the case of photographs taken by daylight the supplementary use of a flashlight will often light up a corner or dark spot which would otherwise fail to register. 

Space precludes more than a suggestion of the real possibilities of scientific well reasoned legal photography but its advantages are so obvious that there is little excuse for the miserably poor attempts at legal photography usually encountered in our courts. 

CHAPTER XIV. 

Criminal Evidence--Expert Witness, Impeachment 

By a combination of evolution and experience the law has adopted rules of evidence to exclude from the consideration of the court and jury facts not proven in the manner which can safely be relied upon. knowledge of the principle and basic rules of the law of evidence is essential to the investigator. Otherwise he may find, when he arrives in court with his case and it is too late to seek other evidence, that the evidence he has gathered cannot, in part, be introduced. 

In the first place the law will not permit any evidence to be introduced upon a trial unless it tends to shed light upon the guilt or innocense of the defendant of the very crime with which he is charged and for which he is being tried. If a man is being tried for murder the fact that he is a reprobate, that he has committed other crimes having no relation to the case on trial or that he had a bad reputation for peace and quiet is not admissible as a part of the case of the people. In such a case only the evidence which logically sheds light upon the question of whether he is guilty of the murder can be received. Then again the law will not permit the introduction of evidence which, though it relates to

CRIMINAL EVIDENCE 

55 the matter on trial, can have no effect upon the ultimate decision or verdict. While there is no serious objection to such immaterial matters they are excluded because of no value, merely occupying time without result, and as likely to divert the attention of a jury from the real issues. Hearsay

Unless it comes within one of the exceptions to the rule, hearsay evidence is inadmissible. Practically defined, hearsay is the testimony of one person as to what some other person has stated in relating a past transaction or what the witness himself has related in the past to some other person. The law in effect says that we must bring the person who knows a fact into court and have him state his evidence under oath. 

Thus the law will not permit the defense to put a witness upon the stand and have him testify that some person other than the defendant confessed to the crime in question. Testimony offered in behalf of either party as to the description of the perpetrator of the crime given by the victim or other person prior to the trial; that an eyewitness who has since died or is too ill to attend court made certain statements of what he saw; that an eyewitness, other than the witness on the stand, identified the defendant at the police station; that a person other than the defendant endeavored to bribe a witness are all examples of inadmissible hearsay.

Among the important exceptions to the hearsay rule and which are admissible are dying declarations and declarations of the defendant tending to prove guilt and which are classed as admissions and confessions. 

It should be noted that the rule of hearsay and its exceptions applies with equal force to actions which are the equivalent of speech. Thus, if the law would, under given circumstances, exclude testimony that a person said "Yes," the same rule would exclude evidence that the person gave an affirmative nod of the head. 

Dying Declarations 

Limited to prosecutions for homicide the law permits either the prosecution or the defense to prove the declarations of the person, whose killing is charged, as to the cause of his fatal injuries and the circumstances of the transaction which led up to those injuries provided that such statement was made under a sense of impending death. Such declarations must be limited to the matters directly concerned with the infliction of the fatal injuries and may not include a recital of a past occurrence prior to the fatal encounter nor will the law permit evidence of the mere opinion of the deceased as to how or by whom the injuries are inflicted. 

In other words a proper dying declaration should be limited to a description of the facts and occurrences immediately surrounding the event in which the fatal injury was inflicted. The prime requisite for the admission of a dying declaration is that the person making the declaration must have, at the time of the declaration, believed that he was going to die. This state of mind may be proved by the express declaration of the deceased indicating that he had no hope of recovery or by the evidence of surrounding facts, circumstances, conduct and requests tending to the same effect, but it is not necessary that the proof should show that he expects death to immediately follow. Neither is it necessary that the declarant shall die immediately after the declaration for, if made under the conditions required, the declaration is admissible even though he live for as much as a month. Further a statement made by the deceased at a time when he expected to recover will be rendered admissible if later, and while under a sense of impending death, the deceased reaffirmed his former statements. The law does not require that a dying declaration be in writing or that it be signed but it is so obvious that a declaration in writing and signed by the deceased will have greater weight with a jury that every effort should be made to obtain such a statement. If all of the conditions of the rule as to the admissibility of dying declarations are present, the fact that at some time thereafter and before his death the deceased entertained a hope of recovery has no effect upon the admissibility of such declaration. 

Admissions and Confessions

Admissions and confessions are similar in the respect that they are each declarations of the defendant tending to prove his guilt but an admission is only the acknowledgment of some fact which in connection with the other facts tends to prove guilt while a confession is a statement from which of itself no other conclusion than that of guilt of the offense charged can be drawn. Thus if a person arrested as a murder suspect were asked how the deceased met his death and he answered: "I killed him," the statement would be a confession but if he answered: "I killed him to keep him from killing me," the statement would be only an admission since, while it conceded the fact that he had caused the death, it does not admit any criminality in the killing.

The admissions of a defendant are receivable in evidence whether they were made freely and voluntarily or not but before a confession will be admitted in evidence it must be preceded by proof that it was made freely and voluntarily, without hope or expectation or promise of immunity or reward and not induced by force or fear. Ultimately and before the confession is received in evidence the court must be satisfied that the confession was made freely and voluntarily. This rule requires that the prosecution prove the free and voluntary character of the confession before the court will permit it to be received in evidence and, furthermore, the defense is given the opportunity of proving that it was not free and voluntary before the confession is received. So strict is the rule that, if there is any doubt as to the free and voluntary character of the confession, it will be excluded by the court. In a case decided by the California Supreme Court it appeared that the officer was asked whether he had not,

CRIMINAL INVESTIGATION 

prior to the statement of the accused, told the latter that it would be "better" for him to tell the truth. 

The answer of the witness was that he could not say whether he had made that statement or not. The court ruled that the confession was inadmissible as it had not been shown that the statement was not influenced by the suggestion of benefit which would be conveyed by the expression including the word "better" which may have been made. This may seem rather far fetched to the officer but it happens to be the law. It illustrates that it is not alone force, fear, threats, or direct promises of immunity or a lesser sentence that will exclude a confession. If it appears that the officer, prosecutor or other person in apparent authority induces a confession by any means suggesting that the prisoner will be in some way benefited if he talks, such inducement renders the confession inadmissible. 

If, however, the confession is free and voluntary it is admissible even though it may also appear that the prisoner was partly under the influence of liquor, suffering from a bullet wound or severe injuries incurred in resisting arrest. 

Accusatory Statements 

When the accused, under conditions which fairly afford him an opportunity to reply to a statement made in his presence and incriminating him, stands mute, makes a false statement or fails to act as it might reasonably be expected that an innocent person would act under the circumstances and in the face of the accusation, both the accusation and the conduct of the accused are admissible in evidence against him. This is true even if such conduct does not amount to an unequivocal admission of guilt. The mere accusation or the accusation followed by a denial from the prisoner are inadmissible since the rule authorizes the admission in evidence only of the incriminating conduct or language of the accused and the accusation itself is permitted to be shown merely to illustrate, complete and make intelligible the evidence as to the ensuing conduct of the accused. It is immaterial by whom the accusation was made as the admissibility of the follow. ing conduct of the defendant is not affected by the making of the accusation by an officer, a co-defendant, a witness or a mere bystander. Thus if, at the scene of a murder, some bystander pointed to another person and said "There is the man who killed him," and the accused, though within hearing, made no denial, evidence of the accusation and the lack of denial is ad. missible at the trial. If one of several bandits confesses to a robbery implicating another prisoner present in the room and that other prisoner is then asked whether he desires to add to or correct the statement and answers in the negative, that entire transaction will be received in evidence. Even though the prisoner should deny the accusation but should also, in his response, couple his denial with a false statement relevant and material to the case, that transaction is also admissible on the theory that the falsehood tended to show a consciousness of guilt. If the silence or conduct of the accused amounts in law to a confession of guilt, such implied confession is subject to the same rules as a direct confession and must be shown to have been free and voluntary. 

Consciousness of Guilt

Proof of acts, conduct and statements of a defendant following the commission of the crime is admissible where it tends to prove a consciousness of guilt. 

As illustrations of what may be proved under this rule may be mentioned:-efforts by the defendant or an associate in his presence to quiet the complainant by an offer or payment of money, an attempt to bribe the arresting officer or a possible witness; escape from jail; a letter declaring an intention to break jail; an assault upon the arresting officer or some other person who tried to assist in the arrest or to prevent the prisoner's escape; false and inconsistent statements with reference to the charge against him; evidence of an intention to fabricate a defense; an attempt to commit suicide; threats against a prosecution witness; nervousness and excitement after the commission of the crime; giving a false name at the time of arrest. While departure or flight from the locality where the crime was committed is also admissible under the theory that it tends to show a consciousness of guilt, this is only true where such proof is coupled with other proof which warrants the conclusion that the defendant knew that he was accused or suspected or had some reason to fear accusation. On the other hand the law does not permit a defendant to introduce evidence that he made no attempt to flee. 

Self Serving Declarations 

Declarations of a defendant subsequent to the crime. which constitute mere matter of recital in his own favor are termed self serving declarations and are inadmissible when offered by the defense for they are mere hearsay and incompetent. If, however, such a statement has any tendency whatever to prove the guilt of the defendant it may be introduced by the prosecution. This is sometimes advisable. For example, if it can satisfactorily be shown that the statement was false in whole or in part it tends to prove a consciousness of guilt. Again, in spite of the fact that the statement has a favorable tendency toward the cause of the accused it may include an admission of a fact valuable to th cause of the prosecution.

Acts and Declarations of Coconspirators 

In legal contemplation a criminal conspiracy is in effect a criminal partnership and, as in a legal partnership in which the acts of one partner within the scope of the partnership business are binding upon his copartners, so in a conspiracy the acts and declarations of each conspirator done in furtherance of the object of the conspiracy are binding on all of the conspirators, and may be proved against any or all conspirators on trial for the crime of conspiracy or for a crime com-

CRIMINAL EVIDENCE 

57 mitted in pursuance of such conspiracy. Acts and declarations made after the object of the conspiracy has been accomplished or after the arrest of the declarant are, however, provable only against the individual performing or making them. These rules are applicable whether the charge is that of conspiracy or some crime committed in pursuance of the conspiracy and, in the latter case, the indictment need not charge or set forth the conspiracy but, in all cases, testimony is not admissible under the theory now under discussion unless preceded or accompanied by proof of the existence and object of the conspiracy. Threats

Evidence of threats is admissible under various theories for the purpose of showing the state of mind and intent of the person making them and the effect upon the mind of the person hearing them or to whom they are communicated.

Threats by the defendant are admissible to show motive, premeditation, intent, state of mind or malice. 

It is not necessary that these threats shall have been immediately before the assault, homicide, or other crime in question. Even though made a year or more before the crime, the evidence of threats is admissible if it tends to prove or disprove any of the disputed facts in the case.

As a general rule evidence of this character will be limited to threats by defendant against the victim of the crime but, when the circumstances show a connection between the threats and the offense charged, as where the person threatened was with the victim at the time of the threat, or where the threat against others was so intermingled with the threats against the deceased that one could not be proved without proving the other, in these cases the evidence of threats against others is admissible.

Threats by the victim of an assault or homicide which were heard by or communicated to the accused may be proved by the defense in support of a plea of self defense to show that the defendant was justified in the killing by a warranted belief that he was in danger of great bodily injury. Since, however, the making of threats by the victim may tend to show his state of mind and intent and tend to shed light upon the question as to who was the aggressor in an affray the law also permits the defense to prove threats made by the victim even though they were not made known to the defendant. Evidence of the threats referred to in this paragraph becomes admissible only when it appears that there is some evidence upon which the claim of self defense can be predicated. With respect to the rule as to threats communicated to the defendant as having been made by the person who is the victim of the ensuing assault, it is wholly immaterial as whether the report made to the accused was true or false for the evidence is received only for the purpose of shedding light upon the state of mind of the accused at the time of the encounter and, consequently, the prosecution will not be permitted to introduce evidence tending to prove that in fact the victim had made no such threats. But, when evidence of his threats is offered to show the state of mind, intent, or malice of the victim the prosecution may offer proof that such threats were not made in order to disprove the existence of such state of mind, intent, or motive. 

It should be noted that threats are not always expressed in words. Threatening motions and gestures, a display of anger, going after a gun or purchasing or loaning a revolver may all be the equivalent of a threat in words and, as such, be provable under the rules just stated. 

Declarations of Deceased

Declarations of the deceased are admissible under a charge of murder resulting from the performance of an illegal abortion to prove the physical condition of the deceased and her avowed purpose of going to the defendant. Involuntary exclamations, either articulate or inarticulate, indicating present pain and suffering are admissible to prove the declarant's condition but recitals as to the person's past condition or the cause of the condition are hearsay and inadmissible unless made under conditions bringing them under one of the exceptions to the hearsay rule.

Not infrequently it is a contention of the defense that death was the result of suicide and not the act of the defendant. In such a case declarations of the deceased tending to indicate an intention of committing suicide are admissible on the part of the defense and, to rebut or eliminate the claim of suicide, the prosecution may introduce declarations of the deceased indicating that the decedent had not contemplated taking his own life.

Efforts are sometimes made by defense counsel to introduce in evidence statements of the deceased tending to support the claims of the defendant such as declarations by the deceased that he feared certain other persons, that he had enemies who he feared withstanding contentions to the contrary such declarawould kill him or exculpating the defendant. Nottions are pure hearsay and do not come within any of the exceptions to the hearsay rule.

Entire Conversation 

It is the broad general rule that when a part of a declaration or conversation has been introduced in evidence by one party, the balance of the conversation may be inquired into by the opposing party on crossexamination or introduced in evidence by a separate witness. Thus, if the prosecution were to introduce part of an incriminating statement of the accused, the defense could prove the remaining portion of that statement mitigating, explaining or eliminating the effect of the portion already in evidence. Likewise, if the defense were to introduce part of a conversation the prosecution is entitled to prove the portions of the con-

CRIMINAL INVESTIGATION 

versation remaining unproved. Difficulty sometimes arises in limiting the rule to the scope of its purpose which is to give to the jury the entire context of what was stated. If two people were to converse continuously for several hours on a number of different subjects, the introduction of what was said in that mixed conversation upon a subject relevant and material to the cause on trial will not permit the introduction in evidence of what was said on some other subject mentioned during the time the parties were talking. Neither does the rule tend to render admissible a subsequent conversation upon the same subject matter. Thus, if a defendant has made a confession which is introduced in evidence by the prosecution, the law does not authorize the defendant to place in evidence a conversation occurring subsequently between the same parties in which the defendant made a statement of his version of the facts constituting or concerning the crime charged. 

Res Gestae

Quite similar to the rule just discussed is what is known as the res gestae rule. The term res gestae means all of the acts, declarations and circumstances constituting and forming a part of one transaction. Res 

Gestae does not alone refer to the criminal transaction itself, but is properly used in referring to any transaction involved in the proof. Thus the affray between the parties resulting in the killing of one of them and the acts and declarations attending the killing are properly referred to as the res gestae, but it is also proper to use the term res gestae with reference to the incident when the accused purchased the revolver used in the killing including his acts, words and conduct and the words of the seller. Thus we have the rule that where a transaction is involved in the cause on trial, the attendant facts, circumstances and declarations forming a part of such transaction or being the spontaneous and immediate product of such transaction are admissible as a part of the res gestae although, but for such connection, such evidence is of a character which would otherwise be excluded. Thus the acts and exclamations of the wife of the defendant occurring at the time of the homicide and in the presence and hearing of the defendant may be proved; the conduct and declarations of the complainant and others in making an assault upon the defendant immediately preceding the felonious assault for which the defendant was on trial; declarations of a party made at the time of procuring a pistol which was found near his body after he had been shot by the defendant; exclamations of the deceased in a homicide case at the time he was shot, and even the declarations of the deceased as to the identity of the person who shot him following almost immediately the firing of the shot have been held admissible as part of the res gestae. 

There is little difficulty in determining whether declarations intimately connected with and forming a part of the very transaction itself are admissible, but it is not always easy to decide whether a declaration following the main event of the transaction will be received in evidence. In this connection we must remember the hearsay rule, that recitals of past occurrences can not be proved by a person who heard them unless the circumstance comes within the exception to the rule. But where there has been no opportunity for the declarant to form the intention to make a recital of what has happened and the declaration is the spontaneous product of what has gone before and the declaration is in fact a part of the transaction and illustrates it, we may say that it is still a part of the res gestae and admissible. 

Other Offenses 

It is a general rule of law that when a defendant is charged with one crime the prosecution will not be permitted to prove that he has committed another crime which is not charged and for which he is not on trial. 

In this connection we must, however, consider another general rule which permits the prosecution to prove all facts which tend to show the guilt of the defendant. 

This leads us to the resulting rule that if the proof of a fact which is competent, material and relevant has a tendency to prove that the accused has committed a crime in addition to that charged, the mere fact that it does tend to prove that other crime will not exclude the evidence which is otherwise admissible. Thus, in a prosecution for homicide, robbery or burglary, it is perfectly proper to prove that immediately preceding the crime the defendant carried a concealed pistol even though the act of carrying the weapon is a separate offense. Evidence of other offenses is frequently of the greatest value in proving intent, motive, knowledge, plan and system. Since, when the question of knowledge is involved, it relates to knowledge of the accused at the time of the offense charged, proof of other offenses of like character for the purpose of showing knowledge must be limited to offenses committed prior to the crime for which he is on trial as it is not material what knowledge he had after the commission of the offense on trial. Thus, in the crime of uttering forged. checks we may prove that, prior to passing the check mentioned in the indictment, the defendant passed other similar checks. When we utilize proof of other offenses for the purpose of showing intent, however, we are not limited to offenses committed prior to the crime charged, but may prove subsequent as well as prior offenses. Since uttering forged checks also involves the element of an intent to defraud as well as knowledge of the fact that the checks are forgeries, we find it to be the rule that in such cases we may prove other similar offenses prior to that charged under that portion of the rule permitting such evidence for the purpose of proving knowledge and intent to defraud and other similar but subsequent offenses under that part of the rule authorizing this form of evidence to prove intent. 

The following illustration taken from the decisions of our courts are examples of permissible proof of other crimes to show knowledge: proof that the defendant murdered a man on a certain date to show that he must have known that a check passed by him and bearing a

59 subsequent date and signature of the deceased was a forgery; proof that the accused had drawn other bad checks on his bank account as a means of showing that he knew that he did not have enough money in the bank to pay the check involved in the charge for which he was on trial; proof of the same false pretenses to people other than the complaining witness as tending to show knowledge of their falsity; proof of a previous robbery to show that defendant knew that the man he shot was an officer; proof of knowledge by prior experience that the means used in the case on trial would produce an abortion; that previous experiments of the accused in poisoning others had taught him the fatal dose of a particular poison; and proof that defendant had stolen the plans of a building to show that he was familiar with the layout of the premises. 

Evidence of other offenses is admissible to prove intent only in those cases where the intent as to the particular offense is uncertain or equivocal and is not admissible where the evidence leaves no doubt as to the intent of whoever may have committed the crime. 

Thus, in embezzlement, if it is clear that someone has embezzled the money of an employer and the accused denies all connection with the embezzlement charged, there is no dispute over the question of the intent to fraudulently appropriate the property, the sole question being as to who was the guilty party and in such a case proof of other embezzlements by the defendant would not be admissible on the theory that the evidence should be received to prove intent. If, however, the accused did not deny the taking of the money in question, but contends that he took it in good faith and believing that he had a right to do so, here evidence of other embezzlements would be admissible to prove that his intent was in fact fraudulent.

In California, for example, burglary is the entering of a building, or other structures named in the statute, with intent to commit larceny or any felony and the law does not require an act of breaking to make up the crime of burglary. Hence, in this state a person who enters a department store with the intention of stealing some of the merchandise is guilty of burglary even though he enters in the middle of the day along with legitimate customers and apparently a prospective customer. As intent is a matter within the mind of the shoplifter and his intent at the time of entering is not proved by the mere fact that he stole something after he entered the building (for he may have formed the intent to steal after he had entered in good faith as a customer) it is valuable to know that if it can be proved that he entered the store on a prior occasion and stole goods the facts of the previous theft may be given in evidence against him and will be sufficient to prove that in entering on the subsequent occasion he entered with intent to steal and that on the latter occasion he was guilty not only of the theft but of burglary also. the crime for which the defendant is on trial arose in whole or in part from the commission of a previous crime. Thus arson may be committed to destroy or render unidentifiable the body of a person murdered by the arsonist. Arson has also been committed to destroy the evidence of theft or other crimes or to destroy records and articles of an incriminatory nature which would tend to prove the arsonist guilty of other crimes. 

Arson has also been committed to perpetrate a fraud upon a life insurance company by placing a corpse in the building and then claiming that the charred remains found in the debris were those of the person whose life had been insured. Kidnapping is often committed for the purpose of committing a rape, robbery or other crime upon the victim. Burglary invariably is perpetrated for the purpose of committing a larceny or some felony. Since it is always permissible for the prosecution to prove the motive for the crime the fact that in proving such motive the accused is shown to have committed a crime other than the crime charged in the indictment or information does not in any degree affect the admissibility of the evidence of the former offense.

Where a criminal conspiracy has been formed it may have as its object the commission of one crime or of a series of crimes. In the latter class of cases evidence is admissible not only of the crime for which the accused are on trial, but of the other crimes committed in pursuance of the same plan and conspiracy. Even where an individual commits a series of crimes in pursuance of the same general plan or system any or all of the crimes so committed may be shown. Thus, even though one embezzlement is charged, proof is admissible that it was but one of a series of embezzlements committed with the same general plan and purpose. Where the conspiracy has as its object the commission of but one crime it is the law that all acts committed by one or any number of the conspirators in pursuance of the common object of the conspiracy are considered the acts of every one of the conspirators and, upon a trial for conspiracy or for the crime which was the object of the conspiracy, whether one or all of the conspirators are on trial, evidence of all such acts, regardless whether they constitute crimes or not, are admissible. 

When offering proof of other offenses the objection is sometimes made that the evidence of the other offense relates to a transaction too remote in time from the principal offense, that is, the offense charged. Such an objection is ordinarily not a valid objection for the remoteness in time of the other transaction goes to the weight and not to the admissibility of the evidence. 

Opinion Evidence 

Since it is the province of the jury to draw inferences and conclusions from the evidence, it is a general rule of the law of evidence that witnesses must state facts and not conclusions. The law does permit a witness to state the appearance of a person or object. Though this, in a measure, involves opinion, the law does permit testimony as to the apparent state of health, mental condition, nervousness, anger, excitement or other state which manifests itself to the ordinary observer. Thus a witness may say that on a particular occasion a person appeared to be intoxicated or that he was nervous or excited, etc. The law here recognizes a difference between the statement of an opinion in the form, "He was excited," and the phraseology, "He appeared to be excited." In at least some of the states the law, in cases where the question of intoxication is involved, permits a witness to state flat-footedly whether or not a person was intoxicated. 

In cases involving the question of insanity the ordinary witness is limited to stating whether or not the person in question "appeared" to be rational or irrational. 

Where, however, the witness is an intimate acquaintance, the law permits him to give his opinion whether the person under consideration was sane or insane even though the witness may have little or no knowledge as to what insanity really is. To be an "intimate acquaintance" within this rule it is not necessary that the witness be a relative or friend of the accused; all that is required is that the witness shall have had a sufficiently close acquaintance with the subject to make observations and comparisons of his conduct over a substantial period of time and to form a reasonably accurate impression and judgment as a layman of his mental state. 

Thus a warden of a penitentiary who had seen a prisoner for several weeks was held qualified as an intimate acquaintance to testify to his sanity. 

Sound and Speed

While it would be objectionable opinion evidence for a witness to state that he heard a sound and that it was a pistol shot, this criticism does not apply to testimony in which the witness states that he heard a sound mony in which the witness states that he heard a sound, and that it sounded like a pistol shot or like the sound of a drum, that it sounded as coming from a particular direction, that it was loud or soft or that he heard sounds which sounded like the shots of firearms, or that there were two different kinds of sounds, one like that of a small calibred pistol and the other like the report of a large calibred pistol or of a rifle or of a shotgun. 

While such testimony is not ordinarily accurate or reliable, a witness may testify to the speed of a street car, a train, an automobile, or of a person running or walking.

Expert Witnesses 

Opinion evidence may be given by expert witnesses in regard to matters falling within the sphere of their special knowledge. Thus physicians, chemists, ballisticians, handwriting experts are frequently called upon to give opinions based upon facts already in evidence. 

It is not necessary that the expert witness be a person who has made a business or profession of the subject with reference to which he may give his opinion. All that the law requires is that the witness have knowledge of a science, trade process, system or business which qualifies him to express an opinion on a matter not of common knowledge. It is, however, extremely dangerous for either party to call as an expert any but the really qualified expert. If an expert witness falls down testimony, but has a tendency to shed discredit upon on the witness stand it not only discredits his entire the entire case of the side which called him. Not only that, but if the expert is mistaken, any presentation of evidence or preparation of a case based upon the opinion of an expert or in support of a theory predicated upon such an opinion will be mere waste motion if the opinion of the expert is erroneous.

Expert evidence is of great importance in the matter of wounds. A competent witness, familiar with the subject, can determine from the character of the wound the kind of instrument which caused it, but such an opinion must always be accepted with reservations. 

Where a bullet has passed through the human anatomy it is frequently possible for the expert to give an opinion and sometimes even to demonstrate which was the entrance and which the exit wound. It should be noted, however, that, the opinion of some experts notwithstanding, it is not always possible to make such a determination. Sometimes particles of clothing, burnt or unburnt particles of powder, etc., carried into the entrance wound clearly fix its identity, but there are conditions under which this factor cannot be determined. 

Where shots are fired at close range the microscope will often disclose powder particles and actual burning of flesh or clothing but a miscroscopic examination is of little value unless made by a person familiar with the substances he is examining. 

Experiments 

The facts of a case are frequently demonstrated or disproved by evidence of experiments conducted after the commission of the crime. Thus evidence of chemical reactions, of experiments to determine the visibility of objects and places from a particular position or whether a particular sound can be heard at a particular distance from its point of origin, whether an automatic in which a particular automatic firearm throws the exudes a visible vapor when discharged, the direction discharged shells, how long a candle will burn, the distance from the muzzle of a firearm to the farthest point at which it will scorch a particular kind of fabric, are frequently introduced under the rule as to experiments.

Experiments with firearms should be conducted with the same type of weapon as that whose use is in question, preferably the identical weapon, the same type and make of ammunition, loaded with the same powder, charge and bullet should be used and if the tests are made to determine the effect upon an object at which the gun is fired the test object should be for all practical purposes identical with the object hit in the actual case. This does not mean that if, for example, an experiment is made to determine the appearance of an entrance and exit wound on a pair of leather puttee leggings while worn, the test must be conducted with the leggings worn by a human being. In such a case the requirements of the law would be satisfied if the leggings at the time of the test were filled with some substance, sawdust for example, of practically the same density and offering substantially the same resistance as human flesh. It is advisable to secure legal advice whenever an experiment is to be conducted, at least if there is any doubt as to the exact method of procedure. 

Character Evidence 

In certain instances evidence is admissible to prove the character of the parties to the crime committed, particularly in homicide and assault cases, the character of the prosecutrix in a case of forcible rape, and to impeach witnesses. Character is proved by evidence of general reputation of the individual in the community in which he is accustomed to live and work. Reputation among a small number of persons, especially of a small number of persons in a restricted group is generally inadmissible. Before a witness may testify to the general reputation of another it must be shown that he is sufficiently acquainted with the people in the community in which the person in question lives (that is, circulates and contacts with others) to render it likely that the witness has been in a position to learn what such reputation is by reason of his having contacted with a substantial number of the persons in that community who would naturally be able to furnish information as to such reputation and from whom the character in question could be learned.

The defendant in a criminal case is permitted by law to offer evidence, through character witnesses, that his general reputation, in the community or communities in which he has lived, for the traits of character involved in the offense charged, is good. If the crime charged is one involving dishonesty, the defendant may offer evidence of good character for honesty and integrity; in sexual cases the provable charater is that as to chastity and virtue; in perjury and cases involving falsehoods, such as larceny by trick and device or obtaining property by false pretenses, the character provable is as to truthfulness; in cases of violence the character provable may range all the way from that for peace and quiet to that for being a violent and turbulent and quarrelsome individual. In other words, if the facts of the case of the prosecution tend to show that the defendant's character in a particular respect is bad, the defense may put on character witnesses to show that his general reputation in this respect in the community in which he lives. is good. Character evidence as to the traits of character involved in the crime charged can only be introduced in the first instance by the defendant, but once he has thus put his character in evidence the prosecution may put character witnesses upon the stand to prove that the general reputation of the defendant in the community for the traits in question is bad. Evidence of defendant's good character for the traits involved is received upon the theory that it is less likely that a person of good character would have conducted himself in the manner claimed under the evidence for the prosecution and that such evidence of good character may in itself raise a reasonable doubt of guilt of the accused.

Proof of good reputation may not only be introduced through the medium of witnesses who have heard that reputation through discussion, but may also be furnished by the testimony of witnesses who have not heard such character discussed but who would quite likely have heard the character discussed had it been the subject of discussion, it being naturally assumed that the reputation of a person for a certain trait of character is good if the people in the community in which he lives have not discussed it. Thus, the character of a truly honest man is not necessarily the subject of discussion and the character of a man who is chaste and virtuous is not likely to be the topic of any general conversation.

In connection with this class of character evidence it should be carefully noted that, since this proof goes to the likelihood of the defendant having committed an act of the character disclosed by the evidence against him, proof of his good or bad character for the traits involved in the offense charged, is limited to his reputation prior to the commission of the offense on trial and evidence of his character at any subsequent time is inadmissible. 

Character of Deceased

While it is true that an assault or homicide is as much a crime if committed upon a person of bad character as

CRIMINAL INVESTIGATION 

if it had been committed upon the most exemplary of men, the rules of evidence permit proof of the bad character of the deceased in a homicide case in support of, and provided there has been previously introduced, evidence tending to support the theory of self defense. 

Unless evidence tending to show self defense has been introduced evidence of the character of the deceased is not admissible and the prosecution cannot introduce evidence of the good character of the deceased until it has been first attacked by evidence introduced by the defense attacking such reputation. Here, as in the matters discussed in former paragraphs, character is provable only through character witnesses testifying to general reputation in the community. 

Most states maintain the rule that the reputation for peace and quiet of the deceased in cases of homicide is not alone sufficient where the issue is self defence, but must be coupled with that person's reputation as a violent, turbulent and blood-thirsty man. Where the reputation just referred to has not been reported to the defendant prior to the fatal affray, evidence thereof may be received on behalf of the defendant as a circumstance tending to show who was the aggressor in a conflict as to which the evidence relating to aggressorship is equivocal, and, since in such a case the evidence is as to the reputation in fact, and not as believed by the accused, the prosecution may meet proof of such bad reputation of the deceased by the testimony of witnesses that the reputation of the deceased in the respect in question was good.

The law also permits the defendant to prove that it was reported to him prior to the affray that the deceased was dangerous, that he was a gunman and a killer, or similar report. In such case the evidence is received to determine whether the defendant, seeing what he saw, hearing what he heard and knowing what he knew, including the reported fact tending to induce a belief that his assailant was dangerous, was justified in believing that he was in danger of death or great bodily injury and that it was necessary to kill to protect himself. 

Since, however, we are in such a case dealing with the influence of a report upon the mind of the accused, the prosecution is not permitted to meet this proof by showing that the report made to the defendant was false and that the deceased was a man of peace and quiet and the prosecution can only rebut such proof by evidence that no such report was made to the defendant, by proof that the defendant did not believe it or that the report had no effect upon his mind at the time of the conflict. 

The foregoing considerations as to character testimony may seem to belong to the province of the trial lawyer rather than to the criminal investigator, but since character testimony may be a vital factor at the trial, the thorough investigator will direct inquiries in all cases in which character might by any possibility be the subject of proof and include his findings in his report. The reason for this suggestion is that cases have been lost because the prosecution was found unprepared at the trial to rebut character testimony introduced by the defense.

Impeachment of Witnesses 

A witness may be impeached by the testimony of character witnesses to the effect that his general reputation in the community in which he lives for truth, honesty and integrity (in some jurisdictions for truth and veracity) is bad. This attack may be made upon the testimony of any person who takes the witness stand even though it be the defendant himself. Evidence is not admissible to show the good character of a witness for truthfulness until that character has been attacked by the party against whom he has been produced, but once such character has been attacked, it may be met by the testimony of character witnesses that such repution is in fact good.

A powerful method of impeaching a witness is to prove that the witness on a former occasion, which may have been in or out of court, has made statements as to a material fact at variance with his testimony at the trial. The procedure is for the cross-examiner to ask the witness whether he did not on a certain occasion, naming the time, place and parties present, make such and such a statement, incorporating the language which the impeaching witnesses will declare he did use. Later the impeaching witness or witnesses are placed on the stand and asked whether the witness or witnesses did not at the time and place and in the presence of the parties named make such and such a statement, here repeating the statement in the same language as put to the original witness. To the investigating officer this should point to the importance of several factors. First, that every witness, especially a possible adverse witness, should be interviewed and his statement taken, in writing if possible, and that the time and place and the parties present be carefully noted together with the addresses of the persons present so that they may be readily located and brought into court if necessary. 

Even the honest witness, misled by bias or interest or belief, is likely to think things over and, consciously or unconsciously, change his story or add to what he originally stated. The dishonest witness, if interviewed at the earliest possible opportunity will not have time to devise a good story to tell and, should he later concoct a version of the transaction, he can easily be impeached if the investigator has done his full duty. 

A witness may also be impeached by proof that he has previously been convicted of a felony, a rule which applies as well to the defendant who takes the witness stand as to any other witness. It therefore shoula be a part of the duty of every investigator to check up on the witnesses, especially the adverse witnesses whose testimony does not appear credible and reliable, and to furnish the prosecutor with the records of any such witnesses. This should be done at the earliest possible opportunity so that exemplified or certified copies of the judgments and other evidence of prior convictions may be secured for the trial.

CHAPTER XV. 

Poisons--The Investigation, Motive 

Generally speaking we may define poisons as including any solid, liquid or gas which when applied externally or introduced internally is capable of destroying life by reason of its own chemical quality and not by reason of mechanical action or temperature. This definition excludes such substances as ground glass, metal particles or other mechanical irritants or abrasives as well as liquids of a temperature which, by reason of the temperature and condition of the person, may cause death. Among the irritant poisons more common in use are those which are strong corrosives, acids and alkalies, mercury and the cyanides. Among what are sometimes called the simple irritants are arsenic, antimony, copper, silver and some vegetable poisons. The narcotic poisons include opium and its derivatives, prominently morphine, cocaine, and gases such as carbon monoxide and natural or manufactured illuminating gas. Nux vomica and strychnine and also carbolic acid are also sometimes used and there is an almost infinite number of poisons which might possibly be used in felonious homicides but which, either because of the difficulty of obtaining them or because they are so rare or unknown, are seldom or perhaps never used. While the vast majority of murders are committed by the use of a comparatively few of the poisons the investigator must be careful not to eliminate the possibility of poisoning merely because the case does not fall within one of the well known and common classes nor merely because the autopsy has failed to disclose evidence of poisoning. 

Autopsies are not always conducted with that degree of care which should be used and instances have been known wherein, though the autopsy was negative, it was later conclusively shown that death resulted from poisoning.

Irritant poisons produce vomiting, purging and abdominal pains and often convulsions which may come on almost immediately after being taken or which may not appear for some hours, especially when taken into a stomach well filled with food. In the corrosive poisons we frequently find evidences of burning on the lips and tongue. The narcotics produce giddiness, stupor and coma and sometimes convulsions. The foregoing symptoms may be produced by causes other than poison but whenever they are encountered the investigator and autopsy surgeon should not eliminate the possibility of poison even though there be at the time no reason to suspect a felonious homicide, even though the death occurred in the home surrounded by a circle of the family and not even if the family physician certify to the death as being caused by heart failure, indigestion or other non-criminal cause. Instances are not at all uncommon in the annals of crime wherein the family physician innocently, though perhaps in some cases negligently or ignorantly, certified to death from disease or natural causes but which later developed into cases of murder. Even a diagnosis of ptomaine poisoning, if correct, does not exclude the possibility of murder for the tainted poisonous food may have been given to the deceased with homicidal intent. On the other hand, not every case of poisoning is murder for there are always the two other possibilities of suicide or accident, the latter class of cases including those in which the deceased was accustomed to take poisons, such as narcotics, arsenic or strychnine, but through carelessness or ignorance took an overdose.

Most prominent among the poisons used to commit murders has been arsenic. Arsenic is quite easily obtainable, a high percentage being contained in poison fly paper, complexion wafers of some kinds and is used by some people as a medicine and as a "beauty tonic." 

Arsenic is a powerful irritant and when taken in poisonous doses produces vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain and prostration. Arsenic has often been used in slow poisonings, small doses being administered over a period of time and eventually proving fatal. The victim gives the appearance of a person taken ill with a painful digestive disturbance which may at times improve a little but gradually wears him down till his death which follows an attack and which may be preceded by a stupor. 

Strychnine produces violent symptoms of suffocation and convulsions or jerking, the patient's mental faculties remaining unaffected and death or recovery being a matter of a few hours. The symptoms are so marked that this poison is hardly one which can be administered without arousing the suspicion that the death was caused by poison and not by some disease or natural cause and is not used by the poisoner who has made an effort to familiarize himself with poisons. While some of the poisoners of the past were unfamiliar with the subject of poisons the vast majority of this class of murderers have made a greater or less study of the subject preliminary to the crime and naturally chose a poison which would produce symptoms easily explained on a reasonable theory other than poison. 

The cyanides are based upon prussic or hydrocyanic acid, a deadly poison with a sweet smell and rather 63

pleasant taste, characterized by the distinct odor of bitter almonds which is easily recognized but which disappears quite rapidly after death so far as an external examination of the body is concerned. If this odor can be detected at the mouth of the deceased we can feel assured that the death occurred at most, only a short time previous. Accidental deaths occur in the use of cyanide in fumigating citrus orchards as a result of inhaling the gas. In homicides the form most often used is that of sodium or potassium cyanide, both of which are quite soluble; prussic acid is rarely used. 

Cyanide produces slowness of breathing, slow and irregular heart action, a choking sensation, blueness of the lips and face, insensibility and sometimes convulsions. Usually death follows very rapidly though not necessarily instantaneously as is popularly believed and some cases are recorded in which the victim lived for several hours before dying. As cyanide in the form of sodium cyanide, potassium cyanide or a combination of these two forms is commonly used in electroplating, the jewel industry, photography and for temering steel it is much more easily obtained than is generally believed. This feature may be availed of in the investigation of cyanide cases as presenting a line of inquiry as to whether any person connected with the deceased was engaged in a vocation in which cyanide was used either by himself or on the employer's premises.

Mercury as a poison is generally in the form known as bichloride of mercury or corrosive sublimate but all salts of mercury are highly poisonous. Taken internally this poison, by reason of its irritating quality, sets up copious purging, salivation, the lips and mouth are apt to be burned white and there is a burning pain in the mouth and stomach. The patient may mention a strong metallic taste. Chronic mercurial poisoning may result from repeated medicinal doses of mercury, handling the metal or inhaling its fumes. In these cases there is an excessive discharge of saliva, bad breath, tender and spongy gums and loosening of the teeth and sometimes the jaw bone becomes diseased, the patient becomes bloodless and weak and may die. 

The case history should easily separate this latter class of cases from those in which death was produced by a single dose.

A complete discussion of all the possible poisons which may be used to commit murder is impossible in the space here at our disposal but the foregoing include those most commonly used in the past. Other possibilities include snake poison, ptomaine and bacterial poisoning, fungus such as toadstools, vegetable poisons, etc.

There have undoubtedly been quite a few murders by poison which have never been discovered or even suspected. Many cases have been recorded in which bodies were exhumed after burial only to discover that the death which was at the time thought to be due to disease was in fact caused by poison. Such cases are often not suspected by even those close to the deceased at the time; in others the suspicions of some neighbor or relative are put down to jealousies or spite. Every case in which any suspicion whatever is suggested, no matter how far fetched it may seem, is worth careful investigation. The best time to investigate any case is when it is fresh and there have been so many cases of poisoning in which the original suspicions were disregarded to warrant this recommendation.

In the investigation of a possible poisoning the officer should ascertain all of the symptoms, complaints and declarations of the deceased, not only immediately before his death but for some time previous, especially complaints of not feeling well, pain or of the manner in which he is being treated. Particular attention should be paid to all the details of the conduct and condition of the deceased immediately preceding his death and the relation in time between the symptoms and the last food or drink taken. In this latter condition the investigator should not accept as true what is told him by those immediately about the deceased who may be interested in concealing the fact of the very food or drink which caused death but should make all effort to check up the truth of those statements. 

Wherever possible all dishes, cups, etc., used by the deceased as well as food residues should be carefully preserved as well as all medicines used by him. This material should be promptly submitted to a chemist for examination. The same should be done with all excreta and vomit of the deceased if obtainable. The premises should be carefully searched for poison. The remarks and statements of all members of the household should be carefully noted. It is rather characteristic for the poisoner to be quite voluble in his or her statements of what has occurred. Murderers of the deliberate variety, either because they have prepared the story they will tell after the crime or because of an excess of desire to divert suspicion are apt not only to talk but to talk too much and to include in their recitals matters which can readily be proven to be false. Particular attention should be given to all explanation of the symptoms of the deceased as related by persons who were about the deceased at the time such symptoms appeared. Inquiry should also be made as to who called the doctor. The promptness with which the doctor was called or the delay in calling medical aid, the person calling the doctor or the person failing to call in such aid when it should have been done, discussions as to calling the doctor are all important. Careful inquiry should be made as to anything in the nature of food or drink, even though it be only a glass of water or a bit of fruit, taken by the deceased shortly before his death, the person who prepared or served the same and whether there was anything unusual in the circumstances, whether as to the person preparing or serving, the kind of food or drink served and particularly whether it was usual and customary or unusual, the manner of service and

The facts in some of the poison cases of the past disclose that the actual poisoner was some servant or mentally deranged individual against whom no suspicion had been directed in the early stages of the investigation. Common motives in poisoning cases are the desire to eliminate a wife or husband, to secure the property of the deceased by inheritance, and it is not at all unusual to find that the poisoner has previously poisoned others for similar reasons. The latter class of cases has been sufficiently numerous to fully justify an investigation into the cause of death of the former husband or wife or of some other person who might have inherited or who may have stood between the suspect and his inheritance from the deceased.

Since poisoning is rarely a matter of sudden impulse but rather the product of deliberation it is reasonable to assume that the poisoner has in some manner familiarized himself with the subject of poisoning. An inquiry should be directed to ascertain whether such has been the case. Cyclopaedias or other books in the home should be examined to see whether there is any evidence of the subject of poisons having been recently consulted. In case of doubt the fingerprint expert may be able to develop a latent finger print-a long shot, but it may succeed. The associates of the suspect may give a lead to the source of the information regarding poisons. It is not unreasonable to run down the possibility that the information has been obtained from books in a public library or borrowed from an acquaintance. In these days of a multiplicity of drug stores it is rather a difficult matter to run down purchases of poison of the kind used, but the mere difficulty of the task should not deter the investigator from making this search thorough. It may be mentioned that there have been a number of poisoning cases in which the poison was purchased quite openly for an expressed innocent purpose and such purchase quite frankly admitted. In such cases the amount purchased, the amount required and used for the expressed purpose and the suitability of the poison for that purpose usually prove interesting and valuable lines of investigation.
