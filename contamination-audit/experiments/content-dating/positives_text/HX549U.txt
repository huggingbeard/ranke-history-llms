CHAPTER I 

When Alan Burgess booked passage for the East Indies, he did so with a feeling of bitter reluctance and heartrending despondency. He was leaving his family behind, those whom he loved most dearlyhis wife Teresa, his two daughters, Dorris and Alice, and his only son, Paul. It meant he would be parted from them for at least two or three years, and much can transpire in that length of time, especially when a man reaches the not altogether comfortable age of sixty. 

Particularly did he dread leaving Paul. The whole

Burgess family had plans of no small magnitude for its young male member, whose talent as a screen actor had already been heralded from one end of the country to the other. Not only did Alan Burgess wish to see his son succeed as a famous screen artist, he wanted to be at his side when that success arrived. 

The Burgess family claimed Los Angeles as its home. Old Grandfather Burgess had brought his family here from New Jersey when the Western metropolis was little more than a Spanish pueblo. 

He had watched it grow, he had grown with it. Of a pioneering, exploiting nature in all things where great financial gain was involved, he had been a highlight in Hollywood in the early days of the motion-picture industry. At one time holding controlling interest in one of the largest studios on the Pacific Coast, he was still identified with its chief subsidiary, the West Coast National Film Corpora tion, but in an honorary capacity only. Almost in his eightieth year, Grandfather Burgess possessed an 5

unusually keen intellect and boasted a physique that even his son Alan envied in secret. 

The Burgess family was well-to-do, but not so aristocratic as one might imagine, in view of the fact that its annual income approximated a quartermillion dollars. Alan Burgess was an importer of 

East Indian spices and the owner of vast plantations on these islands. Old Grandfather Burgess had set him up in business years previous and prosperity had truly come his way. He had spent much of his life in the Indies and had crossed the Pacific all of twenty-six times. Now that the twenty-seventh, urgent as it was, was at hand, he found himself faltering.

All on account of Paul. Now that his son was on the threshold of success, it would be an enviable pleasure to watch him blossom forth an idol of the screen. Paul, the unsullied pride of the house of 

Burgess! He would like to take the young fellow with him on the trip. In that event it could be made a most pleasant voyage. But it was a thing entirely out of question. Paul's place was in Hollywood, now more than ever before.

Paul Burgess was different than the ordinary run of film aspirants in that he had not grown egocentric over a little success. Handsome lad of nineteen, blonde and athletic, and thoroughly masculine,_he had inherited his good looks from his mother. But with Paul, fine features were only a secondary consideration. Of a serious, aggressive nature, he was determined to reach the highest pinnacle of success in his chosen profession. His life flamed with purpose-nothing must daunt him in his undertaking. 

He had hitched his wagon to the farthest star in the firmament of human aspiration. His goal lay on the shining summits-he must climb arduously in order to make himself extraordinary among men. He

must fashion his years in such a manner that Destiny would take notice of him and condescend to carve his name once and for always on the fadeless Plaque of the Illustrious.

Paul had started his career by playing extra parts for the West Coast National. His grandfather had been instrumental in obtaining for him a few minor roles, but it was his portrayal of the blind musician in "Beggar's Serenade" that had actually lifted him out of the quagmire of oblivion and placed his feet on solid ground. Following the release of that picture, he found himself in demand at even the major studios.

On the day preceding Alan Burgess' departure for the East Indies, Paul received notice he had been selected to play an important role in a spectacular costume picture, the filming of which would start immediately. That evening, seated at the dinner table, he proceeded to let the entire family in on the news. "And the best part of it all is this-I'm to be at Mr. Murdock's office tomorrow morning at nine to sign the papers," he concluded, youthful enthusiasm beaming from every angle of his face. "But wait, son," exclaimed his father, as though a sudden catastrophe had struck him, "did you forget 

I sail at that hour? Aren't you going to be at the boat with Mamma and the girls to see me off? Your old dad's going to be gone this time much longer than you'd ever suspect. Then the man caught himself, and stopped short. He would have given much to unsay those few unpleasant words. 99 Paul hung his head. Life was becoming more and more that way of late. "Sorry, dad. You know without my saying it how much I'd like to be there. I intended to right along -only this thing had to come up right at the wrong

time. Mr. Murdock's a busy man, and if I don't keep this appointment with him I may lose the one big opportunity of my life. It's pretty apt to mean a contract for me later on. But if you say so, dad, I'll phone him and see if I can get a later appointment. Only I don't want to miss out in this picture, that's all." 

Alan Burgess tried to swallow a sudden lump in his throat. "Forget it, son. I should have known better.

This other is a trivial thing, at the most. Your appointment with Murdock is vastly more important. 

Think nothing more of it. 'Tis a far greater pleasure to your old dad to know you're getting a break in pictures. Opportunities like that don't come every day."

So Paul kept his appointment with Murdock, for fear he might not get another one, and Alan Burgess set sail for the East Indies in the selfsame hour.

It nearly killed the old man that his son had failed him in this trying moment. He took it as an omen of evil significance. His wife and two daughters had of their own volition accompanied him to the dock, but now that Paul was not there he almost wished he had come alone. His was not an over-sensitive nature, but somehow this cut deeper than a two-edged sword. Of course the boy couldn't help it. No doubt he would have been there to see his father off, had business not thrust itself in the way. 

Nevertheless, it was with a heavy heart that Alan Burgess set out upon his voyage, and it was through some strange intuition that he sensed grave misfortune ahead. Somehow he knew he would never see his family again. "Be good to Paul," were his parting words to his wife and daughters. "Do all you can to help the boy make good. Think what it means to the name of Burgess."

For days following Alan's departure there seemed to hover over the Burgess household a deathlike pall.

Something bordering on the tragic seemed to pervade the very atmosphere of the place. Where there had been laughter and jovial abandon, now only morbid silence stalked. Mother Burgess wore a sad, morose, soul-tortured look continually, and both 

Dorris and Alice, sensing some hidden terror in that look, went about the place like a couple of scared kittens. Even the servants, struck by the sudden melancholy of it all, moved about in deep solemnity, with countenances such as are often seen in funeral processions. Not one word was spoken by anyone regarding the change that had come over the place, but everyone was certain in his or her own mind that Alan Burgess' departure had some great bearing in the matter.

One evening, shortly after Paul had started work in Murdock's costume picture, something occurred which flung the Burgess household into a temporary state of confusion. The family had just assembled in the spacious library, following the dinner hour, when Paul took it upon himself to make a startling and unexpected announcement-startling at least to

Mother Burgess, who believed in keeping her family together, much as a hen would her brood of dayold chicks. "In Heaven's name, Mumsy, is there a curse hanging over this house? What's gone wrong with everybody? This state of affairs can't keep up—why, we'll all go mad." He had something to tell his mother and hardly knew how to go about doing it. "Paul child, calm yourself. What is the trouble? 

Are you feeling ill or something?" Mother Burgess had been startled at the suddenness of her son's question. "No-not yet. But something's got to be done.

This place what's come over it? Why, it's getting more like a morgue every day. Ever since dad left, things haven't been right here. It's really exasperating. Laying all jokes aside, it's getting my nerves so bad I've simply got to get away. If I don't, my work at the studio's going to the bow-wows.' 

Mother Burgess was biting her upper lip. She was wondering how much longer she would be able to keep it a secret-this thing that had been burdening her heart of late and had given her so little rest both night and day. "Calm yourself, Paul Burgess. You should be thankful you have a home as good as this. Try to be just a little appreciative." "I know, Mumsy dear. But it's getting too much. 

I tell you I've got to get away. You don't care if I engage an apartment near the studio till this picture's finished, do you?" "My son, don't try to kid your mother. Remember she's got plenty to think about with your father gone. 99 "But I'm not kidding, Mumsy-honest I'm not.

I've already rented an apartment. Engaged one this morning just across the street from Murdock's studio. Just temporary, you know, till this picture's finished. It's right on Highland Avenue and a perfectly respectable place. I know it sounds funny for me to be talking like this. I've never done anything like it before-always stuck close to home and you and dad-never caroused around much. But now 

I want to make something big of myself, and I can't do it moping my life away around here. You understand-you must understand. You don't mind, do you, Mumsy?" 

Mother Burgess felt a strange weakness creeping over her. The muscles of her face twitched spasmodically and goose pimples broke out all over her

body. So that which she had feared had come to pass. So that which her mother heart had prayed would never happen was about to transpire. "So the old nest isn't good enough for my boy any more, is that it, Paul? Well, this apartment stuff doesn't set well with me. Not when you've got one of the finest homes in the city. Listen to me a minute. That's just the way your Uncle Fred started out. Left home at eighteen for much the same reason that you're leaving and landed in prison on his twenty-first birthday. There's so much wickedness in the world, Paul, and you're so young. Anyway, it's so ridiculous. You, with a fine home like this, going into some cheap, stuffy apartment. No, I don't see it. Blind, perhaps—but I just don't."

Paul was not surprised at his mother's viewpoint in the matter. He had, as a matter of fact, rather expected it. His mother was an ideal mother-there were none better-but she certainly had no idea of the thoughts of a nineteen-year-old son seeking laurels on the highroad to fame. It seemed there was no end to his being treated as a mere child. 

Surely he had a few liberties due one of his age. "What a worm I've turned out to be. Trying to get somewhere as an actor and tied tooth and toenail to a mother's apron strings. Cast for an important part at the studio and treated at home as if 

I weren't dry behind the ears yet. If it's always going to be like this, I don't want to see my name in electric lights. If it's going to continue this way 

I'll give up pictures. Just imagine what Mr. Murdock would think if he were overhearing this conversation. Wouldn't he wonder what kind of boob he had taken under his wing? No fooling, Mumsy, if you had your way about it, you'd have me wearing knickerbockers again inside of a month. Why,

it certainly is ridiculous-too ridiculous even to talk about." "Paul, don't! Why, that's downright wicked," broke in Dorris, the elder of Paul's sisters, and just one year younger than himself. "Can't you see you're hurting mother? Can't you see she's been worried ever since father left? You wouldn't sit there and deliberately try to make matters worse?" "Hurt nothing," fired back Paul. "Mumsy knows 

I'm right. She knows I'm not asking too muchnot any more than any normal boy might under the same circumstances. Heck, she's not going to lose 

Why, I'm not even leaving town. Gosh almighty, a fellow's got to grow up sometime, hasn't he? I know how Mumsy feels about it. It's just her sentimentality getting an upper hand on her better judgment, that's all.' me. 99 

Mother Burgess was smiling-the first smile in days. Her son's last remark had amused her greatly. "A little old-fashioned, perhaps, Paul. But home influence doesn't harm boys your age. If you move into an apartment, that will mean the end of your living here. After that, home life will be too tame. 

You'll be wanting to have your parties like the rest of them. It will lead to worse. It always does.

The whole thing sounds to me like a wayward boy's excuse for leaving home."

Just then the telephone rang. Mr. Murdock's secretary was calling Paul. He must report at the studio the following morning at eight. Director 

Hughes had decided to shoot some exterior scenes up the coast near Santa Barbara and Paul was one of the players needed. 

What utter joy, mused the lad, as he hung up the receiver. Out on location with Sylvia Palmer. Sylvia 

Palmer was the leading lady in the picture and a star of the first magnitude. How he worshiped her-

how ardently he worshiped her. The very thought of her set his heart to throbbing wildly. Everything else was forgotten whenever her name was mentioned. Vast hopes did Paul entertain of some day winning her for himself. But his success was the first consideration.

Sylvia Palmer was an idol of the cinema world. 

Her pictures were featured in all the principal movie magazines. She received fan mail galore. Her name glittered in front of the finest theaters throughout the land. No doubt she would soon be organizing a film company of her own. Since their meeting, Paul's ambition had been born all over again. Why couldn't he become a film star and make himself worthy of her? Far wilder dreams than that had been dreamed and realized in Hollywood. Well, if determination counted for anything, he would make himself worthy of her. 

He certainly would-apron strings or not!

CHAPTER II 

Murdock's costume picture proved a huge success and won for Paul Burgess laurels extraordinary. It also won for him a two-year contract with the 

Meridian Film Corporation. The Meridian was known for its high class pictures and some of the highest paid stars in Hollywood were enrolled under its banner. Paul felt he had indeed gained a long step up the ladder of fame when he landed that contract. From now on he would be a featured player. It did look as if he might see his name in electric lights after all. Movie fans began their hectic correspondence; interviews with Paul Burgess, character actor, appeared in a couple of motionpicture magazines-surely he was on his way to stardom.

He had kept the apartment on Highland Avenue, despite his mother's wishes to the contrary, and was now sharing it with Penny Trent, himself a struggling juvenile still in the ranks of the extra. Penny was a poor chap who had come all the way from 

Pittsburgh to prove his talent as an actor. He had landed in Hollywood hungry and broke. Paul had taken him in for two reasons. First, there was a certain agreeable congeniality about Penny, and secondly, Paul could not bear to see anyone suffer the pangs of want and hunger, especially when it was in his power to aid. That part of his make-up he had also inherited from his mother. 

Now and then Penny Trent secured a day's work in some mob scene, but it was Paul's unfailing generosity that had been his real means of remaining 14

in Hollywood, and it was Paul's encouragement from day to day that had kept him struggling on, eagerly awaiting the "break" that was ever so slow in coming. If, in all Hollywood, there was one genuinely grateful heart, it was that of Penny Trent. He did not understand why Paul should be so considerate of him. It was more like a brother's devotion than anything else. Why, in all Hollywood Paul was the only one who seemed to care even a damn about him. What hurt was the fact that he had no possible way of reciprocating. But even that grew less painful as time went on.

Paul Burgess was a little unusual in one respect. 

His benevolent nature was a trifle too pronounced. 

Hollywood was not exactly accustomed to people like him. His mother knew that when she tried so hard to keep him at home. She was afraid people might try to take advantage of him on that account. She was afraid he would go so far out of his way to accommodate others that it would prove detrimental to his own success. And in a measure she was right. 

Paul, however, looked at it from an entirely different angle. There had been folk who had given him breaks, plenty of them, and he counted it a privilege to be able to pass along the good deed to someone less fortunate than himself.

Sylvia Palmer, the girl of Paul's dreams, was now starring in pictures opposite the brilliant European actor, Wright Figueroa. Her work was considered the best in filmland. She commanded her own salary now, and every large producing company in Hollywood was eagerly awaiting an opportunity of signing her at her own figure. Paul had not seen much of her lately, except in pictures, of course, but he religiously entertained the old hope. Sometimes it seemed to him a mere hallucination of the mindhis hoping to win a celebrity like Sylvia Palmer, who

already had the whole world at her feet, who already had countless thousands from whom to select her mate. But he loved her-beyond words-not her acting, her fame, her wealth, but the girl herself. 

It seemed he had her on his mind constantly. Her latest picture had set his brain awhirl. He and 

Penny had reviewed it together, and for days he could think of no other topic of conversation. He had sent her a huge bouquet of roses on the night of the première and a telegram of congratulations. It wasn't much, but he knew she loved flowers, and the telegram would no doubt receive her personal attention. 

Then came the unexpected.

At a Movie Directors' Ball, given in the Gold Room of the Biltmore, he met Sylvia herself. She was a picture of exquisite loveliness. Never before had he seen her so beautiful. It seemed she looked even more charming off the screen than on. A gorgeous gown, which in itself must have cost a fortune, draped her queenly young body. A diamond necklace lay about her lily throat like a dazzling chain of stars. Her hair shone like mahogany under the blaze of lights. There was something of exotic richness about her that drew one to her with strange, magnetic power. She was magnificence personified.

Women in boxes eyed her through opera glasses. 

Men, old and young, crowded about her, seeking a smile or word of greeting.

Wright Figueroa, Sylvia's leading man, was there, but that was of minor importance. That she herself had come was quite enough. Her very presence brought over Paul a feeling of exuberance and soothing enthusiasm. It made him glad he had comeexceedingly glad. Glad he was alive and young and strong enough to fight for her. He was going to fight for her, no matter what the cost. 

She was

the one girl in all the world he had ever felt he really wanted. He knew his mother, his father, his sisters would be prouder than ever of him if he succeeded in this conquest. For Sylvia Palmer was a girl any respectable family might well be proud of, even had she never set foot inside the temple of the illustrious. 

But the first step-that was the difficult one. As a matter of fact, Paul was little experienced in the art of love-making, except in the movies, of course, and scarcely knew what course to pursue as the surest, especially when he had a personage like Wright Figueroa, screen idol of two continents, to subdue. 

Only once before had he ever really fallen in love.

That happened during his senior year in high school, and came to naught when the girl threw him overboard and married the principal instead.

Paul wondered, as he saw the crowd flocking about Sylvia, if he would have an opportunity to dance with her tonight. Not much, he guessed, for there were a score of young luminaries present, who were far more popular than himself. He pushed nearer the girl. He would at least try to gain her eye and perhaps one of those exquisite smiles. Then the utter joy of it all when Sylvia sighted him among the crowd, singled him out, pulled him aside and congratulated him on his success in obtaining a contract with the Meridian people. "It's really quite wonderful, Mr. Burgess," she went on to say. "I always felt you'd make good.

You seem to have the stuff great actors are made of. 

Somehow I feel a new star is about to appear on the motion-picture horizon."

He smiled at her dizzily. He was nearly swept off his feet. These words coming from the lips of a great screen celebrity. Surely he had anticipated no such pleasure as this. "Thanks a lot, Miss Palmer," he replied, almost

shyly. "I don't know of anyone I'd rather have tell me that than you. But you are the one who should be congratulated." "Call me Sylvia-please. It makes me feel ever so much more human." "Surely-if you say so. It will be a pleasure. 

And now let me congratulate you on that last picture of yours. A marvelous production, and you were superb in it. The public has simply gone mad over it. The prize picture of the year-no doubt about it. Which again makes Sylvia Palmer the greatest little actress in Hollywood. I'm so glad for you, Sylvia. And tonight you look simply gorgeoustoo wonderful for words." "Thanks, Paul," she answered demurely. "And the roses-I almost forgot to mention them. It was magnificent of you to send them. They were so beautiful." 

Just then Wright Figueroa came up and dragged

Sylvia away to the other end of the hall. But the thrill of that brief moment lingered with Paul for days. Sylvia Palmer, Hollywood's greatest cinema star, had actually complimented him on his ability as an actor. Had even gone so far as to predict stardom for him. 

Paul did not see Sylvia again that night. But the very next day on Hollywood Boulevard he caught sight of her and Wright Figueroa in the latter's magnificent car. This car, of special design and imported from Europe, a massive affair costing several thousands of dollars, was one of the attractions of the Boulevard. Every time Paul saw it, he hated it all the more. He was beginning to hate the owner of it, too. What Sylvia could possibly see about

Figueroa to cause her to encourage him in his attentions to her was an unsolved mystery. 

Standing there on the Boulevard, watching the

car fade from view amid the traffic, Paul made himself a solemn promise that he would beat Wright

Figueroa's time, even though he might have to resort to unusual means in order to accomplish it. 

Penny Trent had not attended the ball at the Biltmore. He was a splendid dancer, too, and nothing delighted him more than to attend social functions of this order. But he was still floundering deep in the quagmire of extradom, and since it was a strictly formal affair, he had been quite out of luck. But that is Hollywood for you, catering only to the famous and near-famous. As Penny himself ofttimes expressed it, during his struggle as an extra, "They just don't give a damn for you till the whole world starts taking notice. Then they can't manage to do enough for you." "Better watch my smoke from now on, old cooky."

Paul was addressing Penny during their lunch period the day following the ball. They usually lunched at a little cafe across the street from the Meridian lot. "Last night Paul Burgess staged his great awakening. And mark my words, there's going to be one less film celebrity in Hollywood if a certain party I know doesn't keep his yellow fingers off Sylvia Palmer." "Ouch, but ain't you the desperate one! You don't mean you're going to bump anybody off or anything like that, do you?" inquired Penny, pleasantly amused at his companion's solemn declaration. "No-not if I can help it. But-well, she's got me going, and I'm getting so I can't bear to see any other man around her. Penny, you should have seen her at the Biltmore last night. Dressed like a queen. Pretty as a doll. Gorgeous beyond words. 

Why, all Hollywood's going dippy over that girlabsolutely dippy." 

Penny chuckled.

"And you're part of Hollywood, ain't you? Well, anyway, you're an ambitious cuss. Most anyone would vouch for that. Seems like you're getting out of bounds a little, though, picking on a girl of Sylvia Palmer's standing. They all have to hand it to you for one thing, at least. You've got guts enough to tackle." "Guts-hell! I've had my mind set on that girl for a long time. And I'm going to see it through. 

I'll admit it's the biggest thing I've ever tackled.

But the way I feel right now I don't give a whoop what comes in my way. 

I'm going to get Sylvia 

Palmer or bust!" "You talk like a boy, Paul. Supposing she won't have you, then what? And how do you expect to cope with a man like Figueroa? Everything's in his favor-you know that. Personality, social standing, screen fame, studio prestige. Oh, I'm not trying to discourage you, Paul. More power to you. But it does look like you've picked out a pretty stiff race, to say the least. Human nature is a funny thing. 

Sometimes, when we know we can't get a thing, we want it all the more. Are you, deep down in your heart, honestly in love with Sylvia Palmer, or is it just that you want her so bad because she's so darn popular? Maybe if you were lucky enough to make her once, you'd be able to get her off your mind." "Don't be a fool, Penny. I'm mad about that girl. Stark mad, I tell you. If ever there was anything on earth I wanted, it's she. It makes me afraid. 

Afraid something will happen to my career if I lose out on her."

At the Meridian Film Corporation it was commonly known that Paul had a "crush" on Sylvia, and they wondered if he were really in his right mind. 

His director, the great Jacob J. Erskine, was also aware of the fact, and when Paul's work began to

fall below normal, Erskine blamed it on only one thing. Followed a personal interview, in which the director assured him there was but one remedy for his poor work-that he must, once and forever, get Sylvia Palmer out of his mind, or his film career was as good as ruined. Paul accepted it all good-naturedly, promised to display more professional interest, and thanked Erskine for his kindly advice. 

Still the thought of Wright Figueroa having Sylvia all to himself was worse than maddening. 

He couldn't blame Sylvia. He had never entertained her, had never "stepped out" with her, had not even let her know how madly he really loved her. Well, he would-in spite of his director's warning. Career or no career, Sylvia would be his.

Then all of a sudden came startling and persistent rumors of Sylvia's and Figueroa's engagement. The studio, all Hollywood, was full of them. Paul was frantic. It was more than he could endure. He tried not to believe it. He'd find out to his own satisfaction. 

Then he surprised himself.

Rousing all the courage that was in him, he asked for an evening alone with Sylvia, and was granted it. 

First came dinner at a fashionable downtown restaurant, then the opera, an hour of dancing in the Cocoanut Grove at the Ambassador, and then a long car ride through the moonlight. 

To Paul, it seemed like a night of magic, a night of make-believe. It was like being wafted through regions transcendent, by some mystical, invisible power. It seemed more befitting some youthful, exotic love-dream than actual reality. On they sped, over hills and through vales pungent with the scent of magnolia blossoms. On they raced, their hearts free as the night wind that tugged at their faces.

Then suddenly, on the crest of a low hill overlooking

the sea, they stopped and gazed out over the vast expanse of undulating waters, resplendent in the moonlight's silvery sheen. "A night made especially for romance. One you read about in books," ventured Sylvia, her starry eyes glancing wistfully at him under satin lids and dark lashes. "A night I've always been waiting for," he returned. "A million other nights could never take the place of this one." He moved closer to her and put his arm about her. The ecstasy of it all. This was indeed the nearest approach to heaven he had ever experienced.

The early part of the evening Paul had been exuberantly talkative. But as the night advanced, his conversation lagged. He was thinking. Those hateful rumors. They put fear and trembling in his soul. Still, deep down in his heart, he discredited every word of it. He couldn't believe Sylvia was betrothed when she had given him the night with her. Yet, every time the thought entered his mind, silence sealed his lips, he felt paralyzed with fear.

At length Sylvia found herself doing most of the conversing. She knew Paul was a trifle moody at times-all actors were afflicted that way more or less but why should he be caught in one of those spells on a night like this? Fortunately, she sensed the reason for it and, all because she fancied she herself had found a new interest in life, set about correcting the matter. "Paul," she whispered tenderly, placing her velvety, warm hand on his, "you have heard rumors of 

Figueroa's and my engagement?" 

His pulse quickened, he groped for words. "Why -I-oh, Sylvia dearest, please—please say it—it isn't true? It would kill me-honest to God it would."

"Then you are interested-that much?" "More than words can ever tell." "Then we will be honest with each other. I am not engaged."

New strength surged within him. He crushed his lips against hers; he squeezed her in his arms till she almost gasped for breath. Indeed, life was still worth the living. His Star of Hope was again in the ascendent. He still had something to look forward to-something reverently wholesome and extraordinarily desirable.

CHAPTER III 

Wright Figueroa took it upon himself to stage a house-warming de luxe following the world première of his latest cinema triumph. Sylvia Palmer had helped him plan the party, although Figueroa insisted on making it a much gayer affair than was her desire. Friends and foes were invited, and Paul 

Burgess was among those present, certainly not because he harbored any friendship for Figueroa or that he cared in the least to share his hospitality, but only that he might witness Sylvia as the charming and affable hostess he knew her to be. 

Immediately following the showing of the picture, in Figueroa's spacious Beverly Hills mansion the guests assembled, and soon the party was in full swing. Celebrities and near-celebrities, stars and near-stars crowded the place. Directors, artists, authors, even prize-fighters, filled the house to overflowing. Figueroa had provided plenty of good liquor. Bacardi, champagne, choice imported Italian wines, not to mention a half-dozen cases of ginger ale, featured a part of the entertainment. Incidentally, everybody arrived unusually thirsty. There was music, dance orchestra included. Max Goldstein, Hollywood's celebrated entertainer, pounded the piano vigorously. Ginger Bennett, a Markus Fancho colored dancer, with skirts pulled high, entertained the crowd with her Black Bottom a la Hollywood.

Bob Crosley, Chicago crooner, gyrated through a few of his latest hits.

The air buzzed with unrestrained conversation. 

Raucous laughter, oaths, arguments, and half-audible 24

whispers of assignation deafened the participants. 

The guests danced, milled about, shoving and pushing, still everyone seemed to be having a good time. 

There was petting in the hallways and necking in the more secluded corners. Sylvia did not enjoy the party in the least. The fact that she was playing hostess to this drunken mob only added to her discomposure. It was not her crowd. CHAPTER XIV 

It is the occurrence of the unexpected which at times lends life its zest and makes it a hundredfold more impregnable to the pangs of monotony and stark sameness.

For Sylvia Palmer, the unexpected had actually happened. After all the leading specialists in Europe and 

America had pronounced her case a most hopeless one, and after operation upon operation had miserably failed to restore that which had so ruthlessly been snatched from her, and after even she herself had begun to believe she would never more see the light of day-it was then that the Great Weaver of Destinies took it upon himself to perform a strange and blessed act.

Sylvia's sight did not return suddenly. In fact, many weeks elapsed before she even dared call it a reality. First a tiny shaft of light, then a blurred and indistinct vision, which at times seemed really worse than total blindness, and finally, after what seemed a lifetime of waiting, the complete restoration of sight. But when her vision did return to normal, her world of ardent admirers seemed almost as glad as the star herself. Fan mail started pouring in from the four corners of the earth, congratulating her upon her splendid recovery. Once again the glorious person of Sylvia Palmer would dazzle the screen with its glamour, its charm, and beguiling loveliness.
