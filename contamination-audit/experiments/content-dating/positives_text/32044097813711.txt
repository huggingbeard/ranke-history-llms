The Commonwealth of Massachusetts SUMMARY OF REPORT. 

This report presents the facts and figures on school costs in Massachusetts. It analyzes the State's effort and ability to support public schools. It reviews the question of state aid for schools in considerable detail, along with recent recommendations dealing with school aid. The report gives particular attention to programs affected by legislative proposals referred for study, namely, school foundation aid, school building assistance, school transportation aid, and aid for special classes. It also presents relevant background on other legislative proposals dealing with educational problems in Massachusetts and referred for study. 

CURRENT AND CAPITAL EXPENDITURES. 

Current school operating expenditures have climbed 136 per cent in ten years (from $87.2 million in 1946, to $206.1 million in 1955).

Capital outlay expenditures for school land, buildings and equipment have reached an all-time high of $39.5 million. 

They are now about 16 per cent of total school expenditures. 

Total school costs have increased 179 per cent in ten years (from $87.8 million in 1946, to $245.7 million in 1955). 

This upward trend in total expenditures is greater than the upward trend in operating expenditures because of the growing impact of capital outlays during the last six years. 

A significant part of the increase in public school costs in Massachusetts is due to the greater number of pupils being educated. Most of the increase in public school population has come in the larger towns. 

It is estimated that operating costs for public schools will be $25 million to $35 million higher by 1958. Total. costs, including capital outlays, will probably run between $45 million and $55 million higher by 1958.

[Feb. 

There is a wide range among the cities and towns in school costs and in taxable resources. The upward trend in school costs has not been uniform among the cities and towns.

STATE SCHOOL EFFORT. 

Massachusetts is spending at least $312 per pupil in average daily attendance. This ranks Massachusetts no lower than 11th among the States in this measure of effort. 

Massachusetts residents are spending about the same percentage of their individual incomes for public schools as they were spending twenty-five years ago and before World War II. Massachusetts is achieving a relatively high expenditure for schools despite a drop in rank in individual income per capita. 

About 77 per cent of all school expenditures in Massachusetts are borne by the cities and towns. State aid for schools is 22 per cent; federal aid is only one per cent. 

The state-aid figure includes an allocation of undesignated income and corporation taxes. 

The bulk of the local share of school costs in Massachusetts comes from the local property tax. Massachusetts ranks second among the States in local property tax collections per capita and fourth among the States in local property tax collections as a per cent of individual income. 

STATE AID TO SCHOOLS. 

State aid to schools for the 1956 school year is estimated at $45 million. School aid is apportioned among 16 separate distributions and reimbursements.

School aid increased from $8.5 million in 1946 to $37.4 million in 1955. In terms of state aid per pupil, it is three and one half times higher now than it was ten years ago. 

Fundamental revisions in general school aid and the introduction of new special-aid programs are mainly responsible for the upward trend.

The increase in state aid per pupil has offset only about one fifth of the increase in total expenditures per pupil since 1946. State aid for schools has lagged behind as a

1956.] 

relative share of school expenditures since the 32 per cent peak of 1950.

State support of public schools in Massachusetts is well below the national average state aid of 41 per cent of school expenditures. The major support of public schools continues to be the local property tax. 

The income tax has gradually become the major source of State funds for school aid and in the 1955 fiscal year, amounted to 85 per cent of all school aid. 

SCHOOL FOUNDATION PROGRAM. 

The school foundation program in Massachusetts has all the earmarks of an ideal program, but there are several obvious shortcomings: 

1. The present $130 cost for the school foundation program is way out of date. 

2. The 7-16 age range for calculating state aid is unrealistic. 

3. Failure to change the equalized valuations has prevented the cost of the minimum program from rising. 

4. The local fair share of six times each $1,000 of equalized valuations, which is what municipalities are contributing to the foundation program, is also out of date. 

5. The equalized valuations, used to measure the local fair share, are unreliable.

The school foundation program fails to achieve any high degree of equalization and is not doing very much to reduce the local property tax burden.

Five proposals for increasing school foundation aid are analyzed. If enacted, they would mean increases ranging from $7 million a year in one case to $25 million a year in most of the other cases. Two proposals House, No. 532 and House, No. 1055 would not finance the increased school aid from income tax proceeds. 

SCHOOL BUILDING ASSISTANCE. 

As of November 18, 1955, the total cost of projects approved under the school building assistance act had reached $316 million. The State's share was about $108 million.

[Feb. 

Cities and towns are now spending at the rate of $60 million a year for school construction. 

The State paid out $5.2 million in school building assistance in the 1955 fiscal year. For fiscal 1956, actual payments will run to $8 million.

By the time the school building assistance act expires on June 30, 1959, an estimated $525 million in school construction will have been approved for aid. The State's share of this total will be $176 million. By 1960 the State's annual outlay for school building aid will be about $8 million. 

Review of programs for school building assistance in other States shows that, by contrast, the Massachusetts program is highly developed and the grants are liberal. 

Six proposals for increasing aid for school construction are analyzed. If enacted, they would mean annual State outlays ranging from $9 million to $15.5 million by 1960. 

SCHOOL TRANSPORTATION. 

State aid for school transportation will be $3.4 million during the 1956 fiscal year. This is about 57 per cent of total transportation expense for schools. When the transportation aid law of 1947 was enacted, sufficient funds were supposed to be provided for administrative supervision; these funds have never been appropriated. 

There is no equalization in the formula for school transportation reimbursement. It is possible, assuming the same conditions, for a wealthy community to receive exactly the same reimbursement as a poor community. 

In recent years the General Court has failed to appropriate sufficient funds to cover transportation reimbursements. 

The 1956 General Court will be faced with a deficit appropriation of close to $400,000. 

One proposal for changing school transportation aid is reviewed. This bill would provide full reimbursement for transportation expense beyond the one and one half mile restriction. It would raise school transportation aid by $1.4 million for the 1956 fiscal year. By 1960 the proposal would mean an increase in transportation reimbursements by more than $1.5 million over the present formula.

Chapter 135 of the Resolves of 1955 provided for “an investigation and study relative to increasing state aid to schools" by the Legislative Research Council. It directed the Council in the course of its investigation to consider the subject matter of 20 senate and house documents filed in the 1955 session of the General Court. A brief digest of each of these legislative documents, together with their numbers and petitioners, is presented in subsequent paragraphs of this introduction.

The 20 legislative documents considered in the course of this investigation and study may be grouped into the following five categories: 

1. Proposals increasing state aid for schools or for studying the system of state aid for education. 

2. Proposals increasing state aid for school construction and for extending state aid to modernization of school buildings. 

3. Proposals for capital improvements at the Massa chusetts State Teachers Colleges. 

4. Proposals for liberalizing the existing system of scholarships at the University of Massachusetts and for financial assistance to needy and deserving college students. 

5. Miscellaneous proposals -one liberalizing state aid for school transportation and the other clarifying the law on state aid for classes for mentally retarded children.

SCHOOL FINANCE IN MASSACHUSETTS. 

Introduction. 

Public schools in Massachusetts are one of our most cherished local services. They are also one of our most expensive local services, accounting for at least one third of every local tax dollar for the State as a whole. In many cities and towns the cost of public schools account for a much larger share of the local tax dollar.

1956.] 

Rising school costs throughout most of Massachusetts and mounting enrollments in many towns of the Commonwealth have increased the pressures on local finance. These squeezes on city and town budgets have given rise to more insistent demands for greater state support of public school services. 

It seems quite unrealistic to discuss state aid for schools without searching out the facts and figures on school finances past, present and future. It seems quite unrealistic, also, to discuss state aid for schools without looking into the important factors which explain the trends in school costs. The problem of school finance, after all, is merely a segment of the over-all financial problem of cities and towns. School aid, furthermore, is but one segment of the over-all issue of state aid for cities and towns. 

Because of the limited assignment in this study, however, only the backgrounds of school finance in Massachusetts, the State's effort to support public schools, state aid for schools, and recent recommendations dealing with school aid, will be presented. What each legislative proposal referred under chapter 135 of the Resolves of 1955 means both substantively and financially will be covered in later sections of the report.

Trend in School Costs in Massachusetts.

1955 school year, or by 136.4 per cent.

The following factors explain the trend: 

1. The post-war increase in the number of children of school age. 

2. Shifts in school population within municipalities and between municipalities. 

3. The decrease in the purchasing power of the dollar. 

4. Economic changes in municipalities. 

5. Demands for better quality and greater quantity of school services. 

6. The effect on current costs of higher expenditures for capital outlays. 

7. The rise in the general level of teachers' salaries. 

Capital Outlay Expenditures. The lag in school construc tion during World War II, the rise in school enrollments and the shifting school populations within municipalities since the war, the need for replacing and modernizing obsolescent schools, and higher construction costs have all meant that cities and towns have had to spend large sums for capital outlays for schools during recent years. 

The previous high for capital outlays was $11.8 million in 

1930. During the past five years capital outlay expenditures have climbed to $39.5 million, or more than three times the 

1930 level. Capital outlay expenditures are now in excess of $55 per pupil in net average membership. Since school construction has not yet caught up with the shortage of classrooms, as pointed out in a later section, the peak in capital outlays has not been reached.

Wide Range of School Costs. Just as state-wide figures on school population trends do not tell the story of the population problems of individual cities and towns, statewide figures on school costs likewise fail to tell the story of costs for schools in individual cities and towns. To some extent, the school financing problem of every city and town is unique and there are extreme deviations from the over-all figures in individual cases.

In the 1953-54 school year, expenditures per pupil in net average membership ranged all the way from a low of $150 to a high of $326. For the 39 cities in Massachusetts, for example, there was a spread of $140 in per pupil costs between the lowest and highest figures. For the 96 larger towns, the spread between the lowest and highest costs per pupil was more than $190. For the 216 smaller towns, the gap was even wider.

Wide Range of Taxable Resources. There are also differences in taxable resources of cities and towns as expressed in valuations per pupil. For the cities, the range between the lowest and highest figures for valuations per pupil is over $14,000. At the bottom is one city with valuations per pupil of less than $8,000; at the top are two cities with valuations per pupil in excess of $20,000.

For the larger towns the range between the lowest and highest figures for valuations per pupil is over $20,000.

There are 20 towns in this group with valuations per pupil under $6,000 and two towns with valuations per pupil in excess of $20,000.

For the smaller towns, the range in per pupil valuations is even wider. This group has 68 towns with per pupil valuations of less than $6,000; at the other extreme are 15 towns with per pupil valuations over $20,000. Some of the variations, of course, are due to the different ratios of assessments to true values applied by local assessors. 

Trends in School Costs Vary. - Similarly there have not been uniform changes in school costs over the past decade. 

In the built-up and older communities there were surplus school facilities and undersized classes before inflation took hold and before the rise in pupil numbers became a problem.

1956.] 

In such cities and towns the increases in school costs have been below the average. In expanding communities, however, classroom shortages were accompanied by demands for higher quality of education. When added to inflation, they could only mean sharp increases in school costs. 

Reasons for Variations. Generally, the wide range of expenditures per pupil are due to one or more of the following situations - differences in local appreciation of the advantages of public education with corresponding differences in educational program and educational costs; differences in economy and efficiency of school organization and operation; and differences in taxable resources.

STATE AID TO SCHOOLS IN MASSACHUSETTS. 

Introduction. 

State aid for schools in Massachusetts dates back more than a century. It began in 1834 with the establishment of the Massachusetts School Fund. This was set up as a permanent investment fund, the income of which was to be used for school purposes. The original fund consisted of moneys received from the sale of state lands in Maine and from claims for military service collected by the Commonwealth from the United States government. State Aid to Schools - General. 

Amount and Scope of School Aid. - State aid specifically identified with or earmarked for schools has come a long way since its early beginnings, although it is still a relatively modest proportion of total school support. School aid now exceeds $45 million a year and is apportioned among 16 separate distributions and reimbursements. 

The number of benefiting municipalities varies from program to program. Every city and town in the Commonwealth shares in at least the school foundation program. 

Three fourths of the cities and towns participate in the six major aids distributed for current school purposes. The number of projects approved for school building assistance already involves 224 towns, 32 cities and 10 regional school districts.

Table 16 shows the whole school aid picture in Massachusetts by specific program, including the source of funds to finance it, the statutory reference, the number of cities and towns receiving aid in the 1954-55 school year, the total amount of such aid during that year, and the per cent which the program is of the total. 

There has been considerable revision of school aid laws and formulas since the end of World War II. These changes have meant steady increases both in the total amount distributed and in the amounts available under the separate aid programs. The number of municipalities participating in the various school aid programs has also grown.

THE SCHOOL FOUNDATION PROGRAM IN MASSACHUSETTS. 

Introduction. 

The so-called school foundation program is the most important form of school aid in Massachusetts. The goal of this program, as expressed in the first section of the law establishing it, is "to promote the equalization of educational opportunity in public schools of the commonwealth and the equalization of the burden of the cost of schools to the respective cities and towns.

Theory of School Foundation. 

A Minimum Program for Every Child. The implication of the above statement is that every child in the State is entitled to a minimum standard of educational program and that the policy of the State is to help cities and towns provide this standard. It does not mean that the State guarantees that the child in the poorest town will receive the same education as the child in the richest town. 

The State distributes the additional help needed to bridge the gap between those funds which the city or town ought fairly to raise from its own resources and the cost of the foundation or minimum program. The objective is really to provide a minimum level of education, the cost of which

1956.] 

is acceptable even to those towns which are unable or unwilling to do more than is absolutely necessary. 

Primary responsibility for school financing continues to belong to the city or town. The State merely filters some of the wealth from the richer areas to the poorer areas to ensure a minimum standard of education in areas where taxable resources are relatively low. This is usually done by supplementing local property tax revenues with nonproperty tax revenues raised and distributed by the State. 

A Local Fair Share. Equalization of educational op portunity and equalization of the burden of school costs also imply that all cities and towns should exert similar local tax effort to provide the minimum foundation program. 

Thus the state aid paid to each city and town is the amount by which the cost of the minimum program exceeds the local fair share. The local fair share, in each case, is usually measured by assigning a uniform rate per $1,000 of equalized valuations throughout the State.

What the local fair share is depends, of course, on the amount of state aid that will be available for distribution. 

The local fair share ought to be low enough to leave room for cities and towns to add to or "enrich" their school programs over and above the foundation or minimum level; and it ought to be low enough so as to place no undue burden on local property taxes. 

Present Foundation Program in Massachusetts.

The school foundation program in Massachusetts has all the earmarks of an ideal foundation program. The ingredients are all there the measurements for each ingredient are badly out of date.

Minimum Program. -The cost of a minimum program in Massachusetts is defined by statute as $130 per child. 

The number of persons between the ages of seven and sixteen is the basis for calculating the amount of aid to a city or town. To permit automatic upward or downward adjustment of this figure in accord with economic changes, the law provides for an increase or a decrease of $1 per pupil for each increase or decrease of $100 million in total equalized valua-

[Feb. 

tions of the State, or major fraction thereof. There were 

196 municipalities receiving distributions in 1954-55 under this first foundation or regular formula. 

The foundation program in Massachusetts permits use of an alternative formula for calculating the amount of aid to towns with less than 5,000 population. With the approval of the State Commissioner of Education, distributions to such towns are made on the basis of the number of full-time teachers, principals, supervisors and guidance directors in the public schools. This number is multiplied by 23 and the product is multiplied by $125. There were 93 towns receiving foundation aid under this second foundation formula in 1954-55.

Since many of the smaller towns have lower population densities and smaller classes than usual, the per pupil costs and the overhead costs in such towns tend to be abnormally high. The alternative formula recognizes this by permitting use of the number of school personnel rather than the number of children as the basis for calculating school aid.

Local Fair Share. The ability of a municipality to support its share of the foundation program is measured by a uniform tax effort. The minimum tax effort is the product of six times the equalized valuations of the city or town. 

This tax effort is deducted from the figure obtained by multiplying $130 by the number of children in the municipality between the ages of seven and sixteen.

Amount of Foundation Aid. The amount of aid dis tributed to a municipality is not actually the difference between the cost of the minimum program and the local tax effort, as is the case in an ideal situation. The State distributes only half this difference. To be eligible for foundation aid, a city or town must have spent at least $110 per net average membership, exclusive of transportation and school lunch costs. 

Minimum Aid. Like most school foundation plans, the

Massachusetts law guarantees that no municipality shall receive less than it got under the old law. Six towns received as minimum aid in 1954-55 what they received in 1948.

Special Treatment. Two recent revisions in the school foundation law provide more liberal formulas for certain towns. To encourage the formation of regional school districts, a 1950 amendment provided an additional 15 per cent of the total aid due to members of regional school districts. 

There were 11 towns in 1954-55 receiving aid under this amendment.

An amendment of 1951 gave special treatment to poorer towns which did not gain under the new foundation program. 

It provided that towns of $2 million valuations or less shall receive school aid based on their 1948 distributions plus an additional 25 per cent. There were 45 towns which received aid in 1954-55 under this amendment. 

Weaknesses of the Massachusetts Foundation Program.

There are several obvious shortcomings in the foundation program. These have weakened its effectiveness and have given rise to considerable dissatisfaction. 

The $130 cost

Conclusions. There are two major conclusions that may be drawn from the analysis of the school foundation program 1. The primary objective of a school foundation program is to promote the equalization of educational opportunity and the equalization of school costs. 2. The secondary objective of a school foundation program is to help reduce the local property tax burden. 

Although high property taxes in many cities and towns of Massachusetts have not kept many of them from discharging their responsibilities for public schools, and from maintaining high standards of educational offering, many are doing so at considerable sacrifice. Others are finding it increasingly difficult to maintain such standards.

​SCHOOL BUILDING ASSISTANCE. 

In 1948, a legislative enactment¹ made possible a sharing of school construction costs by the State. Various criteria were established which determine the amount of state assistance. Overall, the State will pay from 20 per cent to 

65 per cent of the approved building cost. 

Appropriations for school building assistance are made. from the proceeds of the income tax. The first actual payments by the State were made in the 1951 fiscal year. 

The School Building Law. 

In 1948, the Legislature passed a law "to encourage the establishment of regional and consolidated public schools and to provide financial assistance to cities and towns in the construction of school buildings." The act provided for appropriations until June 30, 1953. A 1952 amendment² extended appropriations until June 30, 1959, except as additional payments are necessary to carry out the commitments provided by the act. The School Building Assistance 

Commission was set up by the statute to carry out its provisions. 

Purposes of the Act. The state aid is to be used to promote the planning and construction of school buildings. 

The act provides that part or all of the expenses incurred in the preparation of preliminary studies, plans, drawings and estimates for a school may be paid to a town or region with the approval of the Commission. 

For each construction project authorized, the Commission is to determine the estimated cost, which may be equal to the estimated cost or may be a lesser amount. 

The formula to determine a construction grant is an equalizing one. More liberal grants are made to poor communities than to wealthy ones.

Construction Grants. The total construction grant for any approved school project in a city or town is one quarter of the product of the approved cost of the project multiplied by the ratio of the equalized valuation per pupil in the net average membership in the State to that in the city or town.

No grant, however, may be approved for an amount less than 20 per cent or more than 50 per cent of the approved cost.

The construction grant for a school project in a regional school district is one third of the product of the approved cost of the project multiplied by the ratio of the equalized valuation per pupil in the net average membership in the State to the equalized valuation per pupil in the total net average membership of the towns comprising the district. 

No such grant, however, is to be approved for less than 35 per cent nor more than 65 per cent of the approved cost. 

How Payments are made. If a project is to be financed from the proceeds of any sale of bonds or notes to the extent of 50 per cent or less of the approved cost, the grant is paid in five equal annual installments. In the case of other projects, the grant is paid in equal annual installments during the life of the bond issue.

It includes the original equipment and furnishings, whether movable or built in. STATE AID FOR SCHOOL TRANSPORTATION. 

The State provides reimbursements to cities, towns and regional school districts for the transportation expense of pupils to public and private schools.¹ 

In the 1955 fiscal year, 279 communities received a total of $2,921,507 in school transportation aid. This distribution, which is paid from the proceeds of the income tax, amounted to 7.8 per cent of total state aid for schools in 1955. 

The amount of transportation aid certified for payment in 1956 totalled $3.4 million for 288 communities. 

Development of Transportation Aid. 

Reimbursement for transportation of pupils began in 1913. It started as a corollary to the tuition reimburse- 

1 General Laws, c. 71, sections 7A and 16C.

1956.] 

ment already established for encouraging elimination of high schools in small towns while assuring high school education for pupils in small towns without high schools. 

Under the law¹ the State reimbursed certain small towns not maintaining high schools for varying amounts of the transportation expenses incurred in sending a pupil to an approved high school of another town. 

Current Statutory Provisions. 

The transportation act of 19472 completely changed and liberalized the provisions for school transportation reimbursement.

The State now reimburses all cities and towns for school transportation expenses of pupils once daily to and from any school within or without the town of residence in excess of $5 per pupil in net average membership. The law provides, however, that no reimbursement shall be made on account of any pupil who resides less than one and one half miles from the school which he attends, measured by a commonly traveled route. The statute also restricts the amount of grant per pupil for transportation to private schools to the amount of grant per pupil for transportation to public schools.

In 1952, regional school districts were made subject to all laws pertaining to school transportation. The law provides that, when a regional school district furnishes transportation, the Commonwealth shall reimburse the districts for the full costs thereof.

Other Transportation Benefits. 

Although not specified in the transportation law, communities may purchase school busses and receive reimbursement from the State for their cost.

The formula for transportation reimbursement does not contain the equalization principle which is embodied in the general school aid and school building assistance formulas.

STATE AID FOR SPECIAL CLASSES. 

Development of State Aid. - The Commonwealth has long recognized the necessity for providing special education for mentally retarded and physically handicapped children. 

It was not until 1950, however, that any program of state aid was established which recognized that the special education of the handicapped child is more expensive than the education of the normal child.¹ The first program provided for the State to pay a salary differential of up to $500 for each special class teacher of mentally retarded children. 

Primarily as the result of a special commission report of 1953, legislation was enacted in 1954 extending state aid to cover part of the expenses of special classes for mentally retarded children.2 The legislation also provided for the 

State to share the transportation costs of mentally retarded and physically handicapped children, as well as children afflicted with cerebral palsy, attending special schools. A new Division of Special Education was set up in the Department of Education.

The 1955 General Court extended special-class aid to 12-grade regional school districts. The legislature provided for the State to share in the costs of special classes for the physically handicapped. The 1955 legislation also clarified and codified various statutory provisions relative to special class aids. 3 State aid for special classes is now governed by sections 46 through 46F of chapter 71, General Laws, and sections 29A and 29B of chapter 69, General Laws.

Establishment of Special Classes. Cities, towns and 

12-grade regional school districts are required to ascertain annually how many mentally retarded children they have. 

Each such child must be classified as either educable mentally retarded, trainable mentally retarded, or custodial mentally retarded.

Reimbursements for Special Classes. 

Cities and towns are permitted to provide transportation to approved schools for mentally and physically handicapped children as well as children afflicted with cerebral palsy. Communities providing such transportation are eligible for reimbursement of one half the transportation costs.

The State also reimburses cities and towns for the salary differential paid to special class teachers of mentally retarded children. The State pays up to $500 of the differential for each teacher.

The law establishes November 20 as the date for distribution from income tax proceeds to cities, towns and 12-grade regional school districts of aid on account of classes conducted for mentally and physically handicapped children.

Cost of Aid for Special Classes. 

Prior to passage of the 1954 legislation extending special class aid, state reimbursement for special classes was distributed to municipalities as part of the school foundation aids. In the 1955 fiscal year it amounted to $74,952 and only covered the $500 salary differential paid to special class teachers.

As a result of the 1954 laws, the distribution of aid for special classes in November, 1955, was made through the new Division of Special Education for the first time. The special class aid amounted to $1.2 million for 142 cities and towns. This sum included one half the costs of special classes for the mentally retarded, as well as transportation aid for mentally and physically handicapped children, and for children afflicted with cerebral palsy. 

The salary differential for special class teachers amounted to $85,000 and was again included as part of the general school aid distribution.

By virtue of the 1955 legislation, all state aid for special classes distributed in November, 1956, will be made through the Division of Special Education. It will include the salary differential for special class teachers, one half the costs of special classes for both physically and mentally handicapped children, and one half the costs of transportation of these children and those afflicted with cerebral palsy to approved schools. It is estimated that this distribution of state aid will total about $2.2 million.

Loan Programs in the States.

Nearly a dozen States extend scholarship loans, repayable by equivalent years of public service in the State or by cash. 

Most of these scholarship loans apply to teaching, medical, dental, pharmaceutical or nursing education. Florida, Ohio,

Virginia and North Carolina have substantial programs of this type.

Wisconsin is the only State with a broad system of scholarship loans. Under the Wisconsin program, the State Department of Public Instruction may make loans from specific state appropriations to needy and qualified students who could not otherwise continue their education in colleges in the State. Loans are granted upon the student's application when endorsed by his educational institution. The department fixes the terms of the loans and the rules under which they are to be made. 

Scholarship Programs in the States. 

Most States have state-financed scholarship programs.

Many are very limited in type and scope. The largest of the state-financed scholarship programs are found in New York and California. Many of the programs are geared to encourage entry into certain professions and to meet shortages in certain professions. Programs to encourage teaching preparation are the most common. 

Those of Ohio, Florida, Illinois, Michigan and Virginia are good examples.

Programs to encourage nursing preparation are becoming more numerous. These are found in Minnesota, Florida, North Carolina, Rhode Island and New York.

[Feb. 

The New York Program. 

The New York system of state-financed scholarships annually provides over 5,000 four-year scholarships to New York students for use in the private or public colleges and schools of the State. The annual cost, when the program is in full effect, will be at least $7 million. 

The scholarships are both for undergraduate and professional study. They range in amount from $100 to $750. 

The more numerous are the so-called Regents college scholarships ($350 a year), the War Service scholarships for veterans ($350 a year) and the nursing scholarships ($350 a year). All scholarships are awarded on the basis of scores in competitive examinations. Financial need is given no consideration in making the awards.

The California Program. 

The California program, which begins in the 1956-57 college year, is a general education scholarship system. 

There will be 640 scholarships at first and a maximum of 

2,560 scholarships by 1959-60. These will be granted annually and will be renewable for four years. 

The scholarship grant is $600 a year or tuition plus necessary fees, whichever is lesser. The scholarships must be used in California colleges. Special examinations will be given for the first awards, with renewals based on scholastic records. Financial need must be shown. The first year's cost of this scholarship program is estimated at $300,000. 

It will increase substantially as the program becomes fully developed.

By enactment of chapter 760 of the Acts of 1955, this proposal is already in effect. The act provides for contributory group life, accident, hospitalization, medical and surgical insurance for persons employed by counties, cities, towns or districts and their dependents. It provides the following insurance coverage for eligible employees: 

1. Group life insurance of $2,000 on each employee. 

2. Group accidental death and dismemberment insurance of $2,000 on each employee. 

3. The amount of hospital benefits for each employee and his dependents shall provide coverage at not less than an estimated 60 per cent nor more than an estimated 85 per cent of the average of all hospital bills. 

4. The amount of surgical benefits for each employee and his dependents shall not be less than a standard $200 surgical schedule nor more than a standard $300 surgical schedule with provision for hospital medical coverage. 

The employee shall pay 50 per cent of the premium for this insurance - the county, city, town or district shall pay the remaining 50 per cent. The employee's share of the premium must be withheld from his salary or wages. 

The above benefits are minimum benefits which must be provided. The actual amount of the hospital, surgical and medical benefits for each employee and his dependents is left to the mayor, selectmen, county commissioners or district governing board subject to the appropriation made available therefor.

Chapter 760 specifically authorizes cities and towns to appropriate for the purpose of paying part of the premiums for group life insurance, accident, hospitalization, medical and surgical insurance.-

STATE AID FOR SCHOOL CONSTRUCTION IN SELECTED STATES. 

There are three general groups of state aid programs for school construction. 

1. The first group makes separate grants to local school districts for the specific purposes of schoolhouse construction. This type of grant is not necessarily continuing in nature. It is usually quite large although temporary. Some programs are based on equalization; others are not based on equalization. 

2. The second group includes an allowance for school construction in the minimum foundation law. This type of grant is generally part of a permanent program. It is not necessarily large so far as a single year's grant is concerned. 

It is by definition characterized by the principle of equalization, however. 

3. The third group makes loans to local school districts which must be repaid. Generally these loans are characterized by low interest rates. Though these loan programs usually provide an additional source of capital, they do not constitute an assumption of any share of costs of school housing by the State and, therefore, might not be considered as real state aid. New York. New York appropriates funds to assist school districts in meeting the principal and interest payment on bonded indebtedness for construction items. 

Central School Districts, whose claims were approved before 1941, are eligible to receive one fourth of the approved cost of the buildings. The other Central Districts receive building aid based on an allowance of $450 per pupil, which is adjusted upward or downward in accordance with changes in costs of labor and materials since October 1, 1940, minus an amount based on 6 per cent of the full valuation of the property in the district.

The assistance for Central School Districts was established in its present form in 1941. The building aid is established as a continuing appropriation from year to year with no expiration date given. Under the program the State pays its share of principal and interest charges for each year that the indebtedness of the district continues. If no indebtedness is incurred, the State pays its share of the cost in one grant. 

New York also has an emergency school building fund.

School districts faced with a rapid growth or loss of structure through disaster or condemnation may receive advances to be applied on the payment of the principal and interest on bonds issued. Such advances are considered loans which must be repaid to the state within thirty years.
