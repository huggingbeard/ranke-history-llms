SHAKESPEARE 

IN HONG KONG

We sat around in candlelight in unsavory corners as groups for reading: the halt, the lame and even the blind, all ranks, and weary nurses. Between readings I expounded your historical and critical essays—and how Keeling had Hamlet acted aboard the Dragon off Sierra Leon in 1607, etc. This was so successful that, when I was reckoned, by Nip reckoning, fit to shamble out of hospital into prison camp, I had to take those two tomes (how ponderous they felt) with me; and I got them through.

In the prisoner-of-war camp there were, of course, large audiences of soldiers of all types; smaller groups of serious students; as well as amateur actors; and a banker-batman who sang the lyrics. Anyone who wanted was allowed the 

Red Book; but the Black Shakespeare (really to preserve it intact, as well as keep me in monopoly of the prime source of ideas) had to be consulted at my bed's head, and wrapped as a treasure in old newspaper. 

There were lots of lecturers (some heaven-inspired with excellence who had never before attempted even an afterdinner speech) on every conceivable topic; but Shakespeare was the surest draw and constant pabulum. Your wonderful essays were a well that could never run dry of rich information about the bard's background, system of ideas, theatres, companies, and what not - I was reproved for encouraging people to lecture on Shakespeare who couldn't possibly know anything about him before they came into camp! But though there is nothing more depressing for the average schoolboy or under-graduate than concocting an essay out of books instead of experience, believe me it is a first class tonic for a prisoner who has been working on the least savory camp fatigues, to give voice in measured oratory on the elements of some literary topic which he has acquired during the pre-

vious week. There were better audiences for the lecturer who had just discovered Craig on Shakespeare than for the tyro who had just discovered Culbertson on Bridge; and I hope that doesn't make you think that the camp was a gang of hoodlums. A diet of rice and chrysanthemum leaves doesn't make a man a moron unless he's born one. Naturally some of our listeners and talkers were mere converts; like the man in the "bed" next mine, a commander in the navy, of considerable literary talent, who had always been a mocker of bardolators, allergic to the old Vic, and supercilious to the "Shakespeare crowd", till he caught on to the question of what Shakespeare really knew, and went off into a volume of Elizabethan biographies by Sidney Lee, and a complete Plutarch's Lives (again an American book, and the last one expected in such a place). Most of the others were not so much converts as hungry sheep who had looked up in their youth and had never been properly fed. 

There was a young Canadian, from an agricultural college, who, while confined to bed after jaundice, read all the plays (Red Book, from cover to cover) perhaps more as a marathon effort than a literary experience, in something less than two weeks, reading daily from dawn to dusk, with intervals for "meals" during which he was cross-questioned by stable-companions and visitors, who came to scoff. One captain, who was a most serious long term Shakespearean, made a sort of card index, on scraps of paper for a proper post-war card index, of his very thorough reading of you and the text; and getting an artistic-architect to decorate-with reconstructions of Elizabethan theatre-front and back of an elaborate folder summary of what he had learned, gave it to me as a Christmas card. There were those who wanted to "really understand" the tragedies and comedies they had "done" at

school; and there were those who would only join reading the fresh entertainment of Titus, Troilus and Cressida (very topical), Measure for Measure, and Pericles. There were quite a number of men of all ranks who were excellent readers of parts, in all the variety of British accents. And there were the old Colonels who loved a play that was full of quotations (Any Shakespeare play is tops for that, to any man who has lived fifty years on the verge of English intellectual life.) I'm afraid I upset one, in the middle of Henry V, by suggesting that the IVs and V showed Shakespeare in the unusual role of satirist, with the English military system (as distinct from English martial spirit) of the Elizabethan age, as the object of satire; and cavalrymen (the shining Dauphin and his cronies boasting horse on the eve of defeat by the very inferiorly horsed and dirty English) who mistake the means for the end, and go in up to the neck.

I think this is more than enough to let you see what my fellow slaves and I, when less than the dust, owed to your book-without which Shakespeare studies, like so many other camp aspirations, would have wilted. They had still a lot in hand at the end. I parted with the Red Book and my margin scribblings; but your Black Book I brought home, and will take it back to Hong Kong with me, when I set out next month, to start a skeleton university in our ruins. 

When men are reduced to wearing rags and eating garbage, they are still civilized. The ultimate art to which they cling is literature; with Drama as their religion, and rhetoric as their basic intellectual exercise. 

With profound thanks 

Yours sincerely 

Rathu Simpson

Professor Robert K. M. Simpson, M. C., M. A., head of the Department of English at the University of Hong Kong, was born in Aberfeldy in the highlands of Scotland on May 29, 1892.

He took the Honours English Courses at Edinburgh University and was awarded three medals, under the immortal George Saintsbury. Since 1921 he has held his present position and has frequently been elected Dean of the Faculty of Arts at the University of Hong Kong. During his professorship, Professor Simpson has published three works in prose and one in light verse. 

While serving in the Royal Field Artillery in World War I, he was wounded, mentioned in despatches and awarded M. C. In World War II he again served in the mobile artillery until wounded and made prisoner by the Japanese at the surren der of Hong Kong. 

Professor Simpson has now returned to the University of Hong Kong as professor in charge of the Vice-Chancellor's office, where he is actively participating in the reorganization and restoration of that institution.
