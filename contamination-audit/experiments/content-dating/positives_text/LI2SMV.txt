FINANCIAL ASPECTS OF GROWING SOUTHERN PINE IN WASHINGTON COUNTY, FLORIDA INTRODUCTION FLORIDA'S FOREST INDUSTRY 

The forest resource has been one of the outstanding factors in Florida's economic development. By piecing together the fragmentary records an estimate is reached that between 40 and 50 billion board feet of lumber have been cut from Florida's forest land in the past. About 

80 per cent of this lumber has been cut in the last 40 years. This one product represents an f. o. b. mill value in excess of 800 million dollars. 

Since 1889, or in the last 40-year period, Florida has produced turpentine and rosin valued at the still at above 400 million dollars. If other forest product values such as hewed ties, poles, piling, veneers, staves, etc., were available, the volume and value of the forest contribution to Florida's economic development would be much increased. 

But in producing this income the forest industries have been largely reducing forest capital stored up in the virgin forests. The forest has been treated as a mine-a resource to be exhausted of tree growth, then the land cast aside as worthless with no chance of timber replacement. Can the private forest land owner change this conception at this late day and grow new forests as crops of trees-trees for turpentine, trees for pulp, trees for lumber, ties, poles, veneers and many other products?

In 1929 the annual lumber cut at average prices was valued at about 32 million dollars and the turpentine and rosin at 12 million dollars f. o. b. plants. Other forest products would bring this well up toward 50 million dollars, which approaches the annual value of the citrus products of the state. 

Thus the forest industry taken collectively is today seen to be one of Florida's major industries. Can it be continued as such? The lumber business is still largely cutting virgin timber. Each successive revision of the estimates of the standing timber capital in the state not only shows the reduction called for by the intervening cut, but indicates that the previous estimate was too high. In 1920 the estimate of this timber capital was 36 billion feet of pine and 11 billion feet of cypress (Report on Senate Resolution 311). In 1924 an unpublished study indicated the pine stand remaining might not be over 24 billion board feet. 

Although only about 7 billion board feet of all species have been cut into lumber in Florida since 1924, other products have taken their toll. 

Coupled with the drain the stands of virgin pine timber in south Florida are showing up disappointingly low, so that the 1930 estimates of the remaining standing timber now being compiled are placing the remaining timber capital below that of neighboring southern states. Since the lumber industry is now supported by the few remaining virgin timber stands, and because the better second growth is mostly being turpentined, a large decrease in the cut appears inevitable in the next few years.

Florida's home consumption of lumber was placed at 729 million board feet in 1926 and 415 million in 1928¹. Of these amounts 415 and

379 million feet were locally manufactured. With the total state cut hovering around 1,000 million feet, it appears that Florida now is using the equivalent of about one-half its output. The time is not far distant when Florida will need her entire diminishing cut for her own needs. 

This will have to be grown as "second growth" timber on her cut-over lands or Florida will become a timber-importing state. This situation invites a careful examination of the financial aspects of timber growing. 

Will this growing of timber pay? What are the prospects?

There is already present on much of this cut-over land some young forest which has escaped the general annual woods burning. It is this partly restocked young forest that must form the nucleus of the forest properties of the future, on which the continuation of large forest industries depends. The home market will not only absorb the lumber output but turpentine and rosin barrels, berry and citrus boxes, railroad ties, piling for harbor work, poles, veneers, barrels and hampers for truck and vegetables will add largely to the home market for home-grown trees. Naval stores will continue more largely as an export product.

It must not be overlooked that the first paper mill in Florida was built in 1930. Paper mills locate only where they see a chance of a permanent young timber supply, and they expect the market will pay the cost of growing it ultimately. The rush merely to earn a living cutting pulp wood now will give way to prices later that will leave no doubt of the assistance of the paper mill to the stumpage grower. 

Since the larger part of Florida's second-growth timber is longleaf and slash pine, it is to the naval stores industry that timber growers generally turn for early incomes. Florida produced but 5 per cent of the country's naval stores in 1890, but the industry increased rapidly on the virgin timber in the state until the peak was reached in 1910, when Florida produced 54 per cent² of the total gum turpentine for the nation. With the rapid passing of the virgin pine, Florida's percentage of the total gum turpentine has fallen to 31 per cent³ in 1929. While county studies show variable conditions for future production, some further shrinkage seems probable, but naval stores production can be stabilized in a much shorter time than lumber production. 

The entire forest industry situation in Florida may be said to promise a large and early shrinkage in lumber cut, a smaller temporary shrinkage in naval stores output, and an increase in the cut of pulp wood and in the stave and box industries. While the total forest income will undoubtedly fall for a time at least, increasing home requirements would appear to promise improvement of stumpage prices for secondgrowth timber in the future, and thus favor timber growing. 

THE LAND SITUATION 

Florida has a total land area of 35,111 thousand acres. In 1925 

2,280 thousand acres of this, or 6 per cent only, was crop and fallow land and plowable pasture land. The balance, or about 94 per cent, is non-crop land and is largely cut-over forest land except for the open Everglade area in South Florida. Some of this cut-over land is naturally restocking to second-growth timber to varying degrees. This large area of non-crop forest land, coupled with the small present demand for additional agricultural land, adds argument for this inquiry into the

OBJECTS OF STUDY, AUTHORIZATION AND COOPERATION 

From the standpoint of maintaining the important forest industries of the state as well as assisting in determining whether timber growing is a profitable use for some of the large non-crop land area under present economic conditions, a study of the financial aspects of growing timber appears desirable. Private capital and effort may be enlisted in the timber growing project only if a fair profit appears reasonably certain.

In examining the financial aspects of timber growing in general, the United States Forest Service has begun with a study of growing southern pine. The project is one of a number authorized under the 

McSweeney-McNary Forest Research Act. The general study will embrace the entire southern pine belt. A number of county surveys are first projected for counties where the forest resource is important, and the smaller forest holdings of farmers and turpentine operators are a factor in varying degrees. These county surveys are essentially background studies, which are to be followed up by specific forest property case studies for both small and large properties. 

The State Forester's Office of Florida has offered to cooperate in the survey of several counties in this state as a part of the general study, and to publish these surveys as progress reports. Accordingly, Washington, Bradford, Hamilton, and Osceola Counties were selected for cooperative study, and Bay County for a state study, as representing different forest and economic conditions in Florida.

METHODS OF APPROACH 

Few people feel that they can afford to begin to grow timber by planting upon bare land and waiting from 15 to 60 years for an income from naval stores, pulpwood, timber or other forest products, in the meantime carrying taxes, interest, protection and other costs. But large areas of this cut-over pine land have a scattering of left-over trees unmerchantable in the first cut. These trees have served the purpose of seed trees and a very irregular but generally sparse stand of second growth has come in. These stands are now producing some revenue from naval stores, pulpwood, ties and small saw timber. It is on these lands that timber growing as a business is showing the earliest development. It is with these second-growth lands that a study of the financial aspects of growing pine timber must begin. 

It should be pointed out at the beginning that the annual growth per acre of any crop, whether it is cotton or timber, has a profound

Florida Forest Service 

99 effect on the cost of growing per unit, and the chance of finding a favorable margin between such cost and its appraised value. The matter of yield is therefore not a minor matter of "forest description,' but of basic consideration in a financial aspects study. In addition to average yields, optimum yields on small areas are shown as examples of yields that may be attained by forest management generally. 

The costs in the present stage of "extensive" timber growing are limited to interest on property investment, taxes, protection and administration. There are generally no cultural costs and even the last two-protection and administration-are often not incurred to a measurable extent in present practice. 

For comparison with costs, stumpage values are necessary. At first blush, market values of stumpage secured by a simple canvass of sales might appear to be the only desirable element here, and these are obtained. But much stumpage is sold by area, or leased by area for turpentine production, so that unit sale and lease values are often not known. Further, some mill operators cut and turpentine operators tap their own timber, grown on their own lands, so that a stumpage sale value or turpentine lease value is not established. Again, many sales result from some financial pressure on the seller, or are carried out without adequate knowledge on the part of the seller. Further, the marketing of second-growth timber in many localities is a new thing and market values are not stabilized to the same degree as are those for virgin timber.

For financial analysis the present market values are not discriminatory enough as to the effect of tree size on either lumber or naval stores production. Products of small sized trees are intermingled with those of larger size from the same and different forest areas, and general prices only are known by the operator. 

For the above reasons an appraised stumpage and turpentine lease value is believed desirable in addition to the statement of market values. 

The appraised values will generally fall within the limits of market values and should be comparable with market values under conditions where buyer and seller have equal knowledge and financial strength. 

With the appraised stumpage and lease values established, the forest incomes may be computed from the output of forest products. 

These incomes reduced (1) for forest costs such as protection, administration and taxes, and (2) for capital depletion where the products consume more tree volume than is replaced by growth, give net forest income. This net income compared with the cost value of this land. will show the approximate financial attractiveness of the business of growing timber beginning with the forest land as it now is. 

Another comparison that is helpful is to compare the costs and returns from the artificial reforestation of lands in need of it, based on present values.

Definitions 

Forest land—is land originally occupied by forest trees, and not changed to another use. It may now contain virgin timber, or have the bulk of the virgin timber removed as in cut-over land. In the latter case it may contain a scattered remnant of trees of the virgin stand and be restocked to varying de- 'See p. 9 for definition of terms used.

grees with second growth, or be denuded (less than 10 per cent restocked).

Growing stock-means the stand of trees only. 

Forest-includes land and growing stock. It may be virgin or second growth. 

Site-defines the comparative tree-growing quality of an area. 

It is measured by the average height of the dominant trees in feet on a well-stocked area at 50 years of age.

Diameter of a standing tree is taken outside bark at 4.5 feet from the ground-diameter breast high abbreviated d.b.h. 

Volume growth-is the wood laid on by a stand of trees in a given period on a given area. Current annual volume growth is difficult to measure so current periodic annual is substituted by taking the growth for the last or next 5 or 10-year period and dividing by the number of years. Mean annual volume growth is the total volume divided by the total age. 

TURPENTINE OPERATION TERMS: 

A crop of cups is applied to 10,000 cups. 

Turpentine lease is the right to gather turpentine gum from timber owned by another. A lease is made by area of timber or by number of cups hung. It generally covers from one to four years' use and is often paid in advance. 

Front face-refers to the side of the tree chipped first. Back face refers to the second side chipped. On small trees they are usually chipped in rotation, front face 4 to 6 years and then back face 4 to 6 years, with often a rest of 1 or 2 years intervening. But on large trees, particularly when timber is to be logged, as many faces are started simultaneously as the tree can stand, which may be two or more. 

Streak-refers to the ribbon of wood cut off each period (about 1 week in the summer) to keep the wound open and the gum flowing. 32 streaks are regarded as a season's average.

Bar-is the vertical strip of bark and wood left uncut between faces which enables the tree to carry on. Its proper width is extremely important in continuous operation. 

Gum-is the fluid oleo-resin produced by the tree when wounded and is the principal source of the distilled turpentine and rosin.

Scrape is the hardened resin formed on the face of the tree in the chipping season. It is "scraped" off and taken to the still at the end of the season. Longleaf pine produces more scrape than slash pine.

Barrel of turpentine is taken as 50 gallons for statistical purposes. Each barrel or cask is gauged for sale. 

Round barrel of rosin is 500 pounds gross or approximately 420 pounds net. It should be noted that American primary market quotations are based, however, on barrels of 

280 pounds gross (no tare allowed).

Gum or dip barrels used in collecting the gum in the woods vary in contents in different localities from the 50 to 

53-gallon "blue whistler" barrel to the 34-gallon barrel. In making financial calculations on gum purchased, the actual contents becomes important. In Washington County the gum

Unit is a statistical naval stores term covering one 50-gallon barrel of turpentine and 3 1-3 500-pound barrels (gross) of rosin as the actual proportion of the two products produced in distilling gum turpentine.

FINANCE TERMS: 

Market value is the value set by actual sales. It is apt to be rather variable from sale to sale, as pointed out on page 8. 

Realization value is applied to timber stumpage and timber leases for turpentine operation and means the difference between the average market value of the forest products and the cost of production from stump to car, including interest on all production capital, but excluding timber and land. It represents maximum stumpage values in that it includes all pure profit.

Appraised value is applied to timber stumpage and timber leases and is the realization value less a fixed percentage on production cost allowed for profit and risk. For a timberland operator operating his own timber it is more significant than an average market value. It is believed to represent an attainable market value when buyers and sellers are equal in financial strength and knowledge. It is generally reached by some of the sales and lease prices and sometimes exceeded, as in the lease of small sized timber for turpentine when the operator does not fully discount the lower yields of such timber.

Profit-is stumpage or lease realization value less prices paid. 

Gross forest products income-is the total value of forest products f. o. b. plant.

Appraised forest income-is based on appraised stumpage and lease income, which is products value f. o. b. plant, less costs of manufacture and the profit and risk items. 

Net appraised forest income is appraised forest income less forest costs such as protection, administration, thinning, and taxes, etc. 

CHOICE OF WASHINGTON COUNTY 

Washington County was chosen for this study as representative of the rolling upland longleaf pine lands of the state west of Tallahassee. 

It contains rolling upland longleaf pine second-growth lands, cut-over scrub oak-longleaf pine sand hill land largely not coming back to a merchantable stand of longleaf, and a strip of productive loblolly pine. 

There is little slash pine. It has an active turpentine industry, and some lumber and stave production. 

In later studies, Bradford and Hamilton Counties were selected to represent the longleaf-slash pine flatland of northeast Florida, with some pure longleaf hill land in Hamilton County. Osceola County was selected to represent the southern extension of the pine flatlands, and Bay County was added by the State Forester's Office in a similar study to show the western extension of the coast longleaf-slash flatland type.

POPULATION 

The area that is now Washington County was settled about 1820 by emigrants from Alabama, Georgia, and the Carolinas. The county is one of the oldest in the State, and many changes have been made since its original boundaries were set. The present county lines have remained unchanged since 1915. In 1920 the Census shows a population for the county of 11,828, all of which were classed as rural because there were no incorporated places having 2,500 inhabitants or more in 1920. About three-fourths of the total population, or 8,871, were white, while 2,957 were colored. The area as a whole is sparsely settled with only 19.1 persons per square mile. TOPOGRAPHY AND DRAINAGE 

The surface features of the area are for the most part gently rolling. In the northern part of the county the land is usually flat or gently sloping. Oftentimes the level expanses are so near the water table that a depression of a few feet gives rise to a "pond" or wet area and a rise of a few feet causes a dry ridge. In the middle of the county running approximately east and west is a range of red clay hills. This range of hills, of about two miles in width, separates the flatland in the northern part of the county and the sand hills of the southern part, which occupy more than a third of the entire acreage of the county. The rolling surface of the sand hill region is occasionally broken by sink-hole lakes of clear, fresh water.

Except for the flat areas in the northern part of the county the region is well drained; in many places drainage is so complete that it is prohibitive to agriculture. From a maximum height of about 250 feet the elevation of the county decreases to within a few feet of sea level. 

Most of the county is drained from the north to the south by the Choctawhatchee River, which flows into the Gulf about 20 miles from the southern boundary of the county. SOILS The entire State of Florida is in the physiographic province known as the Coastal Plain. Washington County soils may be grouped into three types, as follows: (1) Sandy uplands with clay subsoil; (2) Red, phosphatic loam; and (3) Deep dry sand, including scrub.

In the northern part of the county the sandy uplands type predominates with its characteristic gray sandy loam, with clay subsoil usually within three feet of the surface. The vegetation is chiefly longleaf pine and wire grass with slash pine and cypress in depressions or along streams. The red, phosphatic loam soil is the principal type of the "red clay hills" extending east and west across the county. It supports a forest vegetation of "shortleaf" pine (loblolly-Pinus taeda) and hardwoods. In the southern part of the county the deep, dry sand type predominates with its characteristic white or creamy color, three 3Since the weather data taken in Washington County were incomplete, this information is taken from the 1925 weather report of the Marianna, Florida, Station in the adjacent county.

feet or more in depth. This soil type supports a very sparse vegetation of longleaf pine, black jack and turkey oaks, and wire grass. 

The principal soil series found in the county are Norfolk, Orangeburg, Swamp, Tifton, and Myatt. Since there has been no soil survey made of the region it is difficult to give any exact figures on the areas occupied by these different soil series. The writers' estimate is that the Norfolk Series covers 85 per cent of the county, the Orangeburg 4 per cent, the Swamp Series 8 per cent, and all other soil series 3 per cent. Soils of the Norfolk Series are principally sandy with variations due to the changing size of the particles and amounts of clay or organic material. In the northern part of the county the Norfolk sandy loam, the Norfolk fine sand, and the Norfolk fine sandy loam are widely distributed. These soil types include the bulk of the farming done in the county. They are easily cultivated for cotton and corn, and will readily respond to the application of fertilizers. The Norfolk soils are deficient in humus and, for this reason, the organic fertilizers such as velvet beans and vetch are very helpful in building up the soil. The Orangeburg soils, principally the coarse sand type, are also used for farming. 

This soil is probably the most widely distributed soil in the red hills region, where it supports forest stands of shortleaf pines and hardwoods. Probably the most productive soil in the county is the Tifton, but it is so scarce that it is of little importance. In the southern part of the county, the sand hill region, the Norfolk sand is the predominating soil type. Some attempt has been made to grow farm crops upon this soil but the results have not been encouraging. With large quantities of fertilizers, Satsuma oranges, grapes, and blueberries are produced. The sand hill region has been burned over so often that at present there is a deficiency of organic material in the soil. If the natural organic material, leaves, grasses, and weeds, were allowed to accumulate in the forest this type should support a much denser stand of timber. That the soil of the sand hills is non-agricultural in value is evident from the poor crops produced upon it and from the fact that there is a large amount of land in the county not being cultivated which is more suitable for farming.

Financial Aspects of Growing Southern Pine, Washington Co., Fla. 19 THE FOREST HISTORY 

According to all indications the early settlers of the region found an unbroken forest over the entire area. It is estimated that of this original stand of timber 80 per cent was longleaf yellow pine. It was this timber resource that attracted the turpentine operators and lumbermen from the regions farther north as early as 1880 and 1890. 

In working the large round trees for turpentine the operator cut a deep trough or "box" at the base of the tree to catch the gum and worked out the face above the "box" in about 3 years. In spite of the size and vigor of the virgin timber this method was very injurious to the timber. 

In early lumbering we find the same waste, for only the clear material was merchantable at that time and everyone thought the timber supply to be inexhaustible. Early logging was a culling process of taking only the best trees and only the best logs in these trees. The cattlemen also exerted an influence on the forest through their annual burning. 

Oftentimes grass fires spread to the boxes and faces and burned for a long time in the resinous deposits. Repeated fires hollowed out the bases of the trees and dried up the faces by removing the resinous material in the exposed wood. Once the faces became dry the turpentine beetles bored into the heart of the trunk and decay followed. Strong winds broke off many of the injured trees. The annual fires that burned the grasses of the range also affected reproduction and small trees. 

Depending upon the intensity of burning, these fires must have killed many small trees outright or badly stunted their growth. After 40 or 

50 years of logging, turpentining, and burning, it is remarkable that Washington County has even its present remnant of a forest resource. 

Certainly the present forest represents no constructive effort toward growing a new crop of trees. 

Virgin timber has vanished entirely from the forest land. The present stand consists of remnant growth and second growth trees. 

By "remnant growth" is meant the trees which were once the suppressed components of the original forest. Upon the removal of the dominants these trees, formerly suppressed, have increased their rate of growth and are now usually the dominant trees in the forest and act as seed trees. By "second growth" is meant the trees which have grown from natural seeding in the last 40 years, generally with better light conditions throughout their life period. There is a great deficiency of second growth timber. A few areas which have escaped severe fires are well stocked with young timber, but the majority of the second growth stands is thinly stocked. Over many large areas there is no young timber to grow into turpentine trees or lumber. The present forest that has grown in spite of many handicaps is no measure of what the forest land of the county has produced or can produce. 

The present forest is estimated at about 84 per cent of the original forest acreage.

The small reduction in area is in no way proportionate to the great decrease in the quality and quantity of the timber resource.

The present forest types in the county are the results of both natural factors and the influence of man. The clay soils of the red hills is the natural site for the loblolly pine and hardwoods type. On the dry ridges and sand hills we find the longleaf pine the species best adapted for growth. In the swamps and stream bottoms site conditions favor the growth of cypress and hardwoods. Throughout the flat lands there are conditions of soil and moisture which are conducive to the growth and regeneration of slash pine; however, at present these lands generally support longleaf pine and the slash pine is usually confined to the wetter parts of these areas. The restriction of slash to the "ponds" or depressions is directly attributed to fire. Slash pine is easily killed by fire, especially when it is young. Longleaf is far more resistant to fire than slash pine. As a result of this difference of the two species in resistance to fire, the slash pine is usually confined to the wet places where fire seldom burns, and longleaf is found throughout the flatlands where the soil is well drained. In a few places in the county where the land has not been burned over repeatedly slash pine is found growing on land that is high and well drained. In one instance slash pine was found to be growing well in a stand with longleaf on a dry ridge-a typical longleaf site. According to the owner this tract had not been burned over for 16 years.

FOREST INDUSTRIES AND OUTPUT LUMBER INDUSTRY 

There have been logging operations in Washington County for about 50 years. Shortly after 1910 a decline in lumber production set in. In 1929 about 20.5 million feet of lumber were manufactured largely from second-growth loblolly pine marketed as "shortleaf" pine. Stumpage of both longleaf and loblolly are becoming scarce and production must fall rapidly.

Longleaf no longer exists in the county in virgin stands. Most of the longleaf saw timber that remains consists of scattered remnants not merchantable in the first logging. These have since been worked for turpentine and are now salvaged for lumber. Stands of 2,000 feet per acre of longleaf are regarded as above average. The turpentine faces are usually butted off in the woods. The trees generally yield one or two twelve-foot logs and are not cut closer than 8 inches in the top. Many of the smaller trees are fit only for ties, staves, pulp or fuel wood.

Loblolly is limited in its occurrence to small tracts. On very small areas virgin stands have been known to cut 30,000 board feet per acre. 

Uncut old-field stands of 10,000 feet per acre may still be found but due to the restricted occurrence of loblolly in the county and the fact that most of the type has been cut over, the remaining stand is not large. 

The timber of the old-field stands is often rather knotty and utilization is not extremely close.

One factor in preventing very close utilization is the freight rate on lumber which varies from $0.40 per hundred pounds to Chicago, $0.45 to Detroit, to $0.50 to New York. Local mill men figure 2,100 lbs. for the shipping weight of 1 inch dry surfaced boards, 2,200 lbs. for shiplap and 2,400 lbs. for dimension lumber. Freight rates to northern markets may be put at $8.40 to $12.00 per thousand for these grades. With a rate of $0.32 per hundred from northern Mississippi the stumpage in Florida must be lower, or grades taken out of the woods a little higher, or both. 

As a matter of fact, in 1929 loblolly second-growth stumpage brought about $2.75 per thousand feet lumber tally, while the longleaf, mostly undersized remnants of the virgin forest, sold for $5.00 on the stump.

There is one large band mill of 150,000 board feet per day capacity on the edge of the county, but this mill secures most of its logs from adjoining counties. It cuts both hardwood and pine and estimated its hardwood cut of logs from Washington County at 4 million feet in 1929. 

The portable sawmill and central planer or concentration yard appears to be the combination best fitted to pick up the scattered pine. 

There are three such combinations in the county handling a total of about 15 million board feet in 1929. Usually the planing mill owner has 5 to 8 portable sawmills to supply the yard with rough lumber. All of the portables cut loblolly or "shortleaf", with varying quantities of longleaf that is found available.

In some cases the small mill operator working by contract, logs, saws, and piles at the mill rough lumber at $8.00 to $8.50 per thousand board feet lumber tally if he owns the equipment, or $7.00 to $7.50 if

owned by the planing mill owner. These figures apply to cutting timber that is predominantly loblolly pine. 

For six mills and one planer the following costs were found typical for 1929 for loblolly pine: felling the trees, bucking them up into logs, and hauling the logs to the portable mill cost the operator about $3.70, including supervision, labor, expense, and interest and depreciation on logging equipment. NAVAL STORES 

Working just ahead of the sawmills the naval stores industry became the second most important industry in 1890 in Washington County. At that time lumbering was particularly active in the virgin longleaf pine and maintained its major position among the industries until 1920.

Realization value represents stumpage plus profit- or a maximum stumpage value.

Operating in 90 to 95 per cent longleaf the naval stores industry of Washington County reached its peak in 1905. Between 10,000 and 

12,000 units were produced at that time. This county was then conceded to be the principal inland shipping point of naval stores in the South.

Inroads of the sawmills were felt by 1910 and production was considerably reduced. The change in turpentine practice from the old system of cutting "boxes" into the base of the tree to the cup and gutter method was general and wide-spread but even its increased yield did not compensate for the reduction in number of working trees at that time.

A small percentage of suppressed virgin trees are still being worked. The bulk of the present production comes, however, from remnant longleaf (trees over 40 years old with early growth checked by the virgin overwood) and a small percentage of slash pine located around "branches" and wet flats.

Now the naval stores industry has dropped into third place, agriculture and lumbering both having a higher place. Its present output is only one-third of its peak production and it is still declining, due to the deficient younger age classes in the round timber. 

WASHINGTON COUNTY NAVAL STORES OUTPUT IN 1929 

Washington County, working principally longleaf pine, is one of the principal naval stores producers in western Florida. In 1930 a complete personal canvass showed that 12 operators (including 3 out of the county but working timber in the county) produced 3,176 barrels of spirits and 10,587 barrels of rosin (3 1-3 of rosin to 1 of spirits), from 95 crops of cups, or an average of 33 units per crop. 

A small quantity of gum is produced by farmers or renters (men who chip the trees and put the gum into the barrel) who are paid for the amount of gum they collect. These men are generally supervised by the woods riders of the operators so that their work is regularly and well done to secure the maximum yield. The production from this type of labor is very small in Washington County, totaling about 100 barrels of gum for the year. They are paid by the barrel (50 gallons) for the raw gum at a price of ten times the current market value for one gallon of turpentine.

The longleaf pine land in the county, excluding 100,000 acres of the sand hill type in the southern half of the county not restocking but including that too young for working, too scattered or worked out and burned out, totals 193,700 acres and shows an average of about 4.9 cups per acre.

The estimated area of 52,000 acres of pine land being worked for timber was secured by dividing 18.2 cups found per acre on the sample strips into the 950,000 cups reported hung in the county. The sample strips showed a minimum of 7 cups and a maximum of 50 cups per acre. Five operators working 51 crops showed 21.7 cups per acre, but their lands were perhaps a little better than average. 

Although the average diameter of trees worked is 10.8 inches, it appears that this diameter must fall, as the larger remnant trees of the virgin forest are being rapidly worked out.

METHOD AND COST OF PRODUCTION 

Detailed methods and costs of producing turpentine for the year 1929 were obtained from four operators. Typical procedure and average costs are as follows:

HANGING CUPS 

In December and January the cups and aprons (or gutters) are hung on the trees. The gutters of galvanized iron are either set in two broad axe cuts or nailed in hack grooves sloping downward towards each other. The cups (of clay or galvanized iron) are hung on a nail below the V point of the gutter. With the broad trough cups often a single curved apron is used instead of two gutters. 

This work is done either by the day or by piece work, the latter being more common. Hanging cups and tins costs about $9.75 per thousand in Washington County. One crew of 6 men (total wages $9.50) can hang 1,000 cups and tins per day. A 25 cent allowance is ⚫ made for distributing the cups and tins by the teamster and helpers. 

These costs vary from as low as $8.00 per thousand up to $11.00 per thousand. 1 

Rectangular galvanized cups cost from $37.00 to $45.00 per thousand. A long period of service up to ten years is claimed by most operators who use the more expensive cup. Clay cups cost less than the galvanized cups but, because of breakage through "freezes" and ordinary wear and tear, are losing favor. 

Cups and aprons remain in place generally for two years, when they are raised. This cost of raising is generally less than the initial hanging cost as the cups are on the ground and the faces already made. 

This raising cost varies from $6.00 to $7.50 per thousand cups. 

The cost of one hanging and one raising together may be prorated over 4 years to arrive at the annual cost of hanging and raising cups. 

The cost of cups is generally depreciated over 10 years in Washington County, only one operator claiming but 7 years of service from cups. 

CHIPPING, DIPPING AND SCRAPING 

As soon as the weather warms up in late winter or early spring the face is chipped above the aprons or gutters. This back cut goes up the tree approximately one-half to three-quarters of an inch and into the wood about the same amount. The chipping is repeated in early spring as the weather dictates and once a week thereafter till late fall. Ordinarily 30 to 32 streaks per season are common working practice, making a one-year working face from 16 inches to 24 inches high. The cost of chipping 1,000 trees is $1.00 to $1.25. One man generally works about 6,500 faces per week. 

After 4 to 6 streaks are put on the faces the gum is dipped from the cups into a dip bucket and carried to a dip barrel placed nearby. 

This dipping cost per 50-gallon barrel is between $0.75 and $1.00. 

A certain portion of the gum running down the face towards the cup hardens into "scrape". Longleaf pine gum produces more scrape than does slash pine gum. At the end of the year when chipping stops, owing to cool weather, this hardened gum is scraped off into wooden scrape boxes and packed into barrels. A three hundred pound barrel

of scrape brings the worker about $1.00 for his labor. Occasionally the scrape collecting just above the aprons is "punched off" and included in the dipping throughout the season.

Gum produced by farmers or "renters" is bought at a price per barrel of 10 times the market value of one gallon of spirits of turpentine, around $5.00 per 50-gallon barrel in 1929. Occasionally a little higher price is paid for gum. Scrape, on the other hand, brings less because it yields a smaller amount of spirits. This figure generally runs between $3.00 to $3.50 per 50-gallon barrel but is also affected by the market price of spirits.

HAULING 

Hauling of the gum and scrape from the woods to the still is done by truck or wagon. In the more inaccessible places the wagon hauls out to a central, better located point and from there it is trucked to the still. The total hauling cost generally amounts to about 50 cents per barrel applying to both gum and scrape. Occasionally a longer haul will bring up the cost to 75 cents per barrel. One operator, by using his dippers to haul in the gum, lowered the hauling charge to 5 cents per barrel (not including feed for horses or depreciation on the wagons in this figure). Some of the more remote stills have an additional charge of hauling the spirits and rosin to the railroad. 

STILLING 

In the 4 stills studied the usual charge is nine 50-gallon barrels of gum (stills of 25 barrel "capacity"). The charge is cooked for about 3 hours and produces about 100 gallons of spirits and 5 2/3 to 6 barrels of rosin (500 lbs. gross each).

A charge of fourteen 300-lbs. barrels of scrape yields about 65 gallons of spirits and 7 barrels of rosin, in round figures. There are about 4 barrels of gum to 1 1/3 of scrape used in producing 1 "unit" of product. The above yields approximate the generally accepted ratio of 1 barrel of spirits to 3 1/3 round barrels of rosin.

In Washington County the majority of the operators, especially the larger ones, keep their woods hands in the quarters about the still place. These quarters are generally two-room cabins built at a cost of from $175 to $200 each. This is allowed for in the full term of depreciation, however.

COST OF STILL 

All stills in the county are of the direct heat type with the retort or kettle directly over the fire box. A 25-barrel still would cost-fire box, retort, worm, tank with platform, and shed-around $1,500 installed. However, it is customary to rebuild the fire box and repair the kettle over a period of 10 to 20 years, when it is generally renewed. 

In the case of small operators, such as a 10-crop place, cheaper equipment or second hand equipment reduces this cost considerably, but this is allowed for by taking the full depreciation period of new equipment of 10 and 20 years.

MAINTENANCE 

Regular annual or periodic repairs are necessary in a turpentine operation to keep the equipment operating before it is worked out and rendered useless. Some equipment needs replacements such as new rosin vats and platforms. These total up to approximately $310.00 per year for a 10-crop place.

The kettle and fire box need frequent caulking and rebuilding. 

Some operators rebuild the fire box every year, using the old brick over and only fresh lime and sand being the added cost. The kettle or retort, which is made of copper, is very easily damaged and must be perfectly tight. This is repaired or recaulked frequently. These costs amount to approximately $50 per year.

The cabin buildings and commissary are frequently patched up to give long life in service. These generally last, with a little care, about 

20 years.

Such items as horses, cars, trucks, wagons, etc., are depreciated in a certain period of years under supervision, hauling, and depreciation, so are not included in maintenance. Tools for chipping, pulling and dipping (these are the hack, pullers and dip paddle) generally total about $10 per year per crop. Three whetstones and a cutter for sharpening are included in this figure. 

In a larger operation than the 10-crop place better and more expensive equipment is generally installed. This higher cost is lowered, however, by the increase in number of crops, reducing the cost per crop to as low or a lower figure than the small operation. 

TIMBER LEASES 

Practices of the naval stores industry in Washington County are 

1. Leasing to the turpentine operator by the lot for a cash payment.

2. Leasing to the turpentine operator by the 1,000 cups for cash payment. 

3. Working the timber and receiving pay for the dipping and chipping of the amount of gum "put in the barrel." 

4. Working the timber of the owner, using the equipment furnished by the owner and paying a percentage of the spirits and rosin produced. 

5. Operator working partnership-owned timber. 

Method 1-leasing by the lot-usually puts the advantage of superior knowledge in bargaining in the hands of the operator. This is quite common in the county, pertaining mostly to small numbers of cups. 

Method 2-leasing by the thousand cups-is the most common form of lease. Lease payments vary in current practice from $100 to $200 for a 4-year lease for 1,000 cups. For fifth, sixth, and seventh year working a straight price of $15 to $20 per thousand per year is paid.

The size of timber, the number of virgin (first year cups) cups possible, the percentage of second, third and fourth year working and the number of pulling faces, the proportion of back-faced trees, as well as the general vigor of the trees, all influence the price paid. The present average payments for timber leased in all stages of working in the county is $100 for 1,000 cups for 4 years. This price is affected considerably by the large percentage of third, fourth, fifth, and sixth year faces. This shows an average annual cost of $250 per crop of 10,000 faces for turpentine lease. 

Method 3—the small timber owner comes to an agreement with a nearby operator who pays him an agreed price per barrel for the gum which he produces. This price generally is figured per barrel of gum at 10 times the current market price of 1 gallon of turpentine. The 

1929 figure varied around $5.00 to $7.00 per barrel. The owner (or his helper) chips the trees and dips the gum under the supervision of the naval stores operator. He is paid for hanging the cups and tins or this is handled by the regular crew of the operator. Only a small amount of gum is produced in this fashion, roughly 100 barrels and the corresponding scrape (50 barrels).

Method 4-a small operation managed and worked by an operator for the owner. The owner furnished all cups, tins, hauling and stilling equipment, and required 25 per cent of the gross production of spirits and rosin. The operator supervised the work and paid the labor. This operator's output was only slightly over 5 per cent of the county production. 

Method 5-represents a company or partnership proposition. A fixed amount is paid to the company in leases and the manager—a partner in the business-is allowed a salary from the company. He also derives that part of the profits of the business according to the proportion owned. About one-third of the county production comes under this classification.

1929 A RELATIVELY POOR YEAR 

Although the manufacturing summary shows a profit of $8.45 (above 2.7¹ cents per cup lease) per unit including all costs from tree to market in 1929, the profits previous to this year were considerably more. The reduction in working area and number of trees per acre both reflect in the low county output. These conditions have affected the gross receipts of all operators.

In past years labor costs were higher and a greater investment was tied up in the plant and equipment, yet the unit output and the total number of crops were both much larger. More output and higher prices brought in higher net returns to the operators. Now, with a large percentage of the plant depreciated, profits are still possible but only with depressed wages and timber lease payments. 

The general trend of the turpentine industry in Washington County is on the down grade. The low timber lease return received (average 2.7 cents per cup per year) with only 18.2 cups per acre (for working timber) brings to the owner a poor realization on his land of about 49.1 cents per acre per year, of which taxes alone take almost the half. If this is spread over the entire forest area of the county, and income from timber and grazing added, it shows a very low net income which is still decreasing. (See Table 13).

With present methods of turpentining, the trees which were working in 1929 and the round trees that are 8 inches in diameter or over will soon be worked out. Approximately one-half of the working trees will be depleted by 1933. Upon the round trees, 6 inches in diameter or smaller, the turpentine interests must depend for their working trees in the future. With the present rate of growth, it will take 14 years for the 6-inch trees to reach 8 inches; 24 years for the 4-inch trees to become 8 inches; and 31 years for the 2-inch trees to become 8-inch trees. In actual occurrence trees do not reach a higher diameter class in a solid group, for some trees grow faster than others. For instance, some of the 6-inch trees will be 8 inches in less than 14 years and some will require more than 14 years. 

The influence of trees 6 inches in diameter in 1929 will be felt in the turpentine stand as early as 1941. By 1937 the number of workable trees will have diminished to 7.14 per acre: less than one-third the number in 1929. COSTS AND RETURNS OF FOREST MANAGEMENT 

The principal costs involved in managing forest lands for forest products, naval stores, lumber, pulpwood, staves, poles, box-boards, etc., are: (1) Taxes. (2) Protection. (3) Cultural Costs-Planting, thinning, and possibly pruning for naval stores trees widely spaced. (4) Supervision.

For farm woodlands the most important cost is taxes. Protection and supervision are nominal on the farm. Cultural costs are limited to needed planting and perhaps some thinning. At present none of these costs are incurred by farmers except the one of taxes. With the average depleted forest income for the county put at $0.33 per acre and year (before taxes) and at that limited to the better two-thirds of the forest land, it is evident that many areas are not even earning the taxes. 

Farmers are carrying this land in many cases, paying the taxes from agricultural crops. The reason that forest lands are not earning their taxes and much more is because they are inadequately stocked. The average stand for the working longleaf forest (Table 8) for the best 52,000 acres is but about .2 normal. The average stocking for all longleaf pine land is very much less. 

For commercial forest lands fire protection becomes an important item. The naval stores interests in Washington County are paying about $41.63 per crop in protecting their working trees, mainly by raking around them. With 18.22 cups per acre on operated land this represents a cost of between 7 and 8 cents per acre. It does not protect young trees. With the present state and national cooperative payment of 2 cents per acre within organized protection units, the State Forester's office feels that adequate fire line protection can be had in working naval stores forests at a cost not above that of raking. For forest land

too young for naval stores operation a protection expenditure of 10 cents per acre annually should be included. 

For a 10-crop place of about 5,000 acres an estimate¹6 has been made for fire line construction around small 40-acre blocks of $0.22 per acre for first cost and $0.08 per acre annually for maintenance. 

With governmental cooperation of $0.02 and distributing the first cost over a 10-year period, the cost is seen to be in the neighborhood of $0.10. 

Increasing the size of the blocks would decrease the cost per acre. 

Planting southern pine on a large forest operation in Louisiana was accomplished in 1930 at the following costs: The 1-year trees were set about 6x8 feet apart or 900 per acre, since there is a pulpwood market for thinnings. Labor and trees cost $2.62, overhead $0.50 and fencing $0.70 or $3.82 per acre. Somewhat higher costs were assumed in the planting calculations following.

When naval stores is the principal crop aimed at and no thinnings market is in sight, the planting of only 200 to 300 trees of slash or longleaf pines might well be considered. 

Thinnings16 in natural young stands of naval stores pines should be done at an early age. Extensive experience costs are not available though $0.50 per acre might be a fair estimate for spacing wider than 

8x10 feet over considerable areas where the saplings are not over 10 feet high and are too dense only on a portion of the area. It is altogether possible that for naval stores, costs of thinning very dense natural stands may reach the cost of planting a factor decreasing the financial advantage of natural seeding in few localities. 

Supervision for large forest areas may be spread partly over utilization costs, partly to protection and partly to planting. The remaining cost should not exceed 5 cents per acre.

Returns from managed forests of proper density will greatly exceed those from the present generally decimated accidental stands. 

Forest management has not yet proceeded long enough in southern pine in Florida to cite the larger property incomes produced by management, although a number of properties are now under management. 

Only when their young stands reach merchantability will it be possible to supplement predictions based on yield tables.17

It might be noted in passing that the present county and state tax on forest land of $.22 per acre would take almost 37 per cent of the net ($.59) land income before taxes. While 70 years sounds like a long development period, the figures indicate that each stand begins to show an income at 40 years and before 50 years enough land is working to carry from income all current costs including interest. From 50 years the net income continues to rise to the maximum of $3.23 per acre at 70 years, when the property is established and is producing the same income (prices remaining the same) annually. CAPITAL IS REQUIRED 

The chief practical difficulty in Washington County is that few properties contain enough working timber to meet expenses until very recent reproduction reaches profitable working age at about 40 years (for Washington County). In case of planting it may be shown to require $26.58 per acre to pay taxes alone on one acre for 40 years at the present rate ($0.22 per acre per year). Planting only half of the land would add $6.34 to the cost.

A farmer owning his own land would, therefore, have to lay out in the first 40 years from $25 to $35 per acre in taxes and planting, less any income timber already on the ground might produce. A tax readjustment may reduce this. It should be noted that many farmers are now paying this tax on forest land from farm income, but through failing to build up their forest stand they will not get it back from the forest products. Some few forest property owners in the county, who have seen the importance of young timber in the forest, have acquired working timber with enough young growth to keep the property going during the "building-up period," thus securing a stand of young trees without planting costs.

The present average net forest income of the county, reduced for depletion of capital, is shown above to be $0.33 per acre per year, or, deducting $0.22 for taxes, $0.11 tax free. A comparison of $0.11 with $3.23 from a fully stocked forest managed for naval stores shows the margin of income for improvement that exists in Washington County. 

THE SAND HILL PROBLEM 

Difficult as the rehabilitation of the better longleaf pine areas is on the better soils of the county, the rehabilitation of about 100,000 of the 150,000 acres of sand lands becomes prohibitive for the average private owner. The site quality of this land is not over 50 feet and the growth is a little slower than on the heavier soils. This would lengthen the time to the first gum yields for longleaf probably about 5 years, and also add 10 to 20 years to the final timber rotation. 

Also, a much larger part of the area must be planted to secure a satisfactory growing stock in a reasonable time. Probably extensive experiments with slash pine on these lands should be made at an early date' in an attempt to reduce the rotation period. All these factors indicate this area as difficult for private capital. 

It should be noted, however, that a paper mill has been built at Panama City, within 35 miles of all parts of this area. If slash pine should be demonstrated as successful on these lands, such a close market for pulpwood may alter their economic situation. Artificial reforestation may show a satisfactory return on a pulpwood rotation coupled with naval stores production even for private capital. For this reason an early experiment with slash pine is very desirable.

In order to get a picture of the property tax relation to the financial situation of forest lands in Washington County, it is necessary to examine the general property tax burden. Particularly we should see (a) what the actual tax per acre is, and (b) whether the forest property tax bears heavier on this form of property, compared to sale value, than other forms of property, and also (c) how important a source of income the annual forest land tax is and the problems involved in changing this tax if found desirable.

IMPORTANCE OF THE FOREST PROPERTY TAX TO THE COUNTY 

The suburban real estate acreage taxed was placed at 319,161 acres in 1929. According to the census of agriculture 42,862 acres were farm land exclusive of farm woodland in 1925. Although these figures do not apply to the same year they may be used to approximate the forest land acreage on the tax rolls as around 276 thousand acres. Applying the $0.22 per acre forest land tax would indicate the total forest property tax as about 60 or 61 thousand dollars. This represents between 

40 and 50 per cent of the total property tax for the county and between 30 and 40 per cent of the total county income, and points to an important problem in maintaining the revenue of the county in case of a state forest tax revision.

On the other hand it also emphasizes the importance to the county of finding some way of keeping this property in private hands and on the tax rolls as a very important element in the tax base. 

TAX DELINQUENCY AND LAND ABANDONMENT 

State-owned tax delinquent land increased in Washington County from 7 thousand acres in 1920 to 21 thousand in 1923. It remained stationary from 1923 to 1927 but rose rapidly to 53 thousand acres in 

1928 and almost 70 thousand in 1929.

Comparing acreage assessed with total county area, there was unaccounted for in 1929, 75,000 acres in the county. The taxing authorities stated there were in the hands of the state 69,978 acres as of January 1, 1929, but that they thought this had been reduced somewhat by May, 1930. If the area has been reduced there must be some acreage that has escaped the tax records. A map check of this situation has been begun, but its results will not be known for some little time. The location of this tax delinquent land is generally in the deep sand area in the southern part of the county. Although 75,000 acres are off the tax rolls, some of this land that is still on the rolls is delinquent, but it has not yet been written off as "state tax" land. A small part of it has enough scattered advance growth timber so that it is being held by private owners for current turpentine operation or logging, and another small part has enough young growth so that it is believed that it would interest private ownership with the proposed reduced taxes for 1930 ($1.00 assessment at 50 mills or about $0.05 taxes per acre for state and county taxes-outside of school and road districts). The larger part of the 75,000 acres will remain a government liability, county, state or nation. 

Private ownership of these deep sand lands may never be inviting. 

Neither does it appear probable that the county, burdened as it is with tax delinquent lands, is in a position to hold and protect this area as a county forest. It appears, therefore, that the State or Federal Governments are the only agencies that can consider the acquisition, holding, protection and management of these lands as State or National Forests until they are again restocked with trees. Thus far the State has not adopted a policy with reference to the reforestation of tax delinquent lands. The problem is one that should be given careful consideration when the State forestry program is being formulated.

In summing up the forest tax situation in Washington County the data show that the average forest property tax is about $0.22 per acre per year. The forest lands are assessed at a much higher ratio to sale value than agricultural lands. An equalization of taxes within the county based on sale values would reduce the forest property tax to 12 or 15 cents an acre. The forest property tax forms between 30 and 40 per cent of the total county income in Washington County which points out the large problem of maintaining local income in forest tax adjustment, and also the importance of keeping the large forest property item in the tax base by reasonable taxation. Tax reform is a state-wide problem and the state should be able to work out a better forest tax plan than the present one, which will permit forest property to carry its fair share of taxation without further extending the serious tax delinquency of this class of property.

SUMMARY WITH SUGGESTIONS FOR FINANCIAL BETTERMENT PRESENT FOREST INCOME UNSATISFACTORY 

In Table 20 a forest income from all sources for the county of $0.72 per acre for the year 1929 is indicated. This excludes 100,000 acres of the sand hill land as outside the category of productive forest land for the present. This income must be reduced for the depletion of timber capital still going on through excessive cutting, turpentining, hog grazing, and uncontrolled burning. There is indicated after depletion and before taxes a forest income of but $0.33 per acre including a grazing value which often goes to others than the forest owner. 

Reports of five naval stores operators working 23,500 acres of pine forest showed an appraised forest income from naval stores of $0.81 per acre for 1929. Assuming the county naval stores timber depletion figure of 50 per cent, this income falls to $0.405. 

Individual areas of 20 to 40 acres each showed optimum naval stores incomes by working 30 to 50 cups per acre at appraised values of $1.12 to $1.87 before depletion. Few of these optimum tracts have sufficient young trees to avoid a large depletion deduction. 

There is almost no planning of a continuous succession of tree classes for turpentine operation. Likewise there are no property owners planning for a continuous series of timber cuttings for lumber or staves. The future is left to chance, and annual "light burning" of turpentine lands and grazing lands continues except in the southwestern part of the county where the organization of a state fire control unit is beginning constructive work. 

MEASURES NECESSARY FOR FINANCIAL BETTERMENT 

The following measures are necessary for financial betterment of the forest income in Washington County: (1) The stand of young trees must be increased in number and in uniform distribution over the forest properties. This seems almost trite to state, yet it is this very absence of sufficient trees and the uneven grouping of the accidental growth in the limited areas where trees have come up that prevents a larger income from the new forest. 

The forest land owners have not been taught either the importance of fully stocked forest land for a satisfactory forest income, or the methods to secure uniform reproduction either by natural seeding, or by planting, or both. The 52,000 acres of turpentined forest-the best of the longleaf pine land in the county-are but .22 normal in stocking for timber yields and probably not much over this for turpentine orcharding, since the deficient stand is often crowded into small areas and lacks uniform spacing for proper crown development. The other forest land is even more thinly stocked. The first and most important step for farm woodland owner, naval stores operator, or "shortleaf" timber grower to improve his pine forest income is to get his land fully and uniformly stocked21 with pine trees. This means primarily protection from hogs and uncontrolled fires.

THE POSITION OF THE FARM FOREST LAND OWNER 

The farmer appears to be in the best position financially to rehabilitate his forest land. In the primary step required-that of getting a sufficient set of trees-he can more easily give the proper care to his property and even plant up his acreage, than the large land owner. He often has sufficient labor available, that would otherwise go unused, to reduce the cash investment to a minimum in improving his woodland. 

His taxes he provides for in any case. He has a forest income from fuel, grazing, and extra winter woods labor in addition to that from naval stores leases or logs and pulpwood sold. With the recent establishment of a paper mill just south of Washington County the market for small sized wood will eventually be strengthened. The situation for the farm woodland owner who will plan ahead and cover his land uniformly with growing pine trees promises well for the future. This big factor of income production is in his own hands. The factor of improved naval stores practice in size of trees worked and the conservative method of working is also in his hands. 

THE POSITION OF THE TURPENTINE OPERATOR 

In order to keep in business the turpentine operator may have to increase his own land holdings, and, in cooperation with the state, build up his stand of trees by protection and perhaps by planting. By selecting lands with the best volunteer young growth and with proper forest protection and management measures, he may in many cases be able to set up a continuous business. 

Timber growing in the longleaf areas of the county appears hopeful at present only as it can be accomplished on the farm woodlands or as an adjunct product in naval stores operation, particularly for short rotation pulpwood.

For pulpwood and lumber production alone, the loblolly or "shortleaf" pine holds better financial prospects. Selective cutting should place these stands on a short rotation basis. The limited extent of the type in the county will hold the management of loblolly to small areas, generally in farmer hands.

Finally, in the not far distant future Florida may expect to become a lumber importing state. With an expected increase in the wood barrel and fruit box requirements of the state, the advent of the wood pulp industry and the naval stores industry, all making heavy inroads on the small-sized second growth pine, the growing of large-sized trees for lumber should be stimulated by gradually rising prices over a considerable period of years.
