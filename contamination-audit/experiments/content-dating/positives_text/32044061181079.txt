President's Letter of Transmittal To the Congress of the United States: 

Transmitted herewith is the Fifteenth Semiannual Report on the operations of the Mutual Security Program for the period ending December 31, 1958. The report was prepared by the Coordinator of the Mutual Security Program, with participation by the Department of State (including the International Cooperation Administration), the Department of Defense and the Development Loan Fund. 

The essence of our experience during the seven and one-half years of the program on which reports have been transmitted to the Congress, is that the Mutual Security system is vital to our national security. If we do not persist in the continuous and vigorous application of military, economic, and technical programs making up the Mutual Security system, our foreign policy will be rendered ineffective. This ultimately cannot fail to have the most serious consequences for every living American. 

If we fail to win the contest in which we are now engaged, the potential destruction of our way of life becomes, by the degree of our failure, more and more a dangerous reality. It behooves us all to keep this peril fresh in our minds. 

I trust that this report will provide a yardstick to evaluate the measures we and other members of the great community of nations are taking in the Mutual Security Program to defend our freedoms.

Chapter I General Operations of the Mutual Security Program Purpose of the Program 

In the crisis of Quemoy and Matsu, the Middle East from Lebanon and Jordan to Iran, and Latin America, the Mutual Security Program (MSP) during the last 6 months of 1958 once again proved to be a vital and flexible instrument of U.S. foreign policy. Through timely combinations of military and economic assistance, MSP met the emergency needs of these three areas, just as it did the more 

1 predictable but nonetheless important requirements of all the 63 na- 2 tions and 10 territories receiving some form of U.S. MSP assistance ? during the first half of fiscal year 1959.

The Departments of State (including the International Coopera tion Administration) and Defense and the Development Loan Fund, the agencies charged with meeting these assistance needs, have con- 4 sistently sought to correlate their MSP operations with the political, 4 military, economic, and humanitarian motives of U.S. foreign policy expressed in the Mutual Security Act of 1954, as amended. 

These foreign policy objectives, as related to military programs, have included the furnishing of equipment, other materiel, and training to friendly nations and international organizations in a concerted effort to "promote the foreign policy, security, and general welfare of the United States and to facilitate the effective participation of such nations in arrangements for individual and collective self-defense." 

5 The instruments for achieving these objectives have included the provision of economic assistance in the form of defense support to certain nations receiving military assistance; technical cooperation designed to assist the efforts of the peoples of economically underdeveloped areas to develop their resources and improve their technical knowledge and skills, and other forms of assistance furnished under conditions specified by the President and designed to maintain or promote political or economic stability. 1

They have also included the newer type of economic assistance provided through the Development Loan Fund, which is designed "to strengthen friendly foreign countries by encouraging the development of their economies through a competitive free enterprise system; to minimize or eliminate barriers to the flow of private investment capital and international trade; to facilitate the creation of a climate favorable to the investment of private capital; and to assist, on a basis of self-help and mutual cooperation, the efforts of free peoples to develop their economic resources and to increase their productive capabilities." 

One index of America's progress through the Mutual Security Program in fulfilling these purposes has been the close attention paid by Sino-Soviet bloc leaders to U.S. assistance efforts. Keenly aware of the opportunities for economic penetration offered by intensified aspirations of the peoples of the less developed nations, Communist bloc leaders in 1958 alone extended credits totaling over $1 billion to 18 nations in Asia, Africa, and Latin America, bringing their cumulative total to $2.4 billion. The number of bloc technicians supplied to 18 countries of Asia, Africa, and Latin America rose from 

1,600 in 1957 to 2,800 at the end of 1958.

Economic grants and credits, together with trade offers under bilateral trade agreements, have not been the only means used in this new offensive; they have been accompanied by substantial military programs in several countries in Africa and Asia, skillful propaganda, extensive participation in trade fairs, the subsidization of dealers in bloc goods, and commercial activities by bloc Embassies. 

However, these bloc activities, important as they are, have not provided the sole justification for the Mutual Security Program. Even if the Communist bloc were suddenly to disappear, the need would remain for assistance to less developed and newly emerging countries to help them make the transition to more modern economies. 

Instruments of the Program

Within this amount, programs were approved early in the first half of the fiscal year for 38 countries, most of which are allied with the United States in NATO, SEATO, and other collective security arrangements. This system of military alliances, supported by military assistance and defense support, has developed a strength of 5,800,000 men, with the United States providing only one-sixth of the total; 72,000 aircraft, of which the U.S. portion accounts for

Training programs varied substantially, concentrating for example in NATO countries on support of modern weapons including IRBM's, air defense missile systems, and nuclear-capable aircraft and in some less developed countries on rudimentary tactics and logistic support. One category of economic assistance was particularly closely related to this mutual defense effort. This was defense support, for which 

Congress appropriated $750 million in fiscal year 1959. It is economic assistance required in addition to military assistance to secure a specific contribution to the common defense by other countries in which the United States is helping to support significant military forces. There are 12 of these countries which together maintain forces of over 3 million men and make vital bases and other facilities available for free world defense: Korea, the Republic of China, Viet- 

Nam, the Philippines, Laos, Cambodia, Thailand, Pakistan, Iran, Turkey, Greece, and Spain.

Defense support is administered by the International Cooperation 

Administration. It was again programed in fiscal year 1959 to secure a specific contribution to the common defense by countries in which U.S. military aid is helping to support significant military forces.

The achievement of this objective in fiscal year 1959 as in previous years requires a variety of assistance techniques, including the financ ing of imports of food, industrial raw materials, and other commodi ties; the making of cash grants in a few instances; and project assist ance, notably in some countries in the Far East. 

Most defense support programs were designed to utilize the import technique, that is, commodities were selected for import, generally by private importers, into the countries concerned and were sold for local currencies, with the United States paying for the imports in dollars and the local government putting up the equivalent in local currencies which would be put into special "counterpart” accounts, owned by the local government but to be used by agreement with the United States for selected economic uses. Up to 10 percent of such counterpart funds are reserved for U.S. use.

The bulk of these local currencies was used for such mutually agreed things as the payment and subsistence of troops and other economic expenditures of the local government such as the construction of highways, the payment of local currency costs for defense projects, highways and harbor improvement, agricultural research, and health and sanitation projects. 

A lesser amount of defense support was designed for "project aid," which is used to finance goods or services which, by advance agree ment, are directed toward individual projects, such as the construction of a specific highway, dam, or industrial plant. While these projects may indirectly make a contribution to defense, their primary purpose is to meet a specific economic need of the host country and to provide the economic base which is necessary to maintain the military effort of the countries.

Special assistance (the fiscal year 1959 programs are treated in detail in chapter IV) is economic aid that is necessary to achieve polit ical, economic, humanitarian, or other objectives of the United States in any country where the United States is not providing military assistance in support of significant military forces and may be used to secure or maintain access to military facilities for U.S. forces or to meet economic and other problems related to the existence of these facilities. It may also be used to support programs in health and education, for example the malaria eradication program and assistance to U.S.-sponsored schools abroad, which further U.S. foreign policy objectives by their humanitarian nature and contribution to economic advance. Congress appropriated $200 million in fiscal 

1959 for this purpose.

Technical cooperation is related but differs in that it is directed toward long-term economic advance in less developed nations through spreading technical knowledge and skills. Counterpart or dollar imyear

en ports financed from defense support and special assistance programs for example (and also local currency loans and grants made possible through Public Law 480, Title I, sales of surplus agricultural commodities) were frequently planned in conjunction with technical cooperation projects. Congress appropriated $150 million in fiscal year 1959 for the bilateral technical cooperation program, administered by the ICA. This program was again designed to contribute primarily to the balanced and integrated development of the human resources and capabilities of the assisted nations through training programs, demonstrations, surveys, and similar activities. 

These bilateral programs were complementary to the multilateral technical cooperation programs supported by the U.S. Government, such as those administered by the United Nations, regional organizations like the Colombo Plan, and the Organization of American States (for which Congress in fiscal year 1959 appropriated $1.5 million) and the nongovernmental activities of U.S. private institutions, organizations, and business firms. Technical cooperation was designed, for example, to facilitate lending operations by the 

Development Loan Fund and the World Bank through making pre liminary economic and technical feasibility surveys, and to establish productivity centers, local banks, and assist in the drafting of investment laws.

The operations of the Development Loan Fund (DLF), for which 

Congress appropriated $550 million in fiscal year 1959, are described in detail in chapter V. DLF provides investment capital to less developed countries on flexible terms realistically adapted to borrowers' capacities. Its sole objective is to provide capital for economic development. It does not concentrate on improving technical skills as is done by the technical cooperation program; it does not, like defense support, provide economic assistance to elicit a specific military contribution; and it is not designed, as is special assistance, specifically to promote economic and political stability but rather to promote economic growth. 

A final source of funds, the contingency fund, for which the Congress appropriated $155 million in fiscal year 1959, serves to meet essentially two types of assistance needs. The first comprises those requirements which cannot be foreseen at the time of the annual presentation of the Mutual Security Program to Congress, for example emergency assistance to Lebanon to rehabilitate industrial facilities damaged in that nation's civil strife in the summer of 1958. The second includes requirements which, at the time programs were presented to Congress, were not sufficiently definitive to justify inclusion in terms of a specific amount and kind of assistance in the executive 524977-59-

branch request. One instance of such a requirement in fiscal year 

1959, more fully discussed later, concerned the Turkish stabilization program. The economic problems faced by Turkey were readily apparent for some months prior to the initiation of negotiations in August 1958 on debt postponement and monetary and fiscal reform However, the specific requirement for MSP funds was not know until agreement was reached by all of the participating agencies including the International Monetary Fund, European creditor na tions, and the Organization for European Economic Cooperation. 

Taken together, these categories of MSP assistance, while sub stantial, comprised only a part of total free world assistance efforts in the last half of 1958. They must be considered in the larger frame of reference of other U.S. Government policies, international activi ties and third country economic assistance. 

With last year's long-term extension of the Trade Agreements Act an important step was taken in keeping our own markets open to our free world friends and in developing wider opportunities for our exports. Moreover, through the operation of General Agreement on 

Tariffs and Trade, the United States has cooperated with other coun tries to expand trade and through the International Monetary Fund (IMF) to create and maintain a sound financial basis for trade. 

During this period, ICA's investment guaranty program reached an all time level of activity in support of private investment projects and the program continued to be the principal effort to encourage the participation of private capital in the development of free world countries. Applications for guaranties increased to more than $1 billion with a majority of these in the underdeveloped countries. 

Sound economic development was assisted through U.S. efforts. described in detail in chapter VI, to foster private investment abroad to make needed capital and skills available to less developed areas. 

The United States also participates in the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD), which drew on both public and private resources from the entire free world to make loans for developmental purposes. The Export-Import Bank also contributed to the expansion of trade and economic development, in part made possible by the increase in its capital last year. 

The Agricultural Trade Development and Assistance Act of 1954 

(Public Law 480), as amended also provided valuable help to less developed countries. Selling our surplus foods and fibers for the currencies of the buying country helps provide strength and encour agement to hungry, ill-clad people. Also, a large part of the local currencies resulting from Public Law-480 transactions was made. available for loans to finance development projects in participating countries.

Development financing was also undertaken by several of our European allies, Canada, Australia, and Japan. Germany extended substantial credits to India, Turkey, and Greece. The United Kingdom made large sums available to India and extended credit to Turkey. Canada increased her Colombo Plan grants, and Japan played an increasingly important role in Asian development. As in the last several years MSP operations in Europe in fiscal year 1959 were primarily concerned with military assistance to Amer ica's NATO allies and Spain. Economic assistance was extended only to two nations, Spain and Yugoslavia. The special assistance pro gram for the latter country, which continued in 1958 to help that nation pursue a course of action independent of the Soviet bloc, is discussed in chapter IV. 

Assistance to Spain took the form of military assistance to strengthen this strategically located nation and economic assistance in both the defense support and technical cooperation categories. In view of continuing inflation, the fiscal year 1959 defense support allocation of $50 million was programed principally for surplus agricultural commodities and industrial raw materials; this U.S. assistance helped strengthen the economic base, thereby enabling the U.S.-Spanish defense projects to move forward with the construction of air and naval facilities available for U.S. use. 

The resoluteness of the governments and peoples of the NATO nations was once again tested and not found wanting. The Berlin crisis arose in November 1958, when Soviet Premier Khrushcher issued his 6-month ultimatum for changing the status of that free world outpost. The strength of the pronouncement of the NATO foreign ministers in responding to this ultimatum was a heartening manifestation of the will to resist Communist threats and determination to support the collective security systems. This response also reinforced the will of West Berliners to remain free. In this connection the modest special assistance program for Berlin, discussed in chapter IV, continued to provide tangible evidence of free world support for that exposed city.

Steady progress was achieved in equipping NATO forces with modern aircraft and missiles. In September 1958, the first Thor intermediate range ballistic missiles were delivered to the United Kingdom as grant aid under the military assistance program. On 

November 27, 1958, General Norstad reported that NATO then possessed more than 30 missile battalions of various types equipped with nuclear-capable delivery systems and would have about 100 operational missile battalions by 1963, and, also in November, announce ment was made that West Germany was to receive NIKE battalions as well.

The Middle East crisis provided another test of the effectiveness and flexibility of the MSP. The July 1958 coup d'etat in Iraq had widespread repercussions throughout the Near East and South Asia region, and adjustments were required in the military assistance program for countries in that area. Immediately after the revolution

all movement of programed material to Iraq was suspended, and the Aprograming of new training for Iraqi military personnel in the United States was halted. Iraq has since formally severed its ties with the Baghdad Pact. 

01 During the crisis in Lebanon, the United States, in addition to lodispatching combat forces to assist the Lebanese Government in suppressing armed insurrection, provided through the military assistance program a modest amount of critically needed materiel to bolster 

Lebanon's loyal forces. Emergency economic assistance was also s provided to help industries damaged during the hostilities to resume operations; the help given from special assistance to both Lebanon and Jordan is discussed in chapter IV. 

The security of the latter country was also seriously threatened by the fall of the Western-oriented Iraqi Government, as well as by the et breakup of the Arab Union. Subsequent to the steps taken by the 

United States and the United Kingdom to assist the Government of A Jordan, the Department of Defense, at the personal request of King Be Hussein, conducted a thorough study of Jordan's military establishment. In addition to recommending directly to that Government certain organizational and administrative measures it could take to enhance its defense and security forces, the survey recommended the provision of a modest amount of selected military materiel under the military assistance program.

The events in Iraq immediately subjected Iran as well to strong pressures. The United States, while continuing to provide substantial economic assistance, took immediate steps through the military assistance program to bolster her military forces with selected mate riel items. Our Military Assistance Advisory Group promptly ini tiated joint consultation with the Iranian General Staff to accelerate [and intensify the training of Iranian forces. A comprehensive plan to augment the strength of her units and to carry out certain organizational and training improvements was drafted. Implementation of the plan by Iran has been energetic and purposeful, and U.S. training personnel provided to assist her in critical areas are being effectively utilized.

The revolution in Iraq also gave Turkey cause for concern. military assistance program responded with special measures designed to improve the defensive posture of the Turkish forces. At the same time Turkey's economic problems were mounting, with inflation and an unfavorable trade balance contributing to an unstable internal economic situation, imperiling both economic development and the ability of the nation to maintain its armed forces at agreed levels. The seriousness of these problems led to consultations in Paris during the summer of 1958 between representatives of the Turkish Government

Greece, Pakistan, and other nations in the area continued to receive substantial military and economic assistance, which tended to counter the unsettling influences of the Middle East crisis. India, and Ceylon once again received economic assistance through technical cooperation projects, Public Law 480, and from other sources to facilitate their progress toward economic development. Nepal and Afghanistan continue to receive both technical cooperation and special assistance. 

In the Far East the balanced assistance program of MAP, defense support, and technical cooperation helped to achieve or maintain the military potential and economic strength which has again been shown to be necessary for the Republic of China and other countries in the area because of their exposed position along the perimeter of the 

Communist bloc.

The crisis in the Taiwan Strait in late summer of 1958 demonstrated the effectiveness of the military and economic assistance programs. 

The ability and willingness of the defenders of Quemoy and Matsu to stand up against heavy bombardment, accompanied by attempted naval interdiction and calls to surrender, could be related directly to the quality and quantity of U.S. military training and equipment assistance and to the morale engendered by steady economic progress over recent years. The outcome of the issue in the Taiwan Strait proved beyond doubt that firm resistance backed by adequate military strength in being at the scene of local aggression, when it occurs, can both turn back an aggressor and prevent the spread of brush-fire war into a major conflagration. 

The lesson of that experience is clear. Years of joint planning and cooperative effort by the Government of the Republic of China and the United States went into the training and modernization of the Chinese Nationalist forces. Their superior performance was due in large part to the materiel and training which had previously been provided to them under the military assistance program. The outcome might have been very different had we waited to come to the aid of our ally only after he had been attacked. 10

Local political unrest plagued Latin America generally and the Caribbean area in particular during the period covered by this report.

Limited military assistance was continued under the MSP to 12 Latin American nations, with emphasis on military training in the United States and third countries.

Most MSP efforts in Latin America, however, were as in previous years concentrated on fostering economic development, largely through technical cooperation programs. Many of these programs, dating back in a few instances to the early 1940's, were carried out through joint U.S.-host country servicios in health, education, agriculture, and other fields. As a result of these reductions the executive branch was only able to meet essential program requirements by drawing down military and economic assistance unexpended balances-the "pipeline," which consists of funds committed under specific U.S. Government obligations and set apart until disbursed at such time as military materiel and industrial commodity imports, for example, are finally delivered or contract services are performed. These reductions in appropriations meant that the executive branch had to reduce the rate of obligation during fiscal year 1959 for long-range procurement of goods and services and for long-range projects of all types. This process is called "living off the pipeline" and results in reduced deliveries in succeeding years. As table 3 shows, the pipeline has been reduced to a level approaching 1 year's appropriations rate, and the possibility of further reductions without critical effects on MSP operations is problematical. 

Annual military assistance expenditures in fiscal year 1957 and fiscal year 1958 have been maintained at a level of around $2.2 billion solely by "living off the pipeline." Moreover it was deemed essential to plan expenditures for fiscal year 1959 at this same level, although the seriousness and calculated risk involved in reducing the pipeline still further were recognized. Drawdowns after fiscal year 1959 can only be made at the expense of an adequate defense posture for America's allies.

Similarly, the economic assistance pipeline, excluding the Development Loan Fund, cannot be drawn down much further without seriously curbing or delaying essential assistance programs. It was drawn down from $1.9 to $1.6 billion from June 30, 1957, to June 30, 1958.

This was accomplished by obligating funds earlier in the fiscal year, processing items at a faster rate, and improving management practices. However, the possibilities of any more reductions have been virtually eliminated; on the contrary, as in the case of MAP, unexpended balances may have to be increased to assure continuity in MSP operations and to avoid gaps in delivery of essential equipment, services, and technical advice. 15

Chapter III New Provisions of the Mutual Security Act 

The Congress incorporated several new amendments in the Mutual Security Act requiring executive branch action during the first half of the fiscal year to modify the fiscal year 1959 program. 

Section 537 (f) required that detailed explanations be incorporated in the annual presentation of the Mutual Security Program to the Congress on how proposed country programs are developed. This additional supporting information was prepared and submitted during the course of the fiscal year 1960 presentation.

The Mutual Security Act of 1958 added section 517 to the act of 1954, as amended. This section, which sets up certain planning requirements as prerequisites to agreements or grants constituting obligations of the United States in excess of $100,000 for special assistance, as well as certain other forms of assistance, thus became operative during the period covered by the draft report.

Chapter IV Special Assistance 

Disturbed conditions in the Middle East, beginning with the revolution in Iraq and leading to the temporary stationing of U.S. troops in 

Lebanon and British troops in Jordan, had a major impact on the economies of the latter two countries during this period and, as a result, brought unforeseen demands on the funds available for special assistance.

As revenues fell during the 5 months of civil strife, continuing until October 15, the Lebanese Government became dangerously short of current funds. It became apparent that, without outside assistance, the Government would be unable to meet current expenses and obligations. Special assistance grants totaling $12.5 million for emergency budget support enabled the Lebanese Government to meet payroll requirements and continue its normal functions. 

To Jordan, denied access by land to sources of supply and without adequate port, storage, and rail facilities, the U.S. provided essential petroleum products, initially by air and later through the port of Aqaba, as well as additional petroleum storage and handling equipment. Meanwhile military and other government costs rose sharply while economic activities and trade suffered. 

Other developing conditions in the Near East and Africa also imposed heavy demands on special assistance funds. 

There has been sharp deterioration in Sudan's economic position in the past 2 years. The economy of the country, heavily dependent on cotton exports, was seriously strained in the fall of 1958 when, with most of the previous crop unsold, the 1958 cotton yield was 40 percent below normal. Due to the seriousness of its economic position, the 

Government asked for and received aid during 1958 from several different sources, including a $5 million equivalent drawing from the IMF. In addition the IBRD furnished assistance on railway expenditures and U.K. credit assistance totaled almost $29 million. To help meet Sudan's balance of payments crisis, as well as inaugurate the program of development and agricultural diversification, special 19

assistance for Sudan was increased this year from the planned $6 million to $19.5.

A bilateral aid agreement under the MSP signed with the Sudan earlier in 1958 was ratified by the Sudan in July, and the first projects to be undertaken jointly by the Sudanese and U.S. Governments got underway. Two major projects in transportation were started (1) assistance to the Sudanese Division of Highways, and (2) provision of diesel engines.

As a link between the African and Arab worlds it is important that Sudan maintain its independence. To assist in this task, it is im portant that the United States help the Sudanese solve their foreign exchange problems, encourage the country to broaden the base of its economy, and in general assist the country's economic development. 

Our present program is designed to accomplish these objectives.

Malaria Eradication 

During the reporting period the United States continued support for the second year of the worldwide malaria eradication program. 

This program is a cooperative effort being undertaken by more than 70 nations of the world assisted by the World Health Organization 

(WHO), the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), the United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF), and the International Cooperation Administration (ICA). Its ultimate goal is to eradi cate malaria from these nations in which 1,050,000,000 persons annually are or have recently been exposed to malaria infection. Already malaria elimination campaigns have been extended to areas in which 524,100,000 of these people reside and millions more will be protected this coming year when projects now planned are fully implemented. 

The United States, through bilateral agreements of the ICA, is directly assisting eradication efforts in 26 nations. Also the United 

States is indirectly supporting programs in some 50 additional nations through support of UNICEF and by special contributions to the malaria eradication special accounts of the WHO and PAHO. The bilateral programs for fiscal year 1959 are expected to amount to $19.6 million and the contributions to WHO and PAHO $3 million each for a total of $25.6 million in special assistance funds. 

In December 1958 it was estimated by the World Health Organization that eradication of the disease had been achieved in 13 countries or territories of the world and in significant areas of 14 others in which reside a total population of 116 million. (See table on page 24.). 

Another 26.5 million persons live in areas where disease transmission has ceased but where eradication is not yet confirmed. Together these represent 13.6 percent of the total problem. Programs are in progress protecting 381.4 million additional people (36 percent). Eradication is in the planning stage for areas containing populations of approximately 321 million.

Approximately 205 million persons still live in such areas where no eradication program exists or is planned for the immediate future. 

However, feasible means of eradication have been found for certain of these areas, and Ethiopia, the Union of South Africa, Southern 

Rhodesia, Swaziland, Southern Mozambique, Somalia, and Brazil (including the Amazon Valley) are initiating malaria eradication schemes. 

The technical problems recognized during the early planning of the ICA participation in the worldwide malaria eradication are con- 25

tinually being more clearly defined. The concern that a prolonged campaign would foster development of insecticide resistance in the mosquitoes was well founded. Additional instances of such resistance have been found during the year and certain programs have had to be revised to utilize alternate insecticides or antimalarial drugs. Other technical problems which have particularly hampered antimalarial programs, such as nomadic characteristics of some populations, outdoor transmission of the disease, and rapid deactivation of insecticides by certain mud walls in tropical West Africa, are being studied with promising results. 

Administrative problems which tend to delay full implementation of malaria eradication programs are being attacked jointly by ICA and WHO. The solution to this problem appears to be the modernization of administration methods in certain departments of the host governments to assure the flexibility essential for operation of a nationwide intensive program such as required for eradication of malaria. A program of consultation and training in administrative methods is being implemented.

Coordination meetings between personnel of WHO, PAHO, United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF), and ICA are held frequently and continual liaison is maintained to insure close cooperation and to assure the most effective utilization of technical and financial resources available for malaria eradication activities.

Chapter V The Development Loan Fund 

As of December 31, 1958, the Development Loan Fund had authorized loans and committed funds totaling $638.7 million. This amount, covering a period of approximately a year since starting loan operations, represented an increase of $371.3 million over the June 30 total of $267.4 million. The total of $638.7 million included 64 loan and guaranty authorizations and commitments involving 31 countries. 

Unlike other elements of the Mutual Security Program, the DLF is a revolving fund. Funds made available to it by the Congress need not be obligated within a specific period of time. Such funds, to gether with repayments and earnings, become a part of the organization's capital structure to be used as required. 

At the present time, the DLF is capitalized with appropriations authorized in section 203 of the Mutual Security Act of 1954, as amended. Out of $1,125 million authorized therein, of which $500 million could have become available in fiscal year 1958 and $625 million in fiscal year 1959, a total of $700 million has been appropriated by the Congress; $300 million of this amount was made available for use beginning in fiscal year 1958 and $400 million beginning in fiscal year 1959.

It should be noted that DLF capital will in time be composed of foreign currencies as well as dollars received in repayment. Under existing agreements with the countries of issue, the DLF will be free to lend these currencies for local development activities or sell these currencies to other agencies of the U.S. Government for their use. 

Agreements also provide for use of such currencies to finance local exports and for their conversion into other currencies upon agreement with the country whose currency is involved. 

The DLF operates through four basic organizational units in addi tion to the Office of Managing Director. The Office of the Deputy Managing Director for Finance and Development is responsible for the development of financial policies and programs and for various special programs, such as the promotion of private investment and 28

of local development banks. The review, negotiation, and implementation of specific loan proposals is the responsibility of the Office of Deputy Director for Operations. In addition, the DLF contains an Office of the Secretary which is responsible for maintaining corporate records and general administrative functions and an Office of the General Counsel. 

The small size of the DLF staff reflects an effort to rely as fully as possible upon the technical and other resources and services already available in other Government agencies and private organizations. 

For example, the staff of about 65 now depends on the ICA for the financial administration of loans, including the handling of disbursements and financial record keeping. ICA also performs financial audits of DLF loans and provides general housekeeping services. 

The DLF also turns to ICA, other Government agents, and private to consulting firms for engineering and other technical review and advisory services. The DLF pays for such services out of its assets under an annual administrative expense limitation contained in appropriations legislation. 

In accordance with practices followed by the Export-Import Bank and many other public and private lending institutions, funds are considered to be "committed" when a loan is approved, and funds to implement the loan are set aside and are considered no longer available O for further lending. Funds are formally "obligated" when a loan agreement has been signed with the borrower. 

Loan and guaranty agreements signed with borrowers totaled $256.2 million on December 31; and letters of advice, constituting the formal offer of a loan under specified terms and conditions, had been issued in the amount of $229.9 million. Other commitments, in advance of agreement on specific projects, were made to particular countries experiencing an emergency situation in connection with their loan programs in several instances, and totaled $125.9 million. In addition, there were $26.8 million of authorized loans for which letters of advice to borrowers had not yet been issued. The period saw the 

DLF make the first use of its loan-guaranty power, through which it I was hoped that increased U.S. private investment in underdeveloped 

I areas could be encouraged. In a number of cases, the DLF also participated with other lending institutions in financing common or complementary undertakings.

During the 6-month period, total proposals received by the DLF increased by about $600 million-from $2.2 billion to $2.8 billion. 

More than $250 million of proposals were dropped from consideration. The column of loan proposals still under consideration and on hand on December 31 was about $1.7 billion-slightly higher than the amount on June 30. Against this total of $1.7 billion in loan pro- 29

posals on hand, the DLF had only about $60.0 million in loan funds remaining available for authorization of loans. 

In accordance with statutory requirements, each loan application, before being put through intensive evaluation, is checked to determine whether financing is available from private sources, the Export- Import Bank, the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, or the International Finance Corporation. Each loan is approved by the DLF's own Board of Directors and advice is sought from the National Advisory Council on International Monetary and Financial Problems, whose members include representatives of the Export-Import Bank, ICA, the Department of the Treasury, the Department of Commerce, the State Department, and the Federal 

Reserve System. These procedures assure that the Development Loan 

Fund supplements, but does not compete with, services offered by other public and private sources of financing. 

In reviewing the economic and technical aspects of each proposal the DLF staff draws upon the services of the technical staffs in ICA, other Government and private organizations, and the U.S. Embassies and ICA Missions in the countries concerned, in order to make sure that the loan is economically and technically feasible, that it offers reasonable promise of repayment, and that it may reasonably be expected to contribute to the development of economic resources or productive capacity in the country concerned. 

The possible impact on the U.S. economy of each loan proposal is also carefully examined. The DLF staff considers the anticipated output of the project, the equipment to be procured, and the method by which the loan is to be financed. Where an adverse effect appears possible, the DLF staff consults with appropriate U.S. agencies such as the Departments of Agriculture, Commerce, Treasury, State, and Interior, and with ICA. Each recommendation for approval presented to the Board of Directors includes a section indicating the effect of the proposal upon the U.S. economy, and this question is considered by the Board and by the National Advisory Council.

Chapter VI Private Enterprise 

Major attention was given during the reporting period to consideration of the role of the private sector of the national economy in carrying out the objectives of the Mutual Security Act. It reflected the obvious fact that one of our greatest economic assets as a Nation is the capital, knowledge, initiative, and resourcefulness in private hands. 

In compliance with section 413(c) of the Mutual Security Act, a number of studies were begun on private enterprise and the Mutual 

Security Program: 

A study group was organized by Ralph Straus as a consultant to the Department of State to survey, report, and make recommendations on the role of private U.S. foreign investment in advancing the purposes of the Mutual Security Act and the foreign policy objec tives of the United States. • Representatives from the Departments of Agriculture, Commerce, Defense, Interior, Labor, State, the International Cooperation Admin istration and the Development Loan Fund, formed a group to study and evaluate the impact of the Mutual Security Program upon the 

U.S. economy. 

In addition, at the request of the President a Committee on World Economic Practices, headed by Harold Boeschenstein, was organized under the auspices of the Business Advisory Council to the Department of Commerce. This Committee was asked to study and recommend a combined governmental and private enterprise program to counter the Sino-Soviet bloc economic offensives through the development of a sound, expanding free world economic system. 

All such studies and recommendations were expected to be completed during the next reporting period-January through June 1959. 

In addition to assisting in these studies, officials of the interested governmental agencies reviewed their programs in terms of increased stimulation of private enterprise and promoting of private foreign investments in the areas being assisted under the Mutual Security Program. Meanwhile, the U.S. Government continued its established 38

programs for promoting private enterprise and the investment of foreign private funds in the countries being assisted. 

Many activities undertaken by the U.S. Government in cooperation with other governments through the Mutual Security Program can be said to contribute to the building of a foundation on which private activity can be based. Assistance for many of the basic facilitiesroads, railroads, ports, power, communications-on which private business must rely were undertaken or continued. Technical cooperation programs helped governments to improve their services in the fields of health, administration, agriculture, education, and other services vital to private business development. The commodity assistance programs were important sources of equipment and raw materials for the establishment of new plants and the operation and expansion of existing plants.

No new tax treaties entered into force during the reporting period. 

However, a new protocol to the Convention and Protocol for the 

Avoidance of Double Taxation and the Prevention of Fiscal Evasion between the United States and the United Kingdom did enter into force on October 15, 1958. This protocol liberalized the provisions of the convention as to exemptions and credits on income from royalties and other like sources. Also certain provisions of this convention were extended to some 20 additional territories of the United Kingdom by an agreement effected by an exchange of notes signed on August 19, 1957, and December 3, 1958, and entering into force on the latter date.

A treaty of amity, economic relations, and consular rights between the United States and the Sultan of Muscat and Oman was signed December 20, 1958. Active negotiations looking to the conclusion of commercial treaties were carried on during the period with four additional countries. Treaty proposals were in various stages of consideration by the governments of about 10 countries. New treaty proposals were presented to the government of one additional country. 

During the period of this report the Department of State has continued its standing policy of seeking compliance by other countries with all treaties concerning commerce, trade, or taxes to which both such countries and the United States are parties. The United States has entered into many such treaties with other countries covering a broad variety of matters and designed to protect and advance the interests of American individuals and corporations abroad. These treaties may be either bilateral or multilateral. 

Situations involving possible violations of the obligations of such treaties are brought to the Department's attention by two principal means. American Embassies in foreign countries keep close watch 39

on all developments in those countries that may possibly affect the observance of treaty obligations. Thus the Embassies regularly inform the Department of State of any existing or prospective legislation or administrative regulations or of any general practices or particular situations which might constitute violations by any country of its treaties with the United States. Cases of possible treaty violations are also brought to the Department's attention by private individuals or companies who encounter difficulties in connection with their activities in foreign countries and seek the aid of the Department or of the 

American Embassy concerned.

In situations involving possible treaty violations, present or prospective, the Department of State promptly institutes an investigation as to the facts involved, and secures the opinion of its Legal Adviser as to whether a legal basis for representations to the country concerned appears warranted. If so, the Department normally brings the possible violations to the attention of the country involved and requests that suitable measures be taken to bring its conduct into conformity with the treaty.

Because of the number of such treaties, the breadth of their coverage, and their complexity, situations involving possible violation of treaty provisions frequently arise, and constitute a regular and important part of the business of the Department of State.

Asian Economic Development Fund 

During the period under review, measures were initiated for the implementation of all 13 of the previously approved programs under the Asian Economic Development Fund (AEDF), excluding the com pleted surveys. Nine of these programs are scheduled to be executed through contracts, with the remainder carried out primarily by the cooperating governments. Initial contracts were signed for six undertakings; on the three others, contractors were selected and negotiations are in progress. Personnel are already at work under the first contracts signed with the University of Michigan for English language training and the University of Hawaii for the Thailand phase of the skilled labor program.

Projects generally fall into three major groupings—transportation, telecommunications, and training. Under the first category, the Fund is making $27.1 million available for an Afghan-Pakistan transit sys tem, $5 million to establish a Nepal road net with connections to India, and $20 million on a loan basis for Indian rail and port improvements to facilitate the movement of iron ore to Japan. 

In the telecommunications field there is a $23 million program for Thailand, Viet-Nam, and Laos and a $1.5 million project for Nepal with connections to India.

Training is centered on Thailand and the Philippines. Under this phase approximately $5.5 million is being expended for the following projects: a graduate SEATO engineering school at Bangkok; marine 50

research in the Gulf of Thailand; upgrading labor skills in the Asian 

SEATO countries (the Philippines, Thailand, and Pakistan); English language training in Thailand, Viet-Nam, and Laos; and specialized courses in agricultural credit, labor education, malaria eradication, and public administration in the Philippines. 

Aside from costs for two preliminary surveys (a $2.2 million project for collection of hydrological data on the Mekong River, and $141,000 for a tourism survey) the remainder of the Fund has been transferred to support other programs: malaria eradication, foreign scientists, the Ceylon country programs, and certain administrative operations. 

After deducting transfers for administrative purposes, approximately $3.2 million remained available for obligation as of December 31, 1958. 

American National Exhibit, Moscow 

For several years it has been an important U.S. objective to put on an exhibition in Moscow which would make it possible to bring a detailed and impressive picture of the American way of life to a large number of Soviet citizens. The United States and the U.S.S.R., on September 10, 1958, signed a protocol specifying the summer of 1959 as the time for an exchange of exhibitions of "science, technology, and culture."

Negotiations were concluded for the lease of a site in Sokolniki Park and for the construction on that site of appropriate exhibit buildings (which will be purchased after the exhibit by the U.S.S.R.). 

It was the belief of the executive branch that if a firm commitment was to be entered into for an exhibition in Moscow and a corresponding Soviet exhibition in New York in midsummer it was essential that such a commitment be made prior to January 1, 1959, and that the required funds be available. The total budget estimate for the exhibition was set at $3.6 million, exclusive of the performing arts program of the State Department. A previously available amount of $300,000 left a net deficiency of $3.3 million. 

The only immediately available source of funding appeared to be the Mutual Security appropriation, and it was confirmed that they were legally available for this purpose. This source of financing made it possible to conclude the necessary final agreement on the exhibition with the U.S.S.R. on December 29, 1958, and thus to hold an exhibition in Moscow in the summer of 1959. 51
