THE INSIDE OF THE CUP I. BACKGROUND 

Among the questions disturbing Washington in these tense days, and rapidly attracting national interest, is the attempt to force the elimination of Harry Slattery as Administrator of the Rural Electrification Administration. 

This controversy, undeniably started by the top leaders of the recently created National Rural Electric Cooperative Association, is not merely a squabble over an official job. It has become an issue of far-reaching moment and wide ramifications which has aroused the progressive forces of the country quite as much as the cooperatives and the personnel of REA immediately affected. 

Interest intensifies as September 14th approaches when Congress will return and the Senate Agriculture Committee begin hearings under the Shipstead resolution to investigate this and other REA matters. 

The newspapers have been carrying special articles about the sub-rosa rumors whispered around the Capital City that Mr. 

Slattery has resigned, will resign, or be removed forthwith; that

REA will be transferred from Agriculture to Interior and that the coming investigation of NRECA and REA management will be smothered and amount to nothing-and what not. 

Meantime "Harry" as he is known from one end of the country to the other, keeps his counsel, stays out of the newspapers, and bides his time. When asked he firmly states that he will not resign under any circumstances, but will demand his day in court to meet the charges against him which he welcomes with a confidence which should make his detractors uneasy. 

THE CAMPAIGN STARTS 

The campaign of the small coterie of NRECA leaders to oust the Administrator, smoldering since April 19th, broke into open blitzkreig on May 18th last when the St. Louis Post Dispatch carried a front page story signed by one of its Washington correspondents, headed "RURAL ELECTRIC COOPERATIVES SEEK TO OUST SLATTERY, Association Attacks Him Because He Opposes Their 

Two Insurance Projects." The article told the truth. 

Following this, Mr. Ellis on June 7th sent a "Confidential Memorandum" to cooperatives which have thus far joined his association in effect asking them to urge Secretary Wickard and President 

Roosevelt to remove Mr. Slattery "immediately." Members of

Congress, Washington officials, and others were also solicited. The reason now given is not that Mr. Slattery had double-crossed the insurance program. A quick shift has been made. The new charge is that he was "incompetent."

Since that time Ellis has been attending all possible state meetings of the cooperatives in a strenuous attempt further to enlist them in his warfare. His speeches allege that the Administrator is mentally unbalanced, that he has a shady moral character, and demean his capacity as a public official. 

The Public Utilities Fortnightly of July 8th carried an article by 3

me, "What Is The True Origin of NRECA." It showed that this association was not a grass roots thing but its real subrosa creator was Robert B. Craig, Deputy Administrator of REA (resigned 

May 5th last); that his purpose was control of REA; and that the association was to be used for political and business purposes, including the organization of such enterprises as the insurance concerns Mr. Ellis was promoting. 

Mr. Ellis promptly replied in the next issue of the Fortnightly, July 22nd, in an article "What's Behind the Attack on NRECA?"

His answer was, in effect, that I was working with the private utilities, perhaps unconsciously, and that my real motive was to remain on the payroll as consultant to REA. He charged in addition that "Only a year ago Mr. King was an applicant for the position of Executive Manager of this national association." Lest some readers might credit this attempt to deflect attention from the real issue by a personal attack, let me dispose of it at once. The Fortnightly of August 19th carried a reply letter from me from which 

I quote: 

"I do not care to descend to the 'you're another' level, but if the gentleman can produce such an application signed by me, or the affidavit of a single reliable person that I ever made oral application, I will hand a $100 war bond to the Red Cross or any Arkansas charity Mr. Ellis may name.' 

Several weeks have passed and neither the Red Cross nor any Arkansas charity as yet has claimed the reward establishing Mr. 

Ellis' veracity.

The important part of this effort at rebuttal was a wholesale denial of the truth of my statements, especially those concerning the insurance program. He produced no evidence, however, and did not get down to cases and particulars. Why not, when real evidence is what the country and the cooperatives were expecting? 

MR. ELLIS' USE OF GREAT NAMES 

Doubtless the gentleman thought that his word would be accepted without question since the NRECA at its St. Louis convention had made its first public bow with the written congratulations of the 

President, the Vice President, and the Secretary of Agriculture; also Senators Norris, Aiken, Russell, and Butler delivered addresses as well as other distinguished speakers, and had not Secretary Ickes wound up the program with a notable speech the last night? 

What could be more reassuring and more calculated to inspire confidence?

But that was in January, and this was July. Much had happened. 

Hence, to recreate the atmosphere of January, Mr. Ellis at the outset of his reply, with unexampled boldness, quotes the President's letter; from the speeches of Senators Norris and Butler, and from the letter of Rep. Rankin. He states further that his association "has been definitely and universally welcomed by all real REA and public power leaders. . . ." By such artistry, he conveys the impression that these distinguished men are still sponsors of the NRECA and its works, and perforce, himself as its spokesman. 

This constitutes an outrageous betrayal of confidence all round, since Congressman Rankin is the only man whose name he invokes

that has openly defended him since the insurance racket was exposed, and his attack upon Administrator Slattery became public. 

As a result, the whole cooperative and public power world, as well as the general public, is left in a confused state of mind. They want to know the facts and who is telling the truth, and since real evidence that will clear the air, reveal the true issues at stake and serve to show the kind of men in charge of this farm organization with whom we are forced to deal is needed, I propose to set down some testimony that will stand in court alongside Mr. Ellis' denials and affirmations.

II. THE SUDDEN RIGHT-ABOUT-FACE INSURANCE OR INCOMPETENCY? 

The most amazing thing in this whole episode is the lightning-like change in the attitude of the NRECA leaders toward Administrator Slattery. It has puzzled the cooperatives, Senator Norris, and all progressives as well as the public. Relations were so cordial at the convention in January that Congress and the public thought the Administrator had promoted this association and that it was a child of REA. A main purpose of my Fortnightly article was to explode that idea and indicate its true parentage. Right up to the crucial date of April 19th, when Slattery issued his Stop, Look and Listen letter to the Co-ops on insurance, it was "Dear Mr. 

Harry," and "Dear Clyde" in a correspondence running over two years. This is evidence in black and white. A few examples will be illuminating.

"From the bottom of my heart I congratulate you and for myself, and on behalf of the good people of the State of Arkansas I say to you, that we are eternally grateful for your continued efforts and your success in coming to the aid of the defense program with REA power for the Arkansas aluminum plant. "We in my State regard you as one of the great intellects in Washington. 

There is no living man outside the State who is regarded more highly in Arkansas nor who is looked upon by our State as a better friend than you, Harry Slattery.*

On the contrary, when I learned that such an effort was to be made, I requested an opinion by the Solicitor's office. Even though it may be decided that some certain portion of the funds may be used for this purpose, this Administration is confronted with the question whether it is consistent with good business practice for the cooperatives to put their money in a new business venture."

These brief excerpts reveal two things of especial significance. 

First, no condemnation of Mr. Slattery's official or personal conduct up to April 19th. Second, that the insurance scheme was the most important objective of the organizers of NRECA, outweighing protection of the cooperatives from the power and other interests, advertised as its chief purpose. 

THE "CAT THRASHING" THREAT 

The National Board met in Washington on April 23rd as Mr. Ellis advised the Administrator. For the next step, let us now return to Mr. Ellis' Fortnightly article:

"On April 23rd, said Mr. King, 'President Tate called Mr. Slattery and threatened to drive him out of public life.' This is too ridiculous to merit a reply. But the members of the National Board and I heard that conversation. Mr. Tate made no such threat, nor did he say an unkind word to the Administrator." 

Unfortunately for Mr. Ellis' memory, a young lady of excellent hearing with a sharp pencil was taking down that conversation in 

St. Louis. Here are some of the things she heard: 

Mr. Slattery: "Hello."

Mr. Tate: "This is Tate calling. The Executive Committee is in session here after receiving your letter to Clyde with the statement you sent all the cooperatives about insurance. We are shocked beyond expression. and we want to know what the pressure was that was put on you to make you do it." (A tense exchange followed during which Tate advised "It might be well for you to get your house in order" and Mr. Slattery said "If you people are going to try to smoke me out, you've got a tough job.")

Mr. Slattery: "I never knew all the details of the insurance program, and there's no use in arguing about it now." 

Mr. Tate: "Why, you did know all about it. I beg your pardon, but we kept you fully informed. 

Mr. Slattery: "You never said anything about using excess funds. You'll find you've got the Treasury, the RFC, and everybody else involved in this thing." You never dealt entirely with me; you dealt mostly with Bob 

Craig and Drefkoff." 

Mr. Tate: "We're going to have a "cat-thrashing”—to use an old Southern expression; you, being from South Carolina, ought to be familiar with it. It is not my disposition to tell you just exactly what is on my mind. I am just surprised at you; you know, we are all men, and we all put on our breeches the same way.

We didn't like what you've done and we couldn't understand it. 

(The conversation ended with an agreement to have further conferences for discussion)

The term "cat-thrashing" is not found in the dictionary, but the Administrator tells me that it is a southern colloqualism which means to chase a cat, or cats, out of the house and off the premises, using whips, stones, clubs, guns or other implements of warfare. 

He clearly understood that by its use Tate was threatening to drive him out of his position and public life. He states that Mr. Tate's tone was belligerent and insulting. 

This is only part of a phone conversation in which Mr. Tate, remembering the manner befitting a gentleman with aspirations for the Governorship of Georgia, "Made no threat," and said "no unkind word," as understood by the gentleman from Arkansas.

It is a thousand to one bet that had Administrator Slattery yielded to three months of pressure, climaxed by this bullying, to approve the insurance mutuals the blitzkreig campaign which immediately followed would never have been started. A few days later Messrs. 

Tate and Ellis and the board, saw Secretary Wickard and demanded that Slattery be fired. They wrote a letter to the President, began working on the Co-ops and when called to account for their actions promptly accuse their critics with fighting the farmers and rural electrification.

SLATTERY NEVER KNEW OR APPROVED "IN DETAIL" 

To sustain their present contention that Administrator Slattery is a weakling, the Ellis group now claim that he was in fact the originator of their insurance plan, was familiar with it "in detail". from the beginning and double-crossed his own plan under pressure from the insurance and power companies. 

For example, in his "Confidential Memorandum" of June 7th to the cooperatives, Mr. Ellis wrote the following under the heading 

"How the Insurance Program Originated:" 

"Mr. Slattery stated to your Executive Manager at the time he met with the National Board in St. Louis on September 18-19 that 'insurance is one of the programs that the National Association must work out. The cooperatives have long been advocating such a program. Carmody advocated such a program. A lot of work has been done on it and I have assigned Mr. Drefkoff to that section to get ready for it.' The Board also met with Mr. Slattery on that occasion and he discussed insurance with them and was wholly agreeable to their proceeding with it." 

Again in this same message he says: "Mr. Moore (NRECA insurance man) and Mr. Ellis were asked by the 

Administrator to come to St. Louis for a conference on insurance March 22. 

They were there." 

Still again: "Your Executive Manager will add that in his opinion it would have been impossible for the National to have gotten along with REA during the first year of its existence if it had not endeavored to work out an insurance program." 

This is forceful, apparently candid narrative by the spokesman for "1,000,000 farmers." It is dimmed only by certain aberations. 

Mr. Slattery affirms he had no such confab with Ellis on September 

18-19, 1942, since he was in Tennessee. He states that at no time did he say that "Carmody (Hon. John M. Carmody) advocated such a program" because he never talked insurance with his predecessor. 

In response to my written inquiry, Mr. Carmody writes on August 

30th, “I do not recall ever to have discussed REA insurance or any other kind of insurance with Mr. Slattery. I never advocated such an REA insurance program or any other tricky insurance plan." 

Mr. Slattery states very frankly that he has always believed in the principle of the non-profit, cooperative movement in many fields and that from all he could gather, this was the sort of cooperative that Craig, Drefkoff, Tate and Ellis were talking about when they discussed their insurance program with him. He says he never dreamed they were intending to finance their insurance companies with $1,000,000 borrowed from membership funds and operating surpluses. He says when he discovered this he requested Secretary 

Wickard to obtain a legal opinion on the question and was highly indignant when, before that opinion was secured, Messrs. Ellis and 

Moore came to St. Louis to demand his approval of their plans. 

So, Mr. Ellis' treacherous memory tricked him again. He was not "asked to come.' He just went.

Recounting this meeting in his Fortnightly article, Mr. Ellis says:

"Continued Mr. King, 'On March 25th,* in St. Louis, Executive Manager

Ellis and others came, laid upon his (Slattery's) desk a prepared letter for his signature, endorsing the mutuals, to be mimeographed and sent to all cooperatives, together with a copy of the pamphlet above described." There is not a word of truth in this statement. Executive Manager Ellis never saw any such letter."

The pamphlet above referred to was a 20-page mimeographed document titled "A Comprehensive Insurance Plan for REA Co operatives." I had stated that Mr. Ellis was preparing to send out this document in a sales campaign. It was written in question-andanswer form, that is, a catechism. His comeback follows: "This is positively not true. Neither the NRECA board nor its executive manager ever saw them (i.e., Questions-andAnswers-the "Catechism") before. 

We have since learned that they were prepared and handed to Mr. Slattery by his own Max Drefkoff, head of REA insurance section. We know the association never had any such thought." 

To be classed with Ananias twice on the same page in such devastating fashion would ruffle my temper if I did not have before me Administrator Slattery's memorandum describing this meeting.

Question: How could a business executive ask an administrator to sign a letter the executive had never seen?

The mystery is now cleared. Putting together the facts given in my Fortnightly article of July 8th and those set down in this bulletin, the high points of the story stand out about as follows: 

Deputy Craig in 1940 got the happy idea of an insurance company to go along with his appliance and equipment cooperatives as major parts of his national organization as revealed to Becker. He and 

Drefkoff worked it out together. When the National was organized 

Tate, Ellis, the executive board and, later, insurance expert Moore, adopted and promoted the grand plan. But little matters of detail were not made clear. Administrator Slattery was favorable to the principle of the thing but when he found what kind of jack was in the box and was pressured to endorse the thing he declined. The campaign for his removal followed-of course, based on other grounds but with most reprehensible tactics. 

III. SENATOR NORRIS GIVES KIND ADVICE TO MR. ELLIS 

When the executives of NRECA started to get Slattery out of their way "immediately" and virtually take over control of REA, they sought as noted, the aid of Senators, Congressmen, and officials in Washington, high and low. They also sought to enlist Senator 

Norris, father of TVA and REA.

The Senator was greatly disturbed and pained over the whole situation. He had had confidence in Mr. Ellis and Mr. Craig as sincere leaders in the rural electrification and public power cause. 

But he also had confidence in Harry Slattery whom he had known intimately for over 30 years, who had battled with him for many progressive causes and with whose career he was familiar. 

The trouble was that he had little or no inside knowledge-no real evidence as to the inner workings of either the NRECA or REA. But after sizing up the situation as best he could from conflicting reports from both sides, he wrote a letter to Executive Manager Ellis from his home in McCook, Nebraska which is a model of restrained judgment and friendly advice. The letter, dated June 25th, is reprinted with the Senator's permission as an 10

appendix to this bulletin. His opinion on the insurance plan will be quoted later but at this point his judgment upon the methods employed to remove the Administrator is of interest. "Dear Clyde: "I have just read your letter of June 22nd. In this letter you do as you have done in previous letters; to wit, ask me to join with you and the National 

Rural Electric Cooperative Association in demanding the removal of Harry Slattery as the Administrator of the REA. 

"I am not trying to defend Slattery because in my judgment no charge has been made against him so far as I know that needs a defense. In your lengthy letter that you sent all over the United States in which you demand his removal "immediately," what was said was not in my judgment sufficiently definite to have any legal effect, whatever. I do not believe it would have been admitted in court as making a charge of any kind. "The main thing, as I got it from this letter and from others similar to it coming from enemies of Mr. Slattery, is that your association wanted to organize an insurance company on the cooperative basis and that Slattery for a time seemed to be favorable to such consideration but for some reason changed his mind and become an enemy to this insurance plan. There were some other things but it is safe to say in my judgment that this was the principal burden of your opposition to Slattery. "Now I do not know what this insurance plan was. You have not told it in any detail, so far as I know, to anyone. You have simply assumed that the insurance plan was a good one, that it would save lots of money, and that Slattery was against it and in some mysterious way had prevented you, and is still preventing you, and your organization from putting this insurance plan into effect. "Whether it (Slattery's decision) is right or wrong ought to be determined by proper authority and your organization ought not be so anxious to get him removed as to try to do so by methods that deny him even of a hearing. You have asked me, and I presume many others, to write letters to the President asking that he be removed "immediately." He has not been tried. "Regardless of whether your charges are right or wrong, just or unjust, that, you know, Clyde, is not the fair way to do with a public official. "It is a mistake, in my judgment, for your organization to set itself up superior in authority to the organization that is set up by the REA Act. The officials of the REA must follow the law and they are not required to take the construction of the law that is put forth by your organization. It seems to me that is fair. It is only just. "You are doing a great injury to the REA in my judgment. 

All this is enough to establish conclusively that Mr. Slattery's refusal on sound legal grounds to champion the insurance scheme was what aroused the wrath of the NRECA leaders. Their "gravy train" was side-tracked and the flow of loan money to headquarters suddenly stopped. 

The "Scandal brewing in Congress" over the Administrator existed in Ellis' head and was voiced only by his tongue and those of his group, in talking to Senators and Congressmen. It is curious that the sole proof he offers of this tosh is that there are some "pensioners on the payroll;" all of these are opposed to the policies and tactics of the Craig-Tate-Ellis machine and the writer is No. 1. 

On the point of loans Mr. Ellis in his Fortnightly article once more gives me the lie and declares "Not a dime has been received." 

Well, I have a list of 11 cooperatives in 6 states which from March 9th to April 30th voted to lend a total of nearly $25,000. That, I believe, is only partial. And in the opinion of Robert H. Shields,

Solicitor of the Department of Agriculture, handed to Secretary 11

Wickard on May 7th we find this language: "A reserve of at least $1,000,000 is desired, and it is expected that the average loan of each member will be $2,500 to $3,000. I am informed that some loans as large as $8,000 have already been made." 

In that opinion Solicitor Shields showed that these loans affected Government advances to the cooperatives as did the letter of Mr. 

Slattery on April 19th. Yet, Mr. Ellis asserts "Not a dime of

Federal money was to be used directly or indirectly." 

Will the Executive Manager put the Solicitor in his Ananias Club? 

IV. ANALYSIS OF THE INSURANCE PLAN 

Thousands besides Senator Norris did not know, and do not yet know, "What this Insurance plan was"-that is the inside of it. 

But the subject is of such crucial importance that further inquiry is justifiable. The truth will be found not in the whirlwind of the propaganda but in the still small voice of the by-laws. 

WHAT THE BY-LAWS REVEAL Two companies, the Rural Mutual Casualty Insurance Corpora tion and the Rural Mutual Fire Insurance Corporation, were incorporated on Jan. 6, 1943 in Maryland, two weeks before the first national convention of the NRECA in St. Louis. They are identical in set-up as to control. Hence, I shall quote from the by-laws of the 

Casualty Corporation only.

Purpose and Scope: The kinds of insurance cover a wide range and make dull, legalistic reading. Since they are stated succinctly in the "catechism" which was to be used for advertising purposes, let that suffice here. 

46. (Q) Specifically, what types of insurance may the Rural

Mutuals write in accordance with their charters and by-laws? (A) The Rural Mutuals may, in addition to other types, insure specifically against: 

1. Loss or liability for bodily injury, death by accident, disability, sickness, or disease, including employees' liability and workmen's compensation. 

2. Loss or liability resulting from the ownership of automobiles or other vehicles, excluding losses by fire (fire losses are covered under separate charter). 

3. Loss or damage to machinery or boilers, transmission lines, 

4. Make contracts for hospitalization insurance. 

5. Make contracts for fidelity and surety bonds. 

6. Losses or damage by fire, lightning, hail, frost, snow, or explosion, on all types of property, real or personal. 

7. Losses or damage caused by action of the elements or adverse manifestations of nature.

Members: Article II, Sections 1 and 2 of the By-Laws read: 

Section 1. Members-Each policy holder shall be a member of this corporation while his policy is in effect. 

Section 2. Limitations-Membership in this corporation shall be limited to: 

12

(a) Members of the National Rural Electric Cooperative Association; (b) any persons, firms, or corporations employed by or having contractual relations with said members; and (c) member patrons of the members of the National 

Rural Electric Cooperative Association and their immediate families. 

In his letter to the cooperatives of June 7, Mr. Ellis says of the 

Administrator's letter of April 19th: 

"2. It dealt at length with an alleged proposal of the insurance companies to insure the individual co-op members. No such thing was ever contemplated or discussed with the Administrator. This was done, in our opinion, in an effort to prejudice both the cooperatives and insurance companies against the insurance plan."

In his Fortnightly article Ellis writes "We know the Association never had any such thought" (of insuring individuals and their families). If so, why the above language in the By-Laws, and why was the NRECA in its articles of incorporation empowered to give other services to Rural Electric Cooperatives and other's 66 ?"

Control: Article III provides for an annual meeting of mem bers—that is, policy holders not member cooperatives-to elect the boards of directors and determine policy matters. Any policy holder can attend and vote. Sounds very democratic. But, "delegates" also vote and 10 delegates elected from each of 10 Regional District conventions provided for, cast, each one of them, the number of votes determined by the number of policy holders in his 

Regional District. 

To illustrate; say there are 10,000 policy holders in District 5 (Wisconsin, Iowa and Illinois). John Doe is elected to represent his district. 15 policy holders attend the national meeting. They cast 15 votes and John Doe casts 9,985 votes. Thus is applied the cooperative principle of one member, one vote.

V. THE LARGER ISSUES INVOLVED 

There are two other aspects of the problem raised by the ambitions and activities of the promoters of the NRECA which affect the future of REA and rural electrification. Here I can only point them out and discuss them briefly. They concern the relations between REA and working harmony with the cooperative move- 14

ment generally, and of organized labor. Thus far the NRECA, as managed, has been a disturbing influence. 

Said Mr. Ellis in his Fortnightly reply "The cooperatives are successful, independent, free enterprises, in no way connected with the Federal government . . . they have no desire to disturb the private utilities.”

That theme has run throughout the promotional work of this association from the first. The argument is alluring and seductive to many local Co-op people who naturally like to run their own businesses, unsupervised, and to whom the current propaganda of 

Big Business against "bureaucracy in Washington" sounds sweetespecially to a few men here and there who are ready to sell their cooperatives and REA down the river for a price. 

No doubt centralized direction of any kind since the rise of civilization has developed its evils and dangers. No doubt also it has its advantages, and must be utilized in a machine age like ours. 

We must, in the case of REA, face this question-where would most of the 800 Co-ops be today if they had not had the protective help, technical assistance, and financial, legal and engineering guidance and advice given them over these eight years by Administrators 

Cook, Carmody, Slattery, and their staffs?

Another questions: how long would it take for the private power interests and allied corporations to put a majority of the Co-ops in the ditch, and buy them out, without the continued help of a strong REA, especially in the post-war period? What happened to the "independent" telephone companies? The words and deeds of Messrs. Craig, Tate, Ellis, acquiesced in by their boards of directors, indicate that they intend, through political pressure and by other methods to "run" REA, direct its policies, pick its employees, and usurp the management functions of REA, as far as they can. 

Believe it or not, the application of this doctrine is exactly what the private power interests want. It is in direct line with their own propaganda, and they welcome any split in the forces of public power. Up-to-date cooperatives understand this, and that is one principal reason why some 260 of them have declined to join the NRECA, and why many other are now in a most critical attitude toward it. The political influence of Mr. Ellis with the cooperatives everywhere is vastly over-rated by some Washington politicians, and their sympathisers in government circles. Most of the Co-ops want strong, cordial, fair cooperative relations with REA, and a strong National under sound and decent management which will accomplish the results they desire. 

THE CONSUMER-PRODUCERS CO-OPERATIVES AND UNION LABOR 

It would have been true wisdom for any national association of the cooperatives to have established from the start friendly relations and practical cooperation with the great consumer-producers and credit cooperatives of the nation. These are the working agencies of growing millions of farmers, and are honestly managed. 

Thousands of their members are also members of REA Co-ops. Is it not strange that they have been ignored by the Craig-Ellis-Tate machine? 15

In my Fortnightly article I quoted from an official statement made by Mr. John Carson, Washington representative of the Cooperative 

League of the USA when Mr. Max Drefkoff, head of Insurance

Section of REA was being examined for insubordination and was suspended. Mr. Carson stated that in May 1942, shortly after the incorporation of NRECA, Mr. Drefkoff came to him to inquire about existing cooperative insurance concerns. When Carson asked him why create a new cooperative when there were several in existence to which thousands of REA consumers already belonged, Drefkoff replied "Oh no, we are going to have our own company.

We want our own company that Bob (Craig) and I can control." 

Well, that was exactly what was attempted, and what the NRECA tried to put over. That was also why such powerful and friendly organizations as the National Council of Farm Cooperatives, the Cooperative League of the USA, and the Credit Union National Association, all of them friendly to rural electrification and which desire to work in active sympathy with REA and its cooperatives for the common good, have been astounded by the performances of NRECA in this critical period of our economic development. 

Like astonishment and resentment was engendered by the opposition of Mr. Craig and later of Messrs. Tate and Ellis and their 

Board to the eminently fair agreement reached and procedure established for collective bargaining between the cooperatives and the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers, signed by 

Grover Hill, Assistant Secretary of Agriculture, by Mr. Slattery and by Mr. Craig himself, on behalf of the Government. Yet Craig and his machine inside REA and NRECA afterward misrepresented this policy to the cooperatives, falsely claiming that Slattery was trying to ram unionism down the throats of the farmers, and by that action endangered friendly relations between the cooperatives and the entire organized labor forces of the United States. These unions believe in the cooperative idea, they believe in rural electrification, they want to help, they are ready to play square, they have shown restraint, and if they have not had the good sense to recognize that the activities of the Craig-Ellis machine, did not permanently represent the attitude of progressive farmers, their support of the cause of REA would have long since been withdrawnand the Co-ops need that help. 

The final issue presented is this: Shall a small group of ambitious men be permitted to take advantage of a tremendously needed and powerful movement like rural electrification and, by capturing control of an organization devoted to its welfare, promote their own personal interests? And shall progressive men and women concerned with the success of such a movement keep silent, lie down, play safe, and permit the promoters to succeed?

This sort of thing, bold action on the part of a few and lethargy on the part of the many, account for the wreckage of many cooperatives and labor and consumer and educational and political movements, started for the people, which are strewn along the highway of American life for the past century. 16

Columbian Building, 416 Fifth Street, N. W. 

Washington, D. C. 

Dear Clyde: 

I have just read your letter of June 22nd. In this letter you do as you have done in previous letters; to wit, ask me to join with you and the National Rural Electric Cooperative Association in demanding the removal of Harry Slattery as the administrator of the REA. 

I feel as though I ought to write you rather fully my reaction of this attack on Mr. Slattery. First, I want to say I do not believe

I have any influence, that my action either pro or con would not affect the result in any way. I am a private citizen. I am shorn of all my power or influence. I have no inclination to mix into this attack upon Mr. Slattery and I know that I am not moved by any ill will or prejudice against you or the National Rural Electric 

Cooperative Association. 

I was glad when this Association was formed. I believe in the cooperative idea. I was glad when you were made Executive 

Manager, as you know. Although my influence was probably nil,

I, nevertheless, did everything I could to have you appointed as 

Executive Manager. I have enjoyed your friendship for many years. I was terribly hurt when you were defeated in your fight for the Senate. I have followed your fight in the House for organizations similar to the TVA and for setting up of a TVA in Arkansas and Missouri.

I felt that your work in this new association would be of great assistance in the fight we are making against the private power companies and in the fight for improvement and extension of the REA all over the country. I do not believe I need to tell you this. 

I think you realize it and comprehend it, and I want to say to you now, Clyde, I am not moved by writing this letter by anything in the way of ill will against you or against this organization. I am still proud to be friendly to the organization and I am still proud to be friendly to you. But I do not get your view point and I think 

I ought to tell it first to you.

I have not talked with Mr. Slattery and I have not heard his defense, if anything, that has been made to the insinuations that have been cast against his work as a public servant by yourself and the officials of the association your represent. I am coming to you, Clyde, in a friendly spirit. I hope you will accept what I say in that light. 17

I am not trying to defend Slattery because in my judgment no charge has been made against him so far as I know that needs a defense. In your lengthy letter that you sent all over the United

States in which you demanded his removal "immediately," what was said was not in my judgment sufficiently definite to have any legal effect, whatever. I do not believe it would have been admitted in court as making a charge of any kind. 

The main thing, as I got it from this letter and from others similar to it coming from enemies of Mr. Slattery, is that your association wanted to organize an insurance company on the cooperative basis and that Slattery for a time seemed to be favorable to such consideration but for some reason changed his mind and became an enemy to this insurance plan. There were some other things but it is safe to say in my judgment that this was the principal burden of your opposition to Slattery.

Now I do not know what the insurance plan was. You have not told it in any detail, so far as I know, to anyone. You have simply assumed that the insurance plan was a good one, that it would save lots of money, and that Slattery was against it and in some mysterious way had prevented you, and is still preventing you, and your organization from putting this insurance plan into effect. 

In the first place, I do not know what my idea would be if your insurance plan were fully analyzed and explained to me. Speaking in the most general terms, I would start at the first of that subject with a friendly idea toward cooperative insurance. I believe in it. 

But it is claimed by some letters that I have received or that I have gotten from your own communications that Slattery had no legal power under the REA to use funds appropriated by Congress for the purpose of setting up an insurance plan. Not knowing what the plan was and assuming that Slattery changed his mind about the insurance and decided it could not be put into effect for the reason he could not use funds appropriated by Congress for this purpose, it still remains in my judgment a question upon which he would have a legal right to his opinion.

Whether it is right or wrong ought to be determined by proper authority and your organization ought not be so anxious to get him removed as to try to do so by methods that deny him even of a hearing. You have asked me, and I presume many others, to write letters to the President asking he be removed "immediately." 

He has not been tried. So far as I know, even the charge has not been made in terms that could be accepted. They are indefinite to the extent that I do not now have any definite idea as to what the charges are against him.

As part of your organization, or at least as part of the organization against Slattery, comes the man, Max Drefkoff. He wrote me a long letter enclosing copies of documents which I judged from the contents of his letter were copies of memorandums and letters that he had written to Slattery. There were forty or fifty pages of single-spaced, typewritten matter.

I have had some trouble with my eyes and I was not able to read it. Mrs. Norris spent two evenings reading these so-called charges against Slattery. To my mind, as a lawyer, they contain no charge 18

that would require an answer. 

I got the same idea there, that

Slattery had changed his mind about the insurance business and that at first was friendly, but, on further consideration, decided he had no legal authority to back up such a proposition-especially with funds that had been appropriated by Congress under the REA 

Act. 

I think the general demurrer would have been sustained by any court of law. I did not get a clear and distinct idea as to what the insurance proposition was and I have no idea now what it is. I had no basis to form an opinion whether Slattery would have been justified in going ahead in carrying out this insurance program or whether he had a legal right to do so as Administrator of the REA. 

I confess I got a very distinct idea that back of this attack on Slattery there was some sinister motive. I have had it suggested to me by some letters I have received from local, or at least partially local, sources interested in the state and district associations of the REA that some place behind this scheme, this plan of insurance, there would develop, as the clouds cleared away, a distinct formation of men who were powerful financially and that the real object was to organize an insurance plan, and have the REA assist in having it done, that would, in effect, reward financially the investors who are standing ready behind the scenes. 

I do not know that this is true, but I could not help but think, as I tried to wade through some of these documents, that Drefkoff had really beclouded the issue instead of throwing any light upon it. It seemed to have the threat of monopoly behind it. I could not help but draw the conclusion that there was some sinister motive of a financial nature, that Slattery stood in their way. 

They wanted him removed so that they could put someone of their own liking in his place. I do not know that this is true. I hope it is not. But usually when such a scheme is trying to be concocted, all sorts of charges are made to get someone out of the way and someone in his place who sympathizes with the general plan. 

No matter how noble and well-meaning an organization may be, I do not think it ought to set itself up superior to the law and demand that the REA officials obey it rather than use the judgment they ought to use under the law and ascertain whether they have a right under the law to take the steps that are proposed. 

It seems the charges narrow down to this. Slattery is unfriendly to some insurance plan, a plan not fully described. Notwithstanding the fact that at one time was favorable to cooperative insurance, he would not go along in this plan and, therefore, without any trial, without any ceremony, he must be removed. 

I do not know whether he has been unfriendly to the insurance plan except as I got it from your letters and from Mr. Drefkoff's volume of words that really mean nothing. And I assume he is unfriendly because I assumed that is what you have been telling us and that is why you want him removed. 

Regardless of whether your charges are right or wrong, just or unjust, that, you know, Clyde, is not the fair way to do with a public official. If there are any serious charges against Slattery that disqualify him, he ought to be removed after the hearing and a fair opinion thereby heard. 19

It is a mistake, in my judgment, for your organization to set itself up superior in authority to the organization that is set up by the REA Act. The officials of the REA must follow the law and they are not required to take the construction of the law that is put forth by you or your organization. It seems to me that is fair. 

It is only just. 

You are doing a great injury to the REA in my judgment. I know that your letter demanding Slattery's removal has created in some quarters the suspicion that your organization has set out to control the REA and to run it according to your idea as to how it should be run. I get this from letters that I have received, to the writers of which you have sent this so-called confidential communication demanding Slattery's removal.

I want you to consider this letter in the spirit which I have written it. I have no axe to grind. I know that my days are few. 

But I would hate to see the REA injured in the noble purposes we all had in view when we passed the law. I have not tried to influence anybody. I have not written the President or the Secretary of

Agriculture about it. First, because I did not believe either of them wanted any advice from me. 

Second, because I feared they would consider as I think they would have a right to do the fact that I have been repudiated, and what I have stood for, what I have tried to accomplish has all been put in the shade by my own people. I would not venture to have said a word had it not been for these letters and these communications, especially from you, that seems to me ought to be criticised. And I say this, Clyde, in all friendliness.

I know you are a young man and have a long and useful life before I think you have done a wonderful job in the past and I believe you are capable of doing many similar things as you pass along in life. 

I think I will send a copy of this letter to Secretary Wickard. I hesitate to give advice where my advice has not been asked and where I think perhaps it is not wanted. And yet, so many people have written me, including yourself, about this matter that it seemed to me in defense of my own self I ought to put on record, as I have tried to do in this letter, my sentiments so that at least it will be a satisfaction to me if to no one else. 

It is so abhorrent to me to think of a man being removed from an office without specific charges being made and without a full opportunity to defend himself that I could not have said less. 

As I have said above, with a spirit of friendliness and good will to the great organization which you represent, I am 

Yours very truly,

GEORGE W. NORRIS.
