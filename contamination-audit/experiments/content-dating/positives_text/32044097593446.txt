FOREWORD 

The California Department of Employment is fundamentally concerned with the conditions of employment and unemployment in the State, although the department, in itself, can exert only a limited influence over these conditions. Activities of the Department of Employment are controlled to a substantial extent by employment and unemployment conditions. When employment is increasing, the job placement function becomes more active; when unemployment is high the claims taking and benefit payment functions increase in tempo. 

In order that this report of the Department of Employment's activities during 1951 may have real significance, the reader will appreciate a review of the economic conditions under which the department operated. For that reason, the following resume of employment and unemployment conditions is presented as a foreword to the Department of Employment's 

Annual Report for 1951. 

For the second consecutive year, California, during 1951, saw employment reach its highest level in the history of the State and unemployment decline to its lowest level since the end of World War II. 

During the calendar year 1951, employment in California reached a monthly average of 4,484,000 persons. This exceeded by 239,000 the average number at work in 1950. The rapid acceleration in defense production was an important element in this rise. The normal growth of 

California's population and economy, however, also contributed to the increase. This is indicated by the fact that employment expanded in all industry divisions, some of which are only indirectly related to defense production.

Unlike the situation in most years, employment remained relatively constant during the early months of 1951, averaging 4,303,000 during 

January, February, and March. Commencing in April, the employment trend resumed its normal seasonal pattern, rising steadily until it reached an all-time record high of 4,691,000 in October. The curve dipped sharply in November, but rose moderately again in December. Employment in each month of 1951 was substantially above that for the comparable month of 1950, although increases during 1951 were not so great as those which occurred in 1950. 

Unemployment Reaches Postwar Low

Unemployment averaged 182,000 in 1951, 39 percent less than the monthly average of 299,000 in 1950. Peak unemployment occurred in

January and was estimated at 259,000, down by 45 percent from the January, 1950, level. The 120,000 persons out of work and seeking work in 

October, 1951, constituted the smallest number for any month since 5

World War II. The unemployed group consisted chiefly of persons shifting from one job to another, of seasonally unemployed workers, recent entrants to the labor force, and others who because of lack of qualifying skills, age and other reasons had difficulty in meeting employer specifications. The average unemployment for the year was 3.9 percent of the average total civilian labor force. 

The number of workers in California's labor force averaged 4,666,000 in 1951, an increase of 122,000 over the 1950 average. During the five years from December, 1946, to December, 1951, the State's labor force grew by an estimated 444,000 persons. Despite this substantial growth, the estimated number of unemployed at the end of 1951 was 126,000 lower than the 306,000 in December, 1946. 

The average number of persons employed in manufacturing industries during 1951 was 897,000, an increase of 111,000 over the average for 1950, and the highest figure since 1944. Approximately 46 percent of the total 

1951 gain in average employment was contributed by the manufacturing industries.

The 1951 pattern in manufacturing employment closely resembled that for 1950. In neither year was there a prolonged winter slump; on the contrary, employment in each year started from a low point in January, rising without interruption until it reached a peak in August, then subsided gradually until the end of the year. 

Employment in the nondurable goods group was higher in every month of 1951 than in the comparable 1950 month, with the exception of November. Relatively sharp drops in the canning and preserving of fruits, vegetables and fish in November, 1951, depressed the employment level in nondurable goods during that month to a point somewhat below the 

November, 1950, figure.

Employment Gains in Aircraft Industry 

In the durable goods manufacturing group, employment rose steadily from January through August, 1951. It receded slightly in September owing to a work stoppage in the aircraft industry, resumed its upward trend in October and November, and maintained the November level in December. Although the rise in durable goods manufacturing employment was led by the aircraft industry, most other industries participated. 

One notable exception was the automobile industry in which employment declined moderately during 1951; from June through December automobile employment was below the level for the comparable 1950 months. 

Employment in agriculture, forestry, and fishing established a record peak of 547,000 in October, 1951, exceeding slightly the previous record established two years earlier. A substantial increase in cotton acreage in 

1951, combined with generally favorable weather during the harvest season, stimulated employment on California farms. 

During the first eight months of 1951, employment in contract construction remained well above the 1950 level. In September, however, construction employment started to decline and in October was 11,000

below the record high of 297,000 established a year earlier. Further declines occurred in November and December, primarily as a result of unfavorable weather conditions. 

Employment in trade establishments increased only moderately during most of 1951. As a result, during the latter part of the year, trade employment was somewhat below the level for the comparable months in 1950 when the wave of consumer buying which followed the beginning of hostilities in Korea provided a sharp stimulus to trade. Holiday buying in December, 1951, however, brought employment in trade to the high level of 1,047,000 or 7,000 above that for December, 1950.

Government employment climbed steadily during the first six months of 1951 primarily because of the continued expansion of the civilian work forces in military establishments. In April, 1951, a new record high of 

579,000 was established, exceeding by 7,000 the previous record established in December, 1950. This upward trend continued into May and 

June. After a slight decline in July, government employment continued upward in August and September, dipped slightly in October and Novemper, then experienced the usual sharp rise in December as post offices employed many temporary holiday workers. Employment in government averaged 587,000 persons in 1951, 64,000 above the 1950 monthly average. 

Employment in the service industries tended to increase moderately during 1951 and averaged 735,000 persons over the 12-month period. This was 11,000 higher than the average for the previous year. The number of workers employed in transportation, communication and utilities likevise followed a moderate upward trend during the year and averaged 27,000 or 10,000 above the average number employed in 1950. Employnent in mineral extraction showed no appreciable change during 1951. 

The number employed in finance, insurance and real estate grew modertely during the year.

CHAPTER I PROGRAMS AND ORGANIZATION Functions and Objectives of Department 

The California Department of Employment, which was established by and administers the Unemployment Insurance Act, has five basic functions. They are the operation of a public employment service; the expansion of employment opportunities; the collection of taxes from employers and employees; the payment of unemployment insurance; and the payment of disability insurance.

These functions are performed to fulfill the four basic objectives of the California Unemployment Insurance Act. These objectives are as follows: 

1. To assist in the prompt employment of persons seeking work and to assist employers to obtain qualified employees. 

2. To lessen the hardships to the involuntarily unemployed. 

3. To stabilize purchasing power and thus halt the spread of unemployment and the economic disruption it causes. 

4. To lessen the need for public relief and charity. 

The manner in which the department performed the functions identified above during 1951 is shown in the following very brief summary of its activities. During the year 1951, the department placed approximately 

940,000 persons in employment; it collected 182.6 million dollars in employer contributions and 34.1 million dollars in employee contributions, and it paid 95.1 million dollars in unemployment insurance benefits and 

24.3 million dollars in disability insurance benefits. In addition, through its Division of Development and Stabilization of Employment, the department encouraged full participation of small business in the national defense program by assuming a liaison role between small business, large defense producers, and government procurement officers. In carrying out this activity, the division has been helpful to manufacturers in locating plant sites and labor pools and in acquainting procurement officers with sources of materials. 

Organization of the Department

The Department of Employment is state-wide in its organization, as it is in its functions and activities. The department is composed of the California Employment Stabilization Commission, the California Unemployment Insurance Appeals Board, the director of the department, and six

དྲ divisions. A State Advisory Council on Employment Service is established by law and has a functional relationship to the department. 

The director, who is the chief administrative officer of the department, is appointed by the Governor from among the membership of the commission and currently is the Chief of the Division of Public Employment Offices and Benefit Payments. The deputy director is also chosen from among the commissioners and currently is the Chief of the Division of Accounts and Tax Collections. The three members of the California 

Unemployment Insurance Appeals Board constitute the remainder of the commission. The commission is responsible for studying and making recommendations which would tend to promote employment, reduce or prevent unemployment or provide economic security through social insurance; and for protecting the solvency of the unemployment and disability funds. 

The three members of the California Unemployment Insurance Appeals

Board constitute the final administrative body vested by law with the responsibility of reviewing and deciding appealed matters relating to disputed unemployment insurance and disability benefit determinations and employer tax liability. The appeals board also exercises general administrative direction over the operations of the entire Division of Appeals including area offices of that division. The appeals board also hears appeals from rules and regulations proposed by the director. Members of the board are appointed by the Governor for four-year terms. 

The State Advisory Council on Employment Service is composed of four representatives of the public, four of labor, and four of management. 

Functions of the council include making recommendations to the director regarding employment service problems and assuring impartiality, neutrality and freedom from political influence in the solution of such problems.

The Division of Public Employment Offices and Benefit Payments and the Division of Accounts and Tax Collections are established by law. Each is headed by a chief who is appointed by the Governor and serves at his pleasure.

Other divisions within the department are the Division of Disability and Hospital Benefits, the Division of Development and Stabilization of Employment, the Division of Appeals, and the Division of Administrative 

Services. Three staff sections within the department are attached to the office of the director. They are the Legal Section, the Research and 

Statistics Section, and the Public Information and Education Section. 

A major reorganization of the Division of Public Employment Offices and Benefit Payments occurred during 1951 as the direct result of the national defense mobilization effort. This reorganization was prefaced by action of the United States Secretary of Labor in establishing, in July, "Manpower Areas" in the metropolitan areas of Los Angeles, San Francisco and San Diego. The principal administrative officer of the Division of 

Public Employment Offices and Benefit Payments in each of these areas

was designated as chairman of a permanent advisory committee composed of seven labor representatives and seven management representatives. 

One important function of the area manpower committees is to implement the policy of bringing the work to the worker, rather than moving the worker to the defense plant.

On October 1st, field operations of the division were reorganized in order to ensure that departmental activities in each community could be focussed on any local manpower mobilization problems growing out of partial mobilization. The former division of the State into three administrative areas (each of which not only had included several industrial communities but also had served a far-flung group of agricultural communities) was abandoned. Each of the three major metropolitan communities-Los Angeles, San Francisco, and San Diego-was made a separate administrative area under an area manpower director for defense mobilization. Field services in all other communities were grouped together under eight separate supervisory districts, reporting directly to central office in Sacramento, in order to make sure that the department could provide a fully integrated service that met the special needs of each community. Since the most critical manpower problems expected in these communities relate to agriculture, food processing, and other industries with sizeable seasonal needs, supervision of the offices serving them was integrated with the supervision of the Farm Placement Service. 

There is an identified Farm Placement Service within the department, for which the State Board of Agriculture acts as an advisory group. 

The reorganization enabled the department to meet the manpower requirements of defense mobilization more easily than could have been anticipated a year and a half ago. The manpower mobilization organizations, based on community labor and management participation, have contributed their share to this accomplishment, and put the communities and the department in a state of readiness to meet any problems that may develop in the future.

Of importance also to the department was the appointment by Governor Warren of a Study Commission on Unemployment Insurance, composed of seven members. The commission is examining the entire structure of the unemployment insurance system, drawing upon the accumulated experience of 16 years of operation, and will recommend to Governor Warren such methods of reshaping the system as it may conclude are necessary. The work of the commission will probably extend over a period of one year to a year and a half. 

The need for such a study commission has been evident for some time. 

Sixteen years have brought many changes in the economy of the State and one of the basic objectives of the study commission is to determine whether the unemployment insurance system adequately reflects the current economic situation and, if not, how it can be made to do so. 

The department has made available a competent technical staff to assist the commission. 10

Personnel and Facilities 

The Department of Employment opened the year 1951 with a total of 4,346 employees and concluded the year with 4,125 employees. Thus, during the year the department's work force was reduced by 221 persons. Of the total work force, approximately one-third are stationed in central office in Sacramento and the other two-thirds are stationed in the department's various field offices. The department had a total of 218 offices operating at some time during the year 1951. The breakdown of departmental offices is as follows: central office, 1; farm placement (yearround offices), 17; farm placement (seasonal offices), 15; farm placement (trailer-hut locations), 18; local offices (full functioning), 103; itinerant offices (claims offices), 7; audit district offices, 37; disability insurance offices, 16; and area administrative offices, 4. 

Administrative expenditures by the department during 1951 amounted to $20,091,830. Of this total, personal services accounted for $15,774,070 and nonpersonal services accounted for $4,317,760. 

Most phases of the department's operations are financed by the United 

States Department of Labor, Bureau of Employment Security. Federal funds granted to the department during 1951 totaled $17,680,101 leaving a total of $2,411,729 supplied from state funds. State funds came from three sources, the Department of Employment Contingent Fund providing $28,182, the Disability Fund providing $2,377,738, and the old age and survivor's insurance grant providing $5,809. 

The building program to provide state-owned office buildings for the field offices of the department in place of leased premises continued during 1951. The funds for this purpose have been secured through legislative appropriations from the Department of Employment Contingent Fund. These amounts will be restored to the contingent fund since the 

Federal Bureau of Employment Security will continue to provide a rental allowance for each of these state-owned buildings until the total cost of the project is amortized. Thereafter, the only expense will be for maintenance of the premises. A savings in governmental costs will therefore result from this program. In addition, operations can be carried out more efficiently in the new buildings, since they are specifically designed for departmental needs, and a better service to the public will result. 0 11

CHAPTER II LEGISLATION, REGULATIONS AND JUDICIAL DECISIONS 

New and Amended Legislation and Regulations The California Legislature, during its 1951 Session, made many changes in the Unemployment Insurance Act. Although many amendments were technical in nature, several are of major importance to either employers or employees, or both. Most of these changes in the law also required changes in the department's regulations, which are contained in Title 22, California Administrative Code. 

Some modifications were made in the coverage provisions of the law. 

Real estate salesmen who are licensed by the State of California and who are remunerated solely by commission were excluded. Also excluded are students employed by a school, college, or university regardless of whether the school is exempt from federal income tax and regardless of the wages paid to the student.

The previous administrative definition of agricultural labor which was contained in the department's regulations and the Tax Liability Manual was written into the act and, therefore, was deleted from the regulations. 

As a result, in the future only the Legislature may change the existing coverage or exemptions with respect to agricultural labor. Casual labor is no longer excluded if the individual is paid $50 or more in a calendar quarter and he has worked in at least 24 days during that quarter or the previous calendar quarter. Remuneration which an employee of an organization exempt from federal income tax may receive and still be excluded from the Unemployment Insurance Act was raised from $45 to $50. 

Provisions relative to taxable wages were also amended. Most important of these changes is that which provides that an employer who acquires all or substantially all of another's trade or business may include the wages paid by the predecessor in arriving at the $3,000 limitation on taxable. wages for employees retained by the acquiring employer. This differs from the previous law wherein each employer was required to make contributions on the first $3,000 paid to an individual by him. Other changes brought the California law into conformity with the Federal Unemployment Tax Act in relation to payments made to or on behalf of employees under plans or systems for retirement, sickness or accident, medical or hospital expenses, death, or other similar benefits.

Two of the most important changes relate to charges which may not be made to employers' reserve accounts and to the transfer of employers' reserve accounts. 12

The concept of unchargeable benefits was completely changed. Under the previous law, no benefits were chargeable to the reserve account of any base period employer if the claimant was disqualified for a voluntary quit, or for a discharge for misconduct connected with his most recent employment. Conversely, all benefits were chargeable if the claimant was not disqualified for either of these two reasons. The new provision, which became effective with new claims filed after January 1, 1952, provides that any employer who is entitled to receive a notice of a new or additional claim or a notice of computation may, in response to such notice, submit information disclosing whether the claimant voluntarily quit that employer without good cause or was discharged for misconduct by that employer. The department will consider such information and issue a ruling as to the cause of the separation. If the ruling is that the claimant did voluntarily quit without good cause or was discharged for misconduct, benefits based on wages earned from that employer prior to the separation shall not be charged to his account. Such rulings are appealable in the same manner as appeals from determinations on claims. In connection with this amendment, the Director of Employment adopted a regulation establishing the method by which a ruling is to be requested. 

Reserve account transfers, which formerly were on a mandatory basis, are now on a voluntary basis. Under the amendment, when a change in the ownership of a trade or business occurs, the successor employer may request the department to transfer to him immediately the reserve account of the predecessor. Such an application must be filed within 90 days of the acquisition and the employer must furnish the information required by the department to determine the proper amount of the reserve to be transferred. Applications for transfer may also be made after the 90-day period and prior to the cancellation of the old reserve account, if the predecessor did not reacquire the reserve or, in the case of a severable portion acquisition, if the predecessor did not continue in business. Transfers made in such cases will be effective as of the calendar quarter next succeeding the date of the application. This change also resulted in an amendment to the department's regulations. 

An important new section was added in relation to unemployment insurance payments. This new section provides that a claimant must earn during the year that he draws unemployment insurance the minimum amount of wages for a valid claim in order to receive benefits during the following year. Previously, it was not necessary for a claimant to work during his benefit year in order to qualify for a claim the next year. This section became effective with respect to claims filed after January 1, 1952. 

Disability insurance claims are not affected by this section, however. Two new regulations were adopted to implement this new section of the law. 

Another change increased to 10 days from the date of mailing of the claims notice, the time limit within which an employing unit shall submit information bearing upon a claimant's eligibility. Also increased to 

10 days was the time within which appeals from benefit determinations may be made. Similar changes were made in the applicable regulations. 13

Another amendment redefined the term "trainee" to include individuals who entered military service after June 1, 1950, and whose military service terminated on or before July 1, 1953. This change protects the benefit rights of such persons with respect to any unexpired benefit years and wage credits which they had prior to entry into military service. 

Several major amendments were enacted relating to disability benefits. 

Most important is that which added five additional steps of weekly benefit amounts, raising the maximum weekly benefit payable from $25 to $30. 

This change also became effective on January 1, 1952. Increased high quarter base period earnings are required to qualify for the higher benefit amounts.

Another amendment provided that an individual who is receiving any type of wage payment from his employer while he is disabled may also receive disability benefits in an amount which when added to such payments from the employer does not exceed 70 percent of his wages received immediately preceding disability. Corresponding with that amendment is another which provided that an individual who is in a hospital is entitled to receive the hospital benefits of $8 a day regardless of any amount of remuneration of any type which he may be receiving from his employer during his disability. These legislative actions caused an amendment to one regulation to redefine certain terms and the repeal of another regulation which was no longer necessary.

A further amendment provided for payment of hospital benefits directly to the hospital if the claimant has first given his consent to the department to do so. A regulation was also amended to permit hospitals to submit certifications that the assignments of benefits had been obtained from claimants.

Two other amendments relating to hospital benefits were also enacted. 

The first permits payment of the $8 hospital benefit for a period of less than 24 hours if the claimant is charged a full day's rate by the hospital for that period. The second precludes the payment of hospital benefits to an individual who is furnished or compensated for hospitalization under a workmen's compensation law or any employer liability law if he is receiving or is eligible to receive cash payments under such laws. 

Another change permits payment of disability benefits to an individual who is involved in a trade dispute provided that he establishes that his disability resulted from an accident or that it required a period of hospitalization and that his disability was not caused by and did not arise out of the trade dispute. A regulation dealing with this subject was repealed as being no longer required.

A new section was added which permits an individual who adheres to a faith which depends upon prayer for healing to voluntarily exempt himself from wage-earner contributions and from receiving disability benefits. 

In order to obtain the exemption, the individual must file a prescribed statement with the department and with each of his employers. 14

Another new section, which became effective on January 1, 1952, provides that all employees to whom a voluntary plan of disability insurance is available may be covered automatically if 85 percent of such employees have consented to the plan. Provision is also made for the exclusion of any person who specifically rejects the plan and for the withdrawal of any employee from the plan upon giving reasonable written notice. This amendment was implemented by revising an existing regulation. 

The appeals board was given authority in another amendment to prescribe by regulation the time, manner, method and procedure under which the question of whether the department or an insurance carrier is to pay a disability benefit claim will be determined. 

During the year, the Director of Employment amended several other regulations which deal with subjects not affected by legislative action. 

One provides for a segregation of exempt and nonexempt employment on the basis of the employer's operation with respect to the percentage of services performed in such employment, rather than the hours performed in both types of employment by each individual. 

Another defines the terms "additional claim" and "reopened claim." An additional claim certifies to a week of unemployment which follows a period of employment subsequent to the filing of the first claim. A reopened claim means a claim for benefits for a week of unemployment following a period of ineligibility or disqualification.

A third amendment specifies that a notice of the filing of a new or additional claim shall be given the claimant's most recent employing unit, and that a notice of computation shall be sent to each of the claimant's base period employers.

The fourth amendment provides that voluntary plans of disability insurance for separate establishments may only be approved on a geographical basis. This amendment also provided that in certain circumstances pre-existing disabilities must be covered by the voluntary plan. 

Appeals Board Decisions 

The California Unemployment Insurance Appeals Board issued several important decisions during the year which affected various phases of the department's operations.

Three tax decisions dealt with application of the agricultural labor exemption to certain services. In Tax Decisions No. 1187 and No. 1188, the board held that raising and slaughtering of rabbits and raising of chinchillas do not constitute exempt agricultural labor because neither rabbits nor chinchillas are "livestock" within the meaning of the law. In 

Tax Decision No. 1314, the board held that services performed in extracting, bottling and marketing apple cider are not exempt as agricultural labor. 

A fourth tax decision, No. 1257, held that the employer was estopped to claim a refund of contributions paid on wages of exempt agricultural labor which the employer knew was exempt and which subjected the 15

department to potential benefit payments to the employees for whom the wages were reported.

In April, the department put into effect Benefit Decision No. 5643 which had been adopted in the latter part of 1950. The question in this case was whether Section 58 (a) (2) of the act requires the department, prior to the filing of a valid claim for benefits, to issue a determination whenever an employer submits information to the department in compliance with the section. The board held that a determination did not have to be issued but that the employer must be notified in conformance with the section.

Previously, when a protest was received by the department from an employer and no valid claim was on file, the employer's original protest was returned to him. He was advised to resubmit his protest if and when he was notified that a claim had been filed, and at that time a determination would be made by the department as to whether the protesting employer was the most recent employer and whether the claimant was eligible or ineligible for benefits under Section 58(a)(1) or 58(a)(2).

Under the new procedure, when the department receives a protest, and there is no valid claim on file, it issues to the employer a notice as to whether the information submitted was sufficient to establish a prima facie case for disqualification. The employer's separation notice is retained for about 19 months and, if a valid claim for benefits is filed during this period, the department acts upon the notice and issues the appropriate determination.

The major change in the department was the setting up of files and controls so as to properly process the information submitted by employers.

Thirty-eight decisions of the appeals board issued during 1951 were of sufficient significance to be included as guides to field offices in the determination process. Among these decisions were two which affirmed and two which reversed the department's holdings in important cases. The decisions which reversed the department resulted in operational or administrative changes.

The department was reversed in Benefit Decisions No. 5691 and No. 

5678. The holding in the former case was that a period of disqualification assessed under Section 58 (b) must begin with the week subsequent to the occurrence of the cause of the disqualification in which the claimant first files a valid registration for work. The latter case held that under the claim procedure for partial unemployment a claimant continues with the same week until again re-employed and that a registration for work on any day of the week in effect is a registration on the first day of the week. 

The department was upheld in Benefit Decisions No. 5709 and No. 5711.

The holding in the former affirmed the department's policy of beginning a week of unemployment on the date the claim is filed regardless of the claimant's availability for work. The latter decision affirmed the operating procedure of the local office of marking a claimant's identification card to indicate that instructions were issued for the claimant to register for work. 16

Court Decisions 

During 1951, there were several court decisions in various courts in which the Department of Employment was involved. 

Five cases concerned the contributions provisions of the Unemployment Insurance Act, one of which was decided by the United States Supreme Court. In this case, the Supreme Court upheld a National Labor 

Relations Board decision that unemployment compensation benefits received by an individual who had been discriminatorily discharged should not be deducted from back pay awards to such employees. The court noted that some states permit the recoupment of benefits paid during a period for which the National Labor Relations Board subsequently awards back pay. (NLRB v. Gullett Gin Co., U. S. Supreme Court.) 

The issue of whether a nonagricultural employer was liable for contributions on the wages paid to Mexican nationals in his employ was decided in another case. The district court of appeal held that the employer was liable. The court held that it made no difference that the Mexican nationals could not receive benefits in that they did not earn enough wages. The court also refused to follow the argument that the Mexican nationals were in the employ of a state agency. (Vollman v. CESC, 104 

C. A. 2d 93, 231 P. 2d 137.)

Status of certain hay baling operations was determined in another decision. The district court of appeal held that contract hay baling, that is, a person hiring himself out to bale the hay of others on their farms, is not exempt as agricultural labor. Hay baling was held not to be harvesting, but rather to come under former subdivision (2) of Rule 7.1 of Title 22, C. A. C., which reads: "“* * * drying, processing, packing, packaging ** of materials which are produced on the farm." Under this section, to be exempt, the hay baling would have had to be performed by an employee of the owner or tenant of the farm on which the materials were produced. (Enos v. CESC, 101 C. A. 2d 606, 225 P. 2d 641.)

Another decision dealt with a proceeding for a writ of mandate to compel a sheriff to pay the department money held by him pursuant to a levy of a warrant issued by the department for the collection of contributions owed by an employer. The district court of appeal denied the writ because the employer had assigned the funds before the department had issued its process against the funds. (People v. Biscailuz, 107 A. C. A. 

102, 236 P. 2d 59.)

The fifth tax case involved an earlier proceeding in which the plaintiff had obtained judgment directing the department to cancel certain charges to its reserve account. Plaintiff then brought a contempt proceeding alleging that the judgment had not been carried out. The trial court held that the judgment was carried out and discharged the defendant. Plaintiff appealed to the California Supreme Court, which held that judgments in contempt cases are not appealable and that the proper remedy is certiorari and where applicable habeus corpus. (Breuner v. Bryant, 36 C. 2d 

877, 229 P. 2d 356.) 17

An appellate court decision barred the department from prosecuting an individual under a felony section of the Penal Code for presenting a false and fraudulent claim for unemployment insurance. The court held that the Penal Code section was pro tanto repealed by Section 101(a) of the Unemployment Insurance Act, which provides that it shall be a misdemeanor to wilfully make a false statement or representation or knowingly fail to disclose a material fact in obtaining benefits. Therefore, an individual must be prosecuted under Section 101(a) when he makes a false claim for benefits. (People v. Hayton, 106 A. C. A. 136, 234 P. 

2d 720.)

Two cases dealing with the disability insurance system were decided during the year. In one, the Supreme Court held that the department was entitled to a lien for unemployment compensation disability benefits. against an award of workmen's compensation for permanent disability. (Bryant v. I. A. C., 37 A. C. 215, 231 P. 2d 32.)

In the other case, the court upheld the department's denial of disability benefits to a claimant who had been committed to a state hospital as a sexual psychopath. The claim had been denied because the claimant's unemployment was due to confinement rather than to a disability. While confined he was able and did perform his customary work and therefore was not disabled under the law. (“Claimant” v. CESĆ, Santa Barbara Superior Court; No. 45639.) 18

CHAPTER III SUMMARY OF EMPLOYMENT SERVICE ACTIVITIES Methods and Procedural Changes 

Employment service activities are conducted by the Division of Public 

Employment Offices and Benefit Payments. Broadly speaking, defense manpower needs prompted the more significant changes in employment service policies and operating procedures during 1951. The purpose of these changes was to insure a state of readiness in all offices during partial manpower mobilization. To achieve this end, improvement of all local office programs and functions was sought, new programs undertaken, and liaison with other agencies and groups strengthened. 

In March it became apparent that there were many potential members of the labor force, such as older workers, housewives, and new entrants, who were interested in entering the labor force only if defense work was available. When such individuals reported to the local offices, a complete record of their skills was taken and filed in a special “mobilization reserve file." This file is being used whenever defense work opportunities become available for which the worker is qualified. This preparatory move to register potential defense workers was supplemented by appropriate newspaper and radio publicity. 

In October defense manpower needs resulted in a major change in department policy regarding services to applicants who are members of minority groups. Local offices are required to refer applicants to jobs only on the basis of the applicants' qualifications to perform the work. This policy is in conformance with the armed services procurement regulations, which themselves prohibit discrimination against any employee or applicant for employment because of race, creed, color, or national origin with respect to government defense prime contracts or subcontracts. 

The tightening of labor market conditions throughout California because of increasing defense manpower needs resulted in a number of policy and operational changes designed to place workers on the job with the least delay. In January, the department began direct recruitment for civilian personnel needed by the Department of the Army when applicants could not be found on Federal Civil Service Commission registers. 

A similar agreement was reached in January with the Railroad Retirement Board, which eliminated prior clearance before referring workers to railroad establishments for employment. In June, the department took its part in a national all-out recruiting program for overseas openings with the Army in Japan, Korea and Okinawa. 

The replacement program of the various branches of the armed services produced a large number of discharges. In September, the department 19

began furnishing service at military separation centers for such dischargees, including employment counseling and labor market information. 

A representative assigned to separation centers provided information and assistance to military interviewers and counselors. Dischargees who were looking for work were directed to report to a local office. In September, instructions to the local offices provided that exservicemen and women who served on or after June 27, 1950, were to be regarded as veterans for all operating purposes.

In March, the Department of Commerce provided the department with detailed information on defense mobilization contracts, and it was agreed that the Department of Commerce could refer inquiries of employers regarding defense contracts to local offices. During the same month, the department distributed a booklet entitled "A Guide to Defense Mobiliza tion Contracts" which describes "who does the buying, where they are located and what they buy with regard to production required for the national defense effort." This booklet is being used by employer relations interviewers to increase their services to employers participating in the defense effort.

In May, the first issue of the list of California firms certified as "necessary in whole or in part in the interest of national defense during the emergency period" was released by the Defense Production Administra tion. The department uses this list as a means of advising individuals and groups within the community of those firms planning to expand their plant or equipment facilities. 

In September, the department entered into an agreement with the Selective Service Board to release information on labor market conditions in agricultural areas. Under this agreement, this information was to be used by local draft boards in arriving at deferment policies. 

Services were also sharpened in the field of employment counseling, selective placement, and testing to gear these activities to the objectives of a partial manpower mobilization economy. Added program emphasis was also placed on the speedy and efficient placement of graduates in suitable work, in order to utilize the youth of the State in meeting the growing needs of defense production.

In addition to employment service changes directly connected with defense manpower mobilization, the department in 1951 extended the scope of its employment services through some major procedural and operational developments.

Beginning in March, local offices assisted the Chairman of the Governor's Committee on Displaced Persons in verifying the existence of employment opportunities which had been represented to the committee as commitments to hire displaced persons.

Since 1947, the department has had a formal agreement with the State Personnel Board to administer tests to candidates for civil service positions in clerical classes. By 1951, the program had expanded to include clerks, typists, stenographers, key punch and tabulating machine operators, and 20

also to include referral of successful candidates by the local office to state agencies. In January, the program was further expanded to permit the local office to perform these test services for county welfare departments with merit systems. In May, the program was augmented to provide these services for federal agencies.

The California farm labor program advanced during 1951 through adjustments in assignments whereby specialized farm placement personnel participated more directly in the supervision of farm labor recruitment and placement operations by local offices. A federal directive naming agriculture among those industries eligible to receive "preferential service" in the defense manpower mobilization effort effected improved attention to farm placement activities by local offices of the Department of Employment.

The major revision of administrative areas described in Chapter I provided for further strengthening of the farm labor program. 

The solution of farm placement problems was expedited through improved and more direct lines of communication between the state office and local areas. Provisions for rapid communication was necessary because of local labor recruitment emergencies requiring assistance from the state level, and because of the frequent changes in state and national policies and regulations to be passed on to the local operating point in effecting manpower mobilization, and in arranging for supplemental foreign labor. 

The pre-season analysis of the expected farm labor supply, in relation to the expected rise in labor requirements, indicated a possible shortage of 75,000 farm workers by the October harvest peak. It was estimated that from 40,000 to 50,000 supplemental foreign workers would be needed to meet that part of the total expected shortage for which there seemed no likely source of domestic labor. When the agricultural employment peak was reached in October, there were approximately 528,500 persons working on farms, of whom 36,200 were Mexican nationals, and there was a concurrent demand for approximately 25,000 additional workers who were not available.

Statistical Summary of Activities 

Employment service activities during 1951 were to a large extent influenced by the favorable employment and unemployment conditions in that year and by the trends in the defense effort. Only about 702.000 persons filed new job applications with the department in 1951, 12 percent fewer than in 1950 and fewer than in any year since the end of World 

War II. Men filed 370,000 applications in 1951, down by 21 percent, and women 332,000, about the same number as in 1950. Veterans, who had filed 183,000 applications in 1950, filed only 135,000 in 1951.

Referral to jobs rose by 13 percent to a total of 1,464,000 and placements made by local offices during 1951 totaled 940,000, an increase of 14 percent over 1950.

Increasing farm production accounted for a gain of 14 percent in farm referrals and a 17 percent rise in agricultural placements. 21

Nonagricultural referrals rose by 12 percent and nonagricultural placements gained by 11 percent. The gain in placements was recorded despite the fact that placements during the second half of 1951 were fewer than those in the corresponding period of 1950. 

About 314,000 men and 165,000 women were placed in nonfarm jobs in 1951, the increase from the 1950 levels being a bit larger for men than for women, although the men's advantage was not so great in 1951 as in 

1950. Other indications of a tightening labor market in California were the large increase in placements of youth less than 21 years of age (up 45 percent) and of persons 45 years old or older (which rose by 38 percent).

About 148,000 veterans were placed in nonfarm jobs, approximately the same number as last year. Veteran placements did not rise by the same proportion as placements of other persons because of the substantial decline in the number of veteran job applicants. The department's intensified veteran placement activity during 1950 contributed to the decline of veteran applicants in 1951. About 28 percent of all job applicants referred to jobs were veterans while 31 percent of the people placed in nonfarm jobs were veterans.

Placements of handicapped workers rose even more sharply than total placements, increasing 30 percent over the 1950 figure to a total of 18,909. 

Fifty-eight percent of the handicapped workers placed were veterans and total placements of handicapped veterans rose 26 percent over the previous year. Placements of nonveteran handicapped workers increased 36 percent over 1950.

Shortages of qualified applicants for skilled and professional and managerial job classifications caused declines in the number of placements in these two occupational groups in 1951. About 36,000 skilled workers were placed in 1951, compared with 39,000 in 1950, and 7,400 professional and managerial personnel in 1951, compared with 8,100 in 1950. There were increases in all other major occupational groups. 

Of the 479,000 placements made in nonfarm jobs in 1951, 147,000 were made in manufacturing, up by 15 percent, and 109,000 in wholesale and retail trade, up by 11 percent.

Job openings received increased by a much larger margin than either referrals or placements, and in 1951 reached a record figure of 1,347,000 for the post-war period, up by 30 percent. Agricultural job openings were up by 23 percent while nonfarm openings rose by 35 percent. This increase was experienced notwithstanding a curtailment in employer visiting activities, both agricultural and nonagricultural. In 1951, department representatives made 171,000 visits to employers, 23 percent fewer than in the preceding year.

Counseling interviews declined to 85,000 in 1951, about 16 percent below the previous year's level. The drop affected men only, as counseling interviews to women-numbering 35,000-rose by 3 percent over the high figure for 1950. 22

CHAPTER IV SUMMARY OF TAX COLLECTION ACTIVITIES AND FUND BALANCES Operational Changes 

Operations of the Division of Accounts and Tax Collections were affected to a considerable extent during 1951 by an amendment to the 

Federal Insurance Contributions Act adopted by Congress during 1950. 

This amendment raised the amount of wages on which contributions were due under the Federal Insurance Contributions Act from $3,000 to $3,600 annually. Although the California Unemployment Insurance Act retained the $3,000 limitation and the department extensively publicized the fact, a considerable number of returns was received by the department on which it was apparent that the taxpayer believed the taxable limit had been raised under the California law. When such cases were noted, the employers were informed of the overpayment and adjustments were made on later contribution returns.

A material reduction in the number of corrections in employer tax payments and returns, as well as in correspondence with employers, followed the amendment to Section 11 of the Unemployment Insurance Act which was passed by the State Legislature in January. The amendment, which became effective January 5th, provides that a successor employer could take into his computation of the $3,000 limitation for each employee the wages paid to each such employee by the predecessor employer. Previously, an employer who acquired the business of another employer was required to pay contributions on the first $3,000 he paid to his employees during the year without regard to the wages paid by the predecessor employer.

The number of reserve account transfers required under the act was reduced as the result of the amendment to Section 41.5 which became effective during September. Under the law as amended, the successor employer must request such a transfer before the transfer may be made. 

The prior law made the transfer mandatory. The difficulty with the prior law arose in those situations where a severable portion of a business was sold or transferred. The former law required the procurement of pay roll ratios and other details often difficult to obtain. The amended law provides that such a transfer will be made only on the taxpayer's request. 

Under the amended law, the employer has the right to decide whether or not he wants the reserve account balance of his predecessor. In many instances, the balance is not favorable and it is to the advantage of an employer to decide that he will not request a transfer. 23

On July 1, 1951, the department added a test audit operation to its program. Each audit begins as a test audit. If no discrepancies are disclosed, the assignment is closed as a test audit. However, if the test audit indicates discrepancies, a complete audit is made. As a test audit requires less time than the complete audit, the rate at which audits were made was gradually increased in the last six months. Test audits completed during the year totaled 1,120 and resulted in recovery to the department of $32,006. 

During 1951, the department made a complete audit of 4,650 employer accounts. As the result of a better selection of accounts chosen for audit, there was an increase of 15 percent over 1950 in the number of audits in which errors in reporting were discovered. These audits resulted in a recovery to the department of $628,580, or an average of $135.18 per audit. 

The department made 188 audits for other states (principally Oregon, Washington, Arizona, New York and Illinois) where the employers had moved to California; and other states made 66 audits for California.

Contribution Rates 

The number of subject employers at the end of 1951 was slightly higher than at the end of 1950-243,000 in 1951 compared to 238,000 the year before. Of the 230,000 that were active both on June 30, 1950, and on 

December 31, 1950, at which time they were assigned contribution rates for 1951, 82,000 had insufficient experience to be eligible for a reduced rate. This figure of unrated employers was smaller by 21,000 than the 

103,000 unrated employers as of June 30, 1949. All the unrated employers paid the general rate of 2.7 percent of taxable wages into the unemployment fund. Employers who became subject during 1951 also paid the general rate of 2.7 percent.

Of the rated employers, about 85,000 were assigned the general rate of 

2.7 percent, about 16,000 more than a year previous. These employers paid at the general rate because the ratios of their reserve account balances to their average base pay rolls were not high enough to qualify them for reduced rates. Reduced rates ranging from 1.0 to 2.5 percent for 1951 were assigned to 64,000 employers, compared to 57,000 the previous year. 

However, the distribution of this group of employers was largely concentrated at rate levels 1.0 and 1.5 percent, whereas during 1950 it was concentrated at the rate levels 2.0 and 2.5 percent. As a result of this shift, the average contribution rate for 1951 was 2.3 percent of taxable wages, somewhat smaller than the 2.4 percent for 1950.

Increasing wage rates and soaring employment caused an increase in employers' contributions to the unemployment insurance fund of 19 percent in 1951, to a total of 182.6 million dollars, the highest figure for any year since enactment of the Unemployment Insurance Act. This item of revenue represented 2.3 percent of the estimated 7.9 billion dollars in taxable wages paid by subject employers in 1951.

Contributions were required of an average of about 255,000 employers each quarter-employers who were subject to the act at some time during 24

the quarter. 

Contribution report delinquencies averaged 9,700 per quarter, or an average of 3.8 percent of the subject employers. 

A total of 39 employers was brought to trial during 1951 and charged with misdemeanors under the two sections of the act which make it unlawful to fail or neglect to furnish required reports or to fail to pay employee contributions to the department. All of the 39 cases brought to trial resulted in convictions.

The year 1951 began with a total of 238,117 employers subject to the 

Unemployment Insurance Act. During the year 59,041 new registrations were added, of which 22,814 were ownership transfers of businesses previously established and subject to the act and 36,227 were new businesses. 

Another 6,342 employers were reinstated, thus bringing to 303,500 the total number of employers who were subject to the Unemployment Insurance Act at some time during 1951. From this total is subtracted the 

60,488 businesses which were deleted during the year, leaving a net total of 243,012 active employers at the close of 1951.

The Unemployment Insurance Act also requires a contribution from wage earners, amounting to 1 percent of the first $3,000 earned annually, which is paid into the State Disability Fund. Contributions paid by wage earners during 1951 totaled 34.1 million dollars, about 4 percent more than in 1950. This increase, which was experienced in the face of expanding voluntary plan coverage, was caused by raises in individual earnings. and by expanded employment among the employers who remained covered by the state plan. 

Fund Balances 

At the end of 1950 the Unemployment Fund had a balance of 573.9 million dollars. That amount, less approximately 113.1 million dollars which was available for transfer to the State Disabiltiy Fund, was less than 7.5 percent of taxable wages paid by subject employers during Fiscal

CHAPTER V SUMMARY OF UNEMPLOYMENT INSURANCE ACTIVITIES Methods and Procedural Changes 

The major legislative actions relating to the Unemployment Insurance Act which were discussed in Chapter II necessitated several changes in the unemployment insurance methods and procedures which are carried out by the Division of Public Employment Offices and Benefit Payments. 

A new procedure, which became effective January 1, 1952, relates to the new section of the act which provides that workers prove attachment to the labor force by earning "qualifying wages" between claims. Under the new procedure, determinations on qualifying wages are to be made in the local office. Such determinations are to be based on wage information obtained from the claimant, or on information requested from employers reflecting earnings not yet reported for the previous benefit year.

A second new procedure relates to the provision of the law which relieves employers of charges to their reserve accounts only if they provide the department facts regarding the separation of employees. The new local office procedure provides that when employers respond to notices of claims filed or of awards, the local office will make a "ruling" based on the facts submitted. If these rulings are favorable to the employers, their reserve accounts will not be charged with benefits paid to the claimants involved. In making such rulings, local offices are to be guided by the same factors they would consider in making eligibility determinations. 

This procedure also became effective January 1, 1952. 

New procedures were also established effective in September in relation to the extension of the time in which employers are permitted to respond to the notices of claims filed. 

Defense manpower activities had one direct effect upon unemployment insurance procedures. To meet a need arising from the Korean conflict, the Legislature reactivated provisions of the law which protect the wage credits of workers who are called into the armed forces during partial mobilization, many of whom are being released to the labor market at the expiration of limited enlistments. Unexpired benefits at the time of entrance into the armed forces were re-established, beginning with the first day of the first week following discharge, for individuals who entered military service after June 1, 1950, and whose military service was terminated on or before July 1, 1953.

In 1950 a successful program for recouping overpayments in amounts of $100 or less was conducted by means of civil actions in small claims 26

court. In March of 1951, the program was extended to include civil actions for recouping overpayments in amounts of $150 or more through the 

Attorney General's Office. 

In March, administrative decision was reached on releasing information to other agencies regarding unemployment insurance claimants. An agreement to exchange information with the Industrial Accident Commission was prompted by the need for correlating unemployment insurance and workmen's compensation claims. An agreement was also reached in March with the Veterans Administration, providing for the release of information on claimants for servicemen's readjustment allowances to organizations officially recognized by the Veterans Administration, such as the Red Cross, provided such organizations have a power of attorney from the claimant.

Statistical Summary of Activities 

Unemployment insurance payments made in 1951 reached a postwar low of 95.1 million dollars, a little more than half the 1950 figure and 

62 percent less than the record high 1949 payout. The postwar low was reached despite increasing coverage and the liberalized benefit formula which became first effective in 1948. Low unemployment throughout the year accounted for the favorable 1951 experience. In every month, the payout was lower than in the corresponding month the year before. 

Weeks of unemployment compensated in 1951 totaled 4.4 million weeks, the lowest level for the postwar period and only slightly more than onehalf the 1950 total. About 81,000 claimants exhausted their benefit awards during the year compared with 178,000 in 1950. The average length of benefits for compensated spells of unemployment was 5.2 weeks in 1951, substantially shorter than the 6.5 weeks in 1950. 

Claimants certified to 5.0 million continued weeks of unemployment, of which 4.6 million were compensable, and 418,000 were waiting period weeks.

Initial claims, representing new periods of unemployment, declined to 947,000, off by 32 percent from the 1950 figure. Included in the 1951 figure were 533,000 new claims, of which 445,000 had sufficient wage credits to establish benefit years, and 414,000 additional claims. The number of interstate claims filed by out-of-state residents against California amounted to about 6 percent of the total claim load. The disqualification rate runs at a considerable higher percentage on interstate claims than on intrastate claims although all interstate contacts are by mail. 

Twenty percent of the claimants who established benefit years in 1951 drew no benefits during the year, either because they found employment or left the labor market immediately upon establishing their benefit years. 

The comparable figure for 1950 was 12 percent. 

Approximately 146,000 claimants were disqualified during the year and were denied benefits for varying periods according to the causes of the disqualification. Nonavailability for work was the disqualifying issue in about 47,000 cases. Voluntarily quitting employment without good cause 27

resulted in 41,600 disqualifications. Almost 17,000 disqualifications resulted from failure to seek employment.

Disqualifications of claimants who had left their work because of a trade dispute totaled 5,311 during the year. Trade disputes reported, investigated, and determinations made totaled 629; employees involved totaled 52,580. Claims filed by employees involved in the trade disputes were slightly more than 7,000. 

Continuing efforts to insure that unemployment insurance was paid only to eligible claimants disclosed 13,324 cases in which claimants received benefits to which they were not entitled. Overpayments amounting to $763,743 were established during the year against these claimants. 

Collections of overpayments during the year totaled $708,786, while an additional $191,873 was waived because it would have been against equity and good conscience to require its repayment, or was written off as uncollectible. Of the total overpayment cases established, 2,697 were the result of wilful misrepresentations on the part of the claimant. 

The accumulated total of overpayments, which was $1,118,447 on January 1, 1951, had been reduced to $908,346 at the end of the year. 

The policy of attempting collection through civil actions filed in the small claims court in the various localities in California, inaugurated during the year 1950, was continued during the year 1951. During this year, a program was commenced whereby the Attorney General's Office would initiate civil suit to recover overpaid benefits where the claimants involved ignored the department's administrative demand for repayment. The number of cases referred to the Attorney General's Office for filing of suits totaled 461 involving $116,037. Suits filed totaled 271, involving $89,845. Judgments obtained totaled 61, involving $20,197. The differential between cases referred, suits filed, and judgments obtained, is primarily due to the failure of service of summons and judgments still pending at the close of the year. 

In addition to administrative action against claimants who had received benefits to which they were not entitled, the department successfully prosecuted 742 persons. Only five persons who were brought to trial were acquitted. Conviction of fraud resulted in the imposition of fines or jail sentences, or both, restitution of the money received and the loss of benefit rights based on wages earned prior to the commission of the illegal act.

The department continued in 1951 to act as agent of the Federal Government in the payment of servicemen's readjustment allowances to eligible veterans. During the year, unemployment and self-employment allowances paid to veterans totaled $144,000, a marked decrease from the 2.9 million dollars paid out in 1950 and the 34.5 million dollars disbursed in 1949. The rapid decline occurred because of the termination of allowance payments under the program for most veterans since July 15, 1949. 

The number of initial claims filed for unemployment allowances was 

1,515 in 1951; continued claims totaled 7,426. 28

CHAPTER VI SUMMARY OF DISABILITY INSURANCE ACTIVITIES Methods and Procedural Changes 

During the year 1951, emphasis was laid on analysis of disability insurance and hospital benefit claims procedures and methods to determine need for modification to meet legislation being considered during the early part of the year. This analysis led to improvement in the procedures used by voluntary plan underwriters, the Appeals Division, and the staff of the Division of Disability and Hospital Benefits in handling misfiled claims, and resulted in a substantial reduction in the work required in the preparation of the documents and filing appeals. Other changes resulted which streamlined existing methods.

Due to legislative and rule changes, the division revised its method of obtaining hospital confinement data from hospitals. Through cooperation with the California Hospital Association, the revised forms and procedures were developed and a program of visits to each hospital by staff of the division made it possible for the procedures to be in full operation on September 22, 1951.

The establishment of a voluntary plan file against which all state disability claims can be checked was begun in October. This file makes it possible to identify misfiled claims and permits their prompt referral to the appropriate underwriter for payment. 

All other procedural changes and forms for major legislation effective January 1, 1952, were developed during the year, and training of district office staff was completed in December.

In November and December, the division received notice from insurers that they would not amend some of their plans to conform to new legislation effective January 1, 1952. This resulted in notices of withdrawal of approval being issued to approximately 3,000 employers. At the end of 

1951, after approval had been withdrawn from those plans that could not meet the new benefit requirements, a total of 34,404 voluntary plans was in force. One year earlier, 35,851 plans were in effect and at the end of 

November, 1951, 37,668 plans were in force. About 56 percent of taxable wages paid in 1951 were subject to voluntary plan contributions, compared with approximately 50 percent in 1950.

Statistical Summary of Activities 

Disability insurance claims filed by California employees against the State Disability Fund and under voluntary plans during 1951 were about 18 percent greater than the total of claims filed during the previous year. 

Claims filed against the State Disability Fund, however, declined about 

1 percent while claims against voluntary plan carriers rose 40 percent. 29

3 percent from the 1950 figure.

Payments during the year totaled 22.1 million dollars for basic disability benefits and 2.6 million dollars for hospital benefits. These payments were 8 percent and 4 percent respectively less than the amounts paid during 1950. The number of weeks compensated by basic benefits declined by 7 percent from 1950 and the number of days compensated by hospital benefits was 4 percent less than the previous year. 

Benefit payments under voluntary plans are reported to the department on the basis of terminated claims. In addition, there were 66,847 hospital benefit claims filed resulting in the payment of 3.4 million dollars, covering 433,000 days of hospitalization. 

Average length of basic benefit payments under the state plan was 10.1 weeks for claims reported terminated in 1951, compared with 6.0 weeks for voluntary plan claims. Average duration of hospital benefits was 7.6 days for state plan claims and 6.5 days for voluntary plan claims. 

Although the number of first claims with qualifying wage credits filed under the state plan declined by 3 percent during 1951, the number of disqualifications assessed by district offices was up by 13 percent to a total of about 30,000. Most of the disqualifications were assessed for two reasons: 14,000 because the claims should have been filed with voluntary plans (compared with 11,000 in 1950) and 6,200 because the claimants were receiving sick leave pay from their employers (compared with 5,100 in 1950) and, therefore, were not entitled to disability insurance benefits.

CHAPTER VII SUMMARY OF ACTIVITIES OF DIVISION OF APPEALS Statistical Summary of Activities 

During 1951, the Division of Appeals received 23,601 appeals to referees and disposed of 24,346 such appeals. This was a decrease in both receipts and dispositions compared with the year 1950, during which receipts were 

27,377 and dispositions were 30,825.

About three-fourths of all appeals to referees received during the year related to unemployment insurance benefit cases, and about 77 percent of the cases disposed of during the year related to the same subject. The remainder of the cases were fairly evenly distributed among disability insurance benefits, disputed coverage, tax, servicemen's readjustment allowances and cases for other states. At the beginning of the year, there were 3,155 cases pending before referees, and at the close of the year there were 2,410 cases pending.

Appeals to the Unemployment Insurance Appeals Board during the year totaled 2,941, and the board disposed of 4,340 cases. This was a decrease in receipts and an increase in dispositions compared with the year 1950 during which total receipts were 4,930 and total dispositions were 4,198.

As in the case of appeals to referees, most of the appeals received and closed by the board, 63 percent and 69 percent, respectively, related to unemployment insurance benefits. Tax cases were the second most numerous, followed by disputed coverage matters, disability insurance benefits, and regulation appeals. 

The appeals board had 1,928 cases pending on January 1, 1951; at the close of the year the cases pending totaled 529. Full statistics may be found in a table in the appendix. 

Approximately 82.3 percent of the unemployment insurance appeals received by the board were filed by claimants and approximately 17.7 percent thereof were filed by employers. During 1950, approximately 

85.4 percent of such appeals were by claimants and approximately 14.6 percent by employers.

Oral argument was presented to the board in 105 benefit appeals during 1951 in various cities of the State. A large majority of oral arguments were heard in San Francisco and Los Angeles.

Eight judicial proceedings were instituted during 1951 by claimants or employers to review benefit decisions issued by the board, making a 31

total of 28 such proceedings commenced since the organization of the board in its present form in 1943 and during which time the board has issued 18,802 benefit decisions. In 15 of these proceedings, the decisions of the board have been affirmed, in three instances the decisions were reversed, in one instance the matter was remanded for further proceedings, and nine proceedings are still pending as of December 31, 1951.

Index-Digests of Decisions

During the calendar year 1951, the Division of Appeals undertook the preparation of a comprehensive Index-Digest of Selected Tax Decisions and an Index-Digest of Selected Disability Decisions. Each of these publications will be offered for sale to the public when they are completed at probably the same price as is now being charged for the Index-Digest of Selected Benefit Decisions.
