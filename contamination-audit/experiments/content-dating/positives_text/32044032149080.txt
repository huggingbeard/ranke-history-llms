FORWARD MOVEMENT OF HUMANITY 

Civilization is progressing toward a universal coordinating and unifying relationship, socially, racially, economically, politically and spiritually. The line of progress is not necessarily straight; in fact, as with nature, all movements of humanity are in accordance with the curved line, sometimes forward, at other times backward, but the general direction is a forward movement of humanity. The Dark Ages mani fested a backward movement of humanity. But we can never judge accurately by outward outward appearances.

The pattern of civilization is not for human inspection. Humanity moves in accordance with the law of natural selection, self preservation, adaptation, and the upward urge resulting from the spiritual in man. The law of average is fundamental in determining the nature of the physical conflict of the electromagnetic forces of the universe; so it is with humanity in relationship to the progress of civilization. Mankind, as a fundamental proposition of natural law and human relationships, cannot move forward without taking into consideration the general average of well-being of the peoples of the earth. Isolation was on its way out when the congress of the United States, following the war message of 

President Wilson, declared for the first world war. There was a backward recession when the isolationists succeeded in defeating congressional approval of the covenant of the League of Nations. Now that the United States is in the midst of a second world war, there can be no doubt that isolation from a political standpoint is a forgotten issue. We are part and parcel of a social, economic and commercial world, if not political. The genius of science, modern invention and the new era of industrialization have destroyed time and space in the social, economic and commercial world, as modern destroyed scientific concepts have time and space in the physical universe. Things are not what they appear to be. Ideas in accordance with the universal spirit of things rather than brute force dominate movements of humanity. Those who think in accordance with the forward movement of humanity are always in the right; and it matters not what the present apparent result may be. The struggle for preferment, for aggrandizement either among men at the expense of others or among nations, simply isn't in accordance with the universal principle of right conduct. We cannot overlook any class or race of people.

Athens was an enlightened city long before Christ. But the enlightenment of Athens could not survive in a world of barbarians. The Roman empire as a political structure was far ahead of its era. Ultimately, not even the power of Rome could survive the assaults of the barbarians at its portals. The lesson of Athens and Rome is that the benefits of civilization as a universal rule, must be passed around to the people of the earth. Totalitarianism, mechanism, force and all the characteristics of energy as applied to mankind, ultimately will lead to self-destruction. 

Force ultimately exhausts itself. But the spirit of nature, the spirit of man never dies.

WARS AND POLITICAL UNIFICATION 

We are again in the midst of a great world war. Again, the United 

States of America is beating its plough shares into swords for the salvation of democracy. The issue is clean cut, as it was in the first world war. It is democracy versus totalitarianism; it is freedom versus authoritarianism; spirit versus force; mind versus matter; christianity versus paganism; culture versus barbarism. The issue is not new.

It is as old as David and Goliath; but the struggle has now spread to all parts of the earth. It is all naturally a part of the evolution of civilization, and the development of the social order and the world political state. 

The peoples of the world have been constantly at war since the beginning of civilization. We are prone to believe in nearly every great crisis or in the midst of any great struggle of nations that the destruction of civilization is at hand and the end of the world is near. 

Rome ruled the world, only to be destroyed by the Huns and Vandals. 

With the fall of Rome, it seemed as would almost though civilization would perish, but a greater era was at hand. Following the darkness of the

Middle Ages, we had the Reforma tion and Renaissance. Individualism then had its opportunity and genius flourished.

THE INDUSTRIAL RENAISSANCE 

The re-establishment of world peace will probably be followed by a revival of business and commerce throughout the world that has never been paralleled. The pioneer days are not over for young America.

New vistas will be opened for the progressive spirit of America. The energy of the United States which heretofore has been directed to building an industrial nation from the sage-brush and prairies will have a world outlet, but more particularly in the far East, in China and India, where there will be an opportunity to bring not necessarily a finer culture, but the advantages of modern industrialism to those retarded but deserving peoples. The industrial capacity of the United States will become a world asset. The democratic spirit of America will carry world civilization to a higher level. 

It will be the industrial capital and the energy of the people of the United States that will be in demand. 

We, the people of the United States, succored the peoples of the world with our industrial and labor capital at the end of the first world war, and undoubtedly we will do the same thing again at the end of the second world war. Industrial regimentation will give way to the free spirit of individual initiative and private enterprise. The immediate result will be that credits will be free and money will be easy. But for the purpose of properly safeguarding the citadel of private enterprise in the United States from a disastrous fall, it will be necessary to evolve a system of financial credits under the control and supervision of the federal government. The preservation of modern capitalism and private industrialism, and even the solvency of the federal government itself in the generation following the close of the second world war will mainly depend upon the success of the federal government in the establishment and operation of a federal system of financial control of industry. Inflation will be as it is now one of the most difficult economic problems.

The pattern for stabilizing industry and the inauguration of an effective plan of total employment for the workers of our country and the operation of industry at capacity production is the president's public works program. THE PUBLIC RESPONSIBILITY OF THE STATE 

The state, theoretically, may not be institutional, but practically it is. 

The political state, all over the world today, regardless of the fundamental nature of its government, is assuming direct responsibility for the public welfare. Government is not only in business in the union of Soviet republics, but everywhere. Business is either taken over by government or it is run for the benefit of government. Not only is government engaging in business directly, in one form or another, for example, here in the United States, but government is assuming a large degree of financial responsibility for business enterprises. The problem is not theoretical, but practical. Under the stress and strain of economic conditions and world problems private capital has not been sufficiently strong financially to assume the responsibilities with which business and industrial enterprises have been confronted.

During the depression the government of the United States was required to finance a public works program. Now, during the second world war the government is required to finance industrial construction. More and more, government is getting into business or assuming financial responsibility for business. 

The outlook for the future is not necessarily discouraging to private capital because of the expanding avenues for industrial development; but, on the other hand, there is nothing in the future outlook that leads to the assumption that government will retrench in private or quasi-public activities. The presumption is directly to the contrary. Government activities in the field of private capital will undoubtedly expand because of the growing intricacies of economic and industrial problems, and the international aspect of the politics of the United States. We may fairly assume that whenever and wherever necessary the government of the United States will assume financial responsibility for industrial development, and a degree of supervisory authority over industry.

It is a problem of adjustments in social, industrial and business relationships. By a policy of coordinarelationships, we can be assured of ting social, industrial and business the benefits of the machine age for the general welfare. The interests of both capital and labor are concerned with greater mass-production. 

Naturally, the greater the productivity of capital and labor, the greater will be the profits and earnings of each. The interests of capital and labor are not antithetical, but are identical. It would appear that the institution of private capital can best be maintained by inauguration of the profit sharing principle. It seems to be the most equitable, the most practicable and the most effective for harmonizing the respective viewpoints of each and bringing order out of a chaotic industrial world.

PRACTICALITY OF POLITICAL THEORIES 

The trend of governmental affairs in the United States in the future will not be determined by resort to political theories alone. We do not mean to underestimate the value of political theories; theories in politics as in science and religion are fundamentally necessary; but as in science and religion, theories must harmonize with nature, so, theories in politics must harmonize with the economic, industrial and social necessities of the state.

Government as well as law, in accordance with changes in society, is continuously becoming more complex. We start in the law with an abstract theory, but with the practical application of the theory to the particular case, the theory is subject to continuous differentiation. 

The question, then, primarily, is not what the theory is, for all are agreed in that respect, but how should the theory be applied to the particular case. It becomes a matter of judicial technique. So it is with government. The question is, how shall we apply the democratic theory of government to the practical problems of industry and to the growing complexities of business relationships?

It is fundamentally a question of the technique of statesmanship in the evolution of the democratic theory of government in the republic. 

A fundamental concept in the philosophy of political science is, or has been, that government is not an institution. Government is an instrumentality to administer the political affairs of the state. But under the pressure of modern industrialism, government creates financial enterprises that are owned and operated by the state. Government also assumes to finance private industrial enterprises. How far government in the republic will go in the direction of assuming to finance, direct and control financial and industrial institutions is a question. The problem is not primarily theoretical, but practical. We are primarily concerned with the practical needs of the country. If private finance and industry cannot satisfy the needs of the country economically, financially and industrially, then the government will have to assume such responsibility as is necessary to meet such needs. 

We do not necessarily change our theory of government simply because government assumes to expand

Theory of government, of course, is of exceeding importance. The founding fathers of the constitution of the United States, generally speaking, were political theorists.

They were intent upon establishing a new form of government founded upon the the practical application of their political theories. The federation which had been created after the close of the war of the Revolution had failed. The thirteen original colonies which had won their independence from the mother country could not politically succeed by maintaining their independent political character under the terms of the articles of confederation. A more centralized form of government in which each of the colonies should surrender a part of its sovereign power to a central federal governmental authority was a practical political necessity. It was not a political theory which confronted the thirteen separate and distinct political entities then existing, but a practical problem in the technique of statesmanship. We all know to what extent the delegates who composed the constitutional convention were able to put into practical effect their theories of government in the adoption of the constitution of the United States and how well it has succeeded.

ECONOMICS OF ABUNDANCE 

Economics involves the supply of and demand for human wants. Paradoxically, the machine age witnesses a lack of material goods to supply human wants because of the economic and financial problems of modern industrialism, not because modern industrialism has not the capacity to produce in abundance to satisfy the wants of human society. 

The economic problems of production, distribution and consumption stifle industrialism, (except during the war periods), and are the direct cause of under-production and under-consumption, unemployment and lack of purchasing power, want, misery and distress. Co-ordination of labor and capital, the elimination of unemployment, and providing for labor the natural right and opportunity to earn a livelihood will solve the economic problems, and bring "freedom from want". The economics of this era surtaxes our intelligence because we think in terms of individual and group relationships rather than the social welfare. If indus- If indus- trialism will think in terms of consumption as well as production, we will not be long concerned with business or industrial stagnation in the future. Capitalism will approach a fundamental solution of its greatest problem; finance will find a safe and ready outlet for its reserves and industries will operate to plant capacities. Production for the greatest good for the greatest numbers will solve the problem of "over-production" or "under-consumption" and eradicate the evils of industrialism and the disadvantages of the economics of the machine age. Unless we can evolve a plan of co-ordination between labor and capital, the "struggle for existence" and the process of "natural selection" will deplete the classes as well as the masses until the ravages of "rugged individualism" are stayed by the paternalistic arm of government itself. The autocracy of capitalism must bow to the democracy of natural law in the industrial world, not only for its own preservation, but for the public welfare. 

Inventive genius, obviously, should go forward. Invention should not be stifled and labor saving machinery discarded because the labor problem is intensified by the use of labor saving devices. Rather, technology should be brought into play to remedy the evil. The genius of men should not be a source of evil, but should be technologically utilized as a benefit to mankind. Inherently, any mechanical devise that conserves the use of labor is an advantage to industry and a benefit to mankind.

Whether such a labor saving device operates advantageously for industry and as a benefit to society all depends upon social coordination. 

The machine is constantly crowding in on the worker, rendering his opportunity for employment less certain and his employment inconstant. 

In other words, technological reorganization of industry is lagging behind the progress of invention. Industrial coordination has been unequal to the problem which inventive genius presents. The inventive genius of man has been superior to his capacity for industrial coordination. 

The industrialist is not so much concerned with technological coordination as he is with production and sales. tion and sales. To the industrialist, the problem is one of the creation of

capital in the form of manufactured technologist, commodities. To the the problem is primarily one of industrial coordination. We may assume the capacity of industry to meet the industrial needs of society regardless who the owner of industry may be, but we cannot assume that the welfare of society will be served without industrial coordination.

Capitalism has operated on the basis of production for profit. The result is that the "profit motive" creates a multitude of economic problems that clog the operation of the industrial machine. These economic forces interfere with commerce, stifle individualism, undermine morale, and impair the usefulness, the prosperity and the happiness of the people of our country. Concerned with the "profit motive", under the present system, we are all predatory; we are socially disorganized and continuously engaged in commercial and industrial warfare. The conflict is for the dollar. The efficiency of the individual is greatly impaired. The principle of the greatest good to the greatest numbers is lost sight of in the field of business competition. The industrial strug gle involves primarily such questions as wages, prices, hours of employment, collective bargaining, unemployment insurance, and cooperative production. They, one and all, give rise to economic factors that involve individual well being and the public welfare.

EMPLOYMENT, PRODUCTION AND PROSPERITY 

The primary question in our country for the solution of the economic and industrial problem is employment. For that is the only sound means by which consumers can obtain money or purchasing power with which to buy industrial and agricultural products. Of course, the question is one of exchanging services for goods or commodities. If the people are given an opportunity to sell their services, that is, if employment is provided, then they will have the opportunity to buy goods. 

Over-production or under-consumption will be a thing of the past, and prosperity on a sound basis will be established. The money question naturally is of secondary importance; for, if consumers have regular employment, they will have the money with which to buy goods or commodities.

Employment, industrial prosperity and the general welfare are relative. 

Employment results in industrial prosperity. Industrial prosperity is the result of the sale of goods. Employment and industrial prosperity go hand in hand. The employer and the employee must stand or fall together. The employer provides the work for labor, and then labor purchases the goods that are produced with his labor. the prosperity of industry is largely We may rationally assume that measured by industrial production; or, in other words, the greater the production, the greater will be industrial profits. The question, then, is how can we increase the sale of goods? Obviously, the sale of goods is automatically increased with the greater demand for employment.

Greater demand for labor means increased sale of goods. Clearly, the answer is that national prosperity is largely determined by the proportion of people who are gainfully employed. 

The producing capacity of the people of this country is almost incredible. During times of depression, with twelve million unemployed and twenty million people without means of support, the administration

thought it necessary to control agricultural production. During war times, with an enlistment of ten million men and twenty million more employed in the manufacture of war munitions, we have a producing capacity sufficient to satisfy domestic wants and also replenish the financial needs of European allies to the extent of many billions of dollars.

Obviously, the life of abundance is here in the United States if we can learn to coordinate our man power in production and realize an equitable distribution of industrial products. Clearly, it is to the advantage of capital, labor and the general public that coordinating methods should be applied to the progressive solution of industrial and agricultural problems. The economics of our industrial and agricultural problem, fundamentally, is a matter of scientific understanding; it is non-political, and the impartial, nonpolitical and non-partisan approach should be maintained.

But the trouble is that we are living in a political world of realities and not fancies. Government, fundamentally, is not institutional, but it is an instrumentality to regulate and supervise the orderly functioning of society. We cannot maintain this great experiment in representative democracy if government is to become a business enterprise or industrial machine. The economic problem is to be rightly solved by government maintaining a position of neutrality by and between labor and capital, and aid in a regulatory and supervisory manner the industrial enterprise of the nation.

The new era undoubtedly demands a new political philosophy; not a political philosophy that appeals to class prejudice or emotionalism, but that personifies the sober common sense of the people as a whole. It is a political philosophy of coordination, cooperation and unification. 

The government's moral, if not financial leadership is necessary to correlate the economic problems of labor and capital, and to coordinate their objectives and bring about united cooperation, for the best interests of all to the end of social progress.

INDUSTRIAL EFFICIENCY THE ISSUE 

Without regard to the political aspects of the question, the industrial problem, under private capitalism, communism or a capitalistic state is one of efficiency. The "profit motive" may be relegated to a secondary position, because without an efficient industrial mechanism, there will be no profits. The corollary is, that under capitalism, profits will largely be determined by the efficiency of the industry, the greater the efficiency of industry the greater will be its profits; and likewise, the social value of the industry is determined by the efficiency equation. That is to say, the desideratum under any political order is industrial efficiency. 

We stress the "profit motive" under our present capitalistic society, but in many respects such a state of society intensifies efficiency by reason of competition. INFLATION RUINOUS 

Inflation is on the way, so say publicists and economists. Just when inflation will arrive, if it is not already here, is another question. High prices produce hugh profits; hugh wages induce high prices; high profits produce an astounding increase in market values, and a great increase in the apparent monetary capital of the country. Bank credits continue to rise. The federal government issues a hundred billion dollars and more of government bonds. Price control is resorted to and subsidies are provided. The Administration is doing a good job of it, but has only begun. The real test will come after the war, when the country may undergo an orgy of speculation, to the ruin of many and the detriment of everybody alike. 

We must not overlook the first principle of the money question, which is, that money is a medium of exchange-the exchange of services for goods. We must maintain the relative purchasing capacity of the dollar. The monetary ratio of the economic factors that enter into the cost of living must remain relatively constant. Prices and wages should rise or fall approximately together. 

If the cost of living rises 40%, for example, wages should rise accordingly. There is no particular virtue, except for the time being, in attempting to avert an increase in the wage level, if the level of prices cannot be maintained. We do not disparage the Administration's effort to "hold the line"; but, if the "line" cannot be held, and it cannot, it will be necessary to permit of a graduated increase in wages to meet the higher price levels. Eventually, a higher price level will minimize the burden of the federal debt.
