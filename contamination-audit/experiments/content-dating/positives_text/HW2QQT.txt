8. Physical Problems. In the solution of a problem the first step is to recognize what are the important variable quantities and to represent them by letters. The differential equation is then obtained by expressing the relations between the quantities as equations connecting the letters. 

In this it should be emphasized that the symbols represent corresponding values, and this should be indicated in the definition of each letter. Thus if we know the velocity of a moving particle as a function of the time, we accomplish nothing by writing, “Let v be the velocity." The velocity may have many values. The statement should be, "Let v be the velocity at time t." But if the particle is moving in a vertical line and the velocity is known as a function of the height, the last statement may be of little value. In this case it may be better to take v as the velocity at height h.

Whether the solution of a problem is simple or complicated depends more on the choice of variables than on any other one thing. Thus suppose x, y are taken as the variables and after solving a complicated differential equation we find u(x, y) = c as answer. If we had chosen u as one of the variables the differential equation would have been du = 0, of which the solution is obvious.

43. Method of Undetermined Coefficients. The examples just solved illustrate a mathematical procedure known as the method of undetermined coefficients. When the general form of an answer is known but the precise coefficients are not known, this method consists in introducing literal coefficients in that form, substituting, and finding the value those coefficients must have to satisfy the given equations. 

Thus, in finding the particular integral of a linear equation (Dn + α₁D¤¬¹ + ··· + an-1D + αn)y = f(x), we consider what kinds of functions substituted for y will make the left side have terms of the same kind as those in f(x), take as trial value of y a linear function of these with constant coefficients, substitute in the differential equation, and find the values of the coefficients which make the two sides equal. 

This method can always be used if f(x) is a sum of terms each of which has only a finite number of linearly independent derivatives.* Most linear equations occurring in practical applications satisfy this condition. For example, if f(x) = e² + sin 3x, the term e2 when differentiated one or more times gives only multiples of e², and sin 3x gives only multiples of sin 3x and cos 3x. 47. Simultaneous Linear Equations. Simultaneous linear differential equations containing a single independent variable can be solved by a process of elimination analogous to that used in algebra for solving linear equations. If there are as many dependent variables as equations all but one of these can be eliminated, the remaining one appearing as a solution of a linear differential equation. Its value can be found by solving this equation, and then the other dependent variables can be obtained by substituting in the preceding equations. 

If possible the work should be so arranged that after the first variable is found the others can be determined without integration. If integration is used in determining these other variables, the constants of integration may not all be arbitrary. It is then necessary to substitute the values found in the differential equations to determine the relations between the constants. The number of constants is usually equal to the sum of the orders of the differential equations, but in exceptional cases this is not true.
