FOR BETTY WHO THINKS SHE WOULD LIKE TO BE A COUNTRY CLUB GIRL

THEY were sitting in Jay's roadster with a canopy of stars above them and the heady smell of June in the air.

It was Commencement Night at Miss Hollins' Select School for Girls. Prom Night. The Last Night. 

A faint tattoo of jazz coming from the lighted gymnasium broke the stillness of the summer dark. It was a wailing, plaintive sound that seemed oddly symbolical, Symbolical of the deep-rooted restlessness of the girls who had stood on the platform in the auditorium only that afternoon listening to final admonitions for the future as a preliminary to leaving the ivy-covered walls of one of the smartest finishing schools in Washington.

Gabrielle Barclay had been one of them on that platform. And now that it was over, now that she was finally

graduated, with a diploma in her pocket and a giddy sense of sudden freedom in her heart, she sat beside Jay Farrell in his roadster and decided that it was pleasant to be nineteen and blonde and—free. It was pleasant to have a whole life ahead of one. "Well," she said, "It's all over, and I can't decide whether the school has finished me or I've finished the school." 

Jay laughed. "You probably turned the place into a circus," he said. "Only some of the time. They threatened to bounce me twice but somehow they never quite got around to it. It was sweet of you to come down here for all this fuss and muss over commencement.” "I wanted to." "My family probably bribed you. What was their rush to get back to-night?" Jay put his arms around her suddenly. She peered at him through the gloom and felt her breath catch a little at his nearness. Jay Farrell was the junior partner in one of New York's larger brokerage houses, and, according to the standards set by Gabrielle's family, eligible in every way. He was thirty-four and good to look at. His brown hair was smooth and sleek and his eyes had a way of crinkling when he laughed.

Tantalizing husky voice, ivory white skin, virginal white gown and a scarlet mouth hungry for kisses. Murky, heavy-lidded eyes with the longest lashes in memory.

He schooled his tone with conscious effort. The quivering tensity between them relaxed. Gabrielle was conscious of a vague disappointment. It was as though she had been waiting, with nerves stretched to the breaking point, for the firing of a cannon that didn't go off. She felt, too, vaguely surprised at herself. She hadn't believed herself capable of such violent physical reaction.

Jay threw back his head and laughed. "How about the time you got yourself in the papers for pulling a tantrum at the opera?”

Gabrielle remembered the time quite vividly. It was her first opera and she had fallen quite madly in love with the handsome tenor. Seated in a box with her family and 

Jerry Fenton, an adoring swain from Choate, she had become completely immersed in the seas of romance. Jerry had grasped her wrist.

Then, in a violent flash of anger she had turned upon Jerry and had slapped him with the full force of her open palm across his face. She snapped her fingers. "Pfft! What do we care? We'll live in a little houseyour little house, wherever it is, and I'll learn to cook and

sew on buttons and darn socks for you. I'll show you how the domesticity bug completely ruins a perfectly good 

Country Club Girl!" 

Michael regarded her half fearfully. "I-I wonder why you're being so wonderful," he puzzled. 

She walked over to him again, and, utterly oblivious of anyone around her, put both arms about his neck. "It's very difficult to explain," she told him then, "But it seems that I love you-Professor." "Do you-brat?” “And how!” she murmured her cheek against his. A sudden thought assailed her. "Michael," she said, "What'll all your little students do with you on a boat bound for Italy?" "They'll manage," he said. "I've got a six weeks' leave of absence. I had an idea we might be married on board —provided—” "Provided Gabrielle thought it a good idea too," she finished. "Well-Gabrielle does."

It was then that she realized she still held Jay's letter, unopened. She glanced at Michael. "Do you mind?" she asked. "It's a very special letter -from Jay. He's been-rather a darling about you, Michael."

She tore open the envelope and slipped the letter out.

It was a single sheet of note paper only, and across it was written a single line. There was neither date nor signature. 

That single line read: "Perhaps you know now what I mean when I say—'be happy-darling!' "

The End
