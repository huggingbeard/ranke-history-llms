CHAPTER I  

It was early autumn and the golden sunset cast its halo over this peaceful valley that would never be forgotten. In the distance the lofty mountains rose in their majesty, while the mighty pines, that had withstood the elements for years, stood like sentinels guarding this wonderful land. Indian teepees dotted the landscape and not many miles distant a settlement of white men had come to stay for the land was rich and fair to look upon. The red men were at peace with their white brothers, and the crops at both these settlements were prized by their owners and all looked well.

A river flowed between these settlements. Its waters enriched the land and also furnished them with plenty of food. The red men exchanged their products or paid in corn instead. The Connecticut 

River was to them the father of waters. Traditions of their fathers had taught them to revere its powerful source from which came their prosperity. 

And yet this peaceful valley had its discontented ones for the taxes were getting to be a burden and some of the crops had been failures. Sam Trueblood had always spoken of going northward; and Thomas 

Treadwell, his brother-in-law who had been favored with worldly goods, says to him: "Why do you tempt me to leave this valley that has always been my home and the land of our fathers? Unforeseen perils would possibly come to us; let us be content to settle here."

Mrs. Trueblood was Tom's sister and the family ties had always been very close. Heartrending moments had come to them but still the Truebloods wanted to start northward-not for the lust of adventure but on account of Mrs. Trueblood's constant fear of the Indians. She says to Tom: "Can you forget the tragic story of the death of our forefathers by the hands of the redskins? I fear an uprising sooner or later; though they are many miles away, yet we know their horses are fast and their canoes are many. Tom, I implore thee, will you come with us?" "Molly Trueblood, do not harbor any evil thoughts of these Indians who live in the valley near the banks of the river, for their days of warfare are over. The Iroquois band, that massacred our fathers, have gone to their happy hunting-ground. 

You know the dreadful plague claimed many of them and the severe winters also took their toll." 

It is late winter and preparations for the journey northward are under consideration. The little cousins, Hester Trueblood and Jean Treadwell, are very sad to think of parting. Jean would say: "Don't be sad, Hester, for we will meet again. 

Mrs. Trueblood heard their childish prattle and said, "Why don't you give your locket to Hester, and 

Hester will give you her necklace in return, for happy remembrances.' 99 

The girls gladly make the exchange.

It is April and the Truebloods start on their journey. The snows have melted bringing spring early. 

They follow the river. In several places it has overflowed its banks. The journey is lengthened so the 

Truebloods and company rest a while. "Hester," said Mrs. Trueblood, "what is troubling thee?"

Tears fill the little girl's eyes and she opens the locket and kisses the picture of Jean. "Hester, do not be sad for Mother feared the

Indians or we would never have moved northward." 

Night closes in upon them and Sam Trueblood and Bill Forest see that the land is fertile and feel in their hearts that the promised land is near. 

Bill Forest greets Sam Trueblood with, "Good morning. The day is fair, why not make another day's journey, and then if not quite satisfied, perhaps a couple more?"

Traveling has been hard and at sunset on the next day, Sam turns to Bill and says, "All is well." 

The trusty scout agrees, saying: "Yes, let's rest here. It looks good to me. "9 And so here they find their homes where they will start life anew, for they have come to a fertile land in a peaceful valley guarded by mighty hills which they acknowledge as their fortress of safety. 

The crude carts that carried their household belongings are quickly unloaded. On the journey the carts had been used at night for a shelter for Mrs.

Trueblood and Hester and many others. The men begin to cut trees and hew timber for a cabin is needed to shield them from the prowlers of the wood. 

And they soon thrive in this new land. Their maize yields a bountiful crop, and the pumpkins look like nuggets of gold as they brighten the half-cut field of grain. A year of prosperity has begun, and the following spring sees the cabin replaced with a house and lots of more land being tilled. 

But sad things have happened in their old home in Connecticut, still unheard of by the Truebloods. 

Mrs. Treadwell had hoped to keep the news from 

Hester although Molly Trueblood had received word of the loss of Jean Treadwell.

One day Hester, sitting on the doorsteps of her home, calls to her mother: "Did Jean send any message in Aunt Hettie's letter. I fear something is wrong. What can have happened to Cousin Jean?"

Mrs. Trueblood then relates to her daughter the news that she had received a short time ago, so Hester's days are sad and lonely. She opens the locket and kisses Jean's picture again and again. She rarely spoke Jean's name after this trying ordeal, and, though only a child, it was hard to bear up. 

It so happened that Norman Treadwell and his sister Jean had been walking on the ice near the banks of the river not far from their home. Norman had been gathering his traps. The ice began to break and suddenly the piece that held Jean broke loose; and Norman hurrying on did not notice his sister being carried away. He finally turned around and not seeing his sister in sight, called in vain for her, then rushed madly home to tell his mother. The large cake of ice had broken once more and the piece that held Jean floated swiftly down the river for the current was strong. When Mrs. Treadwell arrived, the floe that marooned Jean had made the bend in the river. They searched in vain, loudly calling her name, but, with no answer, they gave up in despair.

Some Indians, who lived in the valley across the river from them, were hunting. And Chief Loud 

Thunder, who had wandered away from his brothers and was strolling along the banks of the Connecticut, saw the child in the distance and just as the ice broke against the bank, he sprang from the rock on which he stood and rescued the terrified child. Jean, struggling to free herself, was held securely in his strong The child looked down on his massive form and shuddered at his suit of leather and head-dress of feathers. He looked down upon her saying: arms.

"Little maiden, do not be afraid, for no harm will befall you. Our women are kind, as also are our maidens, so have no fear for we will be kind to you; only do as we tell you and all will be well." 

Loud Thunder carried her home in his arms for she was cold and exhausted. The kind attention of the women and the bead work made by the maidens calmed her fears for a while. Jean told her little friends how she happened to come to them and all about her home and people, but her coming to them was not explained by the Chief. A little lad, some older than herself, taught Jean to use the bow and arrow and to ride his pony, and Little Thunder's kindness was an added joy to her. At times she would beg the older folks to restore her to her people. Yet to Little Thunder and the Indian children, she always seemed happy.

After Jean had been in her new home about a week, a big honor was conferred upon her by naming her "Morning Star." With deep brown eyes and waving raven hair, her loveliness was unmatchable and they could justly call her "Morning Star"—a name most honored of their race. Little Thunder faintly remembered it as the name of his own mother who died when he was a little lad, and it pleased him greatly, and he is willing to share his pleasures with the new Morning Star. 

Little Thunder and White Eagle, a shrewd little lad of the tribe, enjoyed their daily horseback rides together. After practising with their bows and arrows, they would ride home side by side. One day 

White Eagle suddenly said: "Who was the little maiden with you yestermorn? 

She looked like a stranger to me. Is she one of our race?" 

Little Thunder answered: "She is called Morning Star."

Meanwhile Loud Thunder was restless and worried for he feared that he might have been seen when he rescued the child from the ice so he called his men to council.

It is evening and dusk found them sitting around the council fire. Loud Thunder spoke: "I have always longed to move northward. Who is willing to follow me to a land where the hunting is good and the food plentiful?" 

An old settler replied: "Some must stay here for our land is valuable and our maize will again yield us a bountiful harvest."

Plans were made and preparations were begun to start northward at midnight the following night.

The time finally arrived and soon their canoes were gliding noiselessly up the river; and, when out of danger of being seen, they landed and pitched their tents on what they hoped was their promised land. 

And here they grew and thrived for several years.

Back in the woods of Connecticut, Norman Treadwell had gone beyond his bounds wandering in search of game. When nearing a large rock, he finds himself face to face with an Indian lad. "Why comest thou here, Stranger?" asks Norman. 

White Eagle replies: "I paddled across the river in search of game. This is my first attempt. My canoe is tied near that rock that overlooks the river." 99 The two boys become friends right away and together they stroll down through the ravine and rest at the bottom enjoying the warm sun and watching it gradually sink behind the hills. "We must be going home before dusk overtakes us," remarks Norman Treadwell. "But we need not fear for nothing will harm us. 

Why did you come alone, have you not any brothers?" asked the Indian lad,

Norman does not hesitate to answer his question for the manners of his new-found friend are pleasing to him. "Dear friend, brothers I have none. Once I had a sister who was my constant companion but we think this river claimed her. The ice was breaking up and we think she was carried away down the stream on a broken cake of ice. We searched in vain but our people finally gave her up as lost. That was several years ago. 

White Eagle is thoughtful, saying, “A little maiden came to our people some years ago, I was only a child then, and she was a stranger to me and she did not look like our maidens. As Little Thunder was her constant companion and our camp was large, we did not get acquainted. One morning I was told that Little Thunder had left with Loud Thunder, the Chief of our people, and many of our tribe had gone with him. The little maid, called 'Morning Star,' had departed with them. A large number of our tribe did not go but stayed in the valley we loved." 

Norman's face brighteed at these words and he said, "If it could only have been my sister! Who kows but that she may yet come back to us some day?"

Norman hurries home and tells his parents what the Indian boy had said, and Mrs. Treadwell renews hope for her daughter's return some day. If this story can only be true! It has given her a glimmer of hope and she waits patiently for the outcome. 

Several moons have come and gone. It is harvest time again. Norman Treadwell receives a letter from his cousin Hester telling of another year of prosperity, of their abundant crops and of many things to be thankful for and ended by saying that good health was enjoyed by all. Bill Forest sent

greetings to everyone and added that they were rewarded by their move northward for every year was bringing them increasing prosperity. Hester also said that her heart yearned for the home of her childhood days. Norman folds the letter and lays it safely away. His love for Hester when a child has never waned for he remembered her bidding him 

Godspeed and promising him she would return to him some day. 

Mrs. Treadwell, looking eagerly at her son, said, "If I had known the calamity that was to befall us, how gladly would I have gone north. The unforeseen perils that I dreaded would not have kept me, neither the thought of leaving my kinsfolk or thrifty home would have concerned me if we had only been spared this sorrow.' Then faintly smiling, she said hopefully, "Who knows what joy the year may bring to us !" 99 

At midday, Norman takes his gun and whistles for his dog. They walk down the path leading to the hunting range and his father calls to him saying, "Norman, do not go out of the clearing, and don't wander out of the bounds. Your mother now says that Jean might be an Indian captive if what that Indian lad told you is true. The red men living down the river valley have seemed friendly for many years but their fathers had a feeling of revenge against our fathers. Tradition says they searched our camps in futile efforts to find a princess who was missing from their camp, and the Indian giver spirit may still lurk in the hearts of their children." 

Norman obeys his father and does not go out of his territory. At dusk he returns with a large turkey. 

Dark clouds gather, the wind is strong, and Norman says: "Tomorrow is Thanksgiving Day. Oh, what a fine bird to grace our table!"

After cleaning his gun and with supper over, they gather by the fire and make plans for the morrow. 

Their kinsfolk are invited, as in years gone by, to the Thanksgiving gathering. 

It was early dawn when Norman Treadwell awoke. He glanced over the landscape and found it covered with a mantle of snow, saying to himself, "Surely this is an ideal day for a feast and there is so much to be thankful for."

The feast was over, the guests had departed and this Thanksgiving Day was nearly ended. Thomas 

Treadwell remarks: "Winter has begun and our crops are safely gathered. Soon we will be taking our corn to the mill and gathering wood for the winter."

The following day was nearing its close. Mrs. 

Treadwell was busy knitting and attending to her household duties, for the welfare of her family was her constant thought. Thomas Treadwell had returned from the mill. Supper is finished, and, in the evening the lighted candles throw their flickering rays across the troubled face of Mrs. Treadwell who says to her husband: "It is nearly eight years since our daughter was lost, and if alive, she is now a woman grown. At times I give up in despair, then again I grasp new courage with the hope that she may return. "Darling, do not grieve any more. Remember the remark you made not many days ago that we cannot tell what luck or kindness might befall us before the year is through. Let us look on the bright side. 

I believe that Jean is safe and will find her way back to us some day. Grieve no more for all will be well." 

Many miles below them, in the southland, is another white man's settlement, where an Indian bids farewell to his white brothers who have reared and sheltered him for a score of years or more. His

kindness to these people would never be forgotten. 

His just decisions and honest dealings made them look up to him as their tower of strength and protection.

The Indian, waving his hand, turns abruptly and walks with a quiet tread. He dares not look behind. 

His horses move quickly for fear that their master might see. When beyond a turn in the road, he climbs a hill and rests against a tall pine tree—a place where he had stood and asked for guidance so many times before. And the Indian, shading his eyes, murmurs a prayer, then gazes for the last time upon the homes of the people he loved. His friendship was as steadfast as the mountains that guarded and surrounded them for many generations. 

Day after day they travel on; and at night rest wherever darkness overtakes them, and, trusting that they meet with no danger, with joy he looks ahead. 

When the sunrise greets the day, they start again on a journey-a mission they will not know until another sun has risen and set beyond the horizon of purple and gold. 

Wending their footsteps eastward to the foot of a pine-covered range, they come to an opening and the Indian looks down upon a peaceful valley. He is thankful for his safe arrival. Then scanning the horizon, his searching eyes behold the homes of a prosperous people. 

He ponders a while, then saddles his horse, and, leading the other, starts down the winding road. 

Suddenly he halts his horses, dismounts and caresses them fondly. They rest for a moment for such a scene as was stretched before them was soothing to both the mind and body of these weary travelers.

CHAPTER II  

At dusk a man riding a horse and leading another is nearing the crossroads. The man dismounts, and, leading his thoroughbreds side by side, saunters slowly up the walk to the Treadwell home for it was the first dwelling at the bend of the road. 

After hitching the ponies to a tree, the man with a quick step walks up toward the house. This was 

April and the Treadwells were standing on their doorstep planning their spring planting and also counting on a bumper crop as had been their reward in the years gone by. They were gazing over their broad meadows gratefully acknowledging that God was good to them in many ways, yet never understanding why the great calamity of losing their daughter should have come to them. 

Mrs. Treadwell, turning around, sees the Indian and hastily says, "Tom, what treachery is he plotting; perchance he is the forerunner of a hostile tribe.' 99 

The Indian steps forward with a "Good evening, white brother; I hope it is well with you. Do not fear this weary stranger, I will not harm you. I live in the southland many miles below. I am a friend of the white man and I left them at peace, in search of the northland." 

Noticing the anxious look on Mrs. Treadwell's face, the Indian gently speaks: "Fear me not. I am 

Lone Wolf-the last man of a lost tribe, and my warfaring days are over. I am at peace with the world." 17

At these sincere words, Mrs. Treadwell's fears are abated. The stranger's eyes gaze upon the rolling valley with a look of contentment and a yearning to rest. Thomas Treadwell bids him stable his horses and return himself to stay in the house with them that night. The morrow would explain the expression Lone Wolf had seen on Mrs. Treadwell's face when he first came to them. 

Rising at early dawn, Lone Wolf sets out to explore the new country but returns in time to join Thomas Treadwell at the corral. After caring for his horses, they return for the morning meal which was steaming and waiting for them. He knew he was welcome for Mrs. Treadwell had carefully set another place at the table. Norman Treadwell was seated when they entered. Their glances at each other were marked with a feeling of friendliness, and from that moment they knew they would be staunch friends. 

As the day went by, the Indian and the boy boasted of their hunting skill. The following morn they wend their way down the valley and enter the forest, the boy carrying an ancestral flintlock and the Indian a heavy gun of the Revolutionary period. 

Drifting away from each other, Lone Wolf climbs a steep cliff and beholds in the distance a valley dotted with tepees. He does not tell Norman what he has seen but decides to explore the situation some other time when he can go alone. Hearing a loud shot, he hurries toward the spot where the report was heard. 

The thick underbrush hindered his progress. The snow lay deep in the valley between them. He looks ahead and at the edge of the forest sees Norman with a large turkey thrown over his shoulder. Norman calls to his companion saying, "Isn't it a beauty?

There will certainly be a feast in your honor. It is much larger than the one that graced our Thanksgiv-

ing table. What did you see on your trip up the cliff ?" 

The Indian simply answered: "I scared several partridges from their woodland home; they found shelter in their heavy brush haunts-the pine trees. 99 But he did not tell Norman what he had seen down the valley when he stood on the cliff above the river. Norman had never gone beyond the bounds to climb to the top of the cliff or to explore the mountains and therefore had never seen the settlement that laid in the valley many miles away or even the river that lay between them.

The two boys turn their footsteps homeward, arriving just as the sun was setting behind the hills. "Norman, I was worried about your welfare for you were gone so long." 

Norman answered, "Mother, we wandered far into the dense pines," and, holding up a large turkey, said, "What do you think of the bird I brought you?" "It is a beauty, my son. 99 "We will feast on it in Lone Wolf's honor," says Norman. "Yes, my boy, it will grace the table on the Lord's Day."

Supper is laid and after the evening duties are done, the family and Lone Wolf gather around the fireside. The night before, the stranger was in no mood to explain how he came to them; but tonight, after the Treadwells have told of the happenings of the years gone by, the stranger willingly tells his story. "In the peaceful home of my forefathers, an enemy attacked us. Our ranks were badly weakened and we fled to whatever safety could be found. My mother sought shelter in the dense forest. I was a babe in arms. For days she wandered hoping to find my father and finally she reached a white man's

settlement and was befriended by them for several years. When my mother died, I was still very young, but their love for me never failed. I loved them as I could love a brother. Some of these children left their homes to build homes of their own, and many changes brought sadness to this once happy village. The thoughts of my loved ones leaving gave me the lust to wander. Vainly I fought the thought but eventually I yielded to the impulse and, bidding a friendly farewell to my white brothers, I started toward the north country which I had so often visioned in my dreams. Something calls me and I must soon again be roaming onward to the land of my dreams.' 99 The friendship that had been kindled between this stranger gave Mrs. Treadwell faith to tell Lone

Wolf the sad event that had befallen them several years ago. The Treadwells and the other settlers had hitherto thought it unwise to mention the story to him as there was a race barrier between them for trickery was indulged in by both the white man and the red skin, and this was the first day that they had known him. Mrs. Treadwell trusts him and believes him sincere and resolves to relate their life story to him for her faith grows stronger as the moments go by, and so Mrs. Treadwell expounds the story of their life, finally telling him of their daughter Jean and her almost certain death by drowning. 

Norman also tells him what the Indian lad, called 

White Eagle, had told him; Mrs. Treadwell saying, "It is eight years since Jean went away. She would now be woman grown. Sad and disheartened and yet in her heart of hearts hopeful that Jean would return to them if this story were true, she added: 99 "I still have faith that she will return to us."

Their love for their long-lost child has kindled a desire in the heart of Lone Wolf to restore the girl

to them and he forthwith resolves to search for her, and he says to them: "I may be able to find your daughter. It may be that the Great Father will guide me to her. If she lives, she may yet be found; if in captivity, I will risk my life to free her; and if all is well, I will bring her back to you. Your kindness to me can never be repaid. I was a stranger when you took me in. I beg you to allow me to linger for a few days while the hunting is good. This land and its people have given me courage to renew my journey, but before I go, I want to go farther down the valley."

In the early hours of the morning, Lone Wolf sets out for the valley. He has told Mrs. Treadwell of the tepees he had seen many miles away on the other side of the river. Samuel Treadwell was making another trip to the mill that day and Norman had to help his father, so Lone Wolf was sure of going alone.

Walking down the road and following the brook, the fragrant breath of spring carried by the balmy south wind gave him new life and cheer to take him on his exploration walk and for the difficult task he was about to undertake. The pussywillows and budding trees told him that spring was here. He reaches a wood near the opening where a partridge had flown to safety some days before. Stealthily he finds his way through and when near the opening, a large turkey appears at the edge of the forest. He raises his gun and in another moment the bird falls to the ground. Hiding his spoil in the crevice of a big rock, he again shoulders his gun and walks slowly down the ravine that leads to the river. When nearing its banks, he sees a canoe near a large boulder. 

He still travels on. He hears the cracking of boughs and he levels his gun for fear of a lurking enemy or possibly a prowling bear that might show fight. His

aim was for defense but not to kill. At the end of the bridle path, he came face to face with an Indian lad who looks upon the tall, erect form of his grownup brother and shrinks in fear. Lone Wolf bids him not to be timid. "I will not harm you. I have seen your canoe by the bank of the river. Tell me, I pray, are you far from your people?" "Yes, my people live several miles below on the other side of the river. I ventured down here where game is plentiful. Only a moment ago, I spied a large turkey far down the path."

Their conversation was barely finished when a large turkey appeared. Leveling his gun once more, a shot rings through the woodland dell and the huge bird falls to the ground. In a moment White Eagle returns with the trophy and presents it to Lone Wolf. "Nay, little brother, it is yours; take it to your people. 99 "Big brother, I thank you kindly. It is the largest bird I have ever seen. As it is late, I must be turning homeward. I hope we can meet again." 99 They walk back to the canoe and while resting on the rock beside the river, the Indian lad tells of the hasty departure of their chief and many of their tribe. "Little brother, did a little stranger leave with them ?" "Yes, a little maid who had come to them left the same night and her name is Morning Star. I did not know her well for she did not stay long in our camp but I know that they went northward." "Little Indian, what is your name?" "I am White Eagle."

Lone Wolf is pensive. With the same story told to him that was told to Norman, he wonders if there was truth in it or if it was merely imagination.

When taking leave, Lone Wolf says: "Farewell to you, my little brother, I wish you a safe return." 'Good-by, you are very kind. I must be going for the current is strong at the bend of the river. 

My big brother, may I ask where you live?" "I came from the southland below us, I am also going north.' 99 White Eagle seats himself in his canoe and is soon gliding down the river. When passing the point where the current is strong, the lad looks back; his farewells are carried back over the peaceful water, the echoes falling gently upon the ears of Lone Wolf who waves in return a fond farewell to his little friend.

Lone Wolf rises from the rock where he has been resting and, shouldering his gun, walks back up the ravine and comes to the pines where he finds the turkey he left there a short time ago. The sun has set when he crosses the brook and again the pussywillows swaying in the breeze give him courage and hope to believe the things he had been told but a few hours before. Spring is the time to grasp a new hold on life; and with courage and faith, Lone Wolf regains strength in the springtime of his life.

The Indian has reached the home of his friends. 

When passing the stables, the horses' whinny announce their master's arrival, and the watchful dog hurries down to meet the one who is so kind to them all.

A gentle rap and the latch is unfastened and Lone 

Wolf stands before Mr. and Mrs. Treadwell with a turkey. Just as he closes the door, Norman enters the room from another door. The boys are facing each other. "Where in the world did you get it?" asks Norman.

"Near the spot where you felled yours." And

Lone Wolf jokingly adds, "The woods are full of them. We will try our luck soon again." 

Mrs. Treadwell is happy for their safe return 

Thomas Treadwell asks Lone Wolf if he had gone beyond the bounds and he frankly admits he had. "Come to supper," calls Mrs. Treadwell. "It is nigh unto an hour since I have waited for you belated boys. I am so thankful you are safe at last. What hindered thee, Norman?" "I tarried with my cousin while waiting for Lone Wolf to return, thinking he would take the road home and never dreaming that he would follow the brook; and, when hearing the horses announce his presence, I hurried home."

The evening meal of steaming samp and delicious fried ham was soon finished. After offering their customary thanks, they go to the living room and gather around the cheery fireside where the bright log throws its light upon their faces. After her duties are finished for the day, Mrs. Treadwell takes her place by the fireside and is busy knitting. It is her usual pastime. "Mother, Cousin John is coming up this evening but he did not mention his father and mother coming along too," says Norman. "However, I shall be expecting them," says Mrs. 

Treadwell jokingly. 

Thomas Treadwell turns to Lone Wolf asking, "What has happened today? There was not much said at the mill except that I overheard the remark that a redskin had made his home in our midst and was not welcome here." "Why should my coming here alarm them?" asks 

Lone Wolf. "If they only knew my mission, their hatred against my race would be quickly forgotten and they would mistrust me no more."

"There are many who think well of thee, and others will see their mistake before you depart," answered Tom Treadwell. "My kind friends, I will now tell you what I saw and heard on my way down to explore the valley that lays before us, where I had seen the tepees dotting the landscape many miles away. Far down the deep ravine, beyond a rock that overlooks the river, I came upon an Indian lad who greeted me in a friendly way, and, being his big brother, he told me that the chief of his people, with many of his tribe, had left one night and were going northward.

A little maid, though not of his race and practically a stranger to him, was also one of the number who left so mysteriously. White Eagle told me this story."

Norman said, "It must be true for he told the same story to me.' 99 "I am thinking it might be your daughter and knowing our men were many against their number, the fear of being searched drove them from their home. White Eagle told me that many had said it was Loud Thunder's long desire to go north and that the opportune time had come."

In the midst of their conversation, a knock is heard and Thomas Treadwell's brother Robert and family arrive to spend the evening. John Treadwell quickly forms a friendship with the Indian stranger.

Only an hour ago Norman had widespread his good qualities in his cousin's home. John Treadwell was several years younger than his cousin Norman but they were always delighted to share each other's company. The boys stick close together and later admit that the evening had been a most pleasant one in listening to stories told beside the candlelight. 

The older people's conversation dwelt upon the approaching spring work and the possible truth of

the story about Jean that their Indian guest had so recently told them. 

John's mother invites the Treadwells and their guest to a house party the following week. Many of their kinsfolk and neighbors were to be there, and Lone Wolf knew he had found new friends. 

John says that he hopes to see him often, and Lone 

Wolf smiles, saying: "Child, it would be a great pleasure to meet you again." 

John bids them "good-night" and departs with his parents, truly wishing he could be on hand to see them in the morning. The Treadwell household close their eyelids in happy slumber for new hope had come to all of them.

The joyful moment has come, and Jean, in her queenly beauty, goes forward to meet her waiting knight. Jean says to her mother: "I am ready, Ĭ know Lone Wolf is waiting."

Then slowly walking arm in arm, they stop at the foot of the stairs. The rustle of the bridal gown falls upon Lone Wolf's keen ear. Quickly turning, he greets her graciously and gently leads her to the western window where they stand under the pine bower. When the solemn words are spoken that make her his own, Lone Wolf kneels, then rises and bestows a kiss upon her lips, saying: "Truly the fairest bud on earth has blossomed for me, and in return may I never withhold the best that life can give."

Jean looks up at the man garbed in leather and fringe, saying: "And I have gained the noblest man in the world," and Lone Wolf smiles and escorts his bride to her fond parents. Her silken gown of lily whiteness clings gracefully to her slender form and everyone admits that her loveliness was never surpassed in Massasoit. Jean's parents fondly embrace their daughter and Lone Wolf speaks: "I gratefully thank you for giving me the loveliest girl in the whole world," and he looks upon his bride and is pleased. "Lone Wolf, we are proud to call you our son for without you we would never have known this happiness and these blessings that are ours." 

Daylight has faded to twilight when the gay party gather in the dining-room to partake of the wedding feast. Many candles brighten the room and shine upon the sumptuous dinner set before them. Someone remarked that it was a feast fit for a king, and

Jean's young cousin John exclaims: "What was said was right," and Jean certainly looked like a queen in her virgin loveliness.

As they rise from the table, Robert Treadwell,

Again they gather around the fireside and the guests join in wishing the happy pair success in all their doings. One by one they then depart for the hour is late and all are thankful for the pleasant time they have had.

The Indian and his bride rest before the burning log as its light shines upon their calm and joyful faces. Lone Wolf breaks the silence: "Jean, what are you thinking about? Tell me where your thoughts are drifting?" "Lone Wolf, my brave and noble one, my thoughts are of thee. This seems like a dream for you did this all for me." "Nay, Jean, my own, the verdict was rendered in Massasoit and you did it all for me." 

Then hand in hand, they watch the dying embers fade away. Again they are happy for they are alone like they had often been before.

Thomas and Hettie Treadwell softly tiptoe towards them and stand upon the threshold unnoticed by their children whose eyes still linger upon the faint glow of the cheerful hearthfire. They are thinking of the days gone by and dreaming of the days to come. The intruders again steal quietly away and they know that such love and faith will be everlasting.

The young couple rise and hand in hand, they stroll to the western window and behold the waning moon, and then going to another window, they gaze toward the southland. "Lone Wolf, my own, are you still longing for the homeland?"

"Not since you are mine, sweetheart. The pines gave me strength. I knew the inspiration they imparted to me was sincere." 

And he kneels before his loved one, saying: "May we ask a silent prayer for the peace and prosperity of Massasoit, not forgetting a prayer for the peace and welfare of my brethren in the southland."

They kneel in silence, each wishing the other health and happiness, and rising, they walk back to the window and watch the moon above the distant cliffs. They are silent, again they are thinking of the days gone by and dreaming of the days to come. 

Their life has just begun. They whisper to one another that all is well and they look for the dawn of a peaceful career. Then turning to Jean, the Indian asks that a tranquil sunset crown their earthly mission in striving to live well. He asks that their love and faith never waver, and Jean answers: "Lone Wolf, I will try, and with your help, I know we will win."

Still standing in silence, they are thinking of the days gone by and dreaming of the days to come.
