I. 

World Trade in 1970 

A. 

Developments in International Trade 

Free world exports rose vigorously in 1970 for the third successive year. Shipments exceeded $279 billion, an expansion of over 14 percent which nearly matched the exceptional gain recorded in 1969. 

The increase in volume of exports, however, was smaller than in the previous year, in part reflecting less dynamic economic growth in the world as a whole. On the other hand, export prices rose much more steeply, as strong demand in most markets led to near capacity production and created labor shortages in Continental Europe and Japan. 

In the United States and some other major economies, meanwhile, upward pressures on prices continued despite lagging production and relatively high unemployment. 

Developed Countries 

The developed countries again expanded trade sharply. The rise of over 15 percent in their foreign sales was substantially higher than the gain recorded by developing nations and boosted their share of free world exports to 81 percent. The European Community (EC), the United States, Japan, and Canada were principally responsible for this strong trade performance. EC exports rose by a sixth, slightly lower than their 1969 rate but still accounting for nearly a third of the free world total. Of the $89 billion in EC shipments, almost half consisted of sales within the Community, as compared with 45 percent in 1968. 

Stimulated by the devaluation of the franc in mid-1969 and by the later revaluation of the German mark, the French export spurt of over 18 percent was the greatest increase registered by an EC country. Germany increased foreign sales more rapidly in 1970 than in the previous year, despite the higher price tag on its products. Deliveries to 

European markets contributed two-thirds of the more than $5 billion gain. Italian exports, on the other hand, posted an increase of one-eighth, the lowest in the Community, as persisting labor unrest disrupted production and limited supplies available for sales abroad.

Shipments by members of the European Free Trade Association (EFTA) advanced by nearly 13 percent to $43.3 billion. Exports from all members except Sweden and Iceland rose more slowly than in 1969. The United Kingdom, by far the most important trader of the group, recorded the smallest rate of growth in deliveries overseas but still accounted for nearly two-fifths of the EFTA gain. Almost half the British advance was attributable to higher prices.

Imports into Western Europe were not quite so expansive as in 1969 but again rose faster than exports. Both EC and EFTA purchases of foreign goods grew by about one-sixth. For the EC, this pace represented a decided slackening; for EFTA it was a sharp increase. The 22 percent advance recorded by the Netherlands ranked highest in the Community, with those of Italy and Germany only slightly lower. 

Although recording some acceleration in imports, the United Kingdom nevertheless achieved by a narrow margin its high-priority objective of a favorable trade balance for the year.

British purchases from both the EC and EFTA rose considerably faster than from the United States and the rest of the world. 

The Canadian trade surplus climbed sharply in 1970 as exports, especially of agricultural products, expanded by nearly 17 percent while imports moved up only slightly. Principally responsible for the lagging inflow were the decline in automotive imports from the United States due to a major strike on both sides of the border in late 1970 and the slower growth of the Canadian economy. 

Japan, for the third consecutive year, increased exports at a faster pace than any other developed nation. In sustaining the economic boom that began in 1965, Japan accelerated purchases sharply to nearly $19 billion, more than a fourth higher than in 1969. Imports of coal, soybeans, steel scrap, nonferrous ores, and machinery were particularly buoyant.

Australian export earnings were about an eighth higher in 1970. lagged behind the 1969 pace, however, largely because of depressed wool prices and reduced sales. On the other hand, exports of meat, chiefly beef, and iron ore and concentrates were well up. New Zealand's deliveries remained virtually static as 1

lower proceeds from wool and butter largely offset stepped-up beef exports, especially to the United States. Backed by ample foreign exchange reserves, the two countries expanded imports at a considerably faster rate than in 1969. Australian increases were widely diversified; New Zealand's focused on transport equipment, notably military aircraft. 

Developing Countries Partial data indicate that exports of developing countries approached $53.5 billion in 1970, while imports reached, and possibly exceeded, the $54 billion mark. Although both levels represented gains of around 10 percent, the share of the less advanced countries in free-world trade again diminished, shrinking from about 19 to 20 percent.

Exports from South and East Asia grew more slowly than in 1969, increasing by about 10 percent to reach $14 billion. Hong Kong, the region's leading supplier, and 

Taiwan continued their rapid export pace of the Sixties. Hong Kong increased sales by more than 15 percent to exceed the $2.5 billion level, while Taiwan expanded deliveries more than twice as fast to reach $1.4 billion. On the other hand, India reported only a modest gain. Although Singapore boosted sales of domestically manufactured goods at a rapid rate, its overall exports suffered because of reduced deliveries of petroleum products to U.S. troops in Viet-Nam and diminished demand and lower prices for rubber. 

The weakened rubber market held Malaysian shipments to the previous year's level. 

Imports into South and East Asia accelerated, climbing by 11 percent to over $19 billion. Hong Kong, Singapore, and Taiwan posted above average increases for the area, chiefly in support of their burgeoning manufacturing operations. In addition, Singapore imported sizable quantities of equipment for offshore oil exploration. In the Republic of Korea, official restraints on bank credit and on the inflow of foreign capital kept the rise to modest proportions. India's imports declined slightly as P.L. 480 shipments from the United States fell, coincident with another excellent Indian grain crop. 

Latin America appears to have duplicated its 1969 export growth rate, shipments in 1970 running some 10 percent ahead of the previous year for an annual level of about $13.6 billion. Brazil posted an increase of more than 18 percent, in large measure due to a sharp rise in coffee prices following a poor harvest. Argentina reported a gain of 10 percent and Venezuela 5 percent. Among the larger traders, only Mexico failed to expand foreign sales, due mainly to drought-stricken corn and cotton crops.

Imports into Latin America rose by about the same rate as exports, climbing to $13.5 billion. Brazil, with substantial reserves, reported a steep, one-fourth advance in imports. Mexico boosted foreign purchases nearly as rapidly, with much of the impetus coming from government expenditures for equipment needed to complete development projects. In contrast, Argentina's purchases expanded by only about 8 percent, notably slower than in 1969, as growth in the national economy decelerated. 

Venezuela again barely increased imports, and even that increase was due mainly to higher prices.

According to incomplete data, deliveries by the developing nations of Africa slightly exceeded $12 billion in 1970, up about a sixth. Nigerian sales advanced by nearly two-fifths to a record $1.2 billion while Libyan exports rose to $2.4 billion, a 9 percent gain. Half-year figures point to strong increases also by 

Algeria and Zambia, although copper sales by the latter was adversely affected by lower prices and accidental flooding in its largest mine. 

The African countries increased their imports substantially to about $10 billion. 

Nigeria reported a surge of over one-half, despite governmental measures to relieve pressures on reserves. On the other hand, Libyan and Zambian purchases at mid-year were lagging behind 1969 levels. 

Exports from the Near East were sluggish, growing by only 7 percent to around $10.8 billion. The United Arab Republic, despite a substantial increase in cotton sales, boosted total deliveries only slightly. Israeli shipments, stimulated in part by government incentives introduced in 1970, rose at the average rate for the region, as did those from Kuwait, in approaching $1.6 billion. Iraq maintained its exports at just above the $1 billion level. 

The Near Eastern countries boosted overseas purchases between 7 and 8 percent, a steeper gain than in 1969, to nearly $8 billion. This step-up reflected in part a faster inflow of goods into Iraq and Lebanon. Israel's import increase, however, slackened after two years of accelerated growth. 2

B. U.S. Foreign Trade Balance of Trade 

The surplus of merchandise exports (excluding military grant shipments) over imports in 1970 was over twice the 1969 level, totalling $2,699 million. This surplus reflects the gain of 14.3 percent in the value of exports as compared with 11.0 percent for imports. The performance of the export sector is even more impressive after the values of shipments are adjusted for price increases. 

On the latter basis, exports increased 8.2 percent while imports grew only 3.6 percent. 

Nearly two-thirds of the increase in the value of imports was due to higher unit values in 1970.

Commodity Exports 

The faster rate of growth in exports as compared with imports in 1970, whether stated in quantity or value, reverses the pattern of the previous nine years.

During this earlier period, the balance of trade slipped from $4.6 billion in 1960 to $1.3 billion in 1969. The trade balance rebound in 1970 to $2.7 billion was mainly due to a $2.0 billion rise in intermediate goods exports and a $2.0 billion rise in capital goods shipments, both record year to year increases for these types of products, which accounted for over three-fourths of the total export gain. Three years of sustained economic growth in major overseas industrial markets accounted for these record increases. 

Coal, Iron and Steel Products Lead Nonagricultural Exports The nonagricultural commodities showing the greatest exports gains were coal (62 percent), scrap (51 percent), iron and steel-mill products (26 percent), machinery (15 percent), and chemicals (13 percent). The weakest performance was turned in by the motor vehicle sector which declined 6 percent, attributable mainly to the 67 day auto strike against the largest U.S. producer. The strike lasted even longer at that company's Canadian plants, preventing deliveries to the Canadian subsidiaries. 

The boom in world steel production and consumption stimulated an upsurge in U.S. deliveries of iron and steel-mill products to foreign markets to a record level of $1.2 billion. Strong foreign demand and reduced export availabilities for Europe because of greater domestic requirements caused U.S. exports to climb 26 percent in 

1970. In the final six months, shipments slackened as a result of a considerable easing in the foreign supply situation. 

The high levels of industrial activity abroad also added strength to exports of coal and steel scrap for use in steel-making. The value of coal loadings was up 62 percent while deliveries of scrap increased by 51 percent. Although high rates of steel production contributed to these gains, higher prices were also an important factor. Japan was the major purchaser of these products, but West European countries also took significantly greater amounts. 

About one-fourth of the rise in office machinery exports represented increased sales of computers and parts. These shipments climbed to $1.1 billion. Deliveries of computer parts and subassembles, many of which are made into finished units at U.S. subsidiaries abroad, rose especially rapidly. West Germany, the United Kingdom, France, Japan, and Hong Kong took the bulk of these shipments. A strong increase was also noted in exports of other types of office machines, particularly statistical machines and parts, to Western Europe, Canada, and Japan. 

Shipments of metalworking machinery rose significantly as a result of a sharp advance in sales of metalcutting machine tools to Canada, the EC countries, and Japan. 

Exports of parts and accessories for such machinery also rose steeply, stimulated by the continued high rates of economic growth abroad. 

Shipments of materials-handling equipment, chiefly tractor-shovel loaders and industrial trucks, advanced by 14 percent in 1970 to a total of more than $600 million. Deliveries to Belgium, Brazil, and Japan scored the largest increases.

Greater mineral exploration activity in the developing countries stimulated a onefourth expansion in exports of mining and well-drilling machinery. 

Exports of all electrical machinery rose to $3 billion, but the 12 percent rate of gain was below the 1969 increase. As in the preceding year, shipments of electronic components contributed most to the advance, led by deliveries of semiconductor devices and parts. A significant portion of these exports was to the

Exports of copper rose strongly because of greater foreign demand and higher prices. Deliveries to the Netherlands, Brazil, and Italy accounted for much of the climb. Aluminum exports also continued to grow though the rate of increase was considerably less than in the preceding year. 

Exports of autos and parts fell in 1970 mainly due to a drop in shipments to Canada. For the year as a whole, automotive exports to Canada fell by 6 percent to $2.9 billion. Deliveries of parts, which comprise about two-thirds of the total, showed the biggest absolute decline, but shipments of new cars also decreased. 

Through the first nine months, deliveries of automotive products to Canadian subsidiaries were about even with their level in the comparable period of 1969. In the fourth quarter, however, shipments plummeted to the lowest level since 1967, mainly as a result of a strike. Exports of passenger cars to other countries fell, but this drop was largely offset by stepped-up shipments of trucks, parts, and accessories. 

Agricultural exports rose $1.2 billion, led by a $394 million gain in soybean exports and a $181 million rise in wheat exports. Cotton exports and vegetable fats and oil exports also increased substantially. 

Commodity Imports Imports showed an above average increase of $4.0 billion in 1970 even though industrial production dropped 3 percent. In the past, domestic industrial activity and import activity have shown similar patterns. Intermediate goods (industrial supplies and materials) accounted for at least half of all imports until 1966. In 1970, even though imported intermediate goods made up a record 3 percent of total goods supply, this group accounted for only 38 percent of all imports. 

Consumer goods (excluding food), capital goods and motor vehicles made up 43 percent of imports as compared with 21 percent in 1960 and 10 percent in 1953. As a result, import trends tended to parallel the trends in consumer and investment spending, which increased 5.6 percent in 1970 inspite of the drop in industrial activity. 

Consumer Items and Investment Goods Lead Imports Consumer goods (excluding food), and capital goods together contributed slightly over half of the total gain in imports in 1970, while industrial supplies and foods accounted for the remainder. 

Approximately one-third of food imports consists of green coffee and unrefined sugar. 

Purchases of consumer goods contributed most to the expansion in U.S. imports for the fifth consecutive year.

The 1970 advance in consumer type imports, however, was more moderate than that recorded in 1969, as U.S. personal consumption expenditures lost some of their buoyancy. Nevertheless, imports still rose faster than total personal outlays so that foreign nonfood consumer items continued to increase their share of expenditures to 2.6 percent from 2.5 percent in 1969. Although some products included in this count were purchased by businesses rather than persons, such as fleet autos, consumer spending for imports still rose faster than total spending. 

Motor vehicle imports, after gaining an average of $1.1 billion per year between 

1965 and 1969, gained only $614 million in 1970. The drop in consumer durable

5. spending and the auto strike in the last half of 1970 in both Canada and the United States account for the slower growth. Arrivals of auto products from Canada rose only 4 percent a huge reduction from the 36 percent increase of 1969. 

Imports of foreign-type automobiles, as opposed to those autos made under the Canadian Automotive Agreement, advanced by one-fourth in 1970 a sharp acceleration from the previous year's 7 percent rise. Nearly all of this advance reflected larger receipts from Germany and Japan. Imports from the latter continued to increase their share of the foreign-type passenger car market at the expense of those from Germany. 

From a 1966 high of 68 percent, the German share fell to 55 percent in 1970, while the Japanese share of the market quadrupled to 24 percent. Imports of motorcycles -- nearly all from Japan 

again showed a strong increase.

Demand for imported clothing and footwear also continued to grow. The 29 percent increase in purchases of footwear was a slight acceleration from the 1969 rate. 

Paralleling the minimal growth in consumer spending for furniture and household equipment, imports of TV receivers expanded only moderately last year. Arrivals of transistor radios showed no change in contrast to a sharp rise in the preceding year. 

Capital goods imports (excluding motor vehicles for business use) posted a larger than average gain of $564 million in 1970, rising from 5 percent of producer durable equipment purchases in 1969 to nearly 6 percent in 1970. Much of this gain reflects the delivery of orders placed in earlier years, especially during 1969, when U.S. industries' new orders, unfilled orders and actual expenditures for machinery and equipment were all at peak levels. 

Machinery imports accounted for nearly all of the $564 million advance in purchases of capital goods. The $132 million increase in arrivals of office machines especially electronic calculators from Japan and statistical machines from Canada led the expansion. Reversing the drop in 1969, purchases of textile machinery climbed by a third as foreign producers continued to hold a price advantage over domestic manufacturers. The rate of increase in imports of transistors and semiconductors, many from U.S.-affiliated firms in Mexico, Singapore, Hong Kong, and 

Korea, accelerated to 29 percent in 1970. Receipts of power machinery and switchgear rose again, as they had for several previous years. 

By contrast, arrivals of metalworking machines declined in 1970 due, in large measure, to the eighteen month decrease in industrial production. The $62 million fall in imports of civilian aircraft was partially offset by greater receipts of parts, mostly from U.S. subsidiaries in Canada. A drop in truck shipments from Canada was attributable to the effects of the auto strike. 

Imports of industrial materials and supplies increased approximately $1 billion in value in spite of the drop in industrial activity. A considerable portion of the increase, however, was due to higher prices. Illustrative of this was the 4 percent drop in quantity of steel imports in spite of the 13 percent increase in the value of shipments. The rise in the value of steel imports accounted for nearly one-fourth of the total increase in the value of intermediate goods imports. 

Although most of the increase in the value of steel purchases reflected higher prices, a minor part can be traced to shifts in the product mix. The more expensive products (tool, alloy, and stainless grades) accounted for 3.9 percent of the total, little changed from 1969. The decline in imports of this metal was attributable to the drop in U.S. production and severe supply constraints in the EC. Among the important suppliers, only Canada showed an increase in deliveries to the United States, and that increase resulted largely from the fact that 1969 receipts were depressed as a result of a long industry-wide Canadian strike. 

After sluggish growth in 1969, the value of imports of industrial and agricultural chemicals jumped by almost one-fifth. Both organic and inorganic chemicals contributed to the increase. Arrivals of manufactured fertilizer also showed a strong rise, partly as a result of higher prices for potassium chloride from Canada. 

Among other major industrial supplies, imports of manmade fiber yarns more than doubled in value to $183 million. Arrivals of tires and tubes also rose, by twofifths. Purchases of wool, on the other hand, fell by over 20 percent for the second year in a row as prices and consumption declined sharply. Lumber imports dropped in response to a fall-off in construction activity after a peak first quarter.

Heavy demand by electric utilities and other industrial users stimulated a sharp $187 million rise in imports of fuel oil. The inflow of crude petroleum, in contrast, declined because of a production cutback in Libya, the closure of an important pipeline in Syria, and tanker shortages.

The impact of inflation on the value of imports also explains, to a considerable degree, the record $920 million increase in the value of food and beverage imports during 1970. Imports of foods and beverages climbed to $6.2 billion, an increase of 

18 percent from the previous year, with coffee imports showing the largest dollar gain, namely $266 million. In terms of quantity, however, coffee imports actually dropped about 3 percent. 

The sharply higher coffee prices were due in large part to a reduction in Brazilian supplies. Sugar and cocoa imports also contributed to the rise in food imports. Because of a weather-damaged Florida crop last season, vegetable imports especially tomato purchases from Mexico advanced strongly. Imports of fish and meat, primarily boneless beef from Australia and New Zealand, posted strong rises, much of them reflecting higher prices.

Area Trade Developments 

Canada 

Following a sizable gain in 1969, exports to Canada, our largest trading partner, leveled off at $9.1 billion in 1970. The slight drop in deliveries mainly reflected conditions in the Canadian economy and the effects of the auto strike, which severely depressed shipments of automotive products in the fourth quarter. For the year as a whole, these exports dropped by $178 million, their first decline since 1961. 

Machinery sales to Canada, the largest foreign market for U.S. machinery, fell slightly as a result of reduced investment expenditures. The decline was centered in telecommunications equipment, especially TV apparatus, and agricultural machinery. 

Agricultural exports, by contrast, advanced strongly, particularly grains and soybeans for transhipment to Western Europe and Japan.

U.S. imports from Canada rose by 7 percent to $11.1 billion. This increase was the smallest since 1963. The slowdown was mainly attributable to automotive products. 

Greater purchases of industrial supplies accounted for more than half of the advance in imports. Crude petroleum and nickel entries were particularly expansive, but iron ore, natural gas, fertilizers, and steel also recorded large increases in value. 

Increased iron ore and steel purchases reflected recoveries from strike-depressed 1969 levels. Increased arrivals of aircraft parts, machinery, and meat also contributed to the rise in imports from Canada. 

Western Europe 

Exports from the United States to Western Europe advanced to $14.5 billion, 17 percent above their 1969 level. Shipments in the first half rose steeply but, after peaking in April-June, declined in the third and fourth quarters.

Two-thirds of the 1970 expansion was attributable to increased exports to members of the Common Market, where heavy investment spending and increased industrial production stimulated demand. A steep climb in shipments of machinery, led by sales of computers and other office machines, accounted for a third of the gain in exports to the Community. Deliveries of chemicals and civilian aircraft also recorded large gains. Reflecting supply constraints in Europe caused by exceptionally heavy demand, U.S. exports of steel rose sharply. Soybeans and corn accounted for a major part of the 23 percent rise in agricultural exports. Despite sluggish economic conditions in the United Kingdom and a huge drop in military shipments, U.S. exports to that country expanded by 9 percent. Greater shipments of machinery, particularly computers, and civilian aircraft contributed most to the gain. 

After a year when U.S. imports from Western Europe did not change, arrivals rose in 1970 by 10 percent to $11.2 billion. Most of this increase consisted of larger purchases from the Common Market, especially West Germany. Steep advances were recorded in imports of automobiles and machinery, while lesser increases were noted in imports of footwear and textiles. Imports from the United Kingdom continued to rise at about the same 4 percent rate as in 1969, mainly on the strength of greater machinery and whiskey purchases. Arrivals of automobiles from that country fell sharply.

-- Japan The continued rapid expansion of the Japanese economy led to an unusual one-third growth in U.S. exports to a total of $4.7 billion. Sales of computers and other types of machinery and civilian aircraft and parts recorded large advances. 

Shipments of coal and iron and steel scrap climbed in value, reflecting the boom in Japanese steel production. Shipments of agricultural products picked up strongly; larger sales of soybeans, wheat, and corn constituted most of the increase. 

Imports from Japan also recorded a sharp increase, rising by one-fifth to $5.9 billion. Spurred by vigorous demand, automobile and motorcycle purchases advanced strongly. The value of steel imports rose mainly because of higher prices. Greater receipts of electronic desk calculators and other office machines led the advance in machinery imports. Further increases were also noted in arrivals of sound recorders and clothing. 

Oceania After declining slightly in 1969, U.S. shipments to Australia and 

New Zealand rose by nearly one-fifth. Substantial economic growth in both countries spurred sales of nonelectrical machinery, motor vehicles, and chemicals. Augmenting these gains was a rise in military shipments. 

Imports from Oceania advanced 5 percent, a much more moderate rate than in 1969. 

Purchases of meat, which account for half of the imports from Oceania, posted a strong rise. Receipts of nonferrous ores also reached higher levels. These increases were partly offset, however, by reduced values for imports of wool and shellfish.

Favorable economic conditions and improved foreign exchange positions in these countries made the increase possible. Larger sales of nonelectrical machinery, chemicals, aircraft, and nonferrous metals contributed to the advance. 

Agricultural shipments, led by corn and soybeans, recovered from the previous year's decline.

Imports from the American Republics also climbed strongly. Greater purchases of foods made up more than half of the 13 percent advance. A. Trade Legislation II. Major Developments in U.S. Trade Policy 

After extensive consideration within the Administration, the President submitted trade legislation to the Congress in November 1969. In testimony presented by Administration witnesses in support of the legislation before the Ways and Means Committee, on May 11, 1970 and thereafter, emphasis was given to the following proposals: to provide a significant improvement in the means by which industries can obtain relief from injurious import competition. The proposal was that the test be as simple and clear as possible: relief should be available whenever imports are found to be the primary cause of actual or threatened injury; and that the further test in the present law, that increased imports also be found to have been caused by a past tariff concession, be dropped. to provide greater liberalization of the criteria of the present law for providing adjustment assistance to individual firms and groups of workers. to equip the President with authority to enter into new trade agreements and to reduce tariffs to a limited degree. The previous authority delegated to him by the Congress expired in 1967. Since then he has been without authority to enter into such agreements and make even small tariff reductions to fulfill our international obligations or to otherwise take such action where it would be in our interests to do so. to eliminate the American Selling Price (ASP) system of customs valuation, under which the value of imports for tariff purposes is set by the price of competitive American products rather than the price of the foreign product, which is the basis of valuation for most products. Under an agreement negotiated during the Kennedy Round, removal of ASP would bring both reciprocal cuts in foreign duties on U.S. chemical exports and the reduction of certain nontariff barriers hampering U.S. trade. Elimination of ASP would also give the United States increased credibility and flexibility in seeking the agreement of other countries to remove their nontariff barriers.

To expand the authority of the President to impose duties or other import restrictions on the products of any nation that places unjustifiable restrictions on American exports so that the authority granted in 1962 with respect to agricultural products would henceforth comprehend exports of all types of products. New authority would also permit the President to take appropriate action against nations that give subsidies or other such incentives to their exporters, resulting in unfair competition against 

American trade in third country markets. to provide for the establishment of Domestic International Sales Corporations (DISC) in order to encourage domestic companies to establish export subsidiaries in this country, rather than to establish subsidiaries abroad. The proposal would allow income tax deferral by domestic corporations selling for export, as is now provided for overseas subsidiaries of U.S. corporations. to accept the imposition of mandatory quotas on textiles because long efforts continued to be unsuccessful in negotiating a system of voluntary restraints which would have provided for orderly expansion of rapidly mounting imports of synthetic and woolen textiles.

In his message accompanying

Such a declaration was not intended to be a blank check because the President pledged to maintain close consultation with the Congress during any negotiations and to submit to the Congress any agreements requiring legislation. 

Unlike authority for tariffs, prior Congressional delegation of authority to the President to implement negotiated agreements on nontariff barriers is probably not feasible. Because of their heterogeneous nature there is no common standard applicable to nontariff barriers that would lend itself to a general delegation of authority. 

Their removal often requires specific legislative action and the exact nature of this action would not be known until negotiations had indicated what would be required. 

Therefore, Congressional action to implement negotiations on nontariff barriers would usually be expected to take place after agreements were negotiated. 

Both Republican and Democratic Administrations have consistently taken the view that the President has inherent constitutional power to negotiate trade agreements with other countries. However, because of the necessity to submit negotiated agreements to the Congress for approval, the President's hand in negotiations on nontariff barriers would be strengthened if the Congress were to declare that it favors such negotiations, appreciates that U.S. domestic law and practice will be involved, and is prepared to consider any agreements on their merits. Although such a statement would not commit the Congress with respect to any specific agreements that might be concluded, it would facilitate U.S. efforts directed toward the elimination and reduction of foreign nontariff barriers. This was the President's purpose in seeking an indication of 

Congressional intent.

Hearings in the House and the Senate on the trade bill did not deal at any length with this question of a Congressional resolution. However, the reports of the House

2. Meat

The Meat Import Act of 1964 requires the President to impose quotas on fresh, chilled or frozen beef, veal, mutton, and goat's meat if the Secretary of Agriculture estimates that meat imports for the year will total 110 percent of the annually adjusted base quota stipulated in the Act. The President may suspend the quotas if he determines that such action is required by overriding economic or national security interests or in order to ensure adequate supplies at reasonable prices. 

Under agreements first negotiated in 1968, the major supplying countries have undertaken to limit their exports to the United States to specified amounts. 

In 1970, agreement was reached with the participating countries for a modest increase above the 1969 level in each country's allocation. The initial overall restraint level for 

1970 was 1,061.5 million pounds, compared to a base quota level of 998.8 million pounds and a quota trigger point of 1,098.6 million pounds.

In previous years meat imports under the voluntary restraint program had remained below the point at which the Act required triggering of quotas. In mid-1970, however, the President triggered and then suspended quotas, and the total global level of imports into the United States was raised to 1,140 million pounds (114 percent of the Act's base quota). In October, the Secretary of Agriculture raised the annual estimate of imports to 1,160 million pounds, because of larger than expected imports not governed by the restraint program. 3. Petroleum 

The report of the Task Force on Oil Import Control was released by the President on February 20, 1970. The principal recommendation of the Task Force, that oil imports be regulated by a tariff system, was not accepted. However, its recommendation for the establishment of an Oil Policy Committee was implemented immediately, and that body met frequently during the year to recommend changes in the import program. 

In March, a ceiling was placed on oil imports from Canada and a licensing procedure was instituted, both procedures being necessitated by the failure of the previous voluntary agreement whereby Canada urged U.S. refiners not to exceed allocations issued to each at the beginning of each year. Regional Arrangements Among the Developing Countries 

Progress toward the broad goal of forming a Latin American Common Market was limited during 1970, and developments in the four existing Latin American integration groups were uneven. 

In the Latin American Free Trade Association (LAFTA) the largest of the groups whose members include 10 South American countries and Mexico there was little progress toward the goal of a full-fledged free trade area by 1980. At the 10th annual conference in November/December, the members were unable to agree on any further tariff reductions of real significance to intra-LAFTA trade, in large part because the Andean Group members were unprepared to negotiate with the other member countries. However, a number of new complementation agreements were concluded, particularly between Mexico, Brazil, and Argentina. Despite this lack of new concessions, intrazonal trade increased at a faster rate than total trade in 1969, thus recovering from relative stagnation in the preceding three years.

Movement was more rapid within the Andean Group, a subregional grouping within LAFTA composed of Bolivia, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador and Peru. The Andean Treaty, signed in 1969, established a broad range of integration objectives with a target date of 1980, as well as a series of specific measures for attainment of these objectives. 

A substantial portion of these specific commitments were scheduled for 1970, and these commitments were fulfilled. One of the most important acts of the Group was the approval by its Commission (the Group's overall policy organ) of a common code for foreign investment and industrial property. The code was designed to establish new and somewhat stricter operating regulations for foreign investment common to all Group countries. Another significant development was implementation of the 1968 agreement to establish the Andean Development Corporation, to which Venezuela also belongs. The 

Corporation is charged with searching out specific investment and development 14

D. Actions Under Articles XVIII, XIX, and XXVIII 

Adjustments or withdrawals of tariff concessions may be made under the provi sions of Articles XIX and XXVIII of the GATT. Article XIX deals with emergency actions in cases where imports are causing injury. Article XXVIII provides regular procedures for the modification or withdrawal of concessions and stipulates that such changes are to be made through negotiation and agreement with affected countries with a view to maintaining a general level of reciprocal and mutually advantageous concessions no less favorable than that in effect before the modification or withdrawal. 

Special provisions are made under Article XVIII for developing countries to modify their concessions where such action is necessary to implement their development programs. 18

As a result of negotiations under Article XXVIII covering concessions withdrawn by other countries, the United States in 1970 received new compensatory concessions involving a wide range of products from Brazil, Chile, Israel, and Surinam. Negotiations with Greece under Articles XIX and XXVIII were concluded and awaiting signature at the end of the year. Negotiations with Japan on compensation for an increase in its tariff on chewing gum were nearing completion. Other countries with which 

Article XVIII or XXVIII negotiations were in progress were: Australia, Canada, the 

European Community, India, Korea, Norway, and South Africa. 

Following notification in 1969 of its intention to withdraw concessions on stainless steel flatware, the United States in 1970 initiated discussions with Japan, the principal supplier, regarding compensation. These discussions were continuing at the close of the year.

Committee on Trade and Development (CTD) 

While the Committee on Trade and Development and its subgroups examined a number of trade problems of developing countries in 1970, dissatisfaction with the rate of progress accumulated and was openly discussed in the December session. 

Developing country members presented arguments in the CTD and in its subgroup, the Special Group on Trade in Tropical Products (SGTP), against the tariff structure of developed countries, which escalates progressively with the degree of processing applied to primary products. Article XII and XVIII consultations are conducted by the GATT Balance of Payments

Tripartite Accord on Electronic Components The Harmonized Quality Assurance System for Electronic Components, often referred to as the Tripartite Accord on Electronic Components because of its origin, represents an effort by West European countries to harmonize standards and quality assurance procedures in order to broaden the market for their electronics industries. The agree- ment provides for the harmonization of specifications and the establishment of inspection and quality control systems in the participating countries. Products from approved manufacturing plants will bear a mark of conformity, signifying their acceptance in any of the participating countries without further testing. 

The system was developed by the Tripartite Committee, consisting of standards experts from France, the Federal Republic of Germany, and the United Kingdom. This 

Tripartite Committee then invited representatives from the member countries of the European Electrical Standards Coordinating Committee (CENEL), which includes the EC and EFTA countries plus Finland, to participate in working out the details and to administer the arrangements for the harmonization of specifications. 

For this purpose, the CENEL Electronic Components Committee (CECC) was established and in April 1970 

accepted the final version of the Basic Rules. A separate organization with similar membership, the Electronic Components Quality Assurance Committee (ECQAC), was established to supervise the inspection and quality control aspects of the system. The participating countries planned to produce the first harmonized specifications and have the system operating in 1971 or 1972.

Beginning in 1968 the United States made representations to the Western European governments that the Tripartite Accord was a potential nontariff barrier to U.S. exports because there was no way for U.S. products to obtain the mark of conformity. In 

October 1969 a U.K. delegation came to the United States to explain the system and to discuss its ramifications with government and industry officials. In early 1970 a 

U.S. government mission went to London, Paris, and Bonn to discuss the possibility of 

U.S. participation, which would require the establishment of U.S. quality assurance and certification arrangements similar to those adopted in the CENEL countries. 

Informal bilateral discussions continued in the fall. In the GATT, the United States had notified the Tripartite Accord as one of the most important nontariff barriers in the standards field. In the spring of 1970, work was initiated in the GATT looking toward the possible development of a code or set of guidelines to govern countries' actions relating to standards. The United States proposed that any such code should stress that international harmonization and certification schemes be open to all countries willing and able to participate in them. 

GATT standards code would apply not only to the Tripartite Accord but to similar harmonization and certification schemes that might develop on a regional basis. Such schemes were anticipated for a wide range of products, such as pressure vessels, gas appliances, fire extinguishers, and pipelines. If formulated or operated in a discriminatory manner the schemes could have a restrictive effect on U.S. exports. 

To facilitate taking the necessary steps domestically for compliance with the requirements of regional standards schemes, the Exeuctive Branch in 1970 began preparation of a legislative proposal for submission to the Congress in early 1971. 22

At its February 1970 meeting in Caracas, the Inter-American Economic and Social Council set up a new mechanism, the Special Committee for Consultation and Negotiation (SCCN), in response to the Latin American desire for a forum for discussions with the United States on specific economic problems, particularly trade issues. Special Committee first met in Washington in April 1970 and established an Ad Hoc Group on Trade. 

The Ad Hoc Group on Trade met several times in 1970, concentrating on a detailed examination of some 800 products for which the Latin Americans requested reduction or elimination of U.S. tariff and nontariff barriers. At the October meeting of the 

Trade Group, the United States announced the following actions: (a) about 500 of the 

800 products requested by Latin American countries would be incorporated in the U.S. generalized preference proposal, including 79 products of special interest to Latin America which were added to the original proposal; (b) the United States could not at that time further liberalize quantitative restrictions; (c) the United States believed it would be useful to examine carefully quarantine, sanitary, and health laws and regulations affecting hemispheric trade, including possible expansion of financial and technical assistance to Latin American countries to help them increasingly meet U.S. standards; and (d) the United States proposed that increased attention should be directed by Latin American governments to developing their exports within the existing commercial policy framework and indicated that the United States was prepared to expand financial and technical assistance for this purpose.

As in previous years, governments cooperated in examining and, in some cases, in seeking solutions to problems affecting a number of commodities. Because primary products are the predominant source of foreign exchange earnings of developing countries, a major aim of those countries in UNCTAD has been to promote intergovernmental consultation and action on their commodity trade problems. A recommendation on "International Action on Commodities" adopted by UNCTAD-II in March 1968 singled out 19 commodities for special attention and has influenced much of the international activity on commodity problems since that date. B. Products Subject to Commodity Agreements

The International Grains Arrangement (IGA) has provided a forum for reviewing the international wheat market but has not lived up to original expectations in maintaining prices at or above the minimum levels agreed to by its members. A world surplus of wheat brought prices well below these minimums in 1969. Prices remained stable at these relatively low levels during the first half of 1970. The IGA continued to function well in other respects. The major objectives of the International Coffee Agreement are to achieve (1) relative price stability at a level which is equitable to producers and reasonable to consumers and (2) a long-term equilibrium of coffee consumption and production.

Cocoa Negotiations aimed at a cocoa agreement, which began in 1962 and were continued intermittently thereafter, reached an impasse in 1969. Automotive Products Trade Act of 1965 

The Automotive Products Trade Act of 1965 permitted the United States to put into effect tariff modifications resulting from the U.S.-Canada Automotive Products Agreement. The Agreement provides, subject to certain limitations, for duty-free trade between the two countries in vehicles and original equipment parts. The Agreement is of unlimited duration, but each government has the right to terminate it on 12 month's notice.

Primarily as a result of this Agreement, two-way trade in automotive products between the United States and Canada has expanded from $715 million in 1964 to nearly $6.2 billion in 1970. U.S. automotive exports to Canada have increased during this period from $639 million to $2.9 billion, while imports have grown from $76 million to $3.2 billion. (These figures are derived from a combination of U.S. and Canadian statistics and adjusted to reflect actual intracompany value for vehicles; they do not include expanded trade in related machinery and other products, nor do they take account of remittances and other capital flows.) 

Also as a result of the Agreement, production in the automotive industries has been largely integrated, and the differential between U.S. and Canadian motor vehicle prices has narrowed. Some progress has been made toward the Agreement's objective of a single North American automotive industry serving a single market. Full information on the implementation of the Automotive Products Trade Act, as well as on automotive production, trade, prices, and employment in 1970 is provided in the Fifth Annual Report of the President to the Congress on the operation of the agreement. The overall performance of Japanese and EC producers in 1970 was substantially in keeping with their undertakings to limit their exports of steel mill products to the United States and to maintain approximately the same product mix and geographic distribution pattern as prevailed in 1968. Japanese exporters exceeded their restraint level by 110,000 tons in 1969, which they agreed to deduct from the 1970 level.

D. East-West Trade 

The Export Administration Act, passed in December 1969, narrowed the range of controls to items whose export would significantly increase the military potential of other nations. It removed consideration of the "economic significance" of exports previously called for in the Export Control Act, and added a policy statement pointing up the importance of exports for the U.S. balance of payments and the growth and stability of the domestic economy. On the other hand, information and technology were added to the group of items whose export can be controlled for security reasons. 

During 1970, the requirement to obtain specific permission (validated license) for the export of some 1,300 commodities to various destinations in Communist Europe was lifted. 

U.S. exports to Communist countries in Eastern Europe totaled $353 million in 

1970, up 42 percent from 1969's $249 million. American imports from these areas rose more slowly from $195 to $226 million, an increase of 15 percent. Trade with these countries remained at less than 1 percent of total U.S. exports and imports, however, as contrasted with over 4 percent of the total for other industrialized countries. 

The U.S. trade with USSR and East Europe (Albania, Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, East Germany, Hungary, Poland, and Romania) rose in 1970 at a higher rate than previous years. Exports to all of these countries climbed, most significantly those to Hungary, Bulgaria and Romania. In the case of imports, there was a decline in the low level of trade with Albania and a slight drop in purchases from Czechoslovakia. The sharpest increase in imports were those from Romania, Hungary, and the USSR. 

The gain in exports to Hungary was made up mostly of animal feed, soybean, wood pulp, and chemicals, while coke and chemicals accounted for most of the rise in shipments to Bulgaria. In exports to Romania, a decline in iron and steel shipments was offset by large rises in cereals, coke, cotton, and ferroalloys. In the case of 

Poland, declines in U.S. machinery and transport equipment sales were heavily offset by sharp rises in exports of vegetable oil, animal fat, cottonseed oil, and cotton, while organic chemical exports also rose. Still noteworthy was the large proportion of agricultural products in U.S. exports to all but the Soviet Union among this group of countries. Much of this latter trade consists of large single transactions. 

The rise in U.S. imports from this area came mainly from the USSR and consisted of metals and ores (platinum, nonferrous ores, nickel and miscellaneous base metals), diamonds and residual fuel oils. The bulk of the increase from Hungary was accounted for by a rise in meat sales. Romania increased her sales of residual fuel oils, organic chemicals, shoes, furniture and clothing.

Economic and structural barriers to a higher level of U.S. trade with Eastern 

Europe included those countries' shortage of hard currencies, their strong tendency toward bilateral or barter trading arrangements, and the historic trading relationship between Eastern and Western Europe. Further restraints on enlarged trade with the USSR and Eastern Europe were the lack of legal authority for the United States to extend most-favored-nation (MFN) tariff treatment to these countries (except to Poland which has received MFN since 1960), the legislative prohibition on Export-Import Bank participation in export financing for these countries, and the existence of more restrictive U.S. export controls than those applied by other major trading countries. E. 

Developments under Safeguard Provisions of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 (TEA) 1. Escape Clause Actions 

The President acted in 1970 to make relief from injury or threatened injury from import competition available to three industries which had sought escape clause relief under Title III of the TEA. On February 21, he reestablished for three years the duty on pianos (except grands) at the second stage of the Kennedy Round reductions and authorized firms and workers in the industry to request certifications of eligibility to apply for adjustment asistance. On February 27, modified escape clause rates on certain sheet glass were continued until January 1, 1972, after which time they are to be reduced to the trade agreement rates in three annual installments; workers in the industry were authorized to request certification of eligibility to apply for adjustment assistance. In the case of the barbers' chairs and parts industry, no tariff relief was proclaimed, but the President authorized adjustment assistance for firms and workers. 29

The Tariff Commission found with regard to umbrellas and metal parts thereof that the requirements of the statute for relief had not been met. Escape clause investigations involving nonrubber footwear and bagatelle, pool and billiard balls were in process at the end of the year. 2. 

Adjustment Assistance to Workers 

Adjustment assistance for workers who experience unemployment or underemployment from import competition may be provided under Title III of the Act. Such assistance can include testing, counseling, training, job placement, cash readjustment allowances, and relocation allowances, if needed and desired. In During 1970, certifications of eligibility to receive adjustment assistance were issued covering over 8,000 employees of 21 firms that produced transmission towers, footwear, glass, pianos, electronic and automotive soft trim. In three cases, the 

Tariff Commission found that petitioners had failed to meet the statutory criteria and in four other cases the criteria were not met for part of the products involved. 

10 cases, the Tariff Commissioners were evenly divided in their vote and in all of those instances the President used his authority to accept as the findings of the Commission the views of those Commissioners who found injury and to certify the petitioners eligible to apply for adjustment assistance. 

At the end of the year, the Tariff Commission had found affirmatively in two additional cases, and requests for certification were under consideration by the Secretary of Labor. An additional 25 Tariff Commission investigations were in process on December 31. 

3. Adjustment Assistance for Firms 

Title III of the TEA provides that firms certified by the Secretary of Commerce as eligible to receive adjustment assistance after a finding of injury by the Tariff Commission may obtain relief in the form of technical assistance, financial help and tax assistance, furnished singly or in combination.

Three firms were found eligible to apply for assistance in 1970. In the case of a firm producing woven fabrics, a majority of the Tariff Commissioners found injury. 

In two other cases, involving companies manufacturing barbers' chairs and women's and misses' dress shoes, the votes were evenly divided and the President broke the tie by accepting the views of those Commissioners who had found injury. One firm withdrew its petition, but the Commission was proceeding with four other investigations at the close of the year. 

4. National Security The national security provision of the TEA, Section 232, empowers the President to adjust imports when the Director of the Office of Emergency Preparedness (OEP) determines, following an investigation, that imports of any product and its derivatives are threatening to impair the national security. On August 14, 1970, the Director of 

OEP reported his finding that manganese, chrominum and silicon ferroalloys and refined metals were not being imported into the United States in such quantities or under such circumstances as to threaten to impair the national security. At the close of the year, investigations were pending on textiles and textile manufactures and miniature and instrument precision ball bearings.

F. 

Action on Unfair Trade Practices Under Section 337, Tariff Act of 1930 

Section 337 empowers the President to exclude imports if the Tariff Commission finds that unfair methods of competition and unfair acts in the importation and sale of goods have the effect or tendency to injure substantially an efficiently and economically operated industry in the United States, to prevent establishment of such an industry, or to restrain or monopolize trade and commerce. Under Section 337 procedures the Tariff Commission makes a preliminary inquiry to determine whether there is good and sufficient reason for a formal investigation and, if so, whether it should recommend to the President issuance of a temporary order prohibiting imports pending completion of the formal investigation when such an order has been requested by the complainant.

During 1970 the Tariff Commission instituted preliminary inquiries respecting lightweight luggage, meprobamate, paper stitchers and certain plastic sheet articles and opened formal investigations respecting panty hose, ampicillin and sphygomanometers. 30

In all seven cases, the proceedings were instituted at the request of firms seeking the issuance of an exclusion order, each complaint alleging patent infringement and other unfair acts in violation of Section 337. At the year's end the seven proceedings, together with a formal investigation respecting tractor parts (instituted in 1969), were continuing. In the tractor parts case, the complaint alleged that acts of an Italian manufacturer and certain U.S. importer-distributors of the manufacturer's product have the effect or tendency to restrain or monopolize trade. 

In 1970 the Tariff Commission sent one report to the President embodying a recommendation for the issuance of an exclusion order. Upon the completion of its preliminary inquiry with respect to ampicillin, the membership of the Tariff Commission was unanimous in ordering a formal investigation but was evenly divided concerning the question of recommending a temporary exclusion order. In such cases, the President is empowered to accept the findings and recommendation of either group of Commissioners as the findings and recommendation of the Commission. A decision by the President was pending at the end of the year. G.

Implementation of Section 22 of the Agricultural Adjustment Act 

Under Section 22 of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1935, as amended, the United States has for a number of years maintained restrictions on imports of wheat and wheat products, cotton and certain cotton waste, peanuts, and certain dairy products. The Act directs the President, on the basis of an investigation and report by the Tariff Commission to regulate imports whenever such imports render or tend to render ineffective or materially interfere with any domestic production or marketing control program, price support or other program undertaken by the Department of Agriculture. To avoid any possible conflict with obligations under the GATT, the 

United States in 1955 secured from the Contracting Parties a waiver covering import quotas under Section 22.

During 1970, the Administration continued to take actions designed to bring about a better balance between supply and demand for the commodities under Section 22 regulation and no changes were made in the import quotas for such products. In the course of the year, however, imports of four additional dairy products (ice cream; chocolate crumb containing 5.5 percent or less, by weight, of butterfat; certain cheeses containing 0.5 percent or less, by weight, of butterfat; and animal feeds containing milk and milk derivatives) rose sharply, displacing domestic milk and butterfat. 

Following an investigation and findings by the Tariff Commission that such imports were interfering with the price support program and after consulting with exporting countries, the President on December 31 proclaimed quotas on the four products, effective January 1, 1971.

H. 

Investigation of the Economic Factors Affecting the 

Use of Tariff Items 806.30 and 807.00

On September 30, 1970, the Tariff Commission submitted to the President a report on the results of its investigation of the economic factors affecting the use of items 

806.30 and 807.00 of the Tariff Schedules of the United States. The President had requested such a report because of the significant growth in imports entering the United States under item 807.00 and because strong conflicting claims were advanced regarding the effects of these tariff provisions on business, labor, consumers, and the balance of payments. Neither item 806.30 nor 807.00 are bound tariff concession items.

Under the provisions of item 806.30, articles of base metal that have been manufactured in the United States, exported for processing abroad, and returned to the United States for further processing, are subject to duty only on the value of the foreign processing. Pursuant to item 807.00, imported articles assembled abroad that contain U.S. manufactured components are subject to duty on the full value of the imported product less the value of the U.S. fabricated components contained therein. 

The Tariff Commission concluded that for the bulk of the trade under items 806.30 and 807.00, and particularly that supplied by industrialized countries, the duty saving has seldom been the major factor upon which the decision to utilize foreign facilities was based. With respect to some imports, especially those from developing countries, the Commission concluded that (a) the tariff preference has served to encourage foreign assembly operations; (b) repeal of these provisions, however, probably would not significantly slow the development by U.S. concerns of production facilities in low-wage areas for their labor intensive operations. 31

The Tariff Commission estimated that repeal of items 806.30 and 807.00 would have a net adverse effect, based on 1969 trade, of $150-200 million on the U.S. trade balance.

The Tariff Commission estimated that foreign operations utilizing items 806.30 and 807.00 provided employment in 1969 for about 121,000 workers in foreign countries. 

The Commission concluded, however, that repeal of the tariff items would probably result in only a modest number of jobs being returned to the United States, which would likely be more than offset by the loss of domestic jobs among the 37,000 workers producing components for export or further processing products imported under these provisions.

Taking into account the Tariff Commission report, the Executive Branch at the end of the year was preparing recommendations to the President on whether these tariff items should be repealed, modified, or retained without change. I.

Actions Under the Antidumping Act 

The Antidumping Act, 1921, as amended, imposes on the Secretary of the Treasury the responsibility for determining whether foreign merchandise is being, or is likely to be, sold in the United States at less than its fair value. If the Secretary of the 

Treasury makes an affirmative determination, the case is forwarded to the U.S. Tariff 

Commission to determine whether an industry in the United States is being or is likely to be injured or is prevented from being established by reason of such imports. In the event of affirmative determinations by both agencies, a finding of dumping is made by the Secretary of the Treasury and a special dumping duty is assessed on all imports into the United States covered by the finding to the extent any dumping margins are found to exist.

In May 1970, the Treasury Department announced a change in policy regarding the acceptance of letters of assurance that dumping margins have been eliminated as a result of price revisions. Such letters are now accepted only in cases where the margins were minimal in relation to the volume of sales involved. Previously the 

Treasury would issue a determination of no sales at less than fair value on the basis of such letters without regard to the size of the dumping margins. Simultaneously with this change, the Antidumping Regulations were revised to provide for a termination of investigation in such cases in substitution for the former determination of no sales at less than fair value, but no terminations by reason of price revisions occurred in 1970.

In December 1970, the Bureau of Customs acquired a substantial number of additional persons to process complaints against price discrimination. This increase was intended to make it possible to move investigations more expeditiously and with greater thoroughness. In furtherance of the high priority placed on effective administration of anti-price discrimination statutes in the Treasury Department, an Office of Tariff and Trade Affairs was established in the Office of the Secretary to facilitate the exercise of policy supervision over this area and to deal with other trade problems associated with the Bureau of Customs.
