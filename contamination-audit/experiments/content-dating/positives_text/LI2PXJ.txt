POPULATION OF PHILADELPHIA AND ENVIRONS IN 1950 FOREWORD 

At the request of the Philadelphia City Planning Commission in March, 1944, the Institute of Local and State Government assisted in the organization of an Advisory Committee for studies of population and economic problems of interest to the Commission. Because of unavoidable delays in the appointment of the staff of the 

Commission, a contract was arranged with the Institute for the initiation of studies "toward analyses of the population of Philadelphia, city and region." 

It was understood that the Advisory Committee would determine the nature of the studies to be made and that its working subcommittees would direct the technical work of the temporary staff appointed for the project. 

Dr. Simon S. Kuznets was primarily responsible for the writing of this report. Dr. David T. Rowlands did most of the statistical work. They were assisted generally by numerous conferences with the other members of the Subcommittee on Population Projection, Drs. Burdick and Hutchinson.

The importance of data on population to intelligent economic planning, Both business and government private or public, can hardly be overstressed. are by men and for men. In a thickly settled urban area in particular, knowledge of the size of the resident population and of its composition is indispensable to a judgment of the area's economic potentialities in the future. For what an area does depends in large part upon the number and skills of the working members of its population; and on the wants of the men and women who work and live in it. lation.

Planning looks to the future in an attempt to see how the available resources can best be utilized to maximize the welfare of an area's popu- What these resources are, can be learned only from seeing how they were used in the immediate past; and how the drives that motivated people to economic effort succeeded, partially and fully, within the basic framework of the country's social institutions. In looking towards the future, planning requires not so much attempts to gauge exactly what will happen forecasts which by the nature of the case are likely to be guesses easily invalidated by the play of shifting circumstances. What is needed rather are projections of past trends, with due consideration of the factors that are likely to carry such trends forward or to modify them. For in considering the feasible tasks of planning, we wish to know what the forces in the immediate and longer past are likely to produce if allowed to continue operating, without serious interference, in the immediate future. projected results of forces operating in economic society in the past will These

Part 1. THE STUDY AND ITS FINDINGS Framework of the Study then set the framework within which policy can be formulated intelligently either to strengthen such forces if their results seem beneficial or adjust them in the direction of a greater yield to social well being. 

This viewpoint was decisive in setting the character of the population estimate for Philadelphia and Environs. This estimate is not a forecast of what such population will be in the year 1950. It is a projection from 1940of the basic factors that produce population change migration -D births, deaths, and projection based either upon actual observations for a part of the period (1940-44) or upon assumptions that the several factors in popu lation change will function during the 1940-50 decade in a way analogous to some comparable period in the recent past. Thus, starting with the known population of 1940 (and for some aspects of it, of 1944) and assuming continuation of basic trends of births, deaths, and migration, we arrive at a judgment of what the population of the area is likely to be by 1950. In fact, population in that year may be below or above the projection below if some transient circumstance (such as a business depression) results in a temporarily intensified drop in the birth rate of greater than usual out-migration from the area; above it if the transient circumstance is favorable. important, it must be remembered that the forces whose results are projected and embodied in the estimates can be modified, if to a limited extent, by deliberate policy of business, government, or other major social institutions. 

True, the factors at play, whose results are being projected, are fairly persistent, particularly births and deaths; and can be affected only by gradual action. But even those can be modified by policy; and the latter is more influential in its possible effects on migration in and out of an area that is only a small part of a big country such as ours. Most

It must, therefore, be kept clearly in mind that the estimates are projections, which can be modified by some transient circumstances beyond man's control as well as by deliberate policy steps. In this latter connection, the estimates are partly a condition that must be accepted; and partly a possible challenge to action. For they indicate, as well as such an indication can be given by past data, the framework of basic conditions as determined by past trends within which policy decisions have to be made. 

The projection yields measures not only of total population, but also of its components by sex, age, and race (white and non-white). A question may legitimately be raised as to why the projection extends to the year 1950 only; and why it fails to provide breakdowns that seem more important for many purposes, such as distribution by family units, a distinction of the labor force and its components, a segregation of local areas finer than just 

Philadelphia county, and the seven neighboring counties (Environs). Before summarizing the findings of the study, we provide answers to the two questions just asked.

In limiting the projection to 1950, rather than extending it a longer period into the future, there was full cognizance of the fact that for many planning tasks it is insufficient to have an idea of the likely population just a few years ahead. Many planning decisions refer to capital items whose economic justification must rest in the needs of a population several decades hence. But two considerations made imperative limiting this initial study of population prospects to the immediate future. The first was the importance of evaluating, on the basis of the fullest evidence available, the immediate effects of the war. With the study begun in 1944 in the midst of a major world conflict, when so many long standing trends appear to have been Population of Philadelphia and Environs in 1950 page 3

And on the then reasonable assumption that the war would be over well within the present decade, (an assumption now justified by events) an estimate for 1950 could most conveniently indicate the combined results of the war and immediate postwar shifts; added, of course, to whatever basic long term trends could be assumed to have continued through the decade. 

The second consideration was availability of data. If a careful study of the effects of war and immediate postwar readjustments were to be made, we had to use to the full the data presently available and use them within time limits within which they could be validly applied to yield a defensible projection. These data are either those provided in the 1940 and the two preceding decennial censuses; or secured for the recent war years. An elaborate projection, based upon a careful consideration of births, deaths, and migration, could not be extended beyond 1950, except with such widening of the margins of error as to render a much cruder and less laborious trend extrapolation as satisfactory and much less costly. 

For these reasons the detailed projection of the population of the Philadelphia area was limited to 1950. And for the same reasons, with the project conceived as a first in a series of studies in the field, the components of the population distinguished were only those that were indispensable to a proper application of demographic factors. Summary of Findings a. Definition of Area and Year 

Three areas are distinguished in the population data used and projections made: Philadelphia county; Philadelphia Environs, comprising the seven counties of Bucks, Chester, Delaware, and Montgomery in Pennsylvania, and Camden, Burlington, and Gloucester in New Jersey; and the Philadelphia 

Camden Industrial Area, which combines Philadelphia county with the seven counties of the Environs.

These area units are subsequently referred to as 

Philadelphia, Environs, and Philadelphia and Environs.

The projection begins with the Census population of 1940 and extends ten years forwards. Since the official date of the 1940 Census is April 1st, the projections for 1950 refer to the population as of the same date in 1950. 

We should finally note that resident population is being estimated rather than population by place of work. This distinction is of importance in a local area such as ours, since many residents of the Environs are employed or work within Philadelphia; and some residents of Philadelphia may be employed or work in the Environs. Indeed, the lines of residence and place of work may cross the boundaries even of the whole Philadelphia and Environs area; although it is to be doubted that the proportional weight of such crossings for the wider area is of quantitative significance. b.

Several important trends stand out. In Philadelphia county proper, the high rates of growth observed in the last quarter of the 19th century decline gradually; with a particularly severe break immediately after World War I.

Against these longer term trends, what do the projections for 1950 suggest concerning the growth of population in the area during the present decade? The answer stated most briefly is that they indicate continuation of past trends, interpreted in terms of the longer term decline of the rate of increase and disregarding such shorter term changes as were due to the depression of the 1930's or the suburban boom of the 1920's. 

The projections thus suggest a moderate growth of population in Philadelphia county, of 4.8 per cent from 1940 to 1950; an appreciably greater relative addition to the population of the Environs, of 12.1 per cent over the decade; a rate of growth for the whole area that is in between the rate for the 1920's and the 1930's; and a further shift in the proportional distribution of the population of the wider area in favor of Environs. 

As already indicated, the projections pass from the known population for 1940 to the estimate for 1950 by separate measures of deaths, births, and migration. While the procedure does not permit a clean-cut separation between the natural increase factors (i.e. balance of births and deaths) on the one hand, and the migration factors on the other, an approximate distinction between these two elements is possible. (1) We can, therefore, see how the projected net change from 1940 to 1950 is derived; of what importance is the part due to excess of births over deaths and that resulting from migration; and how these basic components of change affect differently 

Philadelphia county and the Environs (Table I-2). 

(1) The natural increase factors, as measured here, include births associated with cumulative increase or decrease during the decade due to migration (as distinct from births associated with the population of 1940). 

To this extent natural increase factors are given more weight, and the migration factor less weight, than would have been the case were it possible to segregate the births in migrant families.

Several features of Table I-2 are worthy of note. 

First, the projection suggests that the absolute net addition over the decade to the population of the Environs will be greater than those to the population of Philadelphia county, (about 153 thousand as compared with 92 thousand) this in spite of the bigger initial population in Philadelphia county than in the Environs. Second, this larger absolute and percentage addition to the population of the Environs is not due to a larger absolute addition resulting from excess of births over deaths. The projected absolute additions due to natural increase are about the same in the Environs and in 

Philadelphia county (64 thousand).

And even on a relative scale, the projected percentage additions due to natural factors are not much greater for the Environs than for Philadelphia (5.0 per cent as compared with 3.3 per cent); and a large part of the difference is due to greater relative immigration in the Environs. Third, it follows that the main difference is in the migration factor. The net residual of migration is derived by compounding the effects of migration during the war years (1940-44) with effects of non-war migration of the type that used to characterize the area during the pre-war years. The importance of the migration component in net additions projected for the population of the area, as revealed in Table 1-2, must clearly be recognized in interpreting the projections. Unlike birth rates and death rates, migration rates are subject to rapid changes; and are more easily affected by policy. In trying to project the migration currents for the decade, we have attempted to use fully the available information and supplement it by assumptions that were in reasonable consonance with our knowledge of the processes involved. Thus, the assumptions concerning proportions of war migrants to be retained in the area (2/3 for white males, 1/2 for females, full retention without replacement for non-white); the use of the non-war migration patterns for Philadelphia for the 1920-30 decade (by age groups for the white population) and for the 1930-40 decade (by age groups for the nonwhite) - all were chosen as representing the most probable characteristics of the migration movements. 

But we must equally well recognize the developments that might prevent these movements from taking place. A long delay in residential construction might inhibit the flow from Philadelphia county to the Environs; a deep postwar depression occurring before 1950 might result in a larger loss of people in Philadelphia county and a smaller gain in the Environs than those projected, One must, therefore, exercise judgment in accepting or rejecting the assumptions concerning migration; or in modifying them as time passes and changing circumstances reduce or increase possible expected migration. Nevertheless, the sex structure of a population is an important demographic and economic variable. Table 1-3 shows the longer term trends in it in Philadelphia and Environs; as well as the projection to 1950. 

It will be seen that Philadelphia county has long been characterized by a relatively low male-female ratio. The proportion rose appreciably to almost equality by 1920; but then dropped rapidly again. It is perhaps significant that the decades of the decline in the sex ratio were also decades of a marked decline in the percentage rate of increase of total population.

One may note that the ratio in Philadelphia county (i.e. city proper) tended to be lower than in most of the other big cities of the country. A comparison of Philadelphia with 13 other cities with a population of over half a million showed that in 1940 its sex ratio was lower than in 9 of these cities (Baltimore, Buffalo, Chicago, Cleveland, Detroit, Milwaukee, New York, Pittsburgh, San Francisco), equal to that in 1 (Los Angeles), and higher than that in 3 (Boston, St. Louis, Washington, D. C.). A reference to the industrial characteristics of these urban centers suggests clear association with the sex ratio: it is higher in cities in which industries requiring male labor are dominant and those ordinarily employing female labor are less important, and lower in cities with large proportions of industries using female labor (e.g. government in Washington, D. C., textiles in Philadelphia and Boston). But, as for many social phenomena, it is difficult to demonstrate whether the relative plenitude of male or female labor is the cause or the effect.

C. Migration The procedure for this, for local areas a most dynamic and elusive demographic factor, is based upon a distinction between non-war and war migration. 

The former is the type of migration that was observed in peace years; that can be expected to have continued even during the war, but was not apparent on the surface because it was overlaid by the war migration currents; and that may emerge to surface again in the remaining years of the decade. This report summarizes the findings of a study initiated at the suggestion of and prepared under the general guidance of the Research Program Advisory Committee of the Philadelphia City Planning Commission. It carries the 1950 population estimates, summarized in a companion report, two steps further: namely, estimates of the 1950 labor force by age and sex and of its prospective distribution by industry under conditions of high-level and lowlevel employment. The year 1950 was chosen because it was believed that the reconversion period would be over by that time and that relative economic stability would characterize this year. The estimates are based on decennial 

Census of Population data and therefore refer, in general, to conditions as of the spring of 1950. The data of the report concern residents of the city (co-terminous with the county). of Philadelphia and the seven surrounding counties, (1) hereinafter referred to as the "environs." 

The eight counties covered by this study comprise the Philadelphia-Camden Industrial Area (Census of Manufactures' designation), hereinafter referred to as "Philadelphia and Environs" or as "the area."

The method used in developing the labor force estimates places great reliance on prewar trends in labor market participation rates and on the estimates of the 1950 population by age and sex. The employment projections are dependent on the employment levels and the structural patterns of employment in 1920, 1930, and 1940, a method applicable only in a community (1) Bucks, Montgomery, Chester, and Delaware counties in Pennsylvania, and 

Camden, Burlington, and Gloucester counties in New Jersey.

II. LONG-TERM TRENDS IN THE CITY'S LABOR FORCE 

Changes in the size of the labor force in the city of Philadelphia since 1900 are shown in Table 3. Between 1900 and 1940, the population of working age in the city grew, but by increasingly smaller amounts from decade to decade, while the labor force increased to 1930, declined in 1940, and rose again in 1944. The rapid expansion of the labor force in earlier decades was directly associated with immigration from foreign countries. 

Expansion during World War I and World War II reflects immigration to this area from other regions of the country. The decline in the labor force between 1930 and 1940 was associated with emigration from the city, particularly by male workers.

Between 1910 and 1920 the net increase in the labor force was less than in the previous decade. Slightly over one hundred thousand workers were added, of whom 92,000 were males and 15,000 females. In 1920 the labor force reflected the effects of an expansion in the munitions industries during World War I. There was a net increase of 195,000 in the population of working age between 1910 and 1920, the maximum increase in recent decades. The 

Negro population increased by 50,000 during this period. Expansion of the labor force through immigration from foreign countries stopped after World War I, and the proportion of the city's workers who were foreign-born declined between 1920 and 1930.

Between 1920 and 1930, the labor force increased by 71,000, of whom 41,000 were males and 30,000 females. This increase was smaller than that of earlier decades and accompanied a population increase of less magnitude than in the previous decade, or 145,000 (14 years of age and over). The year 1930 marks a turning-point in growth of the population and labor force in Philadelphia. In that year, the school population of the city reached a maximum, reflecting high birth rates in earlier years, particularly during World War I. It is noteworthy that a decline in the excess of births over deaths in the city had started as early as 1921. These and other population changes affected the size of the labor force in 1940, and will continue to affect it unless the permanent effects of World War II reverse prewar trends. 

Between 1920 and 1930, economic conditions in the city were relatively prosperous after the first postwar depression of 1321-22. Negro migrants continued to come to the city, and a net increase during this decade of 61,000 in the Negro population of working age is associated with an increase in the city's labor force beyond what would have been expected for 1920 survivors.

Between 1930 and 1940, the total population of the city declined by 20,000, although the population of working age increased by 61,000. The labor force declined by 5,000, representing the difference between a net increase of 24,000 women and a net decline of 29,000 men. During this decade there was an estimated loss to the city's expected labor force through emigration of 45,000 workers, over half of whom were males 35 to 54 years of age. That the number of women in the labor force continued to increase while the number of men declined reflects a change in the sex composition of the population as a result of the emigration of males and the relatively greater prosperity (or milder depression) during the thirties of the industries and types of occupations employing women than of the heavy industries employing men. Between 1930 and 1940, the Negro population of working age increased by 23,000.

Between 1940 and 1944, the population of working age increased by 137,000 and the total labor force (including Philadelphia residents in the armed forces) by 161,000. This increase is attributable in part to an estimated immigration of 106,000 persons 14 years of age and over from surrounding areas and other parts of the country. In addition, thousands of young persons normally in school, housewives, and retired and handicapped workers entered or re-entered the labor market. These sources of labor supply more than offset the withdrawal from the civilian labor force to the armed forces, estimated at approximately 172,000 persons, as of May 1944.

In May 1944, the total labor force (including the armed forces) was composed of 709,000 men and 335,000 women. This constitutes the largest labor force on record for the city. Although the proportion of the population of working age in the labor market declined consistently at each decennial interval since 1910, some groups in the labor force have increased, both relatively and absolutely. 

This difference in trend necessitated separate consideration of men and women in particular age-groups and is therefore accorded brief analysis. 

Most men of working age are employed or seeking work, particularly. between the ages of 21 and 65, and the proportion of these age-groups in the labor force remained fairly constant. 

The proportion of boys under 21 in the labor force is influenced by the age at which compulsory schooling stops, the trend in educational requirements for jobs, and the presence or absence of job opportunities for young people. 

For men near or over 65, the availability of income from investments, pensions, savings, or social security benefits determines the number of retired workers and hence the proportion dependent on gainful employment.

In the case of women, similar considerations prevail for the youngest and oldest workers, but there are both social and economic considerations that affect the intermediate age-groups, numerically the most important in the labor force, and hence, determine the proportion of the total female population of working age in the labor force. The major economic consideration in a local labor market is the availability of jobs at which women can be employed. In addition to domestic and personal service occupations, which traditionally employ women, the availability of jobs in clerical, sales, and light manufacturing occupations is important. Within this framework, changes in marriage and birth rates that affect the home responsibilities of women, and changes in the relative proportions of single, widowed, and separated to married women, influence the proportions in the labor market at different age-levels at a given time or from one period to another. 

Since 1910, the proportion of men of working age in the labor force has declined in Philadelphia as a result of increased schooling for young persons and increased rates of retirement and earlier retirement for older persons (Table 4, Chart 1). The proportion of women in the labor market declined from 1910 to 1920, reflecting an increase in the city's marriage and birth rates during World War I and a decline in the hand crafts, such as dressmaking, which employed relatively large numbers of women in earlier decades. The increase in women's labor market participation rates that followed 1920 is attributable to two factors. One is the increase in the employment opportunity for women, and the other, the slight decline in the levels of marriage rates since 1920 which has accompanied a growing deficiency of male relative to female population in the city. IV. TRENDS IN THE INDUSTRIAL PATTERN OF EMPLOYMENT AND A PROJECTION FOR 1950 

The analysis of trends in the distribution of employment by industry, like the analysis of the labor force, is concerned with prewar differences in pattern and their projection to 1950. If the permanent military establishment after the war for the nation is in the neighborhood of (9) 2.5 millions, the 1950 civilian labor force for both the city and the area will be only slightly larger than the total labor force in 1940. 

Because of the relative stability in the size of the civilian labor force over the decade, projected employment patterns are presented on two bases; one, high-level employment at the rate of unemployment in 1920 (the lowest rate for the years covered in this analysis) and two, low-level employment, at the rate of unemployment in 1940. The employment levels on the former base are the equivalent of or approach the levels of civilian employment during the war. The levels on the latter base are the levels of activity actually experienced in 1940. 

In suggesting the probable distribution of these employment levels by industry, emphasis has been placed on the pattern in recent decades.

In addition to specific functions connected with the planning of public improvements in the city, the Philadelphia City Planning Commission has a coordinating function in relation to the work of other community agencies. With respect to both of these functions, a knowledge of the probable size of the postwar labor force and of its distribution by industry is important. Since some residents of the city cross boundary lines to work and some residents of surrounding counties work in the city, and since the economy of the city and area are interwoven in numerous other ways, the estimates of this report are presented both for the city and for the area of which the city is the major nucleus. 

The general assumptions on which the 1950 estimates of labor force and employment are based imply the resumption of prewar trends or structural patterns. Even the population projected for 1950, on which the labor force estimate is based, is no exception to this. The implied population increase in this projection is the net result of a larger gross increase recorded during the war and an estimated gross loss for the decade comparable with that of each of the two decades before the war. In effect, therefore, the population projected for 1950 is weighted by the prewar experience in retention or loss of population. To the extent that the war permanently alters the ability of this community to hold population or, in other words, radically changes the prewar competitive position of the area in the national economy, the assumptions on which these estimates are based are invalid. In the absence of such permanent changes, which are not impossible in the more distant future but are not probable by 1950, the estimates presented in this report are guide-posts for agencies concerned with planning social and economic programs in the community.

The increase in the estimated size of the labor force of the city and area from 1940 to 1950 is the net result of three major factors: an increase in the population of working age, a change in its age and sexcomposition, and the assumed continuation of prewar trends in its pattern of participation in the labor market. The dominating factor from the point of view of absolute numbers is the increase in the population of working age. The labor force increase, as the result of this factor alone, would be 36,000 persons for the city and 104,000 persons for the area from 1940 to 1950. A change in the sex ratio in the population of working age has the effect of reducing the size of the labor force, but the assumed trends in age-specific labor force participation rates applied to the changing age structure of the population more than offset the reduction that is due to the shift in sex ratio. The net effect of all three factors is to increase the total labor force of the city by 46,000 and that of the area by 131,000 workers. The total labor force of the city in 1950 is estimated to be 930,000 workers, 633,000 men and 297,000 women, and of the area, 1,550,000 workers, 1,075,000 men and 475,000 women. If each contributes its share to a larger armed force personnel in 1950 (estimated at 2.5 millions for the country), the civilian labor force of the area and the city is only slightly larger than the total labor force in 1940.

Although the major conditions for postwar prosperity will stem from national and international rather than local policy decisions, awareness by the community of the magnitude of its problems is some assurance that no real opportunities for economic progress will be by-passed. 

One may reasonably hope that employment in the area in 1950 will not fall to 1940 levels. 

There is, however, no assurance that employment will be maintained at levels comparable with those that have prevailed during the war, despite the large deferred demand for housing and consumer goods and a back-log of war savings in the area and nation.

The Philadelphia Area is fortunate in having well-established, diversified manufacturing industries and many other types of economic enterprise, Its reconversien problems will be less serious than in many other areas that have had a highly specialized type of war expansion. Nevertheless, reconversion unemployment in the city and area may reach relatively high levels. It is expected, however, that most reconversion unemployment will be of short duration and that unemployment compensation reserves will be sufficient to finance workers displaced from war jobs until other jobs are secured.

The years between 1945 and 1950, for which no estimates are presented in this report, are expected to be years of numerous and extensive changes in the population and economy. Thousands of workers displaced from shipyards and aircraft and ordnance plants will have to seek other jobs. 

Veterans will be returning to old jobs, where they may displace other workers, or will be seeking new jobs. Although some persons at work during the war who were not in the labor market before the war may retire promptly, it is not clear that all of them will be in a position to retire immediately after the war. The adjustments required in terms of declining take-home pay alone would be important, even if every worker had a job or could secure another job promptly after lay-off from a war plant. If the experience after World War I is repeated, there will be strikes to maintain workers' standards of living and relatively high labor turnover (the "individual strike") as workers seek to make new adjustments in jobs and income. Community planning is needed to minimize the effects of labor disputes and to offer vocational counselling and placement facilities to workers who will nee such aid during the reconversion and postwar years.

DEC On the basis of data which have become available since the publication of the above report, it is now apparent that the estimates of population for 1950 contained therein are considerably lower than should now be anticipated.

The reasons are numerous, but the principal ones may be summarized 

(1) Births and Birth Rates 1940-1945-1950 

The birth figures for the first half of the decade were based partly on estimates. (See report, p.31.) However, a later recheck with actual birth figures for the entire eight counties, which were not available at the time the report was prepared, indicates that the estimate of births for the first five years of the decade was about 2,200 too low. 

Additions by birth for the second half of the decade (1945-1949) were estimated by using the 1940 ratio of children 0-4 years of age to women of child-bearing age (15-44 years) and adding 10 per cent.: (Sec report, p.33.)

According to annual summaries released by the Bureau of the Census, the U. S. crude birth rate for 1940 was only 17.2 per 1,000 population, but rose to 19.8 in 1945, to 23.3 in 1946, and to 26.2 for the first seven months of 1947, or 15.1 per cent, 35.5 per cent, and 52.3 per cent respectively above the 1940 rate. Because of the exceptionally high ratio of young married couples who will be establishing their families during the next few years, it is not reasonable to expect that the birth rate will decline to anything like the 1940 level by 1950, although it may not continue on the 1947 level to that time. Thus a 10 per cent increase in the 

1940 reproduction rate would not be sufficient to account for the increase in additions by birth during the second half of the decade. 

(2) Survivals 1940-1950 

Throughout the original study, 1939 Abridged Life Tables were employed as the basis for surviving the population to 1950. Due to changes which have occurred in the death rate and more particularly in the infant mortality rate - it now appears that estimates of the population surviving to 1950 may also be too low, especially within certain age groups. (See report p.30.)

(3) Retention of War-Migrants 

Because of uncertainty regarding the actual duration of the war, the length of time that would be required for reconversion after the war, and the possibility of a serious depression before the close of the current decade, there may have been a tendency for the authors of this study to be over-conservative regarding the portion of wartime inmigrants that would be retained to 1950. (See report, p.39.)

Evidence from various sources, including unpublished studies by the City Planning Commission staff indicates that the Philadelphia Area has come thus far through the post-war period with no great loss of either population, as evidenced by housing demand, or labor force, as evidenced by levels of employment. While it is still possible that a depression may occur before 1950, its effect upon the population of this Area would undoubtedly be less than if it had occurred at an earlier date.

The Institute was established as a training and service unit July 1, 1937, through a gen erous grant by the Samuel S. Fels Fund, the founder of which has long been interested in the improvement of municipal and state government. It is governed by a board composed of administrative officers of the University of

Pennsylvania and persons prominent in Pennsylvania civic affairs, and is housed in one of the buildings of the University.

The purpose behind the establishment of the 

Institute is threefold: 

1-(a) to train selected college graduates for future service as local or state government officials; (b) to develop in-service training programs for employees already engaged in public work; (c) to educate selected groups of private citizens, including undergraduates, for more effective citizenship; 

2-to provide technical advice and assistance directly to local and state officials, especially those in Pennsylvania; and 

3-to provide research assistance on problems of local and state governments, with special reference to Pennsylvania.

These declared objectives reveal the intention of the founders of the Institute to create an organization which should be useful to local and state government officials in a unique way. It does not duplicate the activities of purely research agencies, nor confine itself to education and training. It does not act as a taxpayers' agent by attempting to reduce governmental expenditures. It does not merely investigate civic conditions and make reports, nor engage in municipal "reform." The Institute leaves the promotion of public economies, the exposure of official wrongdoing, the advocacy of controversial measures, and the militant type of over-all reform to existing organizations, and tries to achieve supplementary objectives. It strives to help the incumbent public officials do the job that is to be done. In this helping process, however, the staff of the Institute endeavors to discover improvements in techniques and to advance the science and art of governmental administration, especially at the local level.
