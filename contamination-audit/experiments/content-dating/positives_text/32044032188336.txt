Section 1 Demonstrative Evidence-General Rules 

From current writings one might suppose that the trial techniques -called "demonstrative evidence" were a discovery of recent years. 

The suggestion is erroneous. A careful survey of the Arizona decisions in the field of scientific, experimental, and demonstrative evidence will disclose a great variety of useful precedents. Many of them are found—perhaps surprisingly-in the earlier volumes of Arizona Reports. The decisions in the area under discussion are widely dispersed in the topics of the Arizona Digest. It is the purpose of this brief article to bring them together-whether from civil or criminal litigation-in one summary (1).

Real Evidence vs. Testimonial 

Whenever a dispute involving facts is at issue there are, in general, two possible methods of persuading the trier of fact (2): 

1)-By real or demonstrative evidence, that is, a view of the very premises in question, or production of the gun, knife, automobile, or other object in question; OR, 2)-By testimonial evidence. 

Demonstrative evidence is such that the jury or judge trying the facts can by the direct use of their senses perceive facts in question, whereas with testimonial evidence the trier of fact is asked to believe that certain facts are true only because a witness states them to be so (3). However, it is important to note that demonstrative evidence may itself be either direct or circumstantial (4): a plaintiff's scar exhibited to the jury is direct evidence of its existence, nature and extent; whereas a photograph of skidmarks is not direct evidence of speed, but is proof of a fact from which the jury may properly infer the ultimate fact of speed.

It is a well-established general rule that demonstrative evidence may be utilized wherever available: whatever the jury may learn through the ear by descriptions of witnesses, they may learn directly through the eye from objects or pictures (5). Further, real or demonstrative evidence such as a photograph is not made inadmissible simply because oral evidence about the object or condition has already been received, for a party cannot limit the number of witnesses or say how much proof shall be produced against him on the controverted issues (6).

The constitutional privilege against compulsory self-incrimination generally does not apply to real or physical evidence such as physical scars, marks or characteristics, tracks, footprints, stains, fingerprints and the like (7).

Foundation Required, Same Condition,

Chain Of Custody 

Where a material object is offered in evidence there is no necessity of showing that the article is exactly and identically the same so long as there is a preliminary showing that no substantial change has taken place in its condition since the time in issue (12). 

And, when an object, photograph or other demonstrative evidence is offered, there is no necessity of showing a chain of custody (13), nor negativing every possibility of tampering (14), nor producing the photographer or other person creating the object (15), so long as some witness can reasonably identify it as being or portraying the object in question, and as being in substantially the same condition as at the time in issue (16).

Even if there has been some change in condition, or even if only a part of an object is offered as demonstrative evidence, it may be received if a showing is made that such changes do not obliterate or change the particular condition sought to be shown (17).

It is generally held that where a physical condition of a party is in issue he has a right to exhibit the injury, scar, or other condition to the jury (18). The trial court has discretion with regard to such displays (19).

Illustration By Sketches, Diagrams Or Other Objects 

The court in its discretion, where it will assist the jury to understand the testimony, may permit witnesses to illustrate points of testimony, by making sketches or diagrams (20). Such illustrations or sketches are not rendered improper because they are not to scale or precisely accurate (21). Where a map or diagram is to be offered in evidence as an exhibit, its substantial correctness must usually be established, but where its purpose is only to illustrate what the witness is saying, the drawing may be permitted if the court feels it will enable the jury to better understand the statements of the witness (22). Likewise a witness may be allowed, in the court's discretion, to illustrate his testimony by the use of models, photographs, physical objects, skeletons, models of the human body, and the like (23). 

Under the same principle, it is generally held that counsel in his opening statement (24), in examination of witnesses (25), and in summation may make sketches on paper or blackboard, and may utilize various objects for the purpose of illustrating testimony or evidence. The use by counsel of such drawings or objects is in the discretion of the trial court (26).

The Arizona cases draw a distinction between unscaled maps or sketches offered solely to illustrate testimony, and accurate maps or drawings offered as actual substantive evidence independent of the oral testimony (27). Where the map is used not as independent evidence but solely to illustrate oral testimony and make it more understandable, the court should on request instruct the jury as to the limited function and purpose of the exhibit (28). 

Under certain circumstances, however, counsel may be guilty of prejudicial misconduct in exhibiting to the jury physical objects or items which are not offered or introduced in evidence (29).

Unpleasant, gruesome, or even repulsive objects and photographs of such objects, may be received in evidence where they bear on material and disputed facts (30). It is no valid objection that such objects or photos may be inflammatory or prejudicial, for one cannot exclude otherwise competent evidence of a gruesome or unpleasant occurrence simply because it may arouse emotions (31).

Weapons And Objects Admissible Under 

"Res Gestae" Rule

Objects such as clothing, weapons, or other items found at the scene of an event which have some immediate connection with the event are admitted in evidence under the "res gestae" rule as part of the circumstances surrounding and explaining the act in issue (43). 

This is true even though the object was not used in the event in question (44). But admission of this kind of demonstrative evidence is error where it cannot be said that the objects were a part of the transaction or issue being tried, nor where they have no tendency to explain it (45).

And Assault Trials

Photographs

As a general rule the jury may be shown photographs of any object or condition which they could properly look at directly if the object were available (57). And it seems generally the law that colored photographs are admissible under the same rules governing black and white pictures (58). Aerial photographs for illustrative or other purposes are admissible upon a proper foundation (59). 

Foundation Required 

It is not indispensable to the admission of a photograph that the photographer be called to testify (60). It is sufficient that any person with knowledge of the subject can testify that the photograph is a reasonably faithful representation of the scene, person, or object portrayed in substantially the same condition as at the times in issue (61).

Admission Where Photo Shows Changed Conditions 

Photographs may be admitted in the court's discretion even where changes from the time in question have occurred in the scene or object pictured, so long as the nature of the changed conditions is sufficiently explained to the jury (64). But photos have been excluded where the object was not in the same condition as at the time in issue (65).

Since nature will, prior to trial, often remove all or most of the signs of injury, where a person's physical condition at a certain time is in issue, photographs taken of the person at or near that time are admissible to show such condition, on a showing that the offered pictures correctly portray the condition at that time (66). And, where it is obvious that wounds shown by a photograph are in better condition than at the time inflicted, the court may properly admit the photos against the objection of the party causing the injury since the changed conditions shown are more favorable to him (67). 

Posed Photographs Of Scene 

The trial court has discretion to admit posed photographs of a scene in question where an eyewitness has placed persons, autos, or other objects in their approximate places at the time of the event in issue (68). Similarly, where there is evidence that at a later time a defendant voluntarily went to the scene of a crime with the officers it is proper to admit pictures taken there (69). 

Witness' Right To Mark Or Point Out Objects 

Where a photograph of a scene or of autos or objects has been received in evidence it is proper to have objects or places shown in the picture marked, pointed out, or identified by persons familiar with the scene (70).

Pornographic Pictures 

In a prosecution for a sex crime, pornographic pictures found under the control or in the possession of the defendant may be admitted in the court's discretion to evidence the defendant's lustful desire or purpose (71).

In criminal actions by court rule the trial judge has express discretion to order a view of the premises in question if it will aid the jury in determining the issues (74). While no Arizona court rule or statute authorizes a view in a civil case, the trial judge has that inherent power, and in his discretion he may direct the jury to view the premises in question (75).

View May Include Objects As Well As Premises 

While the criminal rule authorizing views is couched in terms of "places," it has been held in several decisions, both criminal and civil, that the jury may be permitted to view not only places (76), but vehicles (77), cattle (78), or other objects or chattels in issue.

Either party may request a view. The request should ordinarily be made in the absence of the jury, and a request made in open court before the jury may be grounds for mistrial (79). A view should not be ordered without a foundation that the premises or object are now in substantially the same condition as at the time in issue (80). 

A defendant in a criminal case has an absolute right to be present at a view (81) but this right may be waived expressly or by failure to claim it (82).

Is Knowledge Obtained From View Evidence? 

There is a division of authority on the interesting question whether knowledge derived from a view is independent evidence, apart from the oral testimony and exhibits, which will support findings (87), or whether a view is solely to aid the jury to understand and evaluate the testimony of the witnesses (88). There are no Arizona decisions on this point (89). The writer favors the view that a view is itself evidence. The matter is often academic, but may be of vital importance when the sufficiency of the evidence to support a finding is determined on appeal, for there may be no way to determine whether the jury received additional evidence at the view which might support the verdict (90).

View In Cases Tried By Court 

In an action tried before the court without a jury the judge has an inherent discretionary right, irrespective of statutory authority, to have a view of the premises or objects in question (91). But the presiding judge cannot give judgment on his personal and private knowledge and must take the view in the presence of counsel and/or the parties and in a usual and proper manner (92). 

Section 4 Chemical Tests For Intoxication 

Arizona statutes provide that in prosecutions for driving while under the influence of intoxicants a presumption that the defendant was under the influence of intoxicating liquor will arise where the alcohol in defendant's blood as shown by chemical analysis of blood, urine or breath is 0.15% or more. If the percentage is more than 

0.05% but less than 0.15% no presumption of intoxication arises but such fact may be considered in determining the guilt or innocence of the defendant. But, if there is less than 0.05% alcohol it is presumed that the defendant was not under the influence of intoxicating liquor (93).

Miscellaneous Principles 

The act authorizing use of chemical tests and establishing the presumptions is constitutional (101). Where specimens of breath, blood, or urine are voluntarily given by a party, the results of their analysis may be used against him without violating the constitutional prohibitions against self-incrimination (102). Forcibly restraining a suspect so that his breath may be captured after passing his lips or nose does not violate his constitutional rights (103). The fact that medical science does not have complete unanimity regarding the results of chemical tests for intoxication does not render them inadmissible, but only affects the weight to be given such evidence (104). 

In any event whether or not chemical tests are made, the facts of intoxication or sobriety may be proven by the opinions and testimony of witnesses who observed the person concerned, and the chemical tests need not necessarily be accepted over conflicting testimonial evidence on the issue (105).

Fingerprints It is established in Arizona that evidence of correspondence of fingerprints when established by a competent expert is admissible in criminal or civil proceedings as reliable scientific proof of identity and similar matters (109). Where fingerprint evidence is material the parties may properly be allowed to offer in evidence enlarged photographs of the prints for better understanding of the similarities (110). The constitutional right against self-incrimination is not violated where the fingerprint is voluntarily given by the defendent (111), and the majority rule seems to hold that the constitutional guaranty extends only to testimonial compulsion, so that use of forcibly taken fingerprints will not be violative of the defendant's rights (112). Proof that fingerprints corresponding to those of accused were found in the place where a crime was committed, under such circumstances that they could have only been made at the time the crime was committed, may alone be sufficient evidence to support a conviction (113).

Ballistics Tests 

A properly qualified witness may testify as to markings made on bullets fired from firearms involved in a case and as to similarities or differences in markings on bullets fired in the incident in issue (114). Such testimony may also extend to markings on bullets fired from other weapons not involved in the case to show different or contrasting marks (115). The leading Arizona decision suggests that a witness performing such tests need not necessarily be an expert to testify, the trial court having some measure of discretion in allowing or rejecting such testimony (116). 

Identification By Blood Type 

In homicide cases it is proper to admit evidence as to similarity in blood type between deceased's blood and blood found on defendant's clothing or objects in his possession (117). Such evidence is not excluded merely because it tends to establish a possibility rather than a probability (118). Paternity may be disproven, but not established, by blood grouping tests (119).

Vaginal Smears In Sex Cases 

A properly qualified technician may testify in prosecutions for sexual offenses as to the presence of spermatozva on slides made from vaginal smears taken from the complaining witness (120). Such conclusion is admitted upon evidence showing that the smears came from the complaining witness even though the doctor or technician testifying did not himself take the smears (121). Evidence as to what was found on slides made from such smears may be admitted even though the slides themselves are not produced (122). 

'Truth Serum' Tests 

In Arizona, and generally, statements of a criminal defendant or other person made while under the influence of sodium amytal ("truth serum") are not admitted in evidence since they are hearsay, self-serving, made outside the presence of the court, and because such procedure has not yet established its scientific accuracy (123). 

Lie Detector (Polygraph)

The results of "lie-detector" examinations are not usually received as evidence since the scientific accuracy of the polygraph is not yet sufficiently established (124). Yet such proof may be received where the defendant and state stipulate in advance of a test that the results may be used as evidence (125). 

Sound Recordings 

It is generally held that sound recordings, if relating to otherwise competent evidence, are admissible providing a proper foundation is laid for admission (126).

Out-Of-Court Tests And Experiments 

Evidence of results of tests or experiments made out of court in corroboration or disproof of a material fact may be admissible under the circumstances of a particular case (130). It must be shown, however, that the material conditions which affect the test or experiment were substantially similar to those existing at the time of the event in question (131), and that the evidence will tend to aid rather than confuse the jury (132). But the admission of such evidence and the determination of whether conditions are sufficiently similar to allow the evidence are questions within the discretion of the trial court (133). Where conditions at the time of the test or experiment are not shown to be substantially similar the evidence should be rejected (134). Tests may be made upon the same gun, automobile or other object involved in the facts in issue (135), and in such case the mere possibility that other persons may have had access to the object so that it might have been changed between the event and the test does not render the result of the test inadmissible, but merely goes to the weight to be given it by the jury (136). 

The weight, effect, and conclusiveness of such test is always for the jury, and the Supreme Court will not examine the results of such tests and make findings as to the facts (137).
