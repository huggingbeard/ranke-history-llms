This pamphlet is designed to furnish practical data on the subject of trademarks, and is one of a series dealing with various aspects of the laws affecting small business operations. A trade-mark is of vital importance to the businessman since it secures for him the advantages and good will that accrue from the sale of his product.

This pamphlet points out the essential requirements for a valid trade-mark and the basic provisions of the new Federal trade-mark law (the Lanham Act). 

Not only does this new Act provide added features, but it also requires certain affirmative steps by trade-mark owners who wish to take advantage of these new rights and the protection they provide. The new law unifies for the first time all Federal trade-mark laws in a single statute.

Trade-Marks and Registration under the New Trade-Mark Law (The Lanham Act) The Value of Trade-Marks 

The way a product is packaged may attract a buyer, but an important factor in maintaining a demand for it is the regular use of a distinctive trademark. In the hands of the skillful advertiser, a trade-mark becomes an assurance to the buyer that he is getting what he wants. It helps to mold his buying habits.

A trade-mark is a distinctive word, emblem, symbol or device, or any combination of these, used to indicate or identify the manufacturer or distributor of a particular product. To be valid it must be used on goods actually sold in commerce or on displays associated with the goods, or on tags and labels fixed to the goods. 

A trade-mark is a valuable piece of property. In many cases a company's greatest asset may be the trade-mark identifying its product. Good will built through effective advertising and long standing use of a trade-mark would soon be lost through imitation and downright piracy, if it were not protected by the courts. The new Federal trade-mark law (the Lanham Act), administered by the U. S. Patent Office, affords incentive to trade-mark owners for the registration of marks which were unregistered heretofore, by providing broader and more effective protection. 

The Common Law Trade-Mark and Unfair Competition 

In order to receive protection in the courts, it is not necessary that the trade-mark be registered on either the State or Federal level. A common law trade-mark is a trade-mark which exists independently of State or 

Federal statutes.

Everyone is free to attach to any product which he manufactures or sells any symbol or device which will distinguish his product from those of the same general nature manufactured or sold by others. This trade-mark symbol is an assurance to the public that the product is a genuine article of the original producer. It prevents the substitution of articles made by other producers. 1

Before there was any trade-mark legislation, the trade-mark was protected. 

Where anyone adopted such a mark which closely resembled the mark of another, so that prospective purchasers would be likely to be deceived, or where goods were passed off for those of another, such use of a simulated mark would constitute unfair competition. Basic to the protection of the trade-mark, whether it be a common law or registered trade-mark, is this concept of unfair competition. An attack against those who practice unfair competition has a twofold purpose; first it protects the public from the use of confusingly or deceptively similar marks, and secondly, it protects the trade-mark owner from those who would deprive him of the good will, reputation, and the property rights that flow from the use of the trade-mark. 

Most of the common law provisions relating to trade-marks have been embodied and codified in the new law which is explained in this pamphlet. 

State Registration 

While trade-marks are protected by the courts-whether registered or not— State and Federal trade-mark registration gives substantial benefits to trademark owners. Generally, property in a trade-mark rests upon the laws of the several States. An owner of a trade-mark operating solely in intrastate commerce should, therefore, consider the laws and registration requirements of the particular State in which he does business. Furthermore, some States have penal provisions for the infringement of trade-marks. Businessmen, accordingly, should consider this fact where they do business solely within the boundaries of a given State. 2

Advantages of New Federal Trade-Mark Act (The Lanham 

Act)

Independently of State registration, if the owner of a trade-mark uses the mark in interstate commerce, then there are positive advantages for him in the new Federal trade-mark law which became effective July 5, 1947. 

At the outset trade-mark owners should realize that all rights acquired prior to the effective date, July 5, 1947, are completely protected. Section 49 of the new law states, "Nothing herein shall adversely affect the rights or enforcement of rights acquired in good faith prior to the effective date of this act."

Before analyzing the basic provisions of the new Federal trade-mark act, consider a brief outline of some of the advantages it makes available to the trade-mark owner. These are discussed in greater detail later. (1) Registration of the trade-mark is presumed to be evidence of the exclusive right of the trade-mark owner to the mark on a particular class of product. This evidence of exclusive right may, however, be disproved. The new law further provides that registration is constructive notice of the registrant's claim of ownership. (Constructive notice is such notice as would compel anyone to inquire about the right, interest, and claim of the owner of the registered trade-mark. In other words, it is up to the other person to make a trade-mark search before using a mark if he would avoid the legal consequences of infringement.) (See p. 12.) (2) A trade-mark registered under the new trade-mark law may be incontestable after 5 years, provided that no decision during the 5-year period has been made adverse to the registrant's claim of ownership and the registrant files the required affidavit. After a mark has become incontestable, the certificate of registration is conclusive evidence of the registrant's exclusive right to use the trade-mark. The analysis on pages 9-12 discusses more specifically the requirements for incontestability and the exceptions provided by law. (3) Descriptive words, geographic names and surnames-all of which were formerly not registrable-may now be registered if they are distinctive of the product of the owner. (See p. 15.) (4) Names which are not primarily geographic names, that is to say have little or no geographic significance when applied to particular goods, may now be registered as trade-marks. For example, a court held that "La- 

Touraine" used in connection with coffee, was not considered primarily geographical, although it was claimed in the case that "Touraine" had geographic significance in France. Through consistent and varied advertising, the word "LaTouraine" has developed an arbitrary instead of a geographic meaning. It has no relation to the source of the coffee, the place of manufacture or the place of sale. Similarly, surnames which, under the old law, could be registered only under certain limited conditions, may now be registered under the new law if they are not primarily as surnames. 3

(5) Service marks may now be registered for the first time. These marks cover the marks of laundry companies, cleaners and dyers, repairmen, and the like. (See p. 8.) (6) Collective marks of trade associations, cooperatives and other groups may now be registered. (See p. 8.) (7) Certification marks of underwriting laboratories and others which provide seals of acceptance and approval, may now be registered. (See p. 8.) (8) Where the same or substantially similar marks are used by separate owners in independent territories, the law now makes specific provision for the registration of both trade-marks under certain conditions. This is called "Concurrent Registration." (See p. 13.) (9) It provides for separate assignment of trade-marks independently of the sale of an entire business. It further provides for use of the mark by "related companies." Under this provision the registrant of a trade-mark would be able to permit licensees to use his mark without losing his right to it; provided that he retains control of the mark and no deceptive use is made of it. (See p. 18.) (10) It provides new remedies against infringers who imitate or counterfeit a trade-mark. (See pp. 18-20.) (11) One doing business in a particular locality may bring an action against another person who knowingly uses a false designation of origin either on containers or in connection with goods or services. To come within the provisions of the law, the goods or services must be used or transported in commerce.

The law further provides that an action may be brought by any person who believes that he is, or is likely to be, injured by the use of words or symbols falsely describing or representing such goods or services. Again, such goods or services must enter commerce. (See pp. 18, 19.) (12) The law provides for the Patent Office to keep two trade-mark regis ters, the Principal Register and the Supplemental Register. Where a trade mark does not meet the requirements for registration on the Principal Register, the mark may be registered on the Supplemental Register, provided that it is capable of distinguishing the applicant's goods or services. Such mark may include any trade-mark, symbol, label, package, word, slogan, phrase, surname, geographic name, numeral or device; even a configuration of goods or a bottle of distinctive design would be registrable. (See p. 17.) (13) It permits American exporters to register on the Supplemental Register in order to obtain trade-mark protection abroad, where the requirements of the foreign country are that the trade-mark be registered in the 

United States Patent Office. 

Where the exporter shows he has begun the lawful use of his mark in foreign commerce, the Commissioner may waive the requirement of a full year's use by the exporter and grant registration immediately. (See p. 17.)

(14) If the mark appears on the Supplemental Register, the owner may later have it transferred to the Principal Register provided that the mark has become distinctive of the owner's goods. (See p. 17.) (15) It provides that a certificate of registration shall remain in force for 20 years and may continue to be renewed for the same period of time by application at the end of each 20-year period. The application should be accompanied by the required fee and an affidavit stating that the trademark is still in use in commerce. (16) It provides for filing certified copies of the trade-mark registration, with proper facsimiles of the mark, with the Treasury Department for submission to the customs authority. This has the effect of preventing the importation of goods bearing infringing trade-marks from foreign countries. (See p. 18.) (17) Similar to the old law, the new law provides that where there has been a violation of any right of the registrant of a mark registered in the Patent Office, the court may order that all labels, signs, prints, packages, wrappers, receptacles, and advertisements bearing the registered mark in the possession of the defendant, and all the plates and other means of making them, shall be destroyed. (18) The United States courts have original jurisdiction in cases involving a trade-mark registered under the new Federal trade-mark law. 

There are, in addition to this list, a number of other substantial advantages resulting from the registration of trade-marks under the new Federal trademark act. Not only are new rights created for the trade-mark owner, but also for the first time all existing United States trade-mark laws are unified in a single law. 

The purposes of this law are: 

66* * to regulate commerce within the control of Congress by making actionable the deceptive and misleading use of marks in such commerce; to protect registered marks used in such commerce from interference by State, or territorial legislation; to protect persons engaged in such commerce against unfair competition; to prevent fraud and deception in such commerce by the use of reproductions, copies, counterfeits, or colorable imitations of registered marks; and to provide rights and remedies stipulated by treaties and conventions respecting trade-marks, trade names, and unfair competition entered into between the United States and foreign nations." 

In short, the objectives of the new trade-mark act are to protect the public from deceit and aid the businessman in promoting through the use of a trade-mark his reputation and good will.

Registration under New Law 

At the outset, the rules and regulations of the Patent Office should be referred to in connection with practices affected by the new trade-mark law. 

Let us consider how the registration of a trade-mark is obtained in the Patent 

Office under the new law. 777605-48- LA

The application for registration consists of a written application, a drawing of the mark, five specimens or facsimiles, and the required filing fee. 

The new trade-mark law provides that no trade-mark may be registered if it- (1) "Consists of or comprises immoral, deceptive, or scandalous matter; or matter which may disparage or falsely suggest a connection with persons, living or dead, institutions, beliefs, or national symbols, or bring them into contempt or disrepute." (2) Consists of a flag, coat of arms, or other insignia of the United States, municipality, or foreign nation. (3) Consists of a portrait of a living person without the written consent of that individual. (4) Consists of words or devices which are merely descriptive or deceptively misdescriptive of the character or quality of the goods. (5) Consists of a mark which is "primarily geographically descriptive or deceptively misdescriptive" when applied to the goods of the applicant. (6) Consists of a mark which is primarily a surname.

The Application for Registration 

The rules of the Patent Office require that the written application shall include a request for registration and shall specify: (1) The name of the applicant; (2) The citizenship of the applicant, or if the applicant be a corporation or association, the State or nation under the laws of which organized; (3) The domicile and post office address of the applicant; (4) That the applicant has adopted and is using the mark shown in the accompanying drawing;

(5) The particular description of goods on or in connection with which the mark is used; (6) The class of merchandise, according to the official classification (if the number and title of the class are not known to the applicant, they may be left blank, in which case they will be filled in by the Patent Office); (7) The date of applicant's first use of the mark as a trade-mark on or in connection with the goods specified in the application; (8) The date of applicant's first use of the mark as a trade-mark in commerce, specifying the nature of such commerce; and (9) The mode or manner in which the mark is used on or in connection with the particular goods specified. 

The written application must also include a declaration under oath stating that- (1) The applicant is the owner of the mark sought to be registered; (2) The mark is used in commerce, and specifying the nature of such commerce; (3) No other person, firm, corporation, or association has the right to use the identical mark or one so resembling it that it might be calculated to deceive; (4) The drawing and description truly represent the mark to be registered; (5) The specimens or facsimiles show the mark as actually used in connection with the goods; and (6) All the facts stated in the application are true. 

Drawing of Mark Required 

A drawing of the trade-mark is required. It must be substantially an exact representation of the trade-mark as used with the goods. In the case of a mark not capable of being represented by a drawing, the written application must contain an adequate description. The five specimens or facsimiles of the trade-mark must be specimens of the trade-mark as actually used, whether it be on labels, tags, or containers. 

Trade-Marks Registered under Old Laws 

The registrant of a trade-mark under the old Federal trade-mark laws (1881 and 1905) may avail himself of the benefits of the new law by filing the required affidavit in the United States Patent Office, naming the goods stated in the registration on which the mark is used, and claiming the benefits of the new trade-mark act. He must also pay the required fee. A mark so republished may become incontestable 5 years after republication. (For discussion on incontestability see pp. 9–12.)

Service Marks, Collective Marks, and Certification Marks 

Another important provision of the new law permits the registration, for the first time, of service marks, collective marks, and certification marks with all the advantages that flow from such registration.

1. Service marks, such as marks of laundries, cab services, cleaners and dyers, repairmen, and the like, can now be registered. A service mark cannot be used to represent falsely that the owner makes or sells the goods on which the mark may appear. The law defines a service mark as follows: "The term 'service mark' means a mark used in the sale or advertising of services to identify the services of one person and distinguish them from the services of others and includes without limitation the marks, names, symbols, titles, designations, slogans, character names, and distinctive features of radio or other advertising used in commerce." 29 Echoka

2. Collective marks comprise marks of trade associations, cooperatives, unions, and the like. They can now be registered subject to the provisions relating to trade-marks, so far as they are applicable, and will receive the same protection as in the case of trade-marks. These marks cannot be used to represent falsely that the owner or user makes or sells the goods or performs the services in connection with which the mark is used. The law defines a collective mark as follows: "The term 'collective mark' means a trade-mark or service mark used by the members of a cooperative, an association or other collective group or organization and includes marks used to indicate membership in a union, an association or other organization.” 

3. Certification marks cover marks of laboratories, underwriters' groups and others who provide seals of approval with respect to the maintenance of certain merchandise standards. The registrant may use the mark only as a certification mark and may not use it to apply to goods of his own manufacture or distribution. Furthermore, certification cannot be denied under the law to any person who complies with the prescribed standards. 

The law defines a certification mark as follows: 

"The term 'certification mark' means a mark used upon or in connection with the products or services of one or more persons other than the owner of the mark to certify regional or other origin, material, mode of manufacture, quality, accuracy or other characteristics of such goods or services or that the work or labor on the goods or services was performed by members of a union or other organization.”

P With respect to certification marks, the law provides that a certification mark may be canceled at any time on the ground that the registrant— (1) Does not control, or is not able legitimately to exercise control over, the use of such mark; or (2) Engages in the production or marketing of any goods or services to which the mark is applied; or (3) Permits the use of such mark for other purposes than as a certification mark; or (4) Discriminately refuses to certify the goods or services of any person who maintains the standards or conditions which the mark performs. 

The Federal Trade Commission as well as any person damaged may apply for cancellation of the certification mark.

CERTIFIED Incontestability of Trade-Mark 

One of the most important phases of the new trade-mark law is its incontestability feature. In order that he avail himself of the incontestability provision, an owner of a trade-mark used in interstate commerce would be well advised to register his mark under the new trade-mark law. “Incontestability” means that the law recognizes the registrant of the trade-mark as the exclusive owner of the mark and his rights to it are conclusively established as against all others. Under the old trade-mark law, the effect of registration was to create in the owner a prima facie right to the mark. 

This permitted introduction of evidence which, if adequate, would destroy this right of the owner.

In order to establish incontestability, the law requires that the trade-mark must remain on the Principal Register of the Patent Office for a period of 5

years. The mark will not become incontestable if any decision is made adverse to the registrant's claim of ownership during the 5-year period. 

The trade-mark owner, during the sixth year following registration, must file an affidavit stating that the mark has been in continuous use for 5 years, giving a description of the goods or services in connection with which the trade-mark is used, and a statement that there has been no final decision adverse to the registrant's claim of ownership in the trade-mark or his right to register it. The trade-mark registration is conclusive evidence of its owner's exclusive right "to use the mark in commerce," following the filing of this affidavit at the end of the 5-year period. 

Attacks on Incontestability 

After the 5-year period, this exclusive right of the trade-mark owner may be attacked only on the following grounds: (1) The trade-mark has become the common or descriptive name of the article on which the patent has expired; (See pp. 12, 13.) (2) The registration of the mark was fraudulently obtained; (3) The trade-mark has been abandoned; (See p. 20.) (4) The mark has been assigned and is being improperly used; or (5) The use of the certification mark is not in compliance with the provisions of the trade-mark law. (See pp. 8, 9.)

Any one of these five grounds may be used to seek the cancellation of the trade-mark, either by the Federal Trade Commission or by any person who believes that he is, or is liable to be, damaged by another person using the same or a similar mark.

The law further provides that anyone who is sued for trade-mark infringement, after the 5-year period, may use (in addition to 2, 3, and 4 above) certain other grounds to resist the suit. These additional grounds are the 

(6) Where the claimed infringing trade-mark is used otherwise than as a trade-mark (a) through the use of a party's individual name in his own business, or is used (b) in good faith to describe to users the goods and services, or (c) is a use describing their geographic origin. Any one of these uses would constitute a good defense to a suit by a trade-mark owner. (7) The mark whose use is charged as an infringement was adopted without the knowledge of the registrant's prior use and is a valid concurrent use; (8) The claimed infringing mark has been used prior to the publication of the registered mark, and this defense applies only to the area in which the mark was used prior to the date of publication of the registrant's mark; and (9) The mark has been or is being used to violate the antitrust laws of the United States.

Under (6a) above, the use of an individual's name may constitute a defense to an action where the registered trade-mark owner asserts his ex- 10

clusive right. The trade-mark law makes no provision for the registration of trade names as such. "Trade names" would include firm names, surnames and individual names, names of corporations and companies, etc. 

Although they are not registrable as such, the courts do provide for their protection.

Confusion often arises concerning the use of a surname as a trade name and as a trade-mark. It may be generally said that the trade name is used to differentiate a particular concern or its location from those of others. A trade-mark, on the other hand, distinguishes the product of one manufacturer from those of others. In some instances a surname has been used both as a trade name and as a trade-mark. 

It has been said that a man has the right to use his own name in his trading activities. The answer to the question, therefore, whether one may use his own name as a trade name, is generally "yes," although the extent to which one may use such name is not in itself unlimited. However, a proper name is not generally valid as a trade-mark, since the essence of a trade-mark is its exclusive character. One would, therefore, be well advised in selecting a trade-mark not to use his own name. 

A court stated in one case: 

66* * * The rule is that while, as against persons bearing a different name, a manufacturer's right in his name trade-mark is absolute and exclusive, as against persons bearing the same name no such exclusive right can be set up. If the public have confidence in a man's skill and integrity, his name may be on that account the most advantageous trade-mark he can adopt. But such a trade-mark has the disadvantage that it is exposed to the possibility that some other person bearing the same name may be the manufacturer of similar articles, and have the consequent right to affix his name upon such articles, thereby creating a possibility of mistake on the part of consumers as to the manufacture of the goods they are about to buy." 

The courts have resolved the difficulty in some cases so that when a mark of one producer has come to distinguish his goods from those of another, he can restrain others from using it or any imitation of it which is "likely to cause confusion or mistake or to deceive purchasers."

In brief, every man has a right to use his own name for trade or business, but not to the exclusion of others bearing the same name. The only restraints are those which involve unfair competition in the use of the name. 

Where the use of a proper name involves unfair competition, such use of the name has been enjoined by the courts. When a person has entered into competition with an old established business bearing the same name, the courts may require that person to differentiate his business name from that of his competitor.

In one case the L. E. Waterman Co., long established as manufacturers of the Waterman Fountain Pen, had to meet the competition of one Arthur A. Waterman who also engaged in the manufacture of fountain pens. The court required him to include the phrase "not connected with the L. E. 

Waterman Co." so that he would not benefit from the good will established by the older company. 11

"Constructive Notice" and Users of New Trade-Marks 

Registration under the new law is "constructive notice" of the claim of ownership as against all newcomers who seek to extend the use of their marks into any other territory for identical or similar goods after a trademark owner has obtained registration for the same mark. Former Commissioner of Patents Ooms stated, "This will place the newcomer into any industry under the burden of making a careful search for marks which have been registered that resemble his own in order that he may not thereafter be cut off. He can no longer be an innocent infringer of a registered mark." 

A user of a new trade-mark should, therefore, consider Federal registration of a new trade-mark without delay.

It is important for every prospective trade-mark owner to have a thorough search made of available trade-mark files. In addition to avoiding possible infringement suits, he may prevent a substantial money loss on packaging and advertising if a search discloses that a trade-mark is not available to him. 

Incontestability and Accepted Name of a Product 

It is well to bear in mind that no incontestable rights under the law can be acquired in a name which is the common descriptive name of the article, whether it be patented or otherwise. Certain trade-marks over a period of time take such hold on the public mind that what was once a trade-mark becomes the accepted name of the product. In short, the public associates the name with the product and not with the producer. Marks which have thus become the accepted name of a product have lost their distinctiveness and fall into the public domain. Such marks cannot become incontestable 12

trade-marks, and may be canceled at any time. Trade-mark owners must exercise the utmost caution in preserving the distinctiveness of the mark, and prevent the use of the trade-mark as the accepted name of the product. 

Thus, “celluloid" as a trade-mark became the name of the product and no longer identified the product with its producer. In the same way kerosene and linoleum became the names of a fuel and a floor covering respectively, although they originated as trade-marks. In another case, the word "aspirin,” although a trade-mark of a drug manufacturer, became the name of a given product. In the same way “cellophane,” which was a trade-marked product of the dupont Company, became the name of the product. 

With respect to the product known as Shredded Wheat, which was a trade-mark on a patented product, the court held that it had become a generic term signifying the product. In addition, since the patent expired, the right to make the product and the name under which it is generally known now belongs to the public. 

It would follow that a trade-mark associated with a patented product may fall into the public domain when the patent expires if there is no other practicable name available by which the public may call the article. It may be freely used by others provided, however, that there has not developed a secondary meaning which signifies the origin of the product. 

In this connection an owner of a new product which is patented or is under a secret formula might consider having two names for his product. In the drug industry, for example, it has been the practice to adopt two names for a new drug. One the generic name and the other the trade-mark name. 

This practice is a safeguard against the use of the trade-mark name by the public as the generic name of the product. 

In those instances where a trade-mark becomes so identified with the product that it becomes a part of the public's vocabulary and signifies the product itself, it would seem that advertising has done its job too well. To minimize the possibility of a trade-mark falling into the public domain and becoming a generic word, certain steps should be taken. Vigilance should be exercised at all times in protecting a mark against infringers. A mark must be constantly identified with the owner of the product. Every effort should be used to prevent the use of the mark´as a common name for the product.

Concurrent Registration tion.

Another important provision in the new law permits concurrent registra- Under the old law concurrent registration of trade-marks was not permissible. Where the same or substantially similar marks are used by separate owners who have acquired rights to use the marks in different territories, the law now makes specific provisions for their concurrent registration if the Commissioner of Patents determines that it does not lead to confusion, mistake, or deceit. 13

Under the concurrent registration provision the rightful users of the trademark will be listed on the register. Thus, different users of similar or the same marks, located in different areas of the country, may obtain registration of their marks and secure the benefits of the new trade-mark law. 

The law requires that the Commissioner of Patents give not less than 30 days' written notice to all applicants and users of the trade-mark specified in the application, and set the time and place for a hearing following the 30 days' notice. Any person who may be injured may present his objections to such registration. Under the rules of the Patent Office administering the new law, it is provided that— "Concurrent use. In the case of an application claiming concurrent use, the applicant shall also specify in the statement to the extent of his knowledge any concurrent use by others, designating their names, addresses, and registrations or applications, if any, the goods in connection with which and the areas in which such concurrent use exists, the mode of such use, the periods of such use, and the goods, area, or mode of use for which the applicant desires registration; and the claim of exclusive use made in the declaration shall be made with the stated exceptions."

Finally, under the provisions of the law, the Commissioner determines and fixes the conditions and limitations in connection with concurrent registration. 

Concurrent use of the same trade-mark has created many difficult problems. 

This provision on concurrent use in the new law may eliminate some of those difficulties.

A difficult problem often arises where two claimants use the same mark but operate in separate territories. The Supreme Court has stated that where users of the trade-mark operate in separate markets, remote and removed from each other, with a trade-mark having different meanings in different communities, the rule of priority of use is not applicable. 

The Supreme Court in an early case said: "Into whatever markets the use of a trade-mark has extended, or its meaning has become known, there will the manufacturer or trader whose trade is pirated by an infringing use be entitled to protection and redress. But this is not to say that the proprietor of a trade-mark, good in the markets where it has been employed, can monopolize markets that his trade has never reached, and where the mark signifies not his goods, but those of another."

A court held that a defendant in Wisconsin should not be permitted to use the name "Adams" or "Adam" in connection with the sale of hats, and also enjoined the defendant from using the terms "The Bomber" and "Broadcast Special." It emphasized the part that extensive advertising plays, particularly in radio, and pointed out that Wisconsin is within the natural territory of the expanding business of the Adam Hat Stores, Inc., the plaintiff in the case.

In another case, the trade name "Brass Rail" was used as the name of a restaurant in Massachusetts. However, another owner had previously established a restaurant bearing the same trade name in New York. The court said that, although they did not actually compete with one another, the use of the name by the Massachusetts restaurant might endanger the reputation 14

of the New York restaurant through a confusion of identity. The use of the name by the Massachusetts restaurant was therefore forbidden. 

In connection with a territorial limitation of trade-marks in earlier cases, it is now realized that modern advertising in all of its forms including radio, expands the area of the trade-mark. The courts now emphasize the natural area of expansion of a business and the part played by all forms of advertising in modern commerce. The requirement of actual competition among producers of trade-marked goods is being whittled away. Today advertising and expanding markets have broadened the legal remedies available to trademark owners.

Geographic and Descriptive Trade-Marks Registrable under Certain Conditions 

Another important change in the law provides that marks such as geographic or descriptive words which were not previously registrable trademarks, are now registrable under certain conditions. Under the provision of Section 2 (f) of the law, it is now held that a mark may be registered if the mark has become distinctive of the origin of the goods; that is to say, it has developed a secondary meaning. The law further provides that “the 

Commissioner may accept as prima facie evidence that the mark has become distinctive as applied to the applicant's goods in commerce, proof of substantially exclusive and continuous use thereof as a mark by the applicant in commerce for the five years next preceding the date of the filing of the application for its registration."

An important aspect of a trade-mark is that it must not be descriptive of the product. The value of the trade-mark depends upon its exclusive character. If it describes the product, the courts will not give it protection. 

Such words describe the product, its ingredients, quality, and general characteristics. Words for a trade-mark which are in the public domain cannot be generally protected since they belong to the public generally as part of the body of the language. The right to use these words belongs to all. 

One court said that no one has an exclusive right to use a descriptive word in connection with his goods, and he only has himself to blame if confusion arises when other manufacturers with equal right adopt similar words to describe their product.

Of course, where a trade-mark even though descriptive has been used exclusively and has become distinctive of the product, it may develop a secondary meaning and be protected by the courts. Thus, despite the fact that there is no technical trade-mark, the courts will protect its use where the mark has come to signify a particular manufacturer or seller. 

The general rule would seem to be that, with some exceptions, a geographic name or location is not available as a trade-mark. One exception as to the unavailability of the name of a particular location as a trade-mark would be the exclusive ownership or possession of a piece of property. Such ownership and possession would give the owner the right to use the name of the property to designate his products or services. For example, the 

Manitou Springs Mineral Water Co. was the owner of a large tract of land in the City of Manitou, Colo. This land contained mineral springs and was known as "Manitou Springs." The company which owned the property bottled this water under the registered trade-mark name "Manitou." The court held that the company which owned and controlled the source of the mineral water had the right to be protected in the exclusive use of the name of the product coming from this place. 

There is a sound basis for the inadequacy of geographic names as trademarks. To permit the use of a trade-mark bearing the name of a given location or area would deprive others in that location or area of the right to use the name. One generally has the right to go into a locality and start a competing business with all those who have preceded him in that locality, using the name of the new location in promoting the sale of his goods. As one court said, "There is no exclusive property in a name, even in those who now reside there or carry on manufacture there. It is open to all the world." Thus, it was held that coal mined in a given area did not give a mining company the exclusive right to use the name of the mining area, even though the particular coal was carefully graded and inspected. Accordingly, the right to the name "Pocahontas Coal" was not enforceable as a trade-mark because there is a Pocahontas coal region worked by many coal operators who could freely use the name.

However, in one case, a group of companies using a name for a product which indicated the State where the product was grown, enjoined a packer whose product was grown outside the State from the use of such a State label. Thus, a number of packers of a product grown in California and known as California Pears, enjoined a packer whose pears were grown in Maryland from using the designation “California Pears." In another case, the court said that it would not tolerate the false use of a geographic name which would induce the sale of spurious goods and promote unfair competition. The principle involved is that one may not divert business by a deceptive and false use of the name of a particular community which describes the products of those who produce their goods in that community. 

Under certain circumstances, geographic names may develop into a valid trade-mark. A valid trade-mark may exist where the geographic name takes 16

on a secondary meaning; that is to say, "secondary meaning" would not only indicate the location of manufacture but also the name of the product or its quality as that of a particular manufacturer. This would enable the manufacturer to assert the exclusive right to the geographic name as against everyone not in the same geographic area.

In speaking of secondary meaning, a court said that a descriptive or geographic word could not be exclusively appropriated as a trade-mark. However, it might have been used so long and exclusively as to indicate to the trade and buying public that the word or phrase had come to mean the article was a product of a particular producer and that, accordingly, the words were, to the trade and his customers generally, the producer's trade-mark. 

There are a number of other exceptions which might be briefly referred to in connection with the use of geographic names. Thus, the arbitrary use of a geographic name linked with the sale of bread by a New York baker, who called his product Vienna Bread, was not the appropriation of a right belonging to others, and was, accordingly, considered a valid trade-mark. 

Supplemental Register 

The new trade-mark law provides for the creation of a Supplemental Reg. ister. All marks which are capable of distinguishing the goods or services of the applicant, and which are not registrable on the Principal Register, may be registered on the Supplemental Register provided they have been in lawful use in interstate commerce for one year immediately preceding the filing of the application.

These marks on the Supplemental Register may be canceled at any time if it is found by the Commissioner, at the request of a person who may be injured, that the registrant was not entitled to register the mark. The businessman should seek to create at the very outset a trade-mark which is technically sufficient for the purpose of registration on the Principal Register. 

The Supplemental Register does not offer the advantages of the Principal Register. It provides no incontestable rights, affords no prima facie evidence of ownership or claim of ownership, nor may it be filed with the Treasury Department to prevent importation of products with infringing marks. Marks on the Supplemental Register are not precluded, however, from later going on the Principal Register provided they have become distinctive of the ap plicant's goods.

The use of the Supplemental Register will prove helpful to those engaged in foreign trade. It enables American exporters to obtain trade-mark protection abroad, where foreign countries require that the trade-mark be registered in the United States Patent Office. Where the exporter shows he has begun the lawful use of the mark in foreign commerce, the Commissioner under the new law may waive the requirement of one year's use and grant registration immediately. 17

Right to Assign Trade-Mark 

Another important feature of the new trade-mark law is the right to assign the trade-mark and the good will with which it is used without selling the entire business. Formerly, a trade-mark could be sold only if the business and its good will were sold with it. Under the new law, an owner of several trade-marks can now sell one of the marks without having to dispose of his entire business. It is important to bear in mind that the assigned registration may be canceled if the registered mark is used, by or with the permission of the person to whom the mark has been assigned, to misrepresent the source of the goods or services in connection with which the mark is used. 

The law requires that these assignments be in writing. 

Another point to bear in mind in connection with the assignment of a trademark is that the assignment should be recorded in the Patent Office. A failure to record it might, under certain circumstances, result in destroying the legal effect of the assignment. 

Use of Trade-Mark by Others 

An owner of a trade-mark may permit others to use his trade-mark under the new law. Under the old law, the owner actually had to use the mark himself. The new law now permits a "related company" to make legitimate use of the trade-mark without in any way affecting its validity. A "related company" under the law is defined as “any person who legitimately controls or is controlled by the registrant or applicant for registration in respect to the nature and quality of the goods and services in connection with which the mark is used." If the owner, therefore, maintains control over the identity of the product and avoids any possible deception of the public, he still has a valid trade-mark under the new law despite the use of the mark by others. 

Accordingly, licensees of the owner have the right to use the trade-mark under the appropriate controls of the owner without loss of the trade-mark by the owner.

Under the trade-mark law any owner of a registered trade-mark, registered on the Principal Register, may file with the Treasury Department for submission to the customs authority a certificate of registration with proper facsimiles of his trade-mark, which will have the effect of stopping importation of merchandise which copy or simulate his trade-mark. 

Some Provisions Regarding Violations of the Law 

There is a newly created provision in the trade-mark law which is very important from a competitive point of view. It provides, in substance, that any person who uses a false designation of origin of goods or services or any false description or representation through words or symbols describing the goods or services, and causes such goods or services to be used in commerce, is liable to a civil action. The action may be brought by any person doing business in the locality "falsely indicated, or by any person who believes that he is, or is likely to be, damaged by the use of any such false descrip- 18

Any use in commerce of a reproduction, counterfeit, copy, or colorable imitation of a registered trade-mark--the use of which is likely to cause confusion as to the source of origin—shall subject such user to a civil action. 

Similarly, one who reproduces or counterfeits or copies such trade-marks and applies them to labels, signs, prints, packages, etc., shall be liable to a civil action. There can be no recovery for profits or damages in connection with such reproduction unless the acts were committed with the knowledge that they were intended to confuse or deceive a purchaser. 

Where a registrant of a trade-mark establishes in an action that there has been a violation of his rights under the trade-mark law, the court may order all labels, signs, prints, packages, etc., bearing the registered mark and all plates and other means of making them, destroyed. 

In an action for the violation of any right of the registrant of a mark the law provides that the plaintiff is entitled, subject to other provisions of the law, to recover (1) the defendant's profits, (2) any damages sustained by the plaintiff, and (3) the costs of the action. The registrant must bear in mind that if profits or damages are to be recovered, he must give notice that his mark is registered by displaying with the mark as used, "Registered in 

United States Patent Office" or "Reg. U. S. Pat. Off.” or the letter “R” enclosed in a circle. Where a registrant fails so to mark the goods, neither profits nor damages are recoverable in an action for infringement under the law unless the defendant had actual notice of the registration. 19

The question of infringement is basic to the subject of trade-marks. It is highly technical and involves an extended discussion of the law. 

The important problem surrounding trade-mark infringement lies in the similarity of trade names or trade-marks which not only leads to confusion, but also results in the passing off of one's goods for another. There must be some substantial similarity between the marks in appearance, display, or sound. It is the similarity of the mark owned by another which is basic to infringement. The test of similarity is whether the resemblance between the marks is such as to deceive, or likely to deceive, a prospective purchaser. 

A court speaking of infringement said that the trade-mark owner identifies his goods with his mark, and when the trade-mark appears on the goods of another his business reputation is placed to that extent beyond his control. 

The protection the courts give, extends to goods which the buying public reasonably thinks come from the same source, if they bear the same mark. 

The court went on to say that: "Unless the use by that other is upon goods so unlike his own or in territory so far from that which he has exploited that it will not create confusion, it will be enjoined * * "What has been said respecting infringement applies as well to the cause of action for unfair competition, which is but a somewhat broader phase of the same wrong. The gist of this action is the likelihood that the goods of the defendant will be passed off as those of the plaintiff * 

Abandonment of Trade-Mark under the New Law 

Valuable rights may be lost through abandonment. The new trade-mark law provides that when the use of a trade-mark has been discontinued with intent not to resume it, it shall be deemed abandoned. The courts have held that in considering the question of intent, mere nonuse, in and of itself, is not sufficient to constitute abandonment. The length of time the mark has not ME

been used is but one factor in determining the intention to abandon. Accordingly, intention to abandon the trade-mark is to be inferred from all the facts in the particular case and need not be specifically expressed. 

The law further provides that any act of omission or commission by the owner which causes the mark to lose its significance as an indication of the origin of the product is deemed an abandonment of the mark. There is in the mind of the public an associative significance attached to a trade-mark, and it cannot be said that a trade-mark has been abandoned by nonuse until it has lost this significance. 

Finally, there is a new provision in the trade-mark law which did not exist in any of the old laws which provides that "nonuse for two consecutive years shall be prima facie abandonment." This means that in the absence of any other evidence the mark would be deemed abandoned after the two-year period.

It is important, therefore, for the owner of the trade-mark, who finds that his mark is used by another, to act promptly to protect his trade-mark. He may be charged with consenting to the use of the mark. However, such consent, whether it be express or implied, is always revocable. It is most important to an owner of a trade-mark that he should be vigilant to protect his mark and not permit competitors to use it without asserting his rights.

Fair trade contracts permit sellers to fix the price below which the product may not be resold. This type of agreement is permissible under the resale price maintenance laws in 45 States. In addition, the Miller-Tydings 

Amendment to the Sherman Act exempts from the Federal antitrust laws resale price maintenance agreements valid under State laws. 

Resale price maintenance agreements as to the fixed selling prices to be used by their dealers are not permissible between groups of producers or between groups of wholesalers. There are decisions under the law which prohibit wholesalers or retailers from combining to determine the resale price which a supplier will establish for them.

Exceptions are allowed in resale price laws where goods are below standard specifications, damaged, in closing out sales, or for other reasons. 

Basic to the whole problem of resale price maintenance is the fact that the product must bear the trade-mark, brand, or the name of the producer or distributor.

In some States only the owner of the trademark or brand name, or his authorized distributor, is permitted to establish the resale price. In some States other sellers of the product, in addition to the owner or his authorized distributor, are permitted to establish the resale price. In all States, as well as under the Miller-Tydings Act, the product must be in free, or as sometimes expressed, fair and open competition with products of the same general class produced or distributed by others. 

Distinction between Trade-Marks, Patents, and Copyrights 

There is frequently confusion as to the distinction between trade-marks, patents, and copyrights. The basic distinction is that the patent and copyright are property rights which arise out of a grant by the Government. The trade-mark, on the other hand, is a property right which comes into existence only through use by its owner as a distinctive mark in connection with a given product. It is its use and not the choice which creates the rights which the courts protect. In a patent the right grows out of invention. 

There is no element of invention involved in the trade-mark. 

Many commercial prints and labels are copyrighted and registered in the Copyright Office of the Library of Congress. However, although a copyrighted label or print contains a trade-mark, this does not give greater weight to the trade-mark, nor does it eliminate the requirements imposed on an owner in order to obtain its registration. It is well to bear in mind that a trade-mark as such is not copyrightable. 

Need for Competent Counsel 

Problems relating to trade-marks are often difficult and complex. Their solution requires not only knowledge of the complexities of the law but also calls for technical information, experience, and judgment. In addition, there are important psychological factors involved in the selection of the trade-mark which may make for its success or failure. 22

This pamphlet has sought to emphasize some of the problems of trade-mark law and to point out the principal advantages provided trade-mark owners under the new trade-mark act. Within the brief limits of the pamphlet it is, of course, not possible to cover the wide variety of problems and variations of trade-mark principles. Its chief purpose is to inform the small businessman as to the principal elements underlying trade-marks. 

This pamphlet does not supplant the advice and counsel of a competent and experienced person capable of analyzing the specialized problems resulting from the development and use of trade-marks. Accordingly, it cannot be overemphasized that all businessmen would be well advised to consult competent trade-mark counsel if they contemplate the selection and development of a trade-mark, or become involved in any trade-mark problem. 

Some General Principles 

In concluding this short analysis of the trade-mark law, it would be desirable to summarize some basic trade-mark principles. (1) Use of the trade-mark is essential to its validity. The product must be used commercially; not in a casual way, but in a way that shows the owner intends to adopt the trade-mark. Generally speaking, the right to a trade-mark belongs to the owner who first uses the mark. This is the basic test as between competitors using the same mark in the same territory. 

The trade-mark law provides that a mark shall be considered to be “used in commerce" when it is placed in any manner on the goods or their containers or on displays associated with the goods on tags and labels fixed to the goods, and the goods are sold or transported in interstate or foreign commerce. (2) It must appear that it was adopted for the purpose of identifying the origin or ownership of the article to which it is attached; it must point distinctively to the origin, ownership, or manufacturer of the article so that it may be distinguished from like articles manufactured by others. (3) If its purpose is to identify the product by class, grade, style, or quality, or for any purpose other than ownership, it cannot be considered a valid trade-mark.

An owner of a trade-mark "has no right to the exclusive use of any words, letters, figures, or symbols which have no relation to the origin or ownership of the goods, but are only meant to indicate their names or quality. He has no right to appropriate a sign or symbol, which from the nature of the fact it is used to signify, others may employ with equal truth, and therefore have an equal right to employ for the same purpose." use. (4) Exclusive right to the use of the trade-mark is founded upon prior (5) The mark generally cannot cover words designating locality, sections, or regions of the country.

Serious consideration should be given to the selection of a suitable trademark. The task is not an easy one. At the outset a thorough search should be made to determine whether a particular name is similar to an existing 23

mark or sounds the same, so that the public will not be confused, for the value of the mark is dependent upon its distinctive and exclusive character. 

In addition to being distinctive, a trade-mark should have a psychological appeal, be suitable for the product, be well sounding and be easy to remember. 

The Supreme Court pointed out that: "Whatever the means employed, the aim is the same to convey through the mark, in the minds of potential customers, the desirability of the commodity upon which it appears. Once this is attained, the trade-mark owner has something of value. If another poaches upon the commercial magnetism of the symbol he has created, the owner can obtain legal redress."

A manufacturer or distributor in considering the choice of a suitable mark should consult with competent counsel in this field who could make appropriate search for possible infringement, determine the area in which the trademark could be utilized, and ascertain its general suitability for use on the particular product for which it is designed. 

The businessman should realize that a well-founded trade-mark constitutes one of his greatest assets, and he should be ever alert to protect it. By constant vigilance against infringing marks, and by sound and consistent advertising, this valuable asset may be kept alive in the mind of the consumer, performing thereby its real function in the market place. 24
