ORGANIZATION FOR CARRYING OUT THE ACT The Federal Airport Act is administered by the Federal Aviation 

Administration through the Airports Service, a major subdivision of the agency's Washington headquarters, and by counterpart elements in seven regional and eighteen area offices. As of June 30, 1967, there were 

574 employees assigned to carrying out the Airports program in Washington and the field.

AIRPORT PLANNING 

A greater national interest focused on airports in FY 1967 than at any period in the two decades since adoption of the Federal Airport Act. 

Forecasts indicated that the recent spectacular growth within the air transportation industry would become even more spectacular with the introduction of the newer, high-capacity aircraft. This led to 

This led to a growing concern in both the aviation community and the general public that airport development was not progressing at a suitably comparable rate. The statement that airports in the major metropolitan centers were fast approaching the saturation point was being heard more and more frequently. 

The challenge to the agency in its role of promoting the development of a system of airports responsive to public need was both direct and immediate. 

To meet this challenge it was necessary for the agency to restructure its planning activities. The first step in this direction was to expand the planning horizon from the five-year span of the National Airport Plan to a long-range effort more closely approximating the time required for planning and constructing an airport.

A second step in the redirected planning effort was to consider the requirements of the total airport complex rather than only those facilities which are eligible for financial assistance under the Federal-aid Airport Program. 1

Acting within these revised concepts, the agency instituted several major planning studies in FY 1967. The first of these was an allencompassing airport facility study conducted for the Subcommittee on 

Economic Progress, Joint Economic Committee, U.S. Congress. The study examined such features as the nature and composition of airports, construction and operating costs and user charges, annual trends of capital outlays for airport development from 1959 through 1965, and the needs and prospective capital outlays for airport development through 1975. 

The results of the study appeared as Chapter 14, Airport Facilities of the Joint Economic Committee's December, 1966, State and Local Public 

Facility Needs and Financing Report. In substance, it points out the financial requirements and potential sources of financing, or lack thereof, for the airport development needed to meet future demand. 

Another study undertaken by the agency as a part of its long-range planning effort was one of aviation demand and airport facility requirement forecasts at the major U.S. transportation hubs. The completed report translates aviation activity forecasts into selected airport facility requirements at each of 22 major metropolitan areas through the year 1980, depicting future needs in terms of units and area requirements for terminal aprons, terminal buildings, passenger and cargo facilities and vehicular parking areas. Designed for use in advance planning, the forecasts are intended to assist airport and local government planning officials in the development of comprehensive long-range master plans. The report was completed late in FY 1967, and is available to the public upon request. 

A third document, published primarily for agency use, provided preliminary guidance for use in planning for the introduction of the SST, Boeing 747, and other large capacity subsonic transport aircraft into the air carrier fleet and airport network. The document provides agency personnel in the regions with data for use in advising the public of the anticipated impact of the fast, new, large capacity aircraft on community airport needs. 

It includes such pertinent facts as when and where these aircraft will be put in service and the types of airport development that will be required to accommodate them. The initial document, issued in FY 1967, will be updated as additional data becomes available. 

The National Airport Plan The National Airport Plan covering Fiscal Years 1968 through 1972 

identified the rapidly increasing airport development requirements resulting from the current growth in air transportation coupled with transition from piston to jet aircraft. It predicts that scheduled jet service will be extended from 112 airports to approximately 346 airports by 1972. total dollar requirement of the Plan is estimated at 1.53 billion for facilities eligible for financial assistance under the Federal-aid Airport Program. 2

In formulating the Plan, improved criteria for determining qualification for inclusion were developed and applied. Planning techniques with cost/benefit as a controlling element were introduced to forecast more accurately the most suitable facilities required to increase airport capacity.

The new criteria focused more on the need for access to the air transportation system as a vital factor in the economic growth of communities. Consideration was given to facilities that would permit air taxi service to link smaller communities with airports served by the scheduled air carriers. In the metropolitan areas greater emphasis was placed on providing secondary airports to accommodate general aviation aircraft, thus lessening the traffic burden at locations having a high density in scheduled airline operations.

Coordination of Airport Planning with Other Government Agencies 

Continued close coordination of agency planning with that of other Federal agencies was maintained in FY 1967 to insure the compatibility of airport development with other community and transportation development programs. Of particular significance was establishment of a bi-agency coordination of effort with the Federal Highway Administration, directed toward improved planning of airport access roads, and consideration of airport requirements in Federal-aid Highway Program Urban Area/Regional 

Transportation studies. Through an established policy for timely exchange of information, field offices of the two agencies were kept informed of proposed construction to insure compatibility of airport and highway projects. 

Procedures were also established for review and appraisal of feasibility and planning studies performed by the Office of Technical Assistance of the Economic Development Administration, Department of Commerce. FAA 

interest in these studies stems from the fact that conclusions and recommendations of EDA may support projects that have bearing on airport development.

Planning Guidance to the Public 

During Fiscal Year 1967, the agency published and made available to the public planning guidance in several new areas of study. 

The publication "Airport Capacity Criteria Used in Preparation of the National Airport Plan" presents methods for determining when additional runways, taxiways and aprons should be provided to increase airport capacity to accommodate more aircraft. 3

"Airport Site Selection" recommends to airport sponsors procedures for analyzing potential airport sites. Its intention is to assure that all facets of aviation which directly affect the airport and its environs are considered at the formulative stage of development. "Regional Air Carrier Airport Planning" informs local and state governments of Federal policy relative to the development of a single airport to serve two or more cities and their environs, and provides guidance to such planners for evaluating the feasibility of establishing such regional airports.

AIRPORT DESIGN AND CONSTRUCTION STANDARDS 

Another important consideration in the development of a national system of airports responsive to the needs of current and anticipated aeronautical activity is that of standards for airport design and construction. Continuing study and evaluation of these needs, with aviation safety and cost/benefit as inherent aspects, resulted in the development of several significant changes to existing standards. 

A new agency standard, "Utility Airports," was developed and adopted for small airports serving general aviation aircraft of 12,500 pounds or less gross weight. The concept of utility airports considers airplanes in groups based on their facility requirements. The standard also establishes a means of relating anticipated expenditures to the usability of the airport, taking into account a reasonable growth potential and the effect of operational volumes. 

Another completely new set of standards was developed for a category of airports to serve business jets, most of which exceed 12,500 pounds. 

The physical characteristics and performance capabilities of these airplanes, combined with the user's mode of operation, indicated that higher than necessary construction costs were incurred by applying air carrier airport criteria; thus, the new standards are tailored to the need for planning and developing safe airports for the business jets and other large airplanes used in general aviation. 

Important revisions to standards for air carrier airports to meet the demand of the changing air carrier fleet were also made with full consideration accorded to safety, cost/benefit, and airport usability.

Among these were provision for additional length in the design of runways to permit greater usability under adverse weather conditions, and reduced standard taxiway widths for the smaller air carrier jets.

An airport lighting study was made which resulted in the adoption of the medium intensity lighting system as the recommended standard for all airports, except those at which the more costly high intensity lighting system is justified. The study revealed that the medium intensity lighting system is more economical overall and it has design features that give more reliable performance than the low intensity lighting system. 

The initial installation costs of the low intensity lighting system were somewhat less, but increased maintenance frequently made the overall costs higher.

A new 2,000-candlepower runway centerline light was approved for installation with Federal aid at airports which will have operations in the low (Category II) visibilities. 

A detailed review of pavement design standards resulted in two significant changes in paving criteria. One revised standard calls for variable transverse pavement thickness which provides thicker pavement in the heavy traffic area along the center of the runway, and reduces thickness outside of the center traffic areas. This has the advantage of improving the overall load-bearing capability of the runway without increasing the cost. A second change provides for extension of the thicker pavement at the end of the runway, the "critical area, to better accommodate the weight of jet aircraft which accelerate more slowly than propeller-driven airplanes.

Airport Safety Standards 

A program of airport safety inspections was established on a test basis to identify, report, and correct any potential hazards on and in the vicinity of airports. Varying types of airports in all sections of the country were selected for this inspection. The results of this test inspection program will be used to determine if a continuing program is necessary.

Other tests were conducted on the effectiveness of new and improved firefighting agents, and a series of publications for improving aircraft firefighting and rescue services were issued.

Fiscal Year 1967 Development Activity 

The 1967 Federal-aid Airport Program, announced on December 18, 1966, allocated $72.5 million in Federal matching funds for development at 341 civil airports. Priority was given to development of facilities to relieve traffic congestion at major air terminals and to runway improvement to accommodate jet and other modern aircraft. The program provided for construction of 46 new public airports and improvement of 295 existing airports. 

In formulating the program, the agency screened and evaluated 731 

Requests for Aid totaling $274.1 million in Federal funds. Because the amount requested far exceeded funds available, many requests could not be approved. The approved announced program was limited to development most urgently needed at locations where local financing permitted early commencement of work.

PROTECTION OF THE PUBLIC INTEREST IN AIRPORTS 

Owners of airports constructed or improved with Federal financial assistance are under agreement to maintain and operate the airport facility for public use on fair and reasonable terms, without discrimination and without grant of exclusive rights to conduct any aeronautical activity. The agency pursues an active program of surveillance to assure compliance with these agreements.

In FY 1967, 2,301 airports were subject to Federal agreement. 

Compliance findings at over 2,000 of these showed less than 10 percent to be in violation. Most of the violations noted were minor technical deficiencies.
