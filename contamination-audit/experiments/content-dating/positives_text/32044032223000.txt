RESUME OF THE NEW TAX LEGISLATION 

Important to members of the armed services in connection with the new tax legislation: DON'T GET EXCITED OVER IT! This legis lation, which is decidedly in your favor, contains five outstanding features applying to you: (1) Providing you do not have a large outside income, and if most of that was earned income in the year 1942, the tax for that year is practically forgiven, effective September 1, 1943. (2) Your tax bill for 1943 is considerably reduced. This is due to the fact that the first $1,500 received for military or naval service is excluded from income. Do not allow yourself to be confused, or confuse others, by thinking or saying that you have a $1,500 deduction.

In tax matters there is a distinction between deductions from your taxable income and that part of your income which is entirely excluded from your gross income. For all practical purposes, the $1,500 is excluded from your income before computing your income tax liability just as though you had never received it. 1 (3) The quarterly payments made March 15 and June 15, 1943, on your 1942 income and the estimated payments made September 15 and December 15, 1943 are applied on the payment of your 1943 tax bill. 

The net result is that your 1942 tax bill is practically forgiven, and you have already paid your 1943 income tax bill. (4) There is, as yet, no change in the normal tax, surtax, and Victory Tax rates. The real change is in the method of collecting the tax. For the majority of taxpayers, this is accomplished by withholding the tax at the source, on salaries or wages. (a) The withholding provisions do not apply to members of the armed forces. (b) To place you on a current pay-as-you-go basis, you will be required to estimate your tax bill for the year, on March 15, each year; divide by four and pay one-fourth March 15, June 15, September 15, and December 15. (c) On March 15, 1944, you make out your income tax return as usual, and compare the tax due with the amount already paid. If you have over-paid, a refund is due; if short, you then pay the difference. (d) If your estimate and payments were more than 20% less than 579584 3

the actual tax due, you pay an additional penalty of 6% for the unpaid underestimate. (5) In the event of the death of any member of the armed services after December 7, 1941, and while he is on active duty, his entire unpaid federal tax bill is cancelled.

Tax Rates 

Your normal tax is 6% of your net income, minus personal exemption, credit for dependents, and earned income credited. 

Your surtax, computed on your net income, minus personal exemption and credit for dependents, is 13% on the first $2,000, 16% on the next $2,000, and the rates are graduated from there up to 82% on all over $200,000.

The new Victory Tax is 5% of your Victory Tax net income, less a specific exemption of $624.00 for each individual. This tax is also reduced by 25%. In the case of single individuals and 40% for married couples.

Exemptions 

Personal exemptions for income tax (but not for the Victory Tax) for married persons and for heads of family are $1,200, and for single persons, $500. Credit for each dependent is $350. There is a specific exemption on the Victory Tax net income of $624 for each individual. 

Joint Returns

You are not required to file a joint return if you are married, or are the head of a family. Individual returns by husband and wife, when both have income, may result in a saving in your tax payment. Married couples separated as a result of the war, are still "married persons" for income tax purposes.

New Short (Optional) Form 

This form is continued for those having a gross (not net) income of not more than $3,000. If this form is used, no deductions are allowable from the gross income, for either income tax or Victory Tax. 

Army Pay Exclusion for 1942 

Pay received for military service by all those below commissioned

rank, is excluded from income for tax purposes to the extent of $250 for single persons and $300 for married persons.

Army Pay Exclusion for 1943 

The first $1,500 received for military service is entirely excluded from gross income for all personnel of the armed services, regardless of rank.

Relief Measure 

Regardless of where you are stationed, if your income from army pay is insufficient to pay your tax bill, it may be postponed under the 

Soldiers and Sailors Civil Relief Act.

INCOME TAX AND ARMY PAY 

1. If all the words that have been written about Income Tax were placed end to end, they would reach from here to somewhere else; but who knows where and who cares a hoot? Even if all the profanity inspired by this subject were assembled in one place it would create some kind of a record. That being true, no attempt is made in this writing to cover the entire field of Income Tax. It is hoped, however, that enough information is contained herein for the average Army man to compute his Income Tax and his Victory Tax and to take advantage of any deductions he may be entitled to under the law. In general, the tax which you must pay is measured only by your own earnings and not by the separate earnings of another. For instance, you have no tax to pay on the earnings of any other members of your family, except that earnings of minors appropriated by the taxpayer must be included in the return, and the tax paid thereon, and in the case of husband and wife, the earnings or income of both must be reported and the tax paid thereon, either in a joint return or separate returns. 

2. Where the matter of ownership of income is not clear, the question must be answered by the laws relating to property, rather than taxation. 

Must I File a Declaration of Estimated Tax? 

3. The principle of withholding taxes at the source on wages contained in the new tax law is to place the average taxpayer on a current basis and is designed to collect the Victory Tax, the normal tax and the surtax in the first $2,000 bracket. To place themselves on a current payas-you-go basis members of the armed services are required to declare and file an estimate of their taxes and pay in quarterly installments. (a) If you are single, or if you are married and not living with your husband or wife, you must file a declaration if:- 

Your 1944 income from wages will exceed $2,700.

Your 1944 gross income will exceed $500 and more than $100 will be from sources other than wages. (b) Providing you are married and living with your husband or wife, or if you are the head of a family, you must file a declaration if:- 

Your 1944 joint income from wages will exceed $3,500. 

Your 1944 joint gross income will exceed $1,200 and more than $100 will be received from sources other than wages.

(c) Separate declarations may be made by husband and wife, or they may file a joint declaration. The declaration has no effect on the income tax return, which may be made either separately, or as a joint return. 

4. The form of the declaration will be prescribed by the Commissioner, and will be available before March 15, 1944. The law requires that the declaration shall include the estimated tax (normal tax, surtax, and Victory Tax), and an estimate of credit, if any, for tax withheld at the source; income from tax free covenant bonds, and income tax paid to a foreign government. 

5. To secure figures for your declaration due March 15, 1944, compile a total figure, giving your gross total income for the year. 

From this, subtract the amount excluded for tax purposes; such as rental and subsistence allowance and the first $1,500 received as pay for duty with the armed services.

Then, deduct your permissible deductions and from this figure you deduct your personal exemption and credit for dependents. ($624 deducted, will give your net Victory Tax income). 

The result is your balance, subject to surtax, and to the Victory Tax. 

You may then deduct your earned income credit, and the balance is that subject to the Normal Tax of 6%.

Filing Declarations of Estimated Tax. Under the 1943 pay-asyou-go plan, all taxpayers are required to file a declaration of their estimated income for the calendar year, on March 15 each year. 

Declarations will be made in forms prescribed by the commissioner, and will include the estimated normal tax, surtax, and Victory Tax. 

To Whom Declarations are Made. Declarations of estimated in come tax for the year shall be made to the same collector to whom payments of tax are made. See notes under this heading, paragraph 65. 

Penalties. The following penalties are prescribed: 

"(1) Failure to file declaration of estimated tax. In the case of a failure to make and file a declaration of estimated tax within the time prescribed, there shall be added to the tax an amount equal to 10 per centum of the tax. "(2) Failure to pay installment of estimated tax.-In the case of the failure to pay an installment of the estimated tax within the time prescribed, there shall be added to the tax $2.50 or 22 per centum of the tax, whichever is the greater, for each installment with respect to which such failure occurs. "(3) Substantial underestimate of tax.-if 80 per cent (66-2/3 per cent in the case of farmers) of the tax before credit for amounts with-

held exceeds the estimated tax increased by the credits for amounts withheld, there shall be added to the tax an amount equal to such excess or equal to 6 per cent of the amount by which the tax exceeds the estimated tax so increased, whichever is the lesser." 

Must I File an Income Tax Return? 

6. Every individual who is single, and every individual who is married but not living with husband or wife, must file an Income Tax Return and a Victory Tax return if, during the taxable year, his or her gross income was $500 or over (average $41.66 per month). 

NOTE: The first $1500. received for services in the armed services is not "income." 

7. Every individual who is married and living with husband or wife, must file an income and Victory Tax return if, during the taxable year, his or her gross income was $1200 or over (average $100 per month). An individual who is the head of a family, although not a married person, while entitled to the $1200 personal exemption, must file a return if the gross income was $500 or over. The head of a family is defined as one who has some legal or moral responsibility for one or more of the following dependents: children, parents, grandparents, brother, sister, aunt, uncle, nephew, or niece. It also includes stepsister, stepbrother, and mother-, father-, sister, and brother-in-laws. All dependents must be under eighteen years or incapable of self-support. 

The allowance is $350 for each person physically or mentally dependent other than spouse. 

8. A husband and wife, if living together at the close of the taxable year, may file a joint return in which case the tax shall be computed on the aggregate income, and the liability with respect to the tax shall be joint and several.

Husbands and wives are not compelled to file a joint return. If both have an income, the higher rates in the upper surtax income brackets may be avoided by filing separate returns. E. g.: The surtax on two individual net returns of $2000 each is $520; on a joint return of $4000 the surtax is $580. Higher incomes show a progressively increasing difference. 

9. After you have once filed an income tax return, a record is established in the Bureau of Internal Revenue. The following year you will receive an Income Tax Return blank based on the report for the previous year. However, if your income was not such as to make necessary the filing of a return, none is required. In the event of communications from

the Bureau of Internal Revenue with reference to your failure to file, a simple letter setting forth the facts of your income will suffice. In the event your gross income (regardless of the net) equals or exceeds the amounts in paragraphs 6 and 7, above, a return is required. 

10. If the taxpayer is unable to make his own return, the return shall be made by a duly authorized agent or by the guardian or other person charged with the care of the person or property of such taxpayer. 

11. In addition to your individual return, you are also required to make a report of all taxable payments of interest, rent, etc., made to another person if such payment totals $500 or over for the taxable year. Take for example your rent, if on rental allowances; if it exceeds $500 you must make the required report. (If you pay it to a real estate agent or a corporation, however, the report need not be made.) The report is made on Form 1099, accompanied by Form 1096, and must be mailed to The Commissioner of Internal Revenue, Sorting Section, Washing ton, D. C., not to the Collector, not later than February 15th of each year.

What Is Your Gross Income 

12. For ordinary commercial purposes, your gross income is the total of all income and receipts for the entire year, but this is not necessarily your gross income for Income Tax purposes. They are income for Income 

Tax purposes only if: (a) Congress has the power to tax them. (b) They are "income" as that term has been defined by the courts for tax purposes. (c) They are not excluded from your gross income by the Revenue Act. 

13. Generally speaking, your gross income includes all gains, profits, and income derived: from salaries, wages, or other compensation received for personal services, of whatever kind and in whatever form paid; from professions, vocations, trades, business, commerce, or sales; from dealings in property (real or personal), growing out of the ownership, use, or interest in such property; also from interest, rent, dividends, securities, or profits from any business ventures. Payments received for magazine or newspaper articles, royalties from books, inventions, etc., must be included. Briefly, gross income includes all gains, profits, and income derived from any source whatsoever. 

14. From Army Sources, your income should include the following items: 

10

(a) Pay. Base pay plus longevity pay, including cadets (active list), and increased pay on foreign service, or sea duty. (b) Retired pay. (except that excluded by par. 20h. (c) Increase pay for aviation duty (flight and parachute). (d) Mileage. (Report entire amount received as income; deduct amount of traveling expenses actually paid out.) (e) Transportation of families. (The actual cost of such transportation paid by the government on change of station is considered to be income. 

No deduction allowed.) 

(f) Interest on deposits of enlisted men. (g) Reenlistment allowance paid to enlisted men. (h) One year's pay received by officers wholly retired. (i) Bonus paid to Reserve Officers of the Air Corps upon release from active duty. (j) Allowances of retired enlisted men ($9.50 per month in lieu of rations and clothing; $6.25 for quarters, fuel, and light). (k) All permanent or transient additions to pay. 

15. From Other Sources, you may receive taxable income from: (a) Compensation for personal or professional services. (b) Business income as a proprietor or partner. (c) Profits from sales or dealings in property (real or personal. tangible or intangible). (d) Income from investments in properties or securities, such as rent or interest. (e) Income received from the sale of magazine or newspaper articles, royalties from books, etc. In this connection expense items consisting of typing of manuscripts, cost of illustrations, research work paid another, cost of supplies and in some cases a portion of your rent provided certain space is maintained in your residence for such work, are legitimate deductions from such income and should be deducted in the return. (f) Dividends from a domestic corporation. Any distribution of profits made by a corporation in the ordinary course of business (even if an extraordinary amount), whether in cash or in kind, is a dividend for tax purposes if it is made to the shareholders, out of earnings or profits made during the year or accumulated since March 1, 1913. (g) Stock dividends have been the subject of many court decisions. 

At this writing it is safe to say that dividends paid in stock, or rights to 11

acquire stock, if actually constituting income, are taxable. A dividend of common stock to common stock holders is normally not considered taxable income. A dividend in preferred stock to common stock holders, where both classes have previously been issued, is probably a taxable stock dividend (consult your collector). A safe rule to follow is to distinguish between your investment (i.e., your capital structure) and the income from it. If you received income, it is taxable; on the other hand, if your capital structure increases in value, you have no income unless and until you sell. You are then taxed on the profits. 

16. Capital Gains and Losses. The holding period for short and long term gains and losses has been changed from eighteen months to six months under the present act. One hundred percent of the loss or gain is used in determining the gain or loss under short term items (6 months or less) while 50% of the loss or gain is used in determining the gain or loss under long term items (items held more than 6 months). 

After applying the percentages for the two classes of gains and losses, all gains and losses are considered together. However a net capital loss not in excess of $1000 is allowable as a deduction against income. Losses in excess of this amount may be carried over for five succeeding years to be applied against future capital gains and also up to the $1000 maximum loss per year against other income of the taxpayer. 

Items Which May be Excluded From Gross Income 

17. Rental and Subsistence Allowances. As stated above, all income received is not taxable income. One of the items excluded of particular interest to all Army personnel is allowances for rental and subsistence. 

18. Life Insurance. Amounts received under a life insurance contract, paid by reason of the death of the insured, whether paid in a lump sum or otherwise, may be excluded from gross income. However, if the proceeds are left with the Insurance Company under an agreement to pay interest thereon, then the interest is income for tax purposes. 

In the case of the maturity of endowment policies only that amount of the payment which exceeds the total of the premiums paid in is included in gross income and the same rule applies when the insured. surrenders an insurance contract accepting the cash surrender value. thereof. However in the case of the maturity of an endowment policy in which the payments are made in installments instead of a single sum then such payments are taxed in the same manner as annuities which are taxed on the basis of three per cent (3%) of the aggregate premiums 12

or the consideration paid for such an annuity. For example during 1941 a taxpayer's endowment policy matured on which he had paid premiums totaling $600 a year for twenty years, a total of $12,000. In lieu of a single sum payment taxpayer was to receive twenty payments of nine hundred dollars ($900) each. Three per cent of the $12,000 or $360 must be included in gross income, the balance of $540 being construed as the return of premiums. It will be seen in the example that the taxpayer fails to recover in exempt income the sum of $1200. ($12,000 less $540 x 20). However annuities being written for a fixed period (term of years) or for life, or life with a guaranteed fixed period, the regulations covering amounts which will be included in gross income and the amounts which may be excluded will differ from an endowment policy paid in installments. Using the above example it will be seen that at the end of twenty-three years a total of $12,420 would have been excluded from gross income. However $420 of this would not be an allowable exclusion from the gross income and the taxable income for the twenty-third year would be $780 ($360 + $420) and for subsequent years the entire $900 would be included in gross income. Taxpayers receiving annuities prior to 1934 would be confronted with a somewhat different problem as under the income tax acts prior to 1934 annuities were not taxed until the total premiums paid or consideration had been recovered. In arriving at the taxable income from annuities under the 1934 and subsequent acts all amounts excluded under acts prior to 1934 must be taken into consideration. Using the same example and considering that 8 annual installments had been paid prior to 1934, or a total of $7200 had been received, none of which had been included in gross income, a balance of $4800 would remain ($12,000 $7200) and since $540 would be excluded from gross income in 1934 and each subsequent year, at the close of 1942 $4860 would have been excluded (9 x $540). Then for the year 1942 $60 ($4860-$4800) would be added to the $360 and a total of $420 included in gross income for that year and for all years subsequent to 1942 the entire $900 would be. included in gross income. 

Dividends on premiums are simply a refund and are not income for tax purposes.

(k) For the year 1943, and 1944 the first $1500 received for services in the armed forces is excluded, regardless of rank.

(Note a distinction, Federal and State). (b) Interest on obligations of a corporation, organized under Act of Congress, if such corporation is an instrumentality of the U. S. (see par. 50). 21. Injuries. Compensations for injuries which are not personal may also be excluded from your gross income, such as: amounts received as compensation for destruction or dimunition in the value of your capital assets; breach of promise to marry; alienations of affections; slander suits; libel suits. You may also exclude any alimony paid by you, except amounts allocated by court decree for support of your minor children, and compensation for injury or sicknesses received under accident and health insurance policies, or under workmen's compensation laws. Also damages received from any law suit or the settlement thereof. 

22. Miscellaneous Exclusions from gross income include gifts, bequests, and devises, tax free interest (par. 50), gain from the sale or exchange of property which is not recognized for tax purposes. Recovery of bad debts, prior tax or delinquency account; to the extent of the amount recovered and which originally did not result in a tax benefit. 

23. Bargain Purchases when for your own use and benefit, are not income. For instance, if a friend is liquidating his household goods or other effects and you purchase goods worth $800 for $300, you have derived no profit from the transaction. However, if you sell those same goods for $600 then you have a profit which is taxable income. In some cases a bargain purchase is considered a gift: For example, your father may sell you a set of golf clubs worth $150 for as little as $15. There is no income, so far as you are concerned, but it may have to be included in a gift tax return.

Deductions Allowed 

24. Contributions may be deducted if made within the taxable year, and if made for the use of (1) The United States, any State, Territory or any political subdivision thereof or the District of Columbia, exclusively for public purposes. (2) A domestic corporation, trust, community chest, fund or foundation; organized and operated solely for religious, charitable, scientific, literary, or educational purposes; or for the prevention of cruelty to children or animals. For example, Churches, Red Cross, K. of C., Y. M. C. A., Y. M. H. A., Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, or to The Soldiers and Sailors Club of New York. These contributions are deductible only if no substantial part of the recipient's activities include propaganda or other activities seeking to influence legislation, and if no part of its net earnings is for the benefit of private shareholders or individuals. (Limited to 15% of net income). 

25. Interest. Generally, you may deduct all interest which you pay or 15

incur on indebtedness. You are required to repay all money borrowed, and neither the receipt nor the repayment enters your income tax return. 

This is true whether the money is borrowed for professional, personal, or family use or as a mortgage on real or personal property. The indebtedness need not be evidenced by lien, judgment, or mortgage, or even a note. 

The interest you pay is income to the payee and is therefore deductible by you. Enter interest payment and explain in schedule "E" on your return. 

26. General Taxes may be deducted. For example, poll tax, state income tax, tax on admissions to theatres or other amusements, tax on lodge and club dues, real and personal property taxes (except assessments for street improvements, sidewalks, sewers, etc., which increase the value of your property). Taxes assessed by the United States, the States, (including amounts paid as state or local sales tax) Territories or other political sub-divisions (except income, estate, inheritance, legacy, succession and gift taxes). Postage is not a tax. 

27. Manufacturers' Taxes. Under the Revenue Act, manufacturers' 

Excise taxes are imposed on and paid by the manufacturer. Even if included in the price paid by the consumer, it is still not considered a tax paid by the consumer. Thus you may not deduct taxes included in the price of cigarettes, gasoline, oil, tires and other automobile accessories, firearms, matches, chewing gum, jewelry, furs, radios, phonograph records, and cosmetics. 

28. Consumer Taxes. As distinguished from the preceding paragraph, taxes on telephone, telegraph, transportation of persons or property, safe deposit boxes, club dues, admissions, and stamp tax on the sale of securities are imposed on, paid, and may be deducted by the consumer. 

These taxes are deductible by the consumer even though in some cases the person making the sale is required to collect and report the taxes. 

29. Gasoline Taxes. A distinction must be observed between the application of Federal and State taxes on gasoline. The Federal gasoline tax is imposed on the manufacturer and is not deductible. Neither is the State tax deductible in states which have imposed the tax on the oil company, wholesaler, dealer or distributor. State taxes imposed on the consumer (and this is the general rule) are deductible. Divide annual mileage by average mileage per gallon; multiply by state tax. The Treasury Department has held that the gasoline taxes imposed by the following states are deductible by the consumer: Arizona, Arkansas, Colorado, Connecticut, 16

Delaware, District of Columbia, Florida, Idaho, Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, Montana, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Dakota, Texas, Vermont, Virginia, Washington, West Virginia and Wisconsin.

Gasoline taxes imposed by the following have been held to be deductible only by the distributor or dealer: Alabama, California, Georgia, Mississippi, Nebraska, South Carolina, Tennessee, Utah and Wyoming.

In Utah, if you purchase gasoline in an original container for your own personal use, you may deduct the 4c tax. If you purchase from a retailer, no deduction is allowed. 

30. Automobile Tags. State automobile license fees are ordinary taxes. 

Deduct. 

31. Losses, General. To be deducted from income tax returns, all losses must be evidenced by closed and completed transactions, fixed by identifiable events, bonafide and actually sustained during the taxible year for which claimed. The law pertaining to long term and short term capital losses and gains has been changed (British law influence). Capital assets held less than 6 months are short term and are taken in at 100%; over six months are now long term and are taken in at 50%. Losses which are compensated for by insurance are not deductible. 

It is wise to remember that if you file a joint return, reporting joint income, you can then deduct your joint losses, if any. 

32. Losses from Fire, Storm, Etc., are deductible whether in connection with your personal affairs or your business. It must be established, however, that the loss was actually of some unforeseen or unpredictible event, such as fire, hurricane, unduly severe hot or cold weather, shipwreck, etc. 

33. Losses by Theft are deductible, whether a business or personal loss, but the amount spent to convict the thief is usually not deductible. Hence the loss of expensive jewelry or other personal belongings, including your automobile if used for pleasure and convenience, is deductible. You must, however, establish the fact of theft. If doubt exists as to whether it was lost or stolen the deduction would not be allowed. 

34. Losses, Sickness or Accident. In the case of any loss suffered, by sickness or accident, not the result of your own negligence, or, if you are hit by another's faulty driving, you may deduct all loss in excess of the amount not compensated by insurance or otherwise recovered. The 17

present law contains a provision which may enable the taxpayer to deduct a portion of medical and dental expenses provided the total of such items exceeds 5% of the net income for the year. The first 5% may not be deducted, only that portion which is in excess of the 5% and not to exceed $2500 for married persons or the head of a family, and $1250 for individuals. In arriving at your total of "Medical Expenses" include doctors' and dentists' bills, payments for hospital insurance and for membership in an association furnishing cooperative free choice medical service or group hospitalization. Premiums paid to maintain health and accident insurance may be included as a part of your "Medical Expenses" but any such expenses as you may be compensated for by insurance cannot be counted in your total. 

Example: Richard Roe's net income (medical expense not considered) was $7247.13. He spent $750 for medical expenses during 1942 and is therefore entitled to a deduction of $387.64.

35. Losses Casualty. Where you sustain a casualty loss to property not used in your business (and therefore cannot be depreciated) the amount of your deductible loss is the difference between the value of the property immediately before the casualty and the value immediately afterwards. 

This problem, always confusing, was settled by the Supreme Court on 

January 3, 1939. 

36. Losses, Miscellaneous. Losses from gambling, wagers, or other illegal transactions are deductible only to the extent of gains from such transactions. You may deduct losses from closed banks but only in the year in which the loss was determined to have occurred. Deduct rental of safe deposit boxes if used exclusively for the storage of income producing property. A tenant shareholder in a Co-op Apartment Corp'n may deduct that part of the interest and taxes paid by the Corp'n which is charged to and paid by him. All losses should be reported and explained in schedule "G" of the reverse of the tax return. 

37. Bad Debts. Bad debts, whether of a business or personal nature, may be deducted in the year in which they become worthless. You also have the option of writing off part of a bad debt. Your return should contain a statement in schedule "G" showing how, when, where 18

it was created; the person who owes you, his relationship if any, when due, what you have done to collect and how you determine it to be worthless. Some examples of bad debts are unpaid loans, salary or other fees due you, rents and similar items previously reported as income by you.

If your debtor dies, or if you hold mortgage on property sold for less than your claim, deduct the amount not recovered. Losses or gains in the sale of property by the mortgagor and the mortgagee in foreclosure proceedings are technical (consult your collector). 

38. War Losses. The Internal Revenue Code has been amended by the insertion of a section covering War Losses. Property is deemed to fall in three classes: (1) Property not in enemy countries (2) Property in enemy countries (3) Investments referable to destroyed or seized property (Securities)

It is important to note in this connection that the amendment is retroactive to taxable years beginning after December 31, 1940. It is thus assumed that when the regulations have been promulgated by the Commissioner of Internal Revenue, taxpayers who have suffered losses under this section may file amended returns for the year 1941, provided it is determined that the loss was sustained in 1941 rather than 1942. The procedure for determining the date on which property is to be considered as seized or destroyed, while at first glance appears to be rather involved is simple. Although regulations covering the determination of the dates have not as yet been promulgated, the law states "The taxpayer's choice of a date under (1) and (3) shall be effective only if made within such time and in such manner as may be prescribed by regulations prescribed by the Commissioner with the approval of the Secretary."

Determination of the dates upon which loss was sustained under (1) and (3) above shall be "the latest date, as established to the satisfaction of the Commissioner, on which such property may be considered as not destroyed or seized, and the earliest date, as established to the satisfaction of the Commissioner, on which such property may be considered as having already been destroyed or seized." In other words the date of determination of the loss and the year to which said loss is applicable would be the date on which such area passed into the control of a country which is at war with the United States. Bear in 19

mind that this applies to property in (1) and (3). Determination of the date of property falling in (2) is clearly defined in the law as the date on which war was declared by the United States against such country.

The full value may be charged off in the year applicable, unless covered by insurance or otherwise compensated for, without regard to the possibility of a recovery in whole or in part at the close of the war or some future date but the amount recovered, if any, at some future date, must be returned as income in the year in which received. However, if the taxpayer should recover an amount in excess of the amount charged as a loss and allowed by the Commissioner that excess is not included in the gross income of the taxpayer. 

39. Insignia, Corps Devices, Etc. The cost of items of equipment (as distinguished from the uniform) such as sabers, saber chains, corps devices, full dress belts, epaulets, shoulder straps, aiguillettes, campaign bars and sam browne belts are deductible as a business expense. You may also deduct dues to professional societies and subscriptions to professional journals. 

40. Alimony. In all revenue acts prior to that of 1942 payments of alimony were not deductible neither were they considered as income to the recipient. However the 1942 Act is a complete reversal of this procedure. Payments of alimony to a divorced wife as well as separate maintenance payments made to a wife who is legally separated are deductible for tax purposes and are income to the wife provided: (a) 

There has been an actual divorce or legal separation. (b) The payments are periodic. Lump sum payments are not deductible. However a lump sum agreement to be paid off in periodic installments over a period of more than ten years would give the taxpayer the benefit of the deduction as the installment payments, up to 10% of the principal amount, are considered periodic payments. Where a portion of an alimony payment is specified for the support of a minor child or children, of the husband, that portion does not constitute income to the wife nor is it deductible by the husband but such payments may give him the status of "head of a family" and/or the credit for dependents if he meets the other requirements. 20

Deductions Which Are Not Allowed 

41. Insurance Premiums paid on war risk, government, or commercial policies are not deductible. Neither are the premiums paid to cover increased insurance carried as the result of the hazards of aviation duty. 

42. Uniforms. The cost of uniforms (O.D. and Blues), their repair, and the cost of alteration as the result of promotion or demotion, is held to be a personal expense, similar to like expenses of a civilian nature, and is not deductible. Likewise, depreciation or obsolesence cannot be deducted. The uniform includes caps and chin straps, and gilt buttons on the coats. Boots, shoes, and leggins take the place of shoes in civil life. 

The distinction here seems to be that all articles of the uniform which have civilian dress equivalents are not deductible, but that the items in par. 39 above have no equivalent in civil life and are deductible as a business expense necessary to produce your income. 

43. Officers' Club Dues, even if you are required to join, are not deductible. Any taxes assessed on the dues are deductible, however. 

44. Personally Owned Automobile. The Collectors of Internal Revenue report considerable difficulty with this item in the returns of Army officers. 

This probably is the result of legislation proposed by the War Department, but never acted upon by Congress, to grant officers an allowance for the use of their personally owned cars. At this writing there are no deductions allowed for the use, operating expense, depreciation, obsolesence or other expense to privately owned automobiles, except when the car is required, not voluntarily used, for official government business when there are no government cars available. 

45. Other Deductions Not Allowed are such items as your personal, living, or family expenses. Rents and royalties are not deductible. Loss of pay by sentence of a court-martial, and civil court fines and penalties cannot be deducted. Neither can you deduct any amounts paid out for new buildings or for permanent improvements to property you already own, or for making good any exhaustion thereof, for which an allowance has been made in schedule C, (2). 

Income Tax For Duty in a Foreign Possession 

46. All income received from the United States or any of its agencies is subject to income tax whether the recipient has lived all or any part of the year outside the continental limits of the United States, except in the case of citizens who are residing in a possession of the United States (Philippine 21

Islands, Puerto Rico, Panama Canal Zone, but not in the Virgin Islands or in any of the leased bases-Iceland, Trinidad, etc.), and who can satisfy the following conditions: (a) If 80 per cent or more of the gross income of such citizen for the three-year period immediately preceding the close of the taxable year (or such part of such period immediately preceding the close of such taxable year as may be applicable) was derived from sources within a possession of the United States; and (b) If, in the case of such citizens, 50 per cent or more of his gross income (computed without the benefit of this section) for such period or such part thereof was derived from the active conduct of a trade or business within a possession of the United States either on his own account or as an employee or agent of another.

The salary or other compensation paid by the United States to the members of its civil, Military or Naval personnel for services rendered within a possession of the United States represents income derived from the active conduct of a trade or business within a possession of the United States. 

The benefits of this section apply to Army and Navy personnel stationed for duty in a possession of the United States. 

Considerable misunderstanding exists regarding this item, probably because of a statement printed in a widely distributed income tax pamphlet, which stated that "Income is exempt from taxes if you reside more than 50% of the year in a foreign country." This does not apply to persons receiving their income from the U. S. Government. 

To enjoy the exemption (26 U. S. Code, Sec. 251) two conditions must be met. (1) 80% or more of your gross income must be received within a possession of the U. S., and (2) 50% or more must be received as salary, pay, or allowances. You are not required to compute your tax under Section 251 unless it is to your advantage to do so. 

Notes On Your Real Estate 

47. While there are few, if any, Army personnel engaged in a business or profession other than their military profession, there are no doubt many who own real estate or other income producing property. These properties, when rented to others, are income producing and entitled to an allowance for depreciation. Depreciation for income tax purposes is distinct from "Depletion" (which occurs to mineral lands, gas, and oil wells) and "Obsolesence" (which occurs to machinery and power tools as the 22

result of new inventions). Depletion and obsolesence allowances are highly technical in tax matters and require the services of a competent tax advisor. Depreciation, unfortunately, is not deductible on a building which is used exclusively as your own residence or on the furniture and fixtures therein. Your automobile for personal or family use, your uniforms, and other personal effects likewise have no depreciation allowance. 

If you own a building which is rented to another person, then you have income from rents, and you are allowed to deduct depreciation and also the cost of repairs, alterations, watchman or janitor salaries, etc. Income from rents requires that you use Tax Form 1040 in making out your return. On page 2 you will find spaces provided (disregard column 4) in 

Schedule C(2) to compute your depreciation, which in turn is carried to Schedule C(1). Here you compute your income from rents and deduct your depreciation, repairs, and other expenses. You then carry the "Net❞ only to the face of the tax form. The faster you depreciate your property on the tax form, the sooner you exhaust it. When 100% depreciation has been taken (it is carried forward from year to year) no further credit is allowed. Note also that repairs or additions made by lessee cannot be used by the lessor to increase or diminish the value of the property. Controversies over the percentage of depreciation allowable each year have frequently reached the courts. At this writing we believe it is reasonably safe to say that: 

2% may be taken annually on brick and steel buildings 

3% to 4% may be taken annually on frame buildings 

3% to 

4% may be taken annually on frame residences. 

2% to 22% may be taken annually on brick residences or tenements 

2% to 5% may be taken annually on garages

If a building is received as the result of a death, the fair market value is the cost (estate appraised value). You are required to determine the cost to you, the depreciable part of its cost, its estimated life, and the percentage of annual depreciation you choose. The cost to you is never the amount used for depreciation purposes, for the simple reason that the building must stand on land. The house or other building can be depreciated, but the land has no depreciation allowance. If you buy a house and lot, determine the fair value of each and depreciate the building only. If you are fortunate enough to pick up a bargain, determine the fair market value of each and determine your basis for depreciation as follows. Assume you paid 23

$9000, and a fair market value for the house is $8000 and the land $2000, a total of $10,000. Your method for computing the basis of your depreciation allowance is 8/10ths of $9000 or $7200. To make it clear it may be stated as a simple problem in proportion. Let X equal the basis you must start your depreciation on; then F.M.V. total: F.M.V. house :: 

Actual cost X. Using the same figures we have $10,000: $8000 :: $9000 $7200.

To Compute Your Income Tax 

48. You must first determine your gross income, deductions, and your net income for tax purposes, and decide, by the amount, which of the two forms you will use. (a) Form 1040 must be used by all individuals, single or joint return:

If the gross (not net) income exceeds $3000. 

If there is any income other than salaries, wages, or compensation for personal services, dividends, interest, annuities, or royalties. 

If there is any income from rents. 

If the return is made on the accrual basis or on a fiscal year basis. 

If conducting a business or profession on your own account. 

If you have any gains from the sale of real estate or other property, including shares of stock, bonds, or other securities of a like nature. (b) Form 1040 A may be used by all individuals, single or joint return, if the following conditions are met. Its use is optional, not required. This is the new short form for the small taxpayer. 

If the gross income does not exceed $3000. (First $1500 military pay is not income.)

If the income consists wholly of salaries, wages, or compensation for personal services, dividends, interest.

If individual returns are made by husband and wife living together, each shall be treated as a married person making separate return. Also, this form is not authorized if either spouse makes a separate return on form 1040. Both or neither must use form 1040A. If husband and wife living together file a joint return, they shall be treated as married persons. 

A married person not a head of a family and not living with husband or wife, shall be treated as a single person. 

If this form is used the determination of marital status is fixed by that status on July 1, 1943. 24

Foreign tax credits and tax withheld at the source are not allowed as credits on this form.

One important thing to remember in choosing which form to use for your return is that once you select a form and mail it in you cannot thereafter withdraw it in order to submit your return on another form which you may later find to be more economical. 

49. Personal Exemptions. The personal exemption allowed for a single person or a married person not living with husband or wife is $500. For those married and living with their husband or wife (or anyone who is head of a family) an exemption of $1200 is allowed, with an additional exemption of $350 for each person in addition to the husband or wife dependent upon them for their chief support. $350 is allowed for each dependent after the first; to the head of a family. Dependent for their chief support normally means children under 18 years of age. However, it also includes people of all ages who are mentally or physically incapable of engaging in any gainful occupation and who are financially unable to support themselves. The dependents need not necessarily live in the same household; for instance, a child at college is still dependent, provided it is under 18 years of age. The determining factor is whether or not they are dependent on you for 50% or more of their care and living expenses. 

In the event you decide to use Form 1040 and your conjugal status changes or your dependents increase or decrease during the year, compute the deductions in fractions of months. If you use Form 1040 A your status on July 1, 1943 determines your status for tax purposes. 

If you use Form 1040 and for instance, a child is born in July (before the 15th) deduct 6/12ths of $350, which in this case would equal $175. 

After deducting personal exemptions and credits for dependents, the balance remaining is the balance which is used to compute your surtax. 

50. Interest on Government Obligations. These notes or bonds usually indicate their tax liability and fall into three general groups: (1) interest wholly exempt and excluded from income (see par. 20); (2) interest wholly exempt from normal tax, but not from surtax; and (3) interest wholly exempt from normal tax and from surtax on a principal sum of $5000 or less, and surtax on all over $5000. Enter fully in Schedule “A,” 

Form 1040. The interest on obligations of the U. S. issued after March 

1, 1941, is now taxed the same as private obligations (except bonds issued to finance contracts made prior to that date; e.g., U. S. Housing 

Authority). U. S. Savings Bonds issued prior to March 1, 1941, are 25

tax exempt up to a principal sum of $5000. The $5000 limit is on the cost price. If in doubt, consult your collector. 

51. Earned Income. Earned income credit is for salaries, fees, etc., received for the actual performance of your personal services, less traveling expenses. Dividends, rents, royalties, etc., are not included. You are allowed to deduct 10% of your earned income, but two limitations must be observed. First $14,000 is the top limit on which the 10% may be taken. 

Second, if your entire net income does not exceed $3000, you may take 10% of the actual amount of the net income, regardless of its source. 

On net incomes which exceed $3000, regardless of the percentage of earned income (wages, salaries, professional fees), the earned income credit will not be less than $300. For example, if a taxpayer had no earned net income but had a net income from other sources of $10,000, he would be entitled to an earned income credit on his return of $300. If he had an earned income of $2000 and a net income of $4000, he would be entitled to an earned income credit of $300. In other words, on net incomes in excess of $3000, the earned income credit cannot in any case be less than $300 or more than $1400. 

52. Normal Tax. A normal tax of 6 % must be paid on the net income, minus: (1) personal exemption, (2) credit for dependents, (3) earned

Income Credit and (4) interest on Government obligations. Add these four, subtract from net income. Pay 6% on balance. 

53. Surtax. A surtax of (see table in appendix) must be paid on the net income minus: (1) personal exemption and, (2) credit for dependents.

Add these two, subtract from net income, and consult table of surtax rates for amount of surtax you must pay. 

54. Credits Against Tax. The amount of income taxes imposed by foreign countries or possessions of the United States shall be allowed as a credit against the tax, to the following extent: (a) If the taxpayer signifies in his return his desire to have the benefits, the tax imposed by this title, he shall be credited with, in the case of a citizen of the United States, the amount of any income taxes paid or accrued during the taxable years to any foreign country or to any possession of the United States; and, in the case of a resident of the 

United States, the amount of any such taxes paid or accrued during the taxable year to any possession of the United States. (b) The amount of the credit taken, hereunder, shall be subject to each of the following limitations: 26

(1) The amount of the credit in respect of the tax paid or accrued to any country shall not exceed the same proportion of the tax against which such credit is taken, which the taxpayer's net income from sources within such country bears to his entire net income for the same taxable year; and (2) The total amount of the credit shall not exceed the same proportion of the tax against which such credit is taken, which the taxpayer's net income from sources without the United States bears to his entire net income for the same taxable year. 

TO COMPUTE YOUR VICTORY TAX 

55. In addition to the normal and surtaxes imposed, there is also imposed on each individual, a Victory Tax of 5% on the Victory Tax net income, for the year 1943 (it does not apply on 1942 income). Your 

Victory Tax net income is not necessarily the same as your net income for income taxes.

Victory Tax income means your gross income from all sources. 

56. Exclusions from Victory Tax Gross Income are limited to rental and subsistence allowances received in cash or in kind, which the Congress has no power to tax. (Ct. Clms. Dec., Clifford Jones case); gains from the sale or exchange of capital assets; interest on obligations of the Federal Government or its branches; amounts received as accident or health insurance; retired pay for officers retired for disability in line of duty; pensions, etc., received for sickness or injury as the result of service in the armed forces, and the first $1,500 received for services in the armed forces. 

57. Deductions from Gross Income in order to determine your Victory 

Tax net income, are limited to: (a) Expenses. All necessary expenses incurred to product your income including management and maintenance of property held for the production of income. (b) Interest. All interest paid or accrued during the year on indebtedness incurred to produce your income. (c) Taxes. All taxes that are normally deductible for regular income tax purposes may be deducted from the gross income for the Victory Tax, provided such taxes are incurred in the earning of Victory 

Tax income. (d) Losses. Those incurred during the year from the conduct of a business and not compensated for by insurance. 27

(e) Bad Debts. Any bad debts in connection with the conduct of business. (f) Depreciation. On any property held to produce your income, such as rented real estate. (g) Alimony. Paid by a husband, but not that part which is decreed by the court for the support of minor children. 

58. Deductions not Allowed. Personal and family living expenses; amounts paid for improvements to increase the value of your property, or estate, or to restore any exhaustion thereof; premiums paid on life insurance. 

59. Exemptions. Not to exceed $624 specific exemption for each individual. In the case of joint returns, filed by husband and wife, the specific exemption allowed is $624 for each spouse, or a total of $1248, provided each spouse's income equals at least $624. The excess of specific exemption of one spouse over the income of that spouse cannot be applied against the income of the other spouse. If you use the 

Short Form Tax Return, use Victory Tax rates shown on form. 

60. Credits Against Victory Tax. The act provided for a credit on 

Victory Taxes, in the following amounts: (a) 25% of the Victory Tax or $500, whichever is less, in the case of a single person, or a married person not living with husband or wife. (b) 40% of the Victory Tax or $500, whichever is the less, in the case of a married person living with husband or wife, where separate returns are filed by each spouse. (c) 40% of the Victory Tax or $1,000, whichever is the less, (a) in the case of the head of a family; or (b) in the case of a husband and wife filing a joint return; or (c) in the case of a married person living with husband or wife, where a separate return is filed by one spouse and no return is filed by the other spouse. (d) 2% of the Victory Tax or $100, whichever is the less, for each dependent. (e) If Form 1040A is used. Use rates shown on the form.

62. Payment of Tax-Installments. The taxpayer is required to make payment equal to one fourth of his estimated tax on March 15 of each year, at the same time he files his declaration. The second installment is due on June 15, the third on September 15, the fourth on December 15 of each year. If any installment is not paid on or before the date for its payment, the whole amount of the tax unpaid shall be paid upon notice and demand of the collector. 

63. Penalties. Heavy penalties are imposed: 

For failure to file return; for failure to file return on time; for submitting false return and for wilful failure to pay the tax. 

64. Time for Filing Returns. Returns made on the basis of the calendar shall be filed in the office of the Collector on or before the 15th day of March following the close of the calendar year. year

Returns made on the basis of a fiscal year shall be filed in the office of the Collector on or before the 15th day of the third month following the close of the fiscal year. 

The Commissioner may grant a reasonable extension of time for filing returns, under such rules and regulations as he shall prescribe with the approval of the Secretary. Except in the case of taxpayers who are abroad, no such extension shall be for more than six months.

The grant of extension of time for filing returns of income for previous years up to and including the 15th day of the 6th month following the close of the taxable year, in the case of American citizens residing or traveling abroad, including persons in the military or naval service on duty outside of the United States, is governed by the following regulations: (a) In each case there must be attached to the return an affidavit stating cause of delay in filing. (b) Installments of tax actually due must be paid at the time of filing the delayed return, and the remaining installments must be paid as they fall due. 31

(c) Taxpayers in the military service on duty outside the U. S. may, but are not required to defer the filing of their income tax return, and the payment of tax until:-the 15th day of the third month after their return to the U. S., the 15th day of the third month after discharge from the Army or:—the 15th day of the third month after the end of the war. 

The collection from any person in the military service, of any tax on the income of such person, whether falling due prior to or during his period of military service, shall be deferred for a period extending not more than six months after the termination of his period of military service, if such person's ability to pay such tax is materially impaired by reason of such service. It may be stated, however, that in gift taxes the tax shall apply whether the transfer is in trust (except revocable trusts), or otherwise, whether the gift is direct or indirect, and whether the property is real or personal, tangible or intangible. 

The revenue act of 1942 increased the tax rates on estate and gift taxes. If you inherit an estate, or if a generous impulse prompts you to make a sizable gift, it will be wise to consult a qualified tax advisor The collectors of internal revenue expect you to report all taxable income. They also expect you to take advantage of all exclusions and deductions which are authorized by law.

The United States hereby consents to the taxation of compensation, received after December 31, 1938, for personal service as an officer or employee of the United States, any Territory or possession or political subdivision thereof, the District of Columbia, or any agency or instrumentality of any one or more of the foregoing, by any duly constituted taxing authority having jurisdiction to tax such compensation, if such taxation does not discriminate against such officer or employee because of the source of such compensation." 

From the foregoing it will be seen that Congress has to all intents enacted a reciprocity agreement with the various States. Where the 

Federal Government is permitted to tax the income of State employees, State taxing authorities, in turn, have authority to levy Income Tax on all Federal employees stationed or residing within the boundaries of the State. Not all States have state income tax laws, but federal personnel living in States which have state income tax laws on their statutes books will be required to render a return to the State Taxing authorities, and pay a tax to the State. However, no tax can be assessed or collected by 

State officials on the income of Federal Officers or employees on income received prior to January 1, 1939. 

68. In general it is safe to say that those States which have income tax laws have followed the general pattern in Title 26 of the United States Code, except where such Code comes into conflict with existing State laws covering real estate, inheritance, and gift taxes. All of the amounts taxable by the Federal Government are probably taxable by the States, and naturally all items excluded or authorized to be deducted under the Federal Tax Laws will likewise be excluded or deducted from the State Taxes.

75. Colonel Robert M. and Agnes P. White make a joint return.

Colonel White is a member of the Armed Forecs, is married, and has two dependents. Also, he is in the Air Corps and was on flying duty drawing flying pay for the entire year of 1943. We thus find that his salary as a Colonel, over 30 years with flying duty, to be $9,000, less the first $1,500, which is excluded, making the entires in column 1 and 2, $7,500. During the year, he received dividends amounting to $450, which is carried to line 2; and on line 6, he had a loss of $250.71 from the sale of capital assets. Note that this loss can be deducted from his income, but not from the Victory Tax, making his total income tax income $7,699.29, and his Victory Tax net income $7,950. During the year, he made contributions to charitable institutions amounting to $121, which is entered on line 11, and shown on the attached schedule. 

His payments for interest amounted to $110.11, and taxes paid amounting to $81.91, giving a total deduction of $313.02, which makes his income tax net income $7,386.27. His Victory Tax net income is $7,950, and this figure is carried over to line 1 of Schedule K on page 4, to compute his Victory Tax. The specific exemption of $624 is deducted, leaving an income subject to Victory Tax of $7,326, which is taxed at 5%, amounting to $366.30. Under line 5c, he is married and has two dependents, so he is authorized to deduct 44% of the tax, which, in this case, amounts to $161.17, leaving a net Victory Tax payable amounting to $205.13, which is carried to line 6, and is then entered on line 13 on the upper half of page 4, for use in the computation of his entire tax bill.

Explanation of the Law: 

In the case of Colonel Peter B. Black, we have the following situation: 

1942 tax is $1,266.20 

1943 tax is $910.32

Now, if Colonel Black were a civilian, the law says that his 1942 tax is forgiven entirely; his 1943 tax must be increased by $355.88, to bring it up to the 1942 tax of $1,266.20; and he must pay an additional one-fourth of the 1943 tax of $910.32 or $227.58, making a total tax liability of $1,493.78.
