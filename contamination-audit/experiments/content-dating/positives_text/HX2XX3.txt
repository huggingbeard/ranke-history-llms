INTRODUCTION TO HISTORY OF WYOMING 

Perhaps in all America there is no spot that possesses more features of interest than the famous 

Wyoming Valley.

Of surpassing natural scenic beauty, it has long been famous in poem and song. Underneath its soil nature has stored up mineral wealth in the form of anthracite coal that has outstripped in value all the gold ever mined in the United States.

In its settlement by the Colonial pioneer, it has developed a story of tragedy, courage, fortitude and patriotism, that may have been equalled in the development of civilization, but certainly has not been surpassed

The history of Wyoming has been written many times, and well written for the mature mind only, but there has been a long felt need for a simple story that could easily be understood by the young. 

This is frankly intended to supply that need. 

Every effort and frequent revision has been necessary to tone down the writers inclination to use words that the child mind could not grasp. In this work valuable assistance was given by H. H. Zeiser, Superintendent of Schools of Wilkes-Barré and

D. J. Mahoney, Principal of Conyngham School.

The author is also pleased to acknowledge his obligation to the Wyoming Historical and Geological Society and to Col. Ernest G. Smith, coauthor of the Harvey-Smith History of Wilkes Barré, for permission to use the cuts contained in this volume.

It is the writer's sincere hope that this little work may not only prove to be interesting and enjoyable, but also that it will be an inspiration and an incentive to our boys and girls to learn from this lesson of the pioneer that happiness and success are not to be had at a cheap price, and that courage and industry are indispensable if they would achieve them.

It was attended by a large number of chiefs of the Six Nations and by Teedyuscung and many of his tribe. Teedyuscung took a very important part in the council, and a general peace between the Indians and the English was effected on October 26, 1758. While on his way from Wyoming on the

When Teedyuscung returned to Wyoming after the council, he was considered a great chief. The

Peace was declared.

Missionaries and hunters now made their way up and down the river unmolested. 

First Connecticut 

Party Arrives, 1762

During the summer of 1762, a large party of people from Connecticut arrived. They planted grain and returned to Connecticut late in the fall. They came back in 1763 to harvest their grain and build a number of huts and a block house on the banks of what was after called Mill Creek in the northern section of the present 

City of Wilkes Barré. This was the first white settlement in the Wyoming Valley. 

Meanwhile, forgetting that the "spirit of Indian

revenge may sleep but never dies", Teedyuscung overlooked the hidden meaning of a visit of Iroquois warriors to his town. They were received kindly, and it seems that they brought whiskey with them, of which all partook, including Teedyuscung. During the night of April 19, 1763, while all were asleep, his castle was fired, as well as many of the huts, and the great Chief of the Delawares miserably perished.

The white settlement about a half mile up the river cultivated their lands in peace. They had been Destroyed kindly treated by the Delawares, White Settlement and they feared no harm, for nothing had been done by the settlers to anger their Indian neighbors. Yet suddenly on October 15, 1763, they were attacked without warning. About twenty settlers were killed and a number were made prisoners. The remainder of the settlers fled from the valley. This deed was at first believed to have been the work of the Delawares who lived in Wyoming, but from a number of captives and from those who escaped, it was learned that this bloody deed was the work of the Delawares from the West. After the death of Teedyuscung, his tribe immediately departed, and from 1763 Wyoming Valley never more sheltered any tribes of Indians who could call it "home." Until 1769, neither white nor red men lived in the Valley.

You have learned that the first white settlement in the Wyoming Valley was made on the banks of Mill Creek in the year 1763, near the spot where the General Hospital is now located; that the people who came here were from Connecticut; and that they were treacherously attacked by Indians, who killed a large number of them and drove the rest away. You may, perhaps, think it strange that people from another colony miles away from Pennsylvania should come so far to make a settlement. 

This is the way it happened. A large portion of the land in North America was supposed to belong 

English Claim 

North America to the King of England, because 

English navigators and explorers first saw it and described it. On this account, the English claimed all the land along the Atlantic Ocean from what is now North Carolina, north to Maine and west to the Pacific Ocean.

For a number of reasons, sometimes to seek adventure, sometimes for religious reasons, and sometimes to make money, bodies of men formed companies and asked the King and Parliament to give them the right to settle in North America and to

govern themselves according to the conditions they might find after their arrival. The King complied with these requests and gave these companies descriptions and boundaries of the land they were to have. These papers, signed by the King and his principal officers, were called "grants." The rules for the government of these companies were called "charters."

Charter of 1662 

In 1662, King Charles II granted and gave a charter to people in Connecticut for land lying between certain boundaries and extending from the Atlantic to the 

Pacific Ocean. 

Shortly afterward, the royal governor tried to take this charter away, but the 

Charter Oak people of Hartford preserved it by hiding it in a hollow tree, ever afterward called the "Charter Oak." You have read about this in the history of the United States. 

William Penn and Pennsylvania 

At the same time King Charles II owed Admiral

Penn, an officer in the English navy, a large sum of money which he was unable to pay. In 1681, in order that he might settle this debt, he granted to William Penn, son of Admiral Penn, a large territory in North America, which Penn called

Pennsylvania. The strange part of it all is, that he granted Penn land which extended over the southern boundaries of land that he had granted to

Connecticut nineteen years before. Thus it happened, through the ignorance of people who drew up these grants, that the land of the Wyoming Valley and up the river to the New York line had been given to two different colonies, Connecticut and Pennsylvania.

Neither of these colonies gave this land any attention for about sixty years. No one knew much about it until the visit of Count Zinzendorf in 1742, mentioned in the Indian Story. After that, a number of adventurers travelled up and down the river Some of them went to Connecticut and told of the beauty of the river and the valley, and of the fertility of the soil. Connecticut- Susquehanna Company .

A body of Connecticut people then decided to come to Wyoming valley. They asked the Connecticut government to grant them the right to form a company. 

The Connecticut assembly, having learned that this body of Connecticut people had purchased the land from the Indians, which all such companies had to do before they could rightfully take possession, gave this permission. The company was called the Connecticut-Susquehanna Company. It was granted a charter by Connecticut in 1755. 

Governor Penn of Pennsylvania soon heard of these proceedings. He believed that the land belonged to him,

Did you notice that the inscription says, "chiefly the undisciplined, the youthful, and the aged, spared by inefficiency from the distant ranks of the Republic?" What did Edward Mallery mean by that?

He meant that a war, the Revolution, was being fought far away from Wyoming; and that the boys and old men who fought the British here and lost their lives, were either too old or too young to fight in the Revolution. You may well ask, "Where were the young men of Wyoming Valley, and why were they absent in a time of danger?" 

You will remember in the story of the First

When the Revolutionary War broke out between England and the thirteen colonies, a government, called "Continental Congress", Continental Congress was formed and met in Phila- delphia. This Congress ordered that two companies of soldiers be enlisted in Wyoming Valley to protect the settlement. These companies were raised in August, 1776. One hundred and eighty-seven men were enlisted. 

General Washington needed all the soldiers he could get, as unfortunately, the British had more men than he, and were pressing him very hard.

Wyoming Forces 

On December 12, 1776, Congress ordered the Wyoming men to join Washington's army, and they marched away to the distant army of the Republic, leaving behind them "the youth and the aged" to protect the

Under the laws of Connecticut, the 24th Regiment of militia was then formed in Wyoming Valley and placed under command of Colonel Nathan Denison. 

The men and boys who remained after the regular companies left to join General Washington enlisted in this regiment. There were five companies in all, and these became the principal protectors of the settlers. 

Valley in Danger

This Valley was in real danger because the home of the Six Nations of Indians in New York State was only a little more than one hundred miles north, and the

Indians could float down the Susquehanna River in their war canoes in twenty-four hours. So, you may be sure, the settlers were always on the watch with scouting parties on the Indian trails.

What you have read of the Battle of Wyoming and the abduction of Frances Slocum will show you that the people of this valley suffered much and were at all times surrounded by great danger. 

For about a year after the battle, roving bands of Indians came here and way-laid the brave men who remained after almost all had fled. Some of the settlers were captured, many were wounded, and a number were killed.

A number of years before Wilkes-Barré was settled, France, which possessed Canada; and Eng- 

Cause of French and Indian War land, which governed the thirteen colonies in America, fell into a dispute over territory and made war on each other. Both countries wanted the fertile valleys of the Ohio and the Mississippi. 

This war was called the French and Indian War. 

The Meeting of

Isaac Barré

England sent Colonel James Wolfe with his regiment to help capture a French Fort and town called Louisbourg, situated on a large island on the east coast of 

Canada. The American colonies, such as Massachusetts, Connecticut and Rhode Island also sent regiments to help Colonel Wolfe. In the regiment from England was a young officer named Isaac Barré, who was Colonel Wolfe's principal assistant. In one of the Connecticut regiments was an officer named John Durkee, who was captain of one of the companies. The two men, John Durkee and Isaac Barré, met during the war and became friends. When the war was over, they parted and never saw each other again. 105

JOHN WILKES 

John Wilkes was born in England in 1727. His father was rich, and John received a good education. 

He was not a very good looking man, but he was a pleasant, gentlemanly fellow who could write unkind and unpleasant letters and cause those about whom he wrote 

Wilkes Elected to Parliament to get very angry. He became interested in politics and was elected to Parliament. 

He attacked the King's ministers in a paper that he had published and the King ordered these ministers to have him arrested. They did so, but on a form of paper called a warrant that Wilkes said was 

John Wilkes

Arrested

against the law. Many others had been arrested before on the same form of paper, but Wilkes was the first man who claimed that this was a method of taking from the people their liberties. He was imprisoned in the Tower of London, and when the people took up his cause, the Court ruled that the King and his ministers had no right to arrest or imprison any one with that form of warrant. He was released and from that time no Englishman could be arrested except by the law of the land. 

The King was very angry and would not give up; so he declared Wilkes an outlaw and had Parliament dismiss him. The way this was

Wilkes fled to France but soon returned to England. 

The people again elected him to Parliament. Again he was dismissed. This happened four times in a number of years. Wilkes was imprisoned for twentytwo months and during all this time the people came to his assistance and showed the King and his ministers that they were in favor of him because he was fighting for their rights. This continued for eleven years, and in the end the King had to give up and permit the people to choose the man they wanted to serve them in Parliament, for this was their lawful right.

in which a small band of patriotic Americans, chiefly the undisciplined, the youthful and the aged, spared by inefficiency from the distant ranks of the republic, led by Col. Zebulon Butler and Col. Nathan Denison, with a courage that deserved success, fearlessly met and bravely fought combined British, Tory and Indian Force of thrice their number.
