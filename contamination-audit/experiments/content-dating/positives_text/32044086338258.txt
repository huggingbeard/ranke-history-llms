It was not the intention of the author to write a complete biography of Jona than Fisher but to gather together rec ords of things connected with him which would be of interest to his descendants. 

The result has been this collection of copies of letters, diaries, paintings and other material, and also this brief biog raphy. A complete biography, a truly monumental work, is being prepared by 

Mary Ellen Chase, the title of which will be "Jonathan Fisher, Maine Coun try Parson."

We are indeed fortunate that this work should be done by so gifted a writer as Miss Chase. It so happens that she was born in the very town where he had his pastorate, and this fact would add a personal touch to her work. 

We may rest assured that justice will be done to the story of "this amazing man, as she calls him.

There is a perfect wealth of material from which to obtain information re garding his life history, consisting of diaries, letters and miscellaneous material of all kinds. It is hardly conceivable that a record of any man's life could be more complete. I quote now from a chapter from Mary Ellen Chase's book "A 

Goodly Heritage" written some years ago: "No sketch of the religious environment which I knew as a child can be complete or even accurate without some presentation of the Rev. Jonathan Fisher.

His was a personality manifold, trenchant, permanent. Although he died forty years before I was born, his influence remained with us, half sinister, half auspicious, always engrossing and relentless. It can hardly be given to stamp a community so indelibly as he stamped that of his adoption. Nor was that impress left only about the confines of Blue Hill Bay. He was known in

Bangor where he was a trustee of the Theological Seminary; in Boston and in his college in Cambridge. In the second volume of Sprague's Annals of the American Pulpit, a volume like the first given to the biographies of distinguished 

Congregational clergymen of the Trini tarian persuasion, his life and works hold honoured place. . "His genius was indeed a multifarious one, mechanical, financial, intellectual, social, spiritual. His energy, as well must be, was exhaustless. Once arrived in

Blue Hill with his young wife, he began the erection of a substantial and beautiful home. The design was his own, even to the minutest details; much of the actual building he did himself. During an evening walk he discovered yellow ochre on a portion of the land alloted to him as settlement and was freed from any further anxiety on the score of paint. Practically every piece of furniture he made with his own hands. He constructed a clock which ran for fifty years without repair and with no attention save winding. He built the first windmill in town, and to be served by it a machine for sawing wood. . . . He was, of course, a farmer, raising practically all the food which his family of nine children consumed. He was also the surveyor for the community, utiliz ing his knowledge of mathematics to accomplish the work made possible by the surveying instruments which he invented. He found leisure to adorn the walls of his dwelling with pictures, to paint a portrait of himself as he sat before a mirror, and to paint also a picture of Harvard College as it was in his days as a student. "His full expenses during the five of his seven years spent at Cambridge, including clothing and books, amounted to 605 dollars. He gave liberally to the early charities of his pastorate and managed at the same time to send several of his daughters to boarding school and his two sons who grew to manhood to 

Bowdoin and Princeton. [Correction:

"His intellectual pursuits were never allowed to suffer from the abundance of manuel labours. His Hebrew lexicon, still in manuscript, remains the great achievement of his life. . . . "His social ministrations became early inseparable from his spiritual; he made himself, in fact, alike the arbiter of spir itual destinies, and the referee of all social upheavals. His two sermons on 

Sunday (for the members of the congregation in accordance with the custom of the day ate their lunch in the church or church-yard) seem in comparison, the least of his duties. From his papers we gather that upon his arrival he found his parishioners sadly tending towards Arminianism, and he drew up a coherent statement of belief consistent with his own Calvinism, which statement he induced, perhaps better coerced them to sign." 

To R. G. F. Candage, likewise a native of Blue Hill, I am indebted for an appreciative account of Jonathan Fisher's life. He published a Biographical Sketch from which the following quotations are taken: "In 1796, Rev. Jonathan Fisher, by vote of the town and church, the town parish and church then being one, was settled as the minister, upon the following terms, viz.: He to have a minister's lot of three hundred acres of wild land, two hundred dollars in cash and a barn thirty by forty feet as a settlement. For annual salary, two hundred dollars in cash, the clearing of five acres of land,` the cutting and hauling of thirty cords. of wood, for the first five years, after that he was to have two hundred and fifty dollars in cash, the cutting and hauling of thirty cords of wood annually, and a vacation of five Sundays each year. "It was under the preaching of that remarkable man, the first settled minister of the town, that the writer sat for sev eral years of his boyhood. . . . "In 1795 he received an invitation to preach at Blue Hill, and did so from 

July to November, when the church and town gave him a call to settle as their minister. He accepted the call and in the Spring of 1796 went to Blue Hill, where on July 13th he was ordained and spent the rest of his life as a zealous, faithful and successful laborer in the ser vice of his Divine Master. "He was pastor of the Blue Hill church forty-one years (1796-1837) and then owing, as he stated, to infirmities of age, he desired to resign his pastorate.

For the remaining ten years of his life his time was spent in preaching occa sionally, writing, study, painting (for he was an artist) and in such labor on his farm as health and opportunity per mitted, being to the very last a prodigy of industry. "On Sept. 22, 1847, at the age of nearly seventy-nine, his spirit winged its flight to the presence of Him whom he had faithfully served from early man. hood. "The people of his parish and friends erected a granite shaft to his memory on the spot where his body lies buried, upon which was chisled the motto he had chosen, 'Know Thyself', his name, date of birth, date of death, and that he was pastor of the Congregational Church for forty-one years.

After the death of her husband she supported herself by weaving and many yards of cloth came from her loom. A record of her work, kept by herself, has come down to us: from 1778 to 1822, a period of forty-five years, 9,138 yards.

There existed a strong bond of attachment between him and his mother; he honored her highly and valued her judg ment.

Of his wife, Dolly Battelle (or Bat tle), not much accurate information is available. She passes through the picture as a minister's wife and mother of nine children. I do find, however, the follow ing reference to her made by Major 

Charles W. Stevens in 1897: "An excellent wife and mother, cheerful of disposition and of most amiable character, shared the home duties and ministrations." As Major Stevens was one of her grandchildren, we may assume that he was writing from personal memory of her. 

Jonathan kept a "Common Journal" all his life, but his entries were almost entirely confined to the recording of every-day events. We have, however, a source of knowledge regarding his pri vate life namely, his letters. All his more important ones were copied and bound into books. We find one dated 

May 7, 1797, to his friend Popkin, writ ten in what we may call school-book 

French, quote: "Depuis j'étais marié j'ai été le plus heureux des hommes" and "Notre foyer est le foyer de bonheur," by which we are given to understand that he had been the happiest of men since his marriage, and that their fireside was the fireside of felicity. There is every reason to believe that their's was a happy union.

Reference has been made to his habit of walking, and I and I quote from the "Sketches of my Life", an entry of 

November 21, 1800. These books were made from the contents of his small "Common Journals", somewhat abbreviated. This quotation is interesting, for it had been augmented by a note written some twenty-four years later: "On the twenty first returned wet and weary from Union River. Many such walks I have had between that time and the present (July, 1824) and probably feel somewhat more of the decays of nature on account of them than otherwise I should. I have never possessed a horse, nor does riding commonly agree well with my health. I hope my labors, the sometimes painful, have not been in vain. I may remember that Jesus Christ, my Divine Master, went about on foot long before me.'

It is not in the province of this sketch to pass upon the merits of his sermons, three thousand of them. Students of theological history are conversant with the dogmas and tenets taught at Harvard College in 1792, and which he absorbed. 

It may be truthfully said that anyone reading them at the present time would come across many beautiful and inspir ing thoughts, and would appreciate his fine gift of language. Although he be lieved in the torments of "the lake which burns with fire and brimstone," and referred to them in no uncertain

manner, he seemingly did not dwell unduly upon this phase of his belief. The writer read more than twenty of his later sermons and future punishment was not much emphasized; he dwelt chiefly upon the love and goodness of God and of Jesus Christ, and upon the advantages of living a righteous life, and proclaimed a future life of happiness. 

Regarding his interest in secular matters, the following quotation from one of his sermons, written in 1835, gives his views on the troublesome question of slavery: "The slavery which exists in a great part of this nation is a serious evil and never will the happy suns of the millenial period shine upon it till this evil is removed. It is an evil deeply felt by very many slave-holders themselves, but is so circumstanced that for the present they know not what to do to relieve themselves of it. They cannot do it without the concurrance of others who at present are not willing to concur. character. It is undoubtedly true that his reputation among the townspeople at large was that of a rather harsh and not very lovable man. In this they were probably correct if he were to be judged by the standards of kindliness and affa bility only. His manner was grave and earnest, and as such would not endear him, for example, to the children. His more sterling virtues, however, must have been appreciated by many who accepted him as their spiritual guide and chose him for their pastor for over forty years. 

His mind was so filled with the impor tance of religion that it did not have much room for small talk. His attitude of mind toward young people may be seen in the following quotation: "My dear young friends you may think me hard of heart, untender and unkind, but I do desire the eternal welfare of your souls."

He dearly liked to talk on religious subjects when making his parochial vis its, though sometimes he did not receive the whole-hearted response that he de sired. After one such round of visits he penned the following thoughts: "Hard it is to get near the hearts of people of fashion, partly through want of a tongue in me and partly through want of ears in them." And again: "I have found much the greatest satis faction with those in the humbler sta tion of life." 

"By the constitution of the U. S. A. 

the management of the concern of slav ery within its own limits is left to the legislature of each state itself. Any individual has a right to treat upon the subject of slavery, and to point out its nature and tendency, if it be done in a discreet and becoming manner, but he has no right to interfere directly with the proceedings of the slave-holding states. Individuals of the non-slaveholding states have of late been pointing out freely what they consider to be the evils of slavery. In some cases it has been well done; but in many other cases 

I am satisfied that there has been indiscreet zeal on the part of persons, whose motives I would by no means impeach. 

Without intending evil, they have been the occasion of an excitement, which to me appears already to endanger the peace and unity of the nation, and to threaten us with the horrors of a civil war."

Jona than Fisher, Pastor of the Congrega tional Church in Blue Hill, Maine; in terspersed with extracts from his jour nals, written by himself. Commenced writing these sketches this day, Feb. 20, 1812. 

A sketch of my parentage I have already given. I was born as above noted, Oct. 7, 1768. The place of my nativity was New Braintree, near the center of Massachusetts proper. Here my father lived with his father till after the birth of his fourth child, my sister

Mary. He then removed with his family to the west part of Northampton, now 

West Hampton, in the Spring of 1773. 

I had then entered my fifth year. I still retain fresh in memory a number of things which happened before this removal. The earliest trace of my recol lection is that of being drawn by my young Aunt Sally Fisher on a pillow in a little four wheel cart, across the highway into an orchard, where I well remember she put some apples into the cart with me, among which were some large red ones. From the connected cir cumstances I think I must have been about two years old when this happened. 

Here it may be remarked that those occasional events in the course of Providence which vary considerably from the common currents of our lives, are like way marks to the traveller and greatly assist us in the recollection of scenes ry me, past. . . Hence it may be viewed as a favorable providence, that at some what distant periods of our lives we should experience interesting 'changes.

The view of past events is generally a pleasing one, and may be in many cases very profitable. . . . The second incident of my childhood, of which I have distinct remembrance, happened when I was about two years and seven or eight months old. It was a fall from a high garden wall, the mark of which will go to the grave with me; from the top of the wall I fell, head first upon a stone, which cut hole in the flesh of my forehead. It was in the Spring, for I distinctly remember seeing near when I fell, a number of young shoots of asparagus. I remember being in petticoats, and going towards the house with the blood running down to my feet. . .

I remember being carried once, when quite a child, to meeting, in a new suit of another color, at which time I felt a degree of elation, the fruit, no doubt, of the pride which operates at an early period in every human heart.--Such are several of the incidences which hap pened while I was in New Braintree. 

Were my memory as retentive now as in my early days I might well esteem it a great privilege; but for wise reasons

God ordains that in advanced life that power of attention should decay; it may remind us of that change by death, to which old age must bring us if no calamatous event should anticipate its arrival.

As far as I can judge, I can to this day commit a thing to memory in as short a time as ever, but without unwearied pains to retain it, I soon lose it. I doubt not however, but when the cumbrous clay of this body is thrown off, the soul will have a very clear and quick recollection of all which has passed it. 

Four years of my life were passed in this place (Westhampton). My father's house was but a cottage containing one room, and built of hewn logs, with a stone chimney. With food we were well

provided, and generally had a competency of clothing, tho sometimes we were in rags, and one winter, myself, and my brothers and sisters were with out shoes, and our bare feet were daily accustomed to the snow. My father's cottage I trust however was the habita tion of piety and contentment. . . . . . . My father freuently took a leading part in the service. To these meetings in the simplicity of the times, we were drawn in winter in a kind of sleigh, and for a horse we had a tract able well governed ox; this was then esteemed laudable; but such appearance in the present refined state of society would hardly be tolerable. The change of customs in different places and periods. is such that prudence requires the most humble, wise and good to avoid some things which in themselves are innocent. 

Necessity, however has no law, and duty must never yield to fashion. "Step by step, often very gradually, the blessed God furnishes the mind with the knowledge of divine things, and prepares it for the day of his power, when it is his will to renew the heart, and causes the soul to abound in the love of divine things, as well as the understanding of them. "In the fall of 1776 my father was called and took part as a Lieutenant in the Revolutionary war war with Great

Britain. His term of service commenced with the first of December; on the tenth of March 1777, a few days after the term of his service had expired he departed this life, in comfortable hope of a better, at Morristown in the state of 

New Jersey. "My uncle Avery, with whom I was put live at Holden was generally of good natural abilities, improved by a liberal education; but not popular as a public speaker, nor as far as I can remember, very distinguishing in the doc trines of grace; yet I trust a man of piety. He was in good measure faithful to train me up in the way wherein I should walk, and weekly taught me the assembly's catechism, till the whole of it was well fixed in my memory. . . . "Between the years of ten and fifteen of my age, I began to exhibit some traces of mechanical genius; and a turn for mathematics; spending my leisure time in making buttons, brooches, windmills, snares, traps, purling sticks and the like, and in solving various questions in mathematics; sometimes drawing with a pin on a smooth board, and sometimes on a slate, which led the way afterward to a small measure of proficiency in sketching and painting. "My mother's desire was that I should have a public education, nor was I averse to it, provided I could obtain it, but I saw no prospect of being ever able to defray the expense of it, and appre hended, that should I set out for it, I should fail, and have a smattering in many trades, without perfection in any.

Through my mother's advice and en couraging words I came at length to the determination to pursue my studies; and some time, either in my seventeenth or eighteenth year, I cannot say which, I was permitted to lay aside work, and follow study constantly. After a while my studies became pleasant and my les sons were learned with considerable ease; but in the mean time intervals were stolen, in some sort, from the study of languages, and spent in reading, making figures in geometry, dialing, etc; on this account I made more speed in learning a lesson, than in passing through a book; to this day my propensity is to a similar course. The Short-hand of Jonathan Fisher. 

The following is a short description of his code shorthand, which consists of about three hundred characters all told. Some of the characters are descriptive; for example for mountain; O for foundation; D for moon; A for house; which is modified thus A to mean dweller. Then we find some fanciful ones such as for voice; and for rejoice. Stand is and understand ; up and down are U and . Most of the signs are however purely arbitrary, and many are for words used especially in Scriptural language, such as ~ for grace; for sanctify and for Jerusalem. The prefixes and suffixes are useful, such as; for con; f for tion; 2 for ing, etc. The basic spelling is in a phonetic alphabet of his own invention.

On another page is shown an extract from one of his sermons with the accompanying code symbols. The use of this code was obviously a great convenience.

SERMON NO. 2549. 

JONATHAN FISHER APPENDIX 

Bluehill, Feb. 19, 1825. 

Dan. 5:6-Then the king's countenance was changed, and his thoughts troubled him, so that the joints of his loins were loosed, and his knees smote one against another. 

It has pleased God sometimes to awaken the stupid conscience of some hardened sinner by some unusually some unusually alarming event of providence, and in ancient times by miraculous interposi tion. An instance of this nature was that to which our text relates.-Belshazaar, king of Babylon, knew that God had in a wonderful way humbled the pride of his father Nebucadnezaar, that he had deprived him of his reason seven years, and made him as the beasts of the field, that at the end of seven years his reason was restored, and that he ac knowledged the true God, and praised, extoled, and honored him. But notwithstanding he knew all this, he ran into the same course of pride, that brought such a rebuke upon his father, and became more wicked than his father had been, and lifted up himself against the 

Lord of heaven, threw contempt upon the sacred vessels of his holy temple by drinking wine in them at an impious revel, and praising gods of silver and gold, brass, wood, and stone. The knowledge of the true God had been brought to his mind, and was lodged in his memory, so that it might be recalled, it was now out of view; and his conscience was in a deep sleep.-By a miraculous interposition his sleeping conscience was roused; he was so hardened in wickedness that probably no admonition from the lips or hand of man would have made any impression upon him but to excite his displeasure; but when it was now evening, when the lamp was lighted, and shone upon the plaster of the wall of the palace over against where he sat, he was led to lift up his eyes, at that instant he saw the appearance of a hand, writing upon the wall; he plainly saw it, and knew that the hand was not human, and when it was removed, he saw an inscription in visible characters; but characters which he could not read.-Though he was king of Babylon, a great city, raised to great glory and majesty by 

Nebucadnezaar his father, and tho his dominion was over a great kingdom; tho' he felt secure against human force within the stupendous walls of the city; tho' surrounded by a thousand of his Lords, or Princes, tho' his heart was exceedingly hardened, and tho' a moment before with a stupified conscience he was throwing contempt upon the God of Israel, the sight of this hand and of the inscription of it left behind roused his conscience, the faithful monitor brought suddenly to view his daring impiety, in addition to all his other wickedness; and tho' he yet knew not the meaning, nor interpretation of the writing, he was filled with awful forebodings of the just judgment of the 

God of Israel hastening upon him. He became pale, his soul was thrown into a tumult, the joints of his loins were loosed, he trembled, his knees smote together! Yes, the proud king of Babylon, who a little before was ready to defy the Creator of heaven and earth, now trembled like the poor culprit, that is dragged forth to execution.-There was reason to tremble, his suspicion that the writing might contain his doom was too true; could he have read it, he would have seen at once his doom denounced. 

The writing was, MENE, MENE, TEKEL, UPHARSIN.-The wise men of Babylon could neither read nor interpret it; this put him and his lords into a state of suspense, and threw them all into a state of trouble and astonishment. 

Why the wise men of Babylon could not read the writing is to us unknown; the

Jewish Rabbis say it was because it was written in the old Hebrew, or Phoeni 13

APPENDIX 

cian characters; which the Bablonians did not understand. But the words were 

Chaldee; and their literal meaning this, He hath numbered, he hath numbered; he hath weighed; and they divided.

When the literal meaning of the words were known, still there was need of a 

Daniel to make the particular applica tion; it was this, God hath numbered thy kingdom and hath finished it; thou art weighed in the balance and art found wanting. Thy kingdom is divided and given to the Medes and Persians. In the case of this wicked monarch, his conscience was at this time awakened, but to anticipate his everlasting destruction; it awoke, it made him tremble, but too late for repentance. In the midst of the impious feast, Sirius, having turned the course of the Euphrates, which ran under the walls of the city, had already marched his army through its channel into the heart of the city, and before morning Belshazaar was slain and went into the presence of that God, whose holy vessels he had just been profaning to receive the dreadful weight of his righteous anticipated doom. 

Some of you, my hearers, may now feel strong in your own confidence, you may be without fear; but should 

God let loose your whole conscience upon you, how weak, how pusilanimous you would be! Your spirits would be broken down like the hedge that has been broken by the snows of winter; do be persuaded even now to make your peace with God.

SERMON NO. 806. 

JONATHAN FISHER 

Bluehill, Nov. 18, 1804. 

My dear impetinent hearers: you are already launched for eternity. Your being is begun and it can never be extinguished. Each soul of man is a spark, which is kindled never to be put out.- 

From this time endless ages forth you must endure. Souls never die. Here it is certain that you have no continuing place; one after another your souls will take their flight into an unseen world. 

That there is a God all nature declares; and he must be holy, for he has planted a conscience within us which often ar raigns to his tribunal; a principle by which we may discern a difference, an essential difference between right and wrong.... The plan for the redemp tion of miserable man flows from the eternal wisdom of God. He has revealed it in his word: the blessed Bible is the treasury of this information. Do you believe, Oh my hearers, the sacred pages? 

Reject them, and you plunge into a mist of worse than Egyptian darkness. Reject them and your hazard is awful; strong as the grounds are to believe them to be solemn truth, all the evidence in their favor is directly so much evidence that if you reject them finally, you will be compelled to take up your abode in the lake which burns with fire and brim stone. No less than that do they threaten all unbelievers; and the evidence of their truth is various, copious and stable as mountains. The marks of divinity are stamped upon the face of them, their scope is against wickedness; they give it no good names; yet they reveal mercy for the penitent sinner. The curt lan guage of the Scripture is full of majesty and beautiful simplicity. They teach that we are dying creatures; observation teaches the same. They declare that we have here no continuing city; if we be lieve our senses we must know it. They reveal the wrath of God against all un righteousness. Blessed be God the same Scriptures bring life and a happy immortality to light for all who render them unfeigned obedience. They reveal 

Jesus Christ, a glorious Mediator be tween God and man, through whose obedience and suffering pardon and peace may be obtained. . . . The Scrip tures abundantly declare that the present life is the only state of probation; that at the time of natural death our doom is irrevocably fixed. The date of the pres ent life is not only short but very un 14

APPENDIX 

certain; what is more so? How sudden and unexpected many times is the stroke? 

Oh my impenitent hearers, the short uncertain space between this and the day of your death is the only period in which you are to transfix if ever, the most important concern of a long eternity. This is the only time in which to decide whether you shall hereafter be accommodated with a most convenient, delightful and glorious habitation, or dwell forever a miserable exile from the pres ence of God. Riches and poverty, life and death are set before you in the gospel; choose you this day which shall be your portion. Count the cost and choose wisely. If you choose the portion of the righteous you must expect selfdenial and affliction in this life; for it said in the word of truth that all they who live godly in Christ Jesus shall suffer persecution. If, says the Savior, they have called the master of the house 

Beelzebub, much more will they call those of his household, and again, marvel not if the world hate you, ye know that it hated me before it hated you.

POEMS 

Jonathan Fisher was a very prolific writer of poetry, and a small volume of his verses was published in Portland in 

1834. The following poem was written in May, 1792, while he was still at College. It seems to be partly a study in versification as the stanzas all differ from each other in construction.

The Morning 

For the evening mists are fled, Damps with Eastern winds are gone,

Smiling from his watery bed 

Rises now the morning sun: 

O'er the lovely plain I go, Earth is green 

The sky serene,

Music warbles, zephyrs blow. 

Wake ye sluggards, come and see 

Nature in her gay attire; 

Waste no more the morning hour, Slaves to sleep's enchanting power: 

Come, and hear the feathered choir 

And trace the fields with me. 

Hark what morning songs arise, Nature brings her choicest praise,

Warbling birds attune their lays 

To Him, who framed the skies. 

Nodding flowers and whispering gales, Lofty hills and humble vales

Purling streams and waving trees, Mountains vast and rolling seas, Earth and heaven their powers combine 

To exalt a name divine. 

Blessed spirit now descend. 

Teach me how my voice to raise. 

Teach my lips angelic lays: 

With glowing heart my soul inspire, Kindle there celestial fire, Fill my breast with pure desire; 

And then till time shall end,

Immanuel's name 

Shall be my theme 

And all my labor praise.

My God, my Savior, 'tis my humble plea, That Thou dost take my wayward heart in hand, And mold and frame it as Thyself may see 

Thy work requires a human heart to be;
