NOTE 

We are told that some of the stars which we now see are many light-years distant, that is, the radiance which we nightly behold started centuries ago. 

So in the lives of saintly men. They may have lived ten, an hundred, a thousand years gone by, but the light of their lives constantly remains with us now. 

And so of "Father Nash", one of those devoted Soldiers and Servants of our Blessed Lord, who, here, and there, and yonder, laid the foundations on which rest stately structures of to-day.

1763 In all ages of the Church there have been prophets, leaders and martyrs who were conspicuous by virtue of place or spiritual stature or because of the historical importance of the era in which they lived. Their names are written in the kalendar of saints and commemorated in liturgies; churches are placed under their patronage and buildings adorned with their statues. 

All honor to these mighty warriors for Christ and His Church! "Let us now praise famous men".

But there are multitudes of others, no less faithful and holy, who in lowlier position and obscurer days, have preached and prayed, striven and suffered, for the same Master. Even in their lifetime there were not many who knew and loved them, and in our careless generation they are but dimly remembered or utterly unknown. It is one such that we are recalling to memory. 

Who, then, was "Father Nash"?

The question, if it was then a question, was answered eighty years ago by the Rev. John N. Norton, A. M., in a little volume published by the "General Protestant Episcopal Sunday School Union and Church Book Society" in 1859. Dr. Norton could then write: "There have been few Churchmen who have lived within the limits of the State of New York, at any period during the last sixty years who were not familiar with the name of 'FATHER NASH.' A poor man, and one of the humblest of the Lord's servants, he has earned for himself a reputation which many titled dignitaries might be proud to enjoy."

Daniel Nash was born at Great Barrington (then called 

Housatonic) May 28, 1763, the son of Jonathan Nash and Anna

Maria Spencer, being descended from Lieut. Timothy Nash, born 

1676, a fact which may partly account for Father Nash's vigorous life as a faithful Soldier and Servant in the Church Militant. 

The subject of our sketch was graduated from Yale College in 1785, in the same class with Noah Webster. For nine years he was Principal of an Academy, at Pittsgrove, and afterward at Swedesborough, in New Jersey.

Originally a Congregationalist it is not known precisely when he became a Churchman. Later he used to say, "You may bray a Presbyterian as with a mortar, and you cannot get all his Presbyterianism out of him", but, "I was caught young."

At any rate, he became a communicant of the Church, studied under the Rev. John Croes, afterward the first Bishop of New Jersey, married Miss Olive Lusk, was ordained Deacon by Bishop Provoost, Feb. 8, 1797, and Priest by Bishop Benjamin Moore in September of 1801, and served vigorously for thirty-seven years.

We are told that it was at the suggestion of Dr. Burhans, who had made several missionary journeys through Otsego and adjoining counties, that Daniel Nash both applied for Holy Orders, and offered himself for the missionary work of the Church in Otsego and Chenango counties, instructing and baptizing many, ministering to white people and to Indians, visiting from house to house, and founding numerous parishes, during his long term of service in central New York. He is considered as the founder of the Church in what is now a portion of the diocese of Albany.

One glimpse of Father Nash is from the pen of Bishop 

Chase, who wrote of a visit in 1799: "The writer does not pretend to more sensibility than falls to the lot of most men, but there was something in this meeting between Mr. Nash and himself of a peculiar character, and

calculated to call forth whatever of sensibility he possessed. It was a meeting of two persons deeply convinced of the primitive Apostolic foundation of the Church to which, on account of its purity of doctrine and the Divine right of its ministry, they had fled from a chaos of confusion of other (sic) sects. They were both 'missionaries', though the name was not yet understood or appreciated. The one had given up all hopes of more comfortable living in the well stocked country at the East; and had come to Otsego county to preach the Gospel and build up the Church on Apostolic ground, with no assurance of a salary but such as he could glean from the cold soil of unrenewed nature, or pluck from the cluster of the few scions which he might engraft into the vine Christ Jesus. He lived not in a tent, as the patriarchs did, surrounded with servants to tend his flocks and milk his kine, ‘and bring him butter in a lordly dish'; but in a cabin, built of unhewn logs, with scarcely a pane of glass to let in light sufficient to read his Bible; and even this cabin was not his own, nor was he permitted to live in one for a long time together. All this was witnessed by the other who came to see him, and helped him to carry away his little articles of crockery, holding one handle of the basket, and Mr. Nash the other, and as they walked the road, 'talked of the things pertaining to the kingdom of God.' 

That man, "The writer cannot refrain from tears in bringing to mind the circumstances attending this interesting scene. who was most emphatically called 'Father Nash', being the founder of the Church in Otsego county-who baptized great numbers of both adults and children, and thus was the spiritual father of so many of the family of Christ, and who spent his life in toiling for their spiritual benefit-was so little regarded by the Church at large, and even by his neighbors, that he had not the means to move his substance from one cabin to another, but with his own hands, assisted by his wife and small children, and a passing missionary. Well does the writer remember how the little one-roomed cabin looked as he entered it, its rude door, hung on wooden hinges, creaking as they turned; how joyful that good man was that he had been mindful to bring a

few nails which he had used in the other cabin just left, for his comfort in this, now the receptacle of all his substance. These he drove into the logs with great judgment, choosing the place most appropriate for his hat, his coat, and other garments of himself and his family. All the while his patient wife, directing the children to kindle the fire, prepared the food for? whom? 

Shall it be said a stranger? No! but for one who, by sympathy felt himself more their brother than by all the ties of nature; and who, by the example now set before him, learned a lesson of inexpressible use to him all the days of his subsequent life.” 

CATECHISING THE YOUNG 

"Everyone who has heard of Mr. Nash, is acquainted with his faithfulness in the discharge of that most important duty,catechising the young. It is said of him that so great was his devotion to this mode of instruction, that when, on his missionary travels, he met children on the road belonging to his extensive and scattered charge, he would stop and examine them on the spot. On a certain occasion, when a number of clergymen were assembled for some purpose, and conversation began to flag, one of them, who was almost too good a farmer for the good of the Church, entertained the company with an account of his agricultural operations, and, among other things, of his successful management of sheep. "Father Nash, whose heart was entirely devoted to his Master's work, felt little interest in all this; and when the enthusiastic farmer-parson turned to him, and asked, 'What do you feed your lambs with, Mr. Nash?' the worthy missionary could not resist the temptation of administering a mild rebuke, and answered, 'With Catechism!"

REVIVAL SYSTEM AND YOUNG BEES 

When this system was adopted by various denominations Mr. Nash was wont to say that converts reminded him of little humble bees, which were rather larger when hatched than they were sometimes afterward. "His reference was to the ento-

mological fact, that young bees have a sort of feathery bur about them when hatched, which is soon shed, thus exposing their bodies in their native littlenesses."

FATHER NASH AND JAMES FENIMORE COOPER 

"The great American novelist thus speaks of his [Father Nash's] first service in Cooperstown, in his chronicles of that place: 'On the 10th day of September, 1800, Miss Cooper, the eldest daughter of Judge Cooper*, a young lady in her twentythird year, was killed by a fall from her horse. Her funeral services were preached by the Rev. Daniel Nash, of the Protestant Episcopal Church; and she was interred according to the rites of that Church, which were now performed for the first time in this village. Mr. Nash, so well known in his own Church for his Apostolic simplicity, under the name of 'Father Nash', was then a missionary in the county. From that time he began to extend his services to Cooperstown; and on the first day of 

January, 1811, a church was legally organized, under the name of Christ's Church, Cooperstown. On the same day the Rev. 

Daniel Nash was chosen rector, which office, through the delicacy of the clergyman who succeeded him in its duties, he personally held down to the day of his death, in 1836."

FATHER NASH AND THE INDIANS 

James Fenimore Cooper portrayed Father Nash in his tale of "The Pioneers" under the name of "Mr. Grant" and describes his dealing with the Indians thus: "It was Christmas-eve! Mr. Grant is stopping at the house of Judge Temple, the principal man of the parish. The clergyman is conversing with a converted Indian, who bears the name of the beloved disciple, 'John'. "John', said the divine, when the figure of Judge Temple had disappeared, the last of the group, 'to-morrow is the festival of the nativity of our blessed Redeemer, when the Church has *Sister of James Fenimore Cooper.

appointed prayers and thanksgiving to be offered up by her children, and when all are invited to partake of the mystical elements. As you have taken up the cross, and become a follower of good, and an eschewer of evil, John, I trust I shall see you before the altar, with a contrite heart and a meek spirit.' "John will come', said the Indian, betraying no surprise, though he did not understand all the terms used by the other. 66 'Yes,' continued Mr. Grant, laying his hand on the tawny shoulder of the aged chief,' but it is not enough to be there in the body only; you must come in the spirit and in truth. The 

Redeemer died for all, for the poor Indian, as well as for the white man. Heaven knows no difference in color, nor must earth witness a separation of the Church. It is good and profitable, John, to freshen the understanding, and support the wavering, by the observance of our holy festivals; but all form is but stench in the nostrils of the Holy One unless it is accompained by a devout and humble spirit.' "The Indian stepped back a little, and raising his body to its utmost height of erection, he stretched his right hand on high, and dropped his finger downward, as if pointing from the heavens, and, striking his other hand on his naked breast, he said, with energy,-"The eye of the Great Spirit can see from the clouds the bosom of the Mohegan is bare.' 999 "On one of his visits to the Oneida Indians, about 1830, when he preached by an interpreter, he had occasion to speak of the duty of everyone being more careful to see and correct his own faults than those of others. He expressed this by the following figure: That each one should be careful to keep his own garden thoroughly weeded out, and not busy himself much with the weeds of his neighbor's garden. The interpreter, suiting the action to the words in a very ludicrous manner, stooped down, and with his hands and fingers made the motions as if pulling

weeds, which at the moment seemed almost too much for the preacher's gravity."

MINISTERING TO WHITE AMERICANS 

It is difficult for one not brought up in the Church to comprehend the intense prejudice against it, even fifty years ago, at least in rural regions, largely on three grounds,—that it was pro-English, Romish, and merely formal. Father Nash's methods in meeting such is illustrated in this description of a Church service in a country house of worship, quoted also by 

Mr. Norton from "The Pioneers": "Benches, made in the coarsest manner and only with a view to usefulness were arranged in rows, for the reception of the congregation; while a rough, unpainted box was placed against the wall, in the center of the length of the apartment, as an apology for a pulpit. Something like a reading-desk was in front of this rostrum; and a small mahogany table, from the mansion house, covered with a spotless damask cloth, stood a little on one side, by way of an altar. Branches of pine and hemlocks were stuck in each of the fissures that offered, in the unseasoned and hastily completed wood-work of both the building and its furniture, while festoons and hieroglyphics met the eye, in vast profusion, along the brown sides of the scratch-. coated walls. "As the room was only lighted by some ten or fifteen miserable candles, and the windows were without shutters, it would have been a dreary, cheerless place for the solemnities of a Christmas-eve, had not the large fire, that was crackling at each end of the apartment, given an air of cheerfulness to the scene, by throwing an occasional glare through the vistas of bushes and faces. The two sexes were separated by an area in the center of the room immediately before the pulpit, and a few benches lined this space, that were occupied by the principal personages of the village and its vicinity. This distinction was rather a gratuitous concession, made by the poorer and less polished part of the population, than a right claimed by the favored few.

"Mr. Grant now arose, and announced and commenced the service with the sublime declaration of the Hebrew prophet- "The Lord is in His holy temple: let all the earth keep silence before Him.' The example of Mr. Jones was unnecessary to teach the people to rise: the solemnity of the manner of the divine effected this as by magic. After a short pause, Mr. 

Grant proceeded with the solemn and winning exhortation of this service. "When the clergyman bent his knees in prayer and confession, the congregation so far imitated his example as to resume their seats; whence no succeeding effort of the divine, during the evening, was able to remove them in a body. Some rose at times, but by far the larger part remained unbending,observant, it is true, but it was the kind of observation that regarded the ceremony as a spectacle, rather than a worship in which all were to participate. "The long experience of Mr. Grant had admirably qualified him to perform with success his present duty. He well understood the character of his listeners, who were mostly a primitive people in their habits; and who, being a good deal addicted to subtleties and nice distinctions in their religious opinions, viewed the introduction into their spiritual worship of any such temporal assistance as form, not only with jealousy, but frequently with disgust. He had acquired much of his knowledge from studying the great book of human nature as it lay open in the world; and knowing how dangerous it was to contend with ignorance, uniformly endeavored to avoid dictating, when his better reason taught him it was the most prudent to attempt to lead. "In the present instance he yielded, in many places, to the prejudices of his congregation, and when he had ended, there was not one of his new hearers who did not think the ceremonies less papal and offensive, and more conformant to his or her notions of devout worship, than they had been led to expect from a service of forms."

The compiler of this brief selection of passages from Mr. 

Norton's life of Father Nash, may, perhaps, be permitted to express the opinion that seldom has the situation thus described been expressed with such fine discrimination as here by James Fenimore Cooper. Our Puritan ancestors were, indeed, “addicted to subtleties and nice distinctions in their religious opinions". In this they were anything but dull, and could debate, intelligently, the difference between Calvinism and Arminianism "From morn to noon, from noon till dewy eve."

Their statuesque rigidity, when present at a Prayer Book Service, was probably due to shyness, as well as to fear of bowing themselves in the House of Rimmon. They did not know what to do in an Episcopal service.

And such tactfulness as that of Father Nash, based on love, was no doubt one reason why the missionaries of the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in Lands beyond the Sea (the "S. P. G.") were so rapidly winning people to the Church, until the storm of the Revolution burst upon the Colonies. 

FATHER NASH AND HIS FAMILY 

Mr. Norton makes but brief mention of this relation. Father 

Nash's beloved wife, eight years younger then himself, died seventeen years before her sainted husband. She may well have been worn out by the hardships of their primitive life. 

But it is certain that he was not one of those husbands and fathers who are so absorbed in their work that they have no time left to love their families. From the scant references made it is evident that their family life was one of rare beauty, devotion, and happiness. In a letter to a brother clergyman he wrote: "I never felt discouraged, neither did I feel alone. My wife was then living, a noble-spirited, sensible woman, who, in the room of feeling discouraged, was the first to cheer me on in my arduous labors. "The country was then comparatively a wilderness. Often she gave me a child, and then got on the horse behind me, with

another in her arms, and then we would go to our public worship for a number of miles. She excelled in music, and I understood it well, and we were never confounded in that part of the service; and when the congregation did not understand well how to make the responses, she always did it in a solemn, dignified manner." 

A PROPHETIC TOAST 

Mr. Norton quotes from a friend who wrote: "Perhaps you may not have heard of Father Nash's toast. Two bishops of our Church had been consecrated at the same time and place, the younger of whom, in the estimation of the consecrator, was the senior by the date of his Orders, or his doctorate, or both; so he laid hands on him first, which gave him priority in the line of presiding Bishops. Not long afterward, Father Nash, being in a company of clergymen, when the propriety of what the consecrating Bishop had decided in the matter was discussed, gave his sense of the case by the following toast. Naming the senior in age, he said, 'May he live as long as Moses: his eye be not dim, nor his natural force be abated'. The toast was prophetic. 

The senior in age lived to preside over the House of Bishops. 

The younger never reached that dignity."

FATHER NASH'S LAST DAYS 

The same friend wrote that in his latter years he seldom preached "publically", but never ceased going from house to house, as long as he could go around, and as long as the power of speech remained to him. "He was a man of large stature, being mostly or quite six feet high; well filled out with flesh-rather too fleshy to be very active in his movements; of a fresh and fair complexion, and a benignant expression; dressed decently and comfortably, and with the strictest regard to neatness." After several attacks of illness, he died June 4th, 1836. was buried by the side of his deceased wife in Exeter, but the bodies of both were subsequently removed to Cooperstown, and

APPROBATION OF BISHOPS 

"The diligence and devotion which Father Nash always manifested secured the confidence of all who knew him. The day after his admission to priest's orders, Bishop Moore thus writes to General Jacob Morris of Butternuts, Otsego county: 'Yesterday I ordained Mr. Nash a priest; and it afforded me no little satisfaction to reflect, that the first act of my Episcopal function has been employed in elevating to the priesthood so worthy a man.' He was also a special favorite with the successors of Bishop Moore.' 99 "On entering the church-yard at Cooperstown," remarks Bishop Delancey in his charge on "Religious Training", "I was struck by an elevated and plain monument, bearing the inscription of a name which still delights the ear of multitudes of mature and aged, once children of his pastoral care and which beautifully illustrates the kind of character and intercourse I now urge-FATHER NASH."

The Bishop of New York, in his address to the Convention of 1836, spoke eloquently of his closing years, concluding thus: "It was so ordered, in the course of Providence, that I was, soon after his decease, in the district of country which had so long been the scene of his pastoral labors, and truly gratified was I to witness that best of testimonials to the virtues of the Man, the Christian, and the Pastor, which was found in the full hearts and tender and reverential expressions of the multitudes who, to use the affectionate epithet with which for years they had been delighted to know him, had been bereft of good old Father Nash."
