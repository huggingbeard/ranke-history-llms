Roger Williams, it is well known, had a trading post near Wickford. The exact place of this has never been satisfactorily identified. Judge Potter records that Wilkins

Updike put it at the corner of the Post Road and the present road to North Kingstown Beach. He does not accept or deny the statement, he merely records it as something said by Mr. Updike, who does not include it in his History of the 

Narragansett Church. 

It has seemed to some members of the Society that, as was once stated in the last publication A Map of the Acquidnesset or North Purchase, this is, "a tradition which the deeds in no wise support." One of these members, Mr.

Cyrus P. Brown, called Mr. Chapin's attention to the improbability, in view of the documentary evidence, of this theory or tradition stated by Mr. Updike. Mr. Chapin began an investigation and his results have seemed to your Publication Committee to be so important and to have such a bearing on the history of the settlement of Narragansett, that they have asked Mr. Chapin to put them in form for publication by the Society. This he has done and we present it for the consideration of historians.

OGER WILLIAMS owned both a shallop and a canoe and undoubtedly transported his wares from Providence to Wickford Harbor and from Wickford Harbor to Providence by water, as the paths at that time were not fit for heavy transportation.

It seems improbable that after landing his goods at Wickford Harbor, Williams would have built a trading house in the woods near Devil's Foot Rock where it would have been necessary for him to carry all his goods through the woods overland for a mile and a half, when he might just as well, and much more easily, have built his trading house on one of the many coves of the present Wickford Harbor near where he would have to land his goods. 

It is surprising also that if he were to have a trading house away from the shore, he would place it a mile and a half north of Wickford Harbor, when the chief sachems, his friends, resided several miles south. The placing of his trading house near Devil's Foot Rock would make it necessary for the Indians to bring their goods north to the harbor and then a mile and a half more. 

It also seems unlikely that Williams would build a trading house near a rock with such an ill-omened name as Devil's Foot, for such a name might scare away timid customers.

Potter in 1835, when he published his History of Narragansett, had never heard the "tradition" or "story" that Williams' trading house was located a mile and a half north of Wickford. In 1845, ten years later, he first learned the story from Wilkins Updike and made this annotation in his own copy of his History of Narragansett: “W. 

Updike says Wm's. trading house was where Royal Vaughn last lived north of Spink's Tavern. John C. Reynolds lives there now 1845.”

Williams kept his goats on an island in Wickford Harbor. It seems unlikely that he would have kept his goats over a mile and a half from his house.

Considering the lateness of the appearance of the "tradition" or "story" that the Williams' trading house was near Devil's Foot and a number of reasons for thinking that Williams would naturally build it near Wickford Harbor, I turned to contemporary documents. In a letter of 1649 Williams, writing to John Winthrop said, "For preface this Mr. Smith's pinnace (that rode here at you being with us) went forth the same morning to Newport."2 From this wording one might infer that the pinnace was near or at least in sight of Williams' trading house. Certainly the pinnace would not have been visible from a trading house near Devil's Foot, but would have been visible from a trading house on the shore of Wickford Harbor, near Smith's trading house and near the island where Williams kept his goats and near where Williams kept his pinnace and canoe. 

Williams also dated many of his letters at Cocumcussoc.3 From the deeds of 1659 it appears that Cocumcussoc was the name of the brook which flows easterly into Wickford Harbor southwest of

Smith's house, and it also appears that the land either side of the brook was also called Cocumcussoc. It does not seem probable that the name of the district extended as far north as Devil's Foot for Maskerchugg was the name of the brook at the north end of the purchase, and also of the district on both sides of the brook and the 

Devil's Foot is about halfway between Maskerchugg and Cocumcussoc. The deed of 1659, however, gives three names to the district so that if the northern part was called Maskerchugg and the southern part Cocumcussoc, the central section near the Devil's Foot Rock must have been called Wyapumscut. Quidnesset, another name given to part of this district, derived its name from The Mount near Allen's Harbor and so was applied to the central district near the east shore.

Taking the matter up from an entirely different angle. Why would 

Smith buy Williams' trading house in 1651. Not for the "good will" of the business, for Williams was about to go to England and so leave Smith without any competitor. Not for the land, for land was cheap and Smith could have bought a huge tract of land from the Indians at that time for goods to the value of fifty pounds. 

If he did not buy it for "good will" or for the land, it follows that he must have bought it for the house. In that case it would seem to follow that Williams' house was the better of the two, for houses would rapidly go to pieces in the wilderness and unless Smith planned to move into the Williams house, and keep it up, it would not have been worth his while to buy it. The known facts also point to the probability that Williams had the better house in 1651 for he had resided in it from 1645, while during these six years 1645 to 1651 Smith had been a resident for a long time at New Amsterdam18 and some time at Portsmouth19 and had apparently only used his trading house or shack for the occasional short visits, which his trading with the 

Indians made necessary.

If Williams' trading house was near Wickford Harbor and close by Smith's house, and if Smith bought it because it was a better house and then moved into it and maintained it, and from time to time enlarged it, then it of necessity would follow that Williams' house was on the site of the present Cocumcussoc house and that the present house was built on the ruins of the house which was built by Smith around the nucleus of the Williams trading house.

Several problems, difficult to settle, confront us. Was the Richard 

Smith house standing during King Philip's War built on the site of Richard Smith's original trading house, or on the site of the house he bought from Roger Williams in 1651? When did Smith build his trading house and when did Williams build his? In a petition of the inhabitants of Narragansett, dated July 29, 1679, it is stated that Richard Smith deceased "then liveing at Taunton in the Collony of New Plimoth erected a Trading house on the same Tract of Land where now his son Richard Smith inhabits.” From this wording the inference might well be drawn that although on the same tract of land, the trading house was not located on the site of the house of 1677. From this it might seem to follow that the house of 1677, that is, the present Cocumcussoc house, was on the site of the Williams trading post which Smith bought in 1651.20

The theory that Richard Smith built the first trading house seems at first glance to be supported by the statement of John Greene of Quidnisset22 that Smith did first begin and make a settlement in the Narragansett, before 1639. A close examination of the phraseology of Greene shows that he did not say Smith was the first but said forty years and more ago Smith did first begin, which might well mean that the first beginning of Smith's settlement, that is the erection or organizing of his trading house took place before 1639. The actual settlement of Smith as a permanent resident at Narragansett did not take place until after his purchase of Williams' trading house in 1651 at which time Smith was still "of Portsmouth."

The theory that Smith was the first to build a trading house seems at first glance also to be supported by the statement of Williams23 that Smith broke the ice (at his great charge and hazard) and put up in the thickest of the Barbarians the first English house amongst them. 

This however evidently refers to Smith's English style house which he erected for his permanent residence after the purchase of Williams' trading house in 1651.

It seems probable then that Williams set up a trading house in Narragansett, that is at Cocumcussoc, at least as early as 1637 and perhaps even in the summer of 1636 during his first year at Providence. It is well known that Williams traded extensively with the Indians.

John Oldham, who was the leading English trader dealing with the Narragansetts, was murdered in the summer of 1636 probably about the middle of July 30 This left open an opportunity for considerable trade with the Narragansetts and it seems likely that Williams established his trading house in the latter part of the summer of 1636 for the purpose of securing this lucrative trade with the Narragansetts³1 and that he was soon followed by Wilcox. 

The earliest contemporary statement which might seem to refer to a trading house at Narragansett is the letter³2 written by Williams on 

Sept. 7, 1638 which he dated "At Narragansett." Later, from 1645 to 1651 Williams sometimes dated his letters "Narragansett" and sometimes "Cawcawmsquissick", the latter being the designation of a small tract in the larger district which the former name signified.

THE DEPOSITIONS AND STATEMENTS. 

I 

ROGER WILLIAMS, on July 21, 1679, deposed:

Being now neere to fower score yeare of age yet (by Gods mercy) of sound understanding & memorie, doe humbly & faithfully declare tht Mr. Richard Smith, Senr. who for his Conscience to God left fair possessions in Glostershire & adventured with his relations & Estate to N. Engl, & was a most acceptable Inhabitant & prime leading man in Taunton in Plymmouth Colony: for his Conscience Sake (many differences arising) he left Taunton & came to the Nahigonsik Countrey, where (by Gods mercy & the favor of the Nahigonsik Sachims) he broke the Ice (at his great charge & hazards) & put up in the thickest of the Barbarians the first English Howse amongst them" "I humbly testify that about forty* yeares (from this date) he kept possession, (coming & going himselfe, children & servants,) & he had quiet possession of his howsing Land & Medows, & there in his owne howse with much Serenity of Soule & comfort, he yielded up his Spirit to God (the father of Spirits) in peace.

III 

RANDALL HOLDEN and JOHN GREENE, of Warwick, declared, on 

August 20, 1680: "Honored Sirs,-As concerning that Petition of the Inhabitants of the Narragansett Country, as is sayd, if wee are rightly informed, Mr. Richard Smith hath declared therein that his Father was the first that settled that place and expended a great sume of money for the efectinge therof which assertion, wee of our owne knowledge must declare agst as beinge untrue, forasmuch as wee were inhabitants of the Narrogansett Bay and Country some years before Mr. Richard 

Smith Senr, was heard of at Narrogansett. But in processe of time

Mr. Roger Williams and one Mr. Wilcockes for their advantage of 

Trade set up tradinge houses, and afterwardes Mr. Richard Smith 

Senr., came thither havinge joined in partnership with sayd Wilcockes, whereby hee much augmented his estate, and had no occasion to expend any thinge, for the Indians would not let them have any land to improve nor suffer them to keepe a beast there 

IV PELEG SANFORD, wrote on September 1, 1680: 

"The Towne of Providence being the first Towne, was settled by Mr. Roger Williams and others in the yeare 1635-6, Pawtuxet and 

Rhode Island was settled in the yeare 1637-8, and sometime after at Narrogansett (distant from Rhode Island about eight miles) one Mr. Wilcocks and Mr. Roger Williams obtained leave of the Indians to sett up a Trading house for commerce with the Natives there. 

And some yeares after Mr Richard Smith, Senr, aforesaid (being an

Inhabitant of the Towne of Portsmouth on Rhode Island and a 

Freeman of said Colony) removed to the said Trading house at

Narragansett (being as was said a partner with the said Wilcocks) which commerce and Trade then with the said Natives was the most proffitable imployment in these parts of America, and by which many persons of mean degree advanced to considerable estates.' 25 [25]

V 

SAFFIN, WHARTON and HUTCHINSON declared: 

"And whereas it hath been falcely affirmed by one John Green and Randall Holden of Warwick that thos lands of Narragansett were never purchased by any English, but that the Indians gave all theire lands to King Charles the first of blessed memory, which they would seem to prove by a declaration taken out of Mr. Gortons book. 

To this we answer-1 That a great part of the Lands aforesaid were purchased by Mr Roger Williams, yet living, and by Mr Richard Smith decd, above forty yeares ago, and possessed to this day by his son Mr Richd Smith, and divers others yet alive."26 [26]
