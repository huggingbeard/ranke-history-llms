Each night his mother told him stories as she put him to sleep, stories about little brown bears and the little pigs, the kitties and all the nursery rhymes and very often she would rock him to sleep in the big chair and sing to him. He always knew just what he wanted. He would say, "Sing, Nanny about the little rose bush," or: "about the little children." He was extremely smart and seemed to be utterly unspoiled to the mother.

Everywhere she went with him people commented on his beautiful hair that hung in golden ringlets.

Sometimes she used to lie awake until the solemn dawn admiring his darling face, and listening for his tiny voice to echo "Nanny."

FORSAKEN 

Florence was never quite the same after the death of her beloved baby. The doctor feared she must go to a sanitarium if she did not control her feelings; but she was grief stricken.

The day of the funeral they took her away because she insisted on holding the body in her arms. They had to lock her out of the room, and she banged on the door with her fists and screamed, "I want my baby! 

I want my baby, I tell you!"

So they took her to Ann's house until after the funeral since she was absolutely without reason. All the time she only thought of herself as riding behind the little white hearse that had borne her loved one. 

The doctor said, "No baby is really its mother's.

They are only loaned to them; they are all God's little children, and we must give them up when He wants them. It's a sin to grieve over them."

She could not see it his way and she still was without any sense of reason; for there was so many other little babies unwanted and suffering-and they were let live. "No," she said, "I don't, and I never will. understand why I am made to suffer when I was so good, a good wife and a good mother." 

The memories would not leave her. Often her family found her in tears, with a tiny garment or a little shoe, or a toy, in her hand, crying and repeating over and over, "My baby! my baby! the only thing I had on earth to love."

Most of his little belongings had been packed away and hidden, even the little crib had been taken down. and packed away; but in doing this several things were overlooked. She kept his little curls and would often bury her face in them and cry for hours.

His pictures were her one consolation now. As she

gazed at each one she remembered the darling things he said in each different pose. At night she could almost swear she felt his hands on her face as he said, "Sing, Nanny." 

When she tried to sleep she heard him tell her the little stories over and over about the "Free little 

Bears" and recite the little poems.

She would jump out of bed and tell her husband, "It's no use, my mind is gone. I can feel it leaving me." He was always too sleepy to care.

Memories were forced too closely in upon her, reminding her over and over that it was no use. She was finished-forsaken. 

All she could think of was that darling baby-she could hear the cries from his little bed ringing in her ears and it was like a resistless summons of her soul. 

She thought of how a helpless infant draws forth the wealth of its mother's nature.

She was like one now seeking for hidden treasures as she sent forth her sympathies. She thought of how she had toiled in her delight; then she thought to herself how violent delights have violent ends and that too swift is as tardy as too slow. She felt doomed to destruction.

FRIENDS 

Sister Mae suggested that her friend, William Andrews take Florence out and help her forget and Mr. 

Andrews insisted on taking Mr. Kaufman. Since there was no objection they insisted upon her going as her husband was too busy to take her out. After a prolonged coaxing they eventually had her consent. 

They drove her everywhere almost without knowing where they were driving. She did not feel enthused, as she felt it interfered with her grief in some

way. She was conscious of the pounding of the motor only, but each individual scene brought memories flocking back as she struggled to recall them. "I do not want to forget him," she thought. "His memory is all I have."

A little pulse of irritation and dismay began to beat in her brain, as she begged them to take her home. 

Each person's heart went out to her, and when she reached home she was worse off than ever; for there were too many old haunts there, too many fond, sweet memories that nearly drove her mad.

ON SUNDAY AFTERNOON 

One Sunday afternoon about a month later Florence had begun to look better, yet the lines of her face were obvious and bespoke of pain; for it was like an endurance test.

Mae and Mr. William Andrews had invited her out and later Mr. Teddy Kaufman, better known as 

Thomas Lee Kaufman, joined them. At first she re-

fused again and again, but they insisted, and at last she gave her consent.

It was only a means of transportation to her, no more, no less. "What's the use of all this?" she asked. "Same old scenery, same old world full of cruel people staring at you. 99 

They drove with great speed and she sometimes wished they would hit a pole and let her end it all, for she felt "all washed up." Everything seemed over for her now.

One would have thought she could find consolation in her husband, but every time she thought of him, it sickened her. His words of that dreadful night when he refused to get up came back to her. She always felt that the baby might have been saved had he not thought so much more of his sleep. 

She grew to dislike him because of this. "I never did love him in the beginning, but now I almost hate him for that."

All the time the car was in progress she was thinking, thinking, thinking. Her thoughts were morbid and hateful. They had what seemed a complete tour up the country, combined with excellent service, comfort and pleasure but she was hardly conscious of the car being in motion.

She read a sign as she passed a billboard. It read: "Visit Yellowstone Park. Why wait longer? You and your family." It stabbed her like a knife as she said to herself, "Family-I have no family now." 

When they returned home she thanked all three in turn for their kind attention and retired immediately. 

But sleep was just as difficult as ever and she ended by getting up and going outside again and sitting on the porch in the old porch rocker. Her father was there. The boys had not yet gone. Her mother was on the lawn; but they, she thought, were cold as they

merely said, "That's better, we hate to see you shut upstairs-it's too lonely."

Mothers passed with little babies in strollers, while tiny tots held the go-cart handles. Baby voices were heard everywhere for it was a very warm night.

Her hands seemed cold and clammy; her mouth was dry-she could stand it no longer. She returned upstairs, hurt, blue, heartbroken. Everyone else seemed to forget so soon. "How could they?" she asked. "How could they?" 

No one knew of the pain in her heart when little 

Bobby said, "Where's Carlton? Ain't he going to ever come back?"

She could not answer him, and often sent him away saying, "Go away, Bobby, and play." She simply could not tell him the truth-for what could a baby three years old know of death?

She had often pictured the many changes that might take place in life, but never once had she dreamed that she would be called upon to give up the most precious baby in the world. She never thought it could "honestly" happen with our God.

No one had the power to oppose His desire; she knew that now as she had never known before-it was before her like an open book. She had never been brought up to dissipate life in laughs-she was taught to be serious all her life-from childhood, and now everything she heard seemed merely spoken of as the deceased-and she had no particular wish regarding life or anything. She felt nothing more than an outstanding blunder that was about to be excluded-and she probably negotiated different ends. She appreciated everyone's earnest efforts exercised for her despite her unpleasant predictions, yet serious consequences threatened her own feeble efforts.

Logic and necessity forced her on. When she sub-

mitted punctually but not reluctantly the boys' benevolence hurt her. They inspired her enough into questioning herself, but her thoughts only favored a rebuke. She was afraid despite the fact that her affections could stand very severe storms of vigor.

NIGHTS THAT FOLLOWED 

Many nights after this Sunday she went riding with Mae and the boys, but always she had to be nearly dragged out and put in the car. But Mae insisted, as she thought, "If she doesn't soon snap out of it, her mind will go.' 99

Others told her that another baby would come sooner or later and she would forget.

But they did not know the conditions. She knew that babies are only born of love and she no longer loved her husband. In fact she often thought, "I don't believe I ever did, for I always feared him, and we don't fear the ones we love, and besides I liked him always better at a distance."

These rides continued several nights a week, and each time she could not understand why they insisted on a dreary person like herself going along. She wondered even more as to why Mr. Kaufman always came along and wasted his time. "He is so young,' 99

she thought, "and he must have interest somewhere else." She made herself believe that he was just a sympathetic little boy and she must put a stop to him wearing himself out on such a worthless person as herself.

And tonight when they went for a drive it was different-it seemed like three thousand square miles of mystery! Magic! Grandeur! Nowhere on earth had nature seemed to bestow so many wonderful things as she saw tonight; the trees looked different, they seemed more glorious, the sky seemed to be downthrusting a different light, the stars were more dazzling to her eyes; everything seemed so capricious, and uncertain. Yet she didn't know why.

He seemed entirely unmoved. He was more like a frightened school boy who wouldn't dare the inevitable. He seemed entirely unaware of her observation, but on returning home, for some unknown reason, she felt reluctant, proud, more alive than usual.

It seemed that she had real affection for this boy, and she used a negative mood to keep from expressing it, for she was afraid, afraid now of the effect it had on her.

It was like passing your fingers through a riot of scintillating gems-the tender reverence was too much. for her now.

The urgency seemed to demand that mockery should replace reality if only for a short duration. Her eyes burned under his touch, for he ravished her heart.

She was all aglow for even imitations give you certain privileges she thought as she sat there staring in open space.

Something kept him from answering her-or else his pretense was perfect. Her presence was the only thing that assisted him in remembering. He retaliated by returning in a quaint way, as if to wound his own self-and she, had she stayed a minute longer would have surrendered utterly.

Florence tried to make herself content with her staid little life living a narrow existence in a narrow little world all to herself. Here nothing now could possibly happen. It seemed Cupid had entered her
