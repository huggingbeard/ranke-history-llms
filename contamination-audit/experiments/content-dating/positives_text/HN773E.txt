INTR DUCTION 

OUR car is no longer a pleasure car. It is a war car. 

Our government needs it for transportation-so that it will get you to work on time, regularly. You are needed on your job and your car must be kept in perfect condition to get you there. During these times when replacement of parts is difficult and the service of able mechanics practically unobtainable, you will find that a little knowledge of how to handle your car to best advantage and how to prevent wear and tear will not only pay dividends in saving repair bills, but will increase the life and usefulness of the car itself.

Most cars do not give all-out service because their owners do not know how to get the most out of them. Drivers make expensive mistakes every day. These mistakes are made not so much through carelessness as through ignorance. Many motorists rely on "curbstone opinions"-which are about as reliable as curbstone road directions. 

The purpose of this book is to give simple, reliable facts about your automobile, so that you will know how to get the most service out of it and how to take the best possible care of it to prolong its life. Included are instructions on how to get the most mileage from gas, tires, and mechanical parts. Besides, every motorist should have a general idea of the working of his individual car so as to be able to meet minor emergencies. This information has not been compiled by an organization with a gadget to sell or an ax to grind, and has only one object: To show the motorist

HOW. 

The information and advice in this book will save many hours of time lost by inefficiency and many dollars of repair and replacement bills. In the long run you will find that proper care of your car means cheaper driving as well as safer driving.

There is no more expensive depreciation to an automobile than that caused by unnecessary mishandling and neglect.

Proper care of an automobile-always a matter of concern to the person interested in getting value received out of his car is now a matter of national importance. It is unpatriotic to subject a car to needless abuse, as our country needs every car that is rolling. Thousands of workers must be driven to their jobs in order to keep war work moving. And they must be able to feel reasonably sure that the car will get them there and home again at night, with a minimum consumption of gasoline and rubber. Every inch of mileage in our cars must be cherished so that we can be assured of transportation for the duration.

This book is timely now, as it can be used as a manual of treatment while the emergency lasts. But it is not intended as a wartime formula only. Every statement in this book is good advice-regardless of whether our factories are manufacturing pursuit planes or pleasure cars. Our government will appreciate it now if you heed the advice we give; but after the war is over your pocketbook will appreciate it.

When trouble arises it is best to use a systematic way of locating it. Following a process of elimination will greatly simplify the task. First of all look at your dashboard. Your ammeter should show discharge when the ignition switch is on and the motor is dead. Also glance at the gas gauge to ascertain whether you have fuel. If the motor stops after it has been in operation for a period of time, first see whether you have a sufficient supply of gas. The gauge may not be accurate, so you had better check the tank.

If the gas supply is sufficient, then check the electrical system. Start from the last operation and work back to the source. 

In the ignition system this would mean to see, first of all, if you are getting a spark at the spark plugs. Then check the connections at the plugs and trace back through the wiring from the plugs to the distributor to find out whether you are getting current at the distributor. In the distributor, check to see that the points

have not corroded, and are giving proper contact. From there work back through this wiring to the coil, making sure that all connections are tight. Continue checking connections to the ammeter. By checking the ammeter on the dash you can ascertain whether current is flowing from the battery. If the ammeter shows trouble there is probably something wrong with the battery, and it is best to have that checked by a professional.

If the electrical system is working satisfactorily and you have checked to make sure that the gas supply is sufficient, start at the carburetor and see that there is gasoline flowing into it. Then check back from there through the gas pump and the fuel line to the gas tank.

The foregoing is not an attempt to give you every step in locating any trouble that can arise with an automobile, but is intended to give you a logical pattern to follow in locating emergency troubles which might occur on the road. Location of these troubles may be sufficient to help you get to a repair shop where the trouble can be corrected professionally. 

It is possible for a mechanical break, due to a flaw in the metal, to occur in any part of the driving mechanism and prevent movement of your car. If a car should stop dead with the motor running, the trouble is not due to the electrical system or the fuel supply. Since it is neither of these two, there is nothing that you yourself can do, and you would be better off to call a serviceman, leaving your car on the spot, than to try to remedy the situation yourself. Any structural break can be of such a nature that even to push the car might cause further damage. This situation is rare, but does occur. 

The foregoing has shown how to locate trouble, but it is best not to let trouble develop. Periodic check-ups are the solution to most trouble before it occurs. 

Routine service to a car is as necessary as routine attention to your teeth. Conservation of your car is as important to your pocketbook as to your country. Preventing trouble before it develops saves lives and dollars.

This free service costs money in labor, electricity, water, air equipment, clean rags, soap, towels, etc. Don't forget the service station when you need something more than gasoline and free service.

If a repair or service to a car is unsatisfactory, return to have it corrected just as soon as possible. This is advisable for two reasons: (1) If there is something actually wrong, don't give it a chance to develop further or cause trouble or wear on parts; (2) Some repairs require watching and readjustment which can be made only after the car has been run a while; in other words, the repair job cannot always be done completely and perfectly the first time.

Preparation of Your Car for Winter Driving

Proper preparation of a car for winter driving pays big dividends and saves much time and weary moments. Some owners feel that they cannot afford this service. By spring they will find they have spent at least three times as much money as they would have spent had they properly serviced their cars for winter, to say nothing of all the delay, trouble, and per- manent injury to the motor that cannot be accurately figured.

For trouble-free winter driving 

Change motor oil to proper grade of winter oil. 

Change gear oil in transmission and differential. It is important that these be drained while they are warm. 

Check water for leaks. Check pump radiator hose for condition of rubber and tightness of hose clamps. 

Check thermostat to be sure that it opens and closes properly under required conditions. It is particularly important that it be in perfect order in winter, as it speeds up the heating of the water, which is necessary for the more efficient operation of the motor.

Check cooling system by filling radiator with water and testing the water pump. If it is found to be tight, drain out water and add proper amount of antifreeze solution and water. A mixture with a freezing point of about five degrees above zero is preferable. Even if the temperature should drop to ten below zero, this solution would not freeze solid enough to do damage. It would be of the consistency of snow or slush. When it is necessary to add to the mixture to bring it up to the proper level, add equal parts of alcohol and water. This will maintain the original freezing point.

If a radiator cover is used besides an alcohol solution, it must be removed on warm days and during fast, hard drives, to prevent overheating and loss of the alcohol.

TIRES 

Care of tires is always one of the first requisites of safe driving, but in these days of national emergency, it looms up as the most important element of car conservation. With no new tires available, the ultimate in care must be given the old ones if we want to keep our cars on the street.

Causes of Wear 

Barring accidents, speed and heat are the greatest offenders to tire life. High speed causes slippage and hence more tire-wearing friction between your tires and the road; your brakes must be used much harder, and this scrapes still more rubber off the tread. High speed gener- 

Speed wastes rubber ates terrific tire heat. This softens the rubber and induces extra wear. Driving over hot roads exposed to the summer sun has a similar effect.

Too little air pressure breaks down sidewalls of tire and causes irregular tread wear. Too much air pressure wears out center of tread and leads to broken tire cords. Driving an extremely soft tire pulls the cords loose from the position in which they were originally vulcanized, and the tire may be ruined.

Thin, worn-out tubes often lose air. 

Have your tubes inspected often and necessary repairs made without delay. 

How often tires should be checked for air pressure varies. Once a week is the average, but if you only drive around town, once every two weeks is sufficient. 

Don't inflate a tire to its full inflation in the cool of the morning and drive hard on a hot road all day; pressure increases considerably and makes for a hard and dangerous ride. 

Tires wear faster on hot roads and under hard driving. Recent tests indicate that if you expect to get 30,000 miles from a set of tires at forty miles an hour on roads of normal temperature, you should expect only 12,000 miles at sixty miles an hour.

Wheels out of balance cause spotty tire wear A great deal of spotty wear tire "cupping" and "scuffing"-is caused by wheels' being off balance. A wheel out of balance just one ounce at the tread develops twelve pounds of vibration on the road at sixty miles an hour. This vibration causes a tire to wear smooth at a single point. It is a wise precaution to check wheel balance every six months, or any time the position of the tire on the wheel is changed. Proper balance saves not only tires but also gasoline, wheel bearings, bushings, and king pins. It helps to end shimmy, tramp, and rough riding at high speed.

The test for wheel balance is made by jacking up the car and rotating the wheel. 

If it comes to rest gradually it is in balance. If it swings with a pendulum action as it comes to rest it is out of balance. 

Keep wheels in alignment

When a wheel is a half inch out of alignment, the tire is dragged sideways eighty-seven feet in every mile! Think how much extra tread wear that means. 

Have your wheel alignment checked regularly. Even if your tires are already unevenly worn, when the misaligned condition has been corrected and the tires run a few hundred miles, evidence of the misalignment will disappear. Many of the slight quivers or vibrations that gradually appear as a car is used can be traced to uneven or unbalanced wheels and tires, caused by wear and service. OUCH!

Bumping curbs breaks cords Indications of misalignment are excessive tire wear or difficult steering, which decreases the car's ability to hold to the road.

One of the principal hazards that cause tire failure is a break in the cord caused by bumping curbs, driving over protruding dull objects, overloading, etc. 

Cuts, pinches, and trying to get the last miles out of a tire also cause many catastrophes. If the owner would stop to figure out just how expensive it is to drive on tires that are dangerously thin, he would be less anxious to get that last mile out of them.

Tire pressure should always be checked when tires are cool. The heat generated by a fast run or steady driving increases pressure.

Care of Tires

Each car, with the conditions under which it is driven, presents a separate problem as to air pressure. It may be found that a car that is supposed to carry thirty pounds shakes and rattles considerably, and that if the air pressure is lowered to twenty-five pounds the car will ride much better and almost all body noise will disappear. This frequently happens with a very light car. 

On the other hand, twenty-six pounds of air might seem to be proper for the weight of another model, but there may be a noticeable rumble inside the car. By 15 raising the tire pressure to thirty pounds, the rumble may be reduced and the ride greatly improved.

Naturally, if either of these cars were to carry a heavy load, pulling a trailer or traveling fast up steep hills or mountains, it would be necessary to increase the pressure a few pounds to prevent damage to the sidewalls of the tires by excessive flexing and heating.

If you feel that your car is not guiding as well as usual or seems to be a little swingy, stop and look at the tires. The chances are that one is going soft. You may save a tire and tube by observing this simple precaution. 

Rotate tires every two or three thousand miles. Power transmission through the rear wheels causes rear tires to wear faster. 

High-crown roads are another important factor in fast and irregular tread wear.

Tires wear more on the inside of the tread, toward the car, than on the outside. 

For this reason, you should change the front wheels to the back and the back wheels to the front, reversing the side on which they are used. This will tend to equalize wear and greatly increase tread mileage.

Occasionally jack up the wheels of a car and rotate them-examining for deep cuts, bulges, badly worn places, and rim cuts. If a tire is smooth, or has been regrooved so deep that the breaker strip shows in the bottom of the groove, it is unsafe for fast driving. A tire is considered unsafe when the breaker has a bulge or cut place that extends into the cord. Regrooving is recutting a new non-skid design into a tire that has worn smooth. 

To do this safely, you must be sure that there is plenty of rubber left in the tire, and that the rubber is not dead with age.

Retreading is similar to putting a new pair of soles on your shoes. It is stripping the old tread off the tire and replacing it with a new tread, vulcanized right onto the casing. All automobiles come from the factory with sufficient tools to change a tire, and to make necessary and emergency repairs.

It is advisable to know how to use the tools. Check them over. Acquaint yourself with them. Keep the jack well oiled.

While tire service is usually available, there are times when the only way out is to be able to make a change yourself.

BRAKES 

There is a right and a wrong way of stopping a car. The right way is to bring it to rest gradually by alternate application and release of the brake without depressing the clutch, until the car has slowed down to about ten miles an hour. 

At this point throw out the clutch by depressing the clutch pedal. In this way the engine will help brake the car, and not throw on the brake linings the entire strain of stopping. A good driver, who respects his car and his tires, anticipates his stops and has no need for suddenly jamming on brakes. The only time it should be necessary to apply brakes suddenly is in an extreme emergency. 

Brakes should begin to be effective when the brake pedal is pushed downward one inch. Failure of the foot pedal to cause the brakes to operate efficiently when it is pushed to within two and onehalf inches of the floor board is a sign that the brakes need adjusting. 

If the brake pedal goes over halfway to the floor board before gripping, the hydraulic brakes should be tightened.

Sometimes the pedal may touch the floor board the first time you press on it, and then not travel so far the second and third times you apply pressure, but later may revert to normalcy. This symptom is dangerous and may be a forerunner of hydraulic brake failure. See a brake service station at once for correction. 

Mechanical brakes sometimes take hold quickly because of the self-energizing action of the mechanism. With hydraulic brakes, this fault is brought about by moisture on the brake lining, which causes more friction on the first application.

Alleviate the trouble by holding the brake pedal down a little for a short distance when driving in damp weather. This pressure will dry the brakes. The same procedure should be followed after washing the car.

The purpose of lubrication is to spread a thin film of oil on metal surfaces so as to minimize friction-which results in heat and wear-where two metal surfaces come in contact with each other.

Surfaces of different types require different lubricants, depending upon the amount and type of wear to which they are subjected. The various parts of a car must be lubricated in different ways, depending upon accessibility, use, and contour. 

Oil cushions metal surfaces 

To begin with, motor oil can be poured into the crankcase and by various methods this liquid oil is splashed or circulated, by pressure from the oil pump, onto the necessary parts of the cylinder and camshaft. The oil gauge on the dashboard reports on the behavior of the oil pump.

The oil gauge shows how the oil pump is working 

The oil gauge on the instrument panel tells the driver whether the oil pump in the motor is working. It really registers the resistance to flow rather than amount of flow. An exceptionally low reading should be a warning to see your mechanic, as it may mean loose bearings, a cracked oil line, a bad oil pump, or oil with a dangerously low viscosity index. It may also mean that the oil level is low. If the oil pressure is high after the engine has had reasonable opportunity to warm up, the oil is too heavy or there is an obstruction in the oil line. If the pressure is irregular, have it investigated immediately at a service station. 

Always drive slowly to a service station.

When the motor is operating properly the oil gauge should register from 35 to 40. This is normal. When the motor and oil are cold the gauge may register as high as 60, but in reality the moving parts are getting less oil than when the motor and oil are warm and the gauge at normal. 

Sometimes the oil gauge does not register pressure when the motor is first started, but later it does. This unregu lated action may be noticed if the cold test of the oil is not low enough or water has mixed with the oil. Most of the trouble in the electrical system can be traced to loose, worn, or dirty connections. It is not possible to receive a shock by working with a bare wire around the battery, under the instrument panel, or around the headlights. An annual check-up of the electric wiring system will undoubtedly save many automobiles from being destroyed by fire, while adding to the dependability of the car itself.

The amount of gas mileage given by any automobile depends greatly upon driving conditions. A car that may get twenty miles to a gallon when it is being driven at thirty-eight miles an hour probably will go only fifteen miles on a gallon of gasoline if driven sixty-five miles an hour. Gasoline consumption increases as higher speeds are attained.

Rapid consumption of gasoline when a car is traveling at high speed is caused by wind resistance and extra friction in moving parts, which causes the oil film to be removed faster than it can be replaced.

Ethyl is highly beneficial to any highcompression motor and more economical to use. Contrary to popular impression, it does not remove carbon from the motor. It does, however, retard the deposit of carbon. It is a very good policy to fill the tank with ethyl gas if you have a hard drive to make or are in mountainous country. If a motor is properly tuned and adjusted to ethyl gasoline, it is decidedly more economical to use than the cheaper fuels, and gives extra performance and satisfaction.

Other factors tending to reduce gasoline mileage include alternately slowing down and speeding up, low tires, improper ignition timing, worn or weak spark plugs, improper carburetor settings, too much or too heavy oil in the crankcase, poor lubrication of the wheels, tight brakes, overloading, haphazard lubrication of the universal joints, transmission and differential, and frequent stopping and starting. 

To increase gas mileage the motor, first of all, should be analyzed and checked by a good mechanic who has the necessary equipment to do a thorough job.

Then there is the man who buys a car to keep the rest of his life, and goes into how many more of this and that car A has than car B, and usually ends up by buying from the company with the best salesman.

Cars are all good, each one incorporating the ideas and principles that its manufacturer deemed important. And so it is best to try out several makes under like conditions and select the car that offers you the most of the things you like, whether they be initial cost, economy of operation, sound insulation, speed, type of brakes, ride, or over-all appearance.

It might be well to mention that a comparatively new and valuable feature is sound and vibration insulation. While this may seem of little importance when trying out a new car-everything seems so solid and quiet-one is likely to overlook the fact that after a year or so the motor will be getting noisy and the wheels, fenders and body will be giving out miscellaneous sounds. A combination of noises brought into the body, which acts as a sound box, will give the impression that the shiny new model you bought a year before is not standing up as well as you expected. If such noises disturb you, you are certainly in for three four of dissatisfaction. Instead years of a quiet-riding car you have just a means of transportation. Sound and vibration insulation does not do with away these noises, which are usually harmless, but it scientifically deadens them, preventing the amplification of such sounds, which tend to cause fatigue. or Scientific soundproofing is not just a haphazard use of acoustical material. It has been carefully thought out by engineers. Its purpose is to deaden scientifically the sound of metal against metal.

This is done by using rubber to prevent vibrations from crossing from one part to another, so that throughout the life of the car less noise will be heard inside the body, whether from the motor, the rear axle, or from the outside. 

Now nearly everyone wants to take care of a new car, and makes an honest effort to do so. However, the economical life of many cars has been greatly shortened by lack of knowledge on the part of their owners.

When you get delivery on a new car, be sure to make the following check-up: 

Check to see that the radiator is full of water, and in cold weather has the proper amount of antifreeze. 

Check the oil to see that the crankcase is full and the oil still clean and light in body. 

Check air in tires and see that they are properly inflated. In shipping and storing cars, tires are often inflated as high as 60 pounds to prevent damage.

Wax the finish of the car within the first month and about four every months thereafter. The wax forms a protective coating which allows you to dust the finish every few days if necessary, so that you can keep your car looking bright all the time.

If the surface of the waxed finish becomes lusterless and discolored it should be given a treatment known as a rub-out, which simply removes the layer of wax down to the solid part of the finish, in order to remove the impurities held by the wax. The surface may then be rewaxed successfully and the deep, rich color restored.

If the finish of a car is allowed to go from six to ten months without being waxed and the owner then tries to polish it, he may be disturbed because he sees a lot of color coming off on the cloth and probably thinks that the polish is taking the paint off. The polish is not taking the paint off; the past months of punishment have caused the surface of the finish to "bloom up" or oxidize, and the polish is just doing its duty by removing this dead film. 

Those who are afraid that polish will take the paint off find that the elements do the same thing, but in about a third of the time. Check was made of a car eleven years old that had been polished and waxed so many times the nickel plating on the radiator shell was down to the copper bonding coat, but in spite of all that rubbing and waxing, the finish was in excellent condition. It was hard, and the color and luster were nearly as good as when new. There were only a few places on the hood and cowl where the undercoat slightly showed through. 

Other cars of the same make and model had long since been repainted or junked, but this old model, which was nearing the 100,000-mile mark, was nothing to be ashamed of. 

In summing up all this: If you keep the finish waxed and the upholstery pro- 59 tected and clean, your car will be worth much more on a trade-in, to say nothing of the satisfaction you will have in driving a clean and shiny automobile. 

Keep your new car looking new 

Everyone wants to know how fast to drive a new car. Manufacturers have different recommendations, but if you drive up to thirty miles an hour for the first 500 miles, then not over forty-five miles an hour for the next thousand miles, you will save much future motor troublesuch as excessive oil consumption and unnecessary repairs and replacement of moving parts. After 1,500 miles, higher speeds are permissible, but never for a very long stretch. Sustained high speed should be avoided for at least the first 

2,500 miles. 

Another cause of excessive wearespecially in cold weather is driving just a few blocks at a time. You see, a motor must run for at least fifteen minutes before it is warmed up, in order to expand the motor parts to the point where they are working in their proper clearances, and until the oil is properly warmed, circulated and splashed to all tight motor parts. Until it is warmed up and thinned out the oil reaches only the edges of the tight motor parts.

On the first trip or so in a new car, everything is so different from your old car that you may be somewhat confused by the instrument panel. However, just as soon as possible, you should observe and make mental note of the indicator which gives the temperature of the motor. It should never be allowed to go over 180° and should operate between 

120° and 160°, depending on the weather and type of thermostat in the cooling system. 

How often to change oil in a new car is always a question in the owner's mind. 

The safest answer is at the first 250 miles, and be absolutely sure that this is done after fifteen or twenty minutes of driving.

The reason for this first change is to remove the last trace of molding sand or any other foreign material that might have found its way into the motor in assembling and testing at the plant. Use 

SAE No. 10 on this first change for summer as well as winter. 

The next change should be after you have driven 750 miles, and each 1,000 to 1,500 miles thereafter. In cars equipped with efficient oil filters, oil may be driven longer, depending on driving conditions, the frequency with which the oil is changed, and the time of year. The oil filter should be changed in the spring, when the oil is drained. Spring is advisable because winter is the time when most contamination accumulates and fills and clogs the filter so that it becomes much less efficient. 

Keep a record of all service on your car so that you can tell without guessing when the oil was changed, the car lubricated, the front wheels removed, cleaned and repacked with new grease, gear oil changed, spark plugs and ignition serviced, and dates of other services that mean so much to the economy and life of the car.

It is advisable also to co-operate with the dealer from whom you purchased your car in regard to any trouble with it. 

All dealers give a free check-up and tuneup after a number of miles. The instruction book coming with a new car tells when to have the first service and when it will be advisable from then on. 

In conclusion, bear in mind that when you bought a new car you bought a good car, and regardless of a few minor faults that may crop up in the beginning, don't be disappointed over your choice. Every car manufacturer strives to give the American public the best in his line, so don't let some Tom, Dick or Harry unsell you on your model if it is a little troublesome at first. Only after having your car for quite a while can you determine its true worth. Don't jump to conclusions.

There are many men who do little tinkering jobs around the car. There are many more who would like to, and who could considerably reduce their repair bills, but who are afraid that their ignorance would only increase their troubles.

The following pages are specifically intended for those people.

After all, no one will take care of the little things about your automobile that you will. Therefore, this is to help those who would like to learn just what they can do on their automobiles, and what they should leave to a specialized serviceman.

First of all, obtain an instruction book for your make and model of car. There is one with each car when it is sold, but in the event it is lost you may be able to get one from your dealer or from someone who has a car of the same make and model as yours. For those who want to perform major repairs, there is also a detailed shop manual put out by the maker of your car. 

Study your instruction book or shop manual carefully, especially concerning the part of the car you intend to repair or adjust. If your tools are of the average assortment, it probably will be advisable for you to purchase a set of 12-point box-end wrenches. They are necessary in working around recent makes of cars. If you don't have tools, go to your autosupply store and ask the clerk to help you select a set of practical hand tools for your home and garage. In a short time they will save you many times their cost.

One thing to keep in mind is that even though motors and other parts of the car may be dirty on the outside, it is of utmost importance that the inside and working parts be thoroughly cleaned before reassembling. Even the smallest particle of dirt and grit will prevent proper fit or adjustment, to say nothing of the rapid wear it will cause.

The question is then asked-what can I do? There are plenty of things you can do, and even better than anyone else. For example, keep the various parts around the body, chassis, wheels, fenders, bumpers, hood, exhaust pipe, etc., tight. You can adjust the fan belt or spark-plug points, clean and tighten the battery cables, see that the battery is tight in its holder, and keep the motor cleaned off. 

Above all, wipe the porcelain on spark plugs and the bakelite parts of the distributor and coil frequently to keep them free from dirt and oil. To do this, be sure the ignition is off and then use a cloth dampened with kerosene. The electricity at these points is of a high voltage and will short-circuit across the slightest conductor. This high voltage is caused by the breaker points' suddenly breaking the sixvolt circuit that passes through the coil. 

The sudden dying out of the magnetism in the coil causes a current of electricity of very high voltage and low amperage to flow from the outer winding of the coil. 

It is the high voltage which penetrates the body and causes shock. Dirt on these high-voltage parts holds moisture and causes trouble, especially in damp weather or early in the morning. Dirt and oil act as a conductor that causes the high voltage to leak away instead of jumping across the spark-plug points as intended.

Nowhere else in the electric system can you get a shock.

It is important that the motorist of today realize that he is not driving the same sort of car he drove twenty years ago. Formerly a car was left on a car dump with the same spark plugs that were in it when it was purchased. The oldtimer remembers this and thinks that he is just being sold a bill of goods when he is told he needs new spark plugs after ten thousand miles of driving, but this is not true. Motor design has been so changed in recent years and cars have been so improved in operating efficiency that what was once true of car care and car construction no longer holds. 

The reason for this change in sparkplug life is this: Compression ratios have been greatly increased, and require that the ignition system operate at complete efficiency. Deviation from par will cause

A spark plug wears out for three reasons: First, and commonest—the electrodes burn down because of the arcing action in making the spark that ignites the combustible mixture in the engine cylinder. Second, the repeated heating and cooling of the plug breaks down the insulating qualities of the porcelain part of the plug, which then permits leakage of current, thus weakening the spark. 

Third, a carbon deposit forms on the inside of the plug and short-circuits the flow of current so that the spark does not occur properly at the points. 

It has been definitely proved that it is good economy to replace spark plugs with new ones if they are less than 95 per cent efficient. Tests indicate that you pay for a new set of spark plugs every 4,000 miles after you have driven a set 10,000 miles in modern high-compression motors. Why not have them? 

Spark plugs are made in different heat ranges in order to give the best performance and longest possible life. It is important that this heat range be proper for each make and model. Check on this closely when installing new plugs. If the recommended plug is burning up too rapidly, an extremely hard driver may use a step colder plug in order to get full satisfaction for 10,000 miles.

The rest of the electric system needs little attention other than to keep all the six-volt connections clean and tight. Dirty, worn, or loose connections cause resistance to the flow of this low voltage, which results in hard starting, flickering of lights, and excessive burning out of bulbs.

It is well to check over the wiring occasionally to see that the wires are not 63 rubbing against the metal part of the body or frame. If they do rub, the insulation will wear off and a short circuit will result, causing a burned-out fuse, or even a fire. Wires in such condition are most likely to be under the car or where they go through the metal to the lights or horn. 

Much care should be taken when installing electrical accessories, to be sure the wires are put in keepers or taped in such position that they will not rub and wear off the insulation. And, as previously cautioned, be sure you clean and tighten the point of connection with the electrical circuit. 

Work by gauge, not by guess

Anyone with a fair knowledge of mechanics can make his own motor tune-ups.

The first thing to learn when working around the motor is that the success and life of the job depend on how closely you follow the specifications and adjustments of the various parts. 

For example, if the breaker points in the distributor are to be set at 18 thousandths, set them at exactly that. A few thousandths more or less will considerably affect the operation of the motor. 

This opening is different on various makes and models, and is determined by the engineers in accordance with the taper of the distributor cam, capacity of the coil, and such other things as enter into structure, performance, and long life of such parts.

Accuracy is important also in connection with the opening between the electrodes on spark plugs, piston ring clearance, valve clearance, bearing clearance, and tightness of the fan belt. Too much or too little causes improper operation or excessive wear. 

When checking and resetting the points on spark plugs, be sure to bend the side electrode and not the center one, as the center electrode is set into the ceramic, which will crack under pressure. 

Oil around a spark plug or cylinderhead bolt does not indicate that the cylinder is pumping oil. A loose plug or one with a bad gasket will collect oil from the combustion chamber. Tighten it.

When doing a motor tune-up, always put the ignition system in condition first, then the carburetor. Very often what appears to be carburetor trouble will be gone when the ignition system is adjusted. 

When the ignition is O.K. and after you have driven the car twenty or thirty miles an hour for several miles, remove a spark plug. If the end of the por- celain exposed in the combustion chamber is a light chocolate color, the highspeed jets of the carburetor are not far off for good operation. If there is a soft black soot on the plug, you had better take the carburetor in for a complete check and overhaul, for this is a sure sign of too rich a gas mixture. If a cylinder is pumping oil the plug from the cylinder will have excessive carbon and oil inside the plug.

In modern cars, wheel and tire balance play a big part in the smoothness of a car from thirty-five miles an hour up. It is highly inadvisable to use a tire with even a small blowout patch in it on a front wheel. Much steering trouble can be traced to a front tire that is worn flat in spots or has a boot in it that throws the tire off balance. 

A front tire on which the tread is wearing rapidly and which has a little featheredge on one side of the ribs is a sure sign of misalignment. If the featheredge is on the inside of the tire, it indicates too much toe-in adjustment and if on the outside, it indicates insufficient toe-in adjustment.

Occasionally check the steering-gear assembly for loose joints or bent rods. 

Front-wheel bearings should not be adjusted too tight, neither should they be too loose. The best way to be sure of the proper adjustment is: With the bearing assembly parts all in their proper places, tighten the adjusting nut as tight as you can conveniently with the muscle in the arm, using a 10-inch wrench. Then back the nut off until it clears the washer, and with a pair of pliers or the wrench run the nut up slowly until it just comes up against the washer and bearing. Insert the cotter pin, and if the hole isn't in the right place loosen the adjustment a little. 

Always adjust any ball or roller bearing in favor of the loose rather than tight. 

Keep your headlights properly focused to the road and in good condition. No one factor affords more safety to you and the oncoming motorist when driving at night. 

Remember, and appreciate, the importance of following your instruction book or shop-manual specifications on the clearances and adjustments of various mechanical parts. Work by gauge, not by guess. 

In doing your own repair work go slowly, be clean and accurate, and tighten each part as you work, so that things. will not loosen up in a short time.
