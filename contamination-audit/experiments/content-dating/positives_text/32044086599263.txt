Faulty pronunciation of a foreign language is due to the pronunciation of the new language with the habits of articulation of the mother tongue. French is pronounced with an 

English accent if the habits of English pronunciation are used in its pronunciation.

The following pages contain an analysis of the differences between English and French habits of speech and instruction for practicing drills (given on the records) for the elimination of English habits and the formation of French habits in the American (English) pronunciation of French¹.

The drills are based on fundamental differences in the control of the breath in relation to the movements of the vocal organs: the tongue, lips, jaw, etc. These differences concern both vowels and consonants. 'The theories and drills in this Manual are based on earlier work and on experiments subventioned by the Modern Foreign Language Study (American Council on Education) and The Modern Language Association of America (Commonwealth Fund) and published in part in Modern Philology and the Pub lications of the Modern Language Association.

RECORD No. 2 EXERCISES TO AVOID DIPHTHONGIZATION 

In English, in passing from vowel to vowel, the expulsion of breath is continuous, the shift of position from one vowel to the other begins early and procedes slowly and gradually; the two vowels merge into each other and become a diphthong. They are usually felt as one sound and are sometimes represented by a single letter as in the spelling of the pronoun (I). 

In French, the position of the first vowel is taken with precision before beginning to pronounce and then held throughout the duration of the vowel. The expulsion of breath is then suspended and the shift in position from the first to the second vowel is made in silence and quickly. The air current is set in motion again only when the position for the second vowel has been fully taken and is again completely arrested before the position has been changed or left. Sometimes the break between two vowels is so brief that it may not be detected by an unpracticed ear, but no matter how short the pause,the vowels remain pure and undiphthongized. Such combinations occur usually at the end of one word and the beginning of the next. 

It is necessary to avoid confusing sound and spelling; a single letter frequently represents a diphthong in English; and two or more letters, a single vowel in French. French has no constantly changing sounds or diphthongs.

CONSONANTS 

The position of the tongue, lips and jaw is held much longer for a French consonant than for an English consonant, and in French this position is held without change, whereas in English there is a slow change or loosening up of the position. Like French vowels, French consonants are "pure"; like English vowels, English consonants are "diphthongized."

In English, in passing from consonant to vowel, the movement of tongue, jaw and lips is comparatively slow and the expulsion of breath continuous: the consonant and the vowel are "diphthongized" or merged gradually the one into the other without any break between them. In French the position of the consonant is held without change and then the movement of transition from consonant to vowel is made 12

rapidly and at the same time, the current of air passing through the mouth is arrested or interrupted to be set in movement again when the vowel position has been taken. In French, a clean-cut consonant is followed by a clean-cut vowel with a little break in between. 

In English, in passing from vowel to consonant, the movement of tongue, jaw and lips is comparatively slow and the expulsion of breath continuous: the vowel and the consonant are "diphthongized" or grad. ually merged the one into the other without any break between them. 

In French, the position of the vowel is held without change and then the movement of transition from vowel to consonant is made quickly. the current of air passing through the mouth (or nose or both) being interrupted while the shift in position is made, and set in motion again when the consonant position has been taken. In French, a clean-cut vowel is followed by a clean-cut consonant with a little break in between.

In French, all vowels and consonants, no matter where they may occur, are pronounced as if initial, i.e, they are pronounced while the position for the vowel is held or while the position for the consonant is held and left, but never while the position for either is being taken. 

The shift into position for both vowel and consonant is made in silence and during a suspension of the movement of the current of air through the mouth. Vowels and consonants pronounced with this mechanism may be said to be "initial," thus (è) in mais is "initial": (m) is not pronounced with and gradually merged into the (è), but the expulsion of breath is suspended after pronouncing (m) and while the position for (è) is being taken, then (è) is pronounced as a pure “inital" vowel. 

In fil, (1) is not pronounced as the position for (i) is being left and the position for (1) is being taken, but this shift in position is made in silence. While the shift in position is being made, the expulsion of breath is suspended, and (1) is pronounced while the position for (1) is held and left, (1) is "initial." In English may, (ay) is pronounced as the vowel position is being taken, (m) and (ay) are gradually merged into each other, (ay) is a "final" vowel, and consonant and vowel form a "diphthong." In feel, (1) is pronounced as the position. of (i) is being left and the position for (1) is being taken. As in may, the expulsion of breath is continuous; (1) is a “final consonant”; the (i) and (1) are merged into each other and form a "diphthong" or the (f), the (i) and the (1) are pronounced with a continuous expulsion of air and a constant gliding movement, merge into each other and form a "triphthong."

English has two kinds of vowels: "initial," in initial position, pronounced while the position for the vowel is being held (and left), and "final," after consonants, pronounced while the position is being taken and held (and left, if followed by another consonant in the same syl- 13

lable). Likewise English has two kinds of consonants: “initial,” like (m) in may, pronounced by means of a following vowel and forming a "dipthong" with it, while the lips are holding and leaving the position for the consonant; "final," like (m) in somebody, pronounced with a preceding vowel and forming a "diphthong" with it while the lips (or tongue) are taking and holding the position of closure. 

French has no "final" consonants and French “initial" consonants serve even at the end of a syllable or before another consonant. These 

French "initial" consonants are produced by means of an indistinct vowel like explosion (not written) that may be either voiced like the explosion following (d) in the English word rubbed, or voiceless like the explosion following (t) in stopt.

A person speaking English may be compared with a trombone player who blows into the horn and at the same time shifts the slide from A to B. In spcech, the jaw, tongue, or lips are the slide; vowels and consonants are ever-changing and are obtained as the slide—lips, tongue, jaw-moves into, through and out of position. In French, with the slide at a point A, the player blows into the horn, produces a cleancut note; shifts the slide to B while not blowing through the horn; then with the slide at B begins blowing again. This gives pure "initial” vowels and pure “initial” consonants without merging or “diphthongization" with the preceding or following sound.

The position of the tongue, lips, etc., is held much longer for a 

French consonant than for an English consonant, and in French this position is held without change, whereas in English there is a slow change or loosening up of the position as the consonant passes over and merges into the vowel. In French, the tongue and lips move as much as possible with the jaw, and not more or less independently of it as in English.

French consonants are pronounced with the teeth nearly closed or closed. The jaws open but little for the closed vowels, but in a decisive manner for the open vowels. After an open vowel the teeth are brought together or nearly together before pronouncing a consonant though the consonant may belong to the same syllable. 

The French pronunciation of all consonants is more energetic than in English, and for the voiced consonants the vocal cords vibrate much more strongly and longer than in English. 14
