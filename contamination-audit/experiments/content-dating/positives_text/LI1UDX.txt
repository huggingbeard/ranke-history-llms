AGRICULTURAL DEVELOPMENT In The Pittsburgh District D. C. KIMMEL HIS STUDY WAS UNDERTAKEN to provide the basic infor mation essential to a program for promoting desirable development of agriculture in the Pittsburgh area.

In requesting this investigation, the Allegheny Conference on Community Development and the Agricultural Committee of the Chamber of Commerce of Pittsburgh recognized the interrelationship between agriculture and industry and the importance to the entire community of understanding the role of agriculture in the economic life of the area.

The basic information provided by this study included: 

1. Enumeration and description of the factors which determine the nature and extent of agricultural activity in the area. 

2. Description of the current agricultural industry and the individual farm enterprises in the area and the trends which have led up to the present type of agriculture. 

3. Presentation of conclusions as to prevailing problems and probable future developments in agriculture.

The study was based on the principle that the soundest guide to the agricultural enterprises best adapted to an area is to be found in the trends which are leading toward the establishment of a particular type of agriculture. Seeking to engage in the most profitable type of farming, farmers in a particular area will tend toward the production of those commodities in which they have the greatest comparative advantage. Adjustments are constantly being made to changing conditions but many individual farmers will not keep pace because of lack of information or emphasis on the desirability of change. A study of this type will indicate the direction of desirable adjustments by these farmers. It is recognized that special conditions of individual farmers may mean that certain operations are well adapted for individual farms even though they are of minor importance and not well adapted to agriculture in the area as a whole.

WHAT DETERMINES THE TYPE OF AGRICULTURAL ACTIVITY 

The type of agriculture in an area is determined by a variety of factors. These include many physical factors such as rainfall, temperature, soils, and topography. Other factors are economic, biological and personal. The interaction of all these factors determines the type of agriculture in any area. 

Soils and topography. There is considerable variation in the character of soils and topography in western Pennsylvania. Conditions within a county, within townships and even within fields on individual farms vary considerably. In general, however, the soils and topography in the Pittsburgh District are far from ideal for the more intensive agricultural uses and are not above average for the state in agricultural value. The topography of much of the area is rather hilly which places the land at a disadvantage in the use of modern machinery. Because of its topography much of the area may be used to its best advantage in the production of grasses to be utilized by livestock. 

Climate. In general, the climate in the Pittsburgh District is rather cool and moist. There is, of course, considerable variation throughout the District and from year to year. The climate is not generally a limiting factor to most types of agricultural production common to the general region in which the District lies. The climate is particularly well adapted, however, to the production of grasses. 

Biological factors. - Biological factors may be either constructive or destructive in their effect on the type of agriculture. Some of the constructive biological factors which have influenced agriculture in the Pittsburgh District are the development of hybrid corn and the improved breeding of animals, particularly dairy cows. Some of the destructive biological factors include the various plant and animal diseases and pests. Bangs disease is a particularly important disease among cattle in the District and requires further attention. The existence of blight in oats is an example of a type of biological factor which has been partially overcome by plant breeding. Tomato and potato blight are still important problems in the area. 

Economic factors. - From an economic standpoint, the most important characteristic of the Pittsburgh District is nearness to market.

The presence of Pittsburgh and many smaller cities, together with the location of the area in the populous Northeast, assures an excellent nearby market for the agricultural products of the area. This factor of nearness to market provides the greatest competitive advantage in

A vigorous program of promoting improved agricultural practices is conducted by the Agricultural Extension Service. The best available information applicable to the agriculture of the region is available free from the County Agents at the Agricultural Extension Office in each county. The program of Vocational Agriculture is an important step in the training of agricultural youths in the high schools throughout the area. Additional efforts to improve agriculture in the area would yield great returns if directed toward the support of the programs of these agencies already established.

The trend in average size of farms over a period of years has been downward. Between 1850 and 1945, the average size of District farms declined from 144 to 86 acres, fig. 5. Indications are that the long time downward trend in the number of farms in the District will continue. Some decline in the total acreage of farm land will contribute to this. As a result of mechanization, the average size of commercial farms may increase. Variations in the number of small part time farms may occur with variations in industrial activity.

Conservation of farm land. The proper conservation and improvement of farm land is a necessary part of the process of maximizing present returns and assuring future returns from farming. 

One of the oldest and most widely used practices for conserving and maintaining the fertility of the soil is the use of crop rotations. 

The use of some of the newer conservation practices such as strip cropping or contour planting of intertilled crops is practiced on a much smaller proportion of farms than could benefit from employment of such practices.

Soil conservation programs are being actively promoted in the District by the Soil Conservation Service of the United States Depart- 6 The indicated 1940-1945 decline in plowable pasture was due to a change in census definition. 14

ment of Agriculture, the Soil Conservation Commission of the Pennsylvania Department of Agriculture and the Pennsylvania Agricultural Extension Service. The work of each of these organizations deserves the support of everyone interested in the agriculture of the region. Research in various soil conserving practices is being conducted by the Pennsylvania Agricultural Experiment Station in various parts of the State. Results already available would be of immense value if applied more generally throughout the District. 

Pasture is one of the most neglected land classes in the District as far as application of improvement and conservation practices is concerned. Pasture is abundant in most of the District in relation to crop land, and this may explain why pasture improvement has progressed less rapidly than conservation practices on plowable land. 

Among the improvement practices which are being followed by some farmers are tillage and reseeding, application of lime, and fertilizer, and mowing of pastures. Contour furrows sometimes have proved invaluable. The application of organic matter, lime, and fertilizer to pastures increases the amount and improves the quality of grass produced. Mowing of pastures at intervals during the season maintains the grass in an immature stage of growth at all times. Such grass is more palatable to cattle and higher in feeding value. These practices are in line with recent discoveries made at the 

U. S. Regional Pasture Research Laboratory and the Agricultural Experiment Station at State College. 

Farm woodlots are a second class of neglected farm land. Misuse of woodlands consists of improper harvesting of timber and their use as pasture for livestock. Approximately 37 per cent of all farm woodland in the District was pastured in 1944. This practice is undesirable since seedlings are destroyed and the natural reproduction of the forest is made impossible. The livestock grazing the woodland receive an inadequate food supply, and the destruction of forest cover diminishes the waterholding capacity of forest soils. 

There is a need for the adoption by farmers of a system of woodlot management directed toward the maintenance of a continuous supply of lumber for farm use or for sale. 

The problem of conservation and improvement of land extends beyond the borders of the individual farm to land that was formerly in farms but is now owned by other commercial interests such as mining and lumbering companies. In some instances farm land acquired by these interests has been completely ruined for all future uses while in others the land has not been permanently destroyed. 

Strip mining is a non-agricultural use of land which creates a problem for the entire community. In the process of transporting coal from the mining area, highways are often damaged. Where stripping operations are localized, the destruction of land and roads leads 15

to abandonment of the area. There is no means of supporting population and hence no source of income for maintaining local government.

During the period 1944-47, almost 12,000 acres of District land were involved in surface mining operations. Despite the fact that restoration of land subjected to surface mining is physically possible, there is little activity in that direction. Areas of land which have been strip mined but have not been reclaimed create a problem on which some form of community action appears desirable. 

Farm mechanization. – The extent of increased use of machinery in the District is indicated by the fact that in 1945 the average investment in machinery was $24.35 per acre of improved land while in 1900 the investment was only $3.01. The labor required for farm operations has declined as a result of this mechanization. The cost of producing farm products has not, of course, declined in proportion to the reduction in labor.

One of the most significant changes in the method of performing farm work has been the shift from horses and mules to tractors as the source of farm power. In 25 years, between 1920 and 1945, the number of tractors on District farms increased 390 per cent while horse and mule numbers declined 48 per cent. In 1945, 50.4 per cent of all District farmers kept horses and 39.6 per cent owned tractors.

The decline in horse and mule numbers has been nation-wide since 1920, fig. 8. On District farms, the peak in horse and mule numbers was reached in 1890. In that year 210,000 horses and mules were reported on District farms. Fifty-five years later, in 1945, only 

65,000 were reported. On Pennsylvania farms, the horse and mule population was also at its peak in 1890 but the rate of decline between 1890 and 1945 was less than in the District. 

Trends in the number of tractors on farms are shown in fig. 9. 

Data on the number of tractors on United States farms first became available in 1910. Since then there has been a pronounced upward trend. Census data on a state and county basis first became available in 1925. In both the District and in Pennsylvania, the trend has been sharply upward.

Increased mechanization in hay making has also contributed to higher investment in machinery. Although no one method of hay making predominates in the District, the use of such equipment as pick-up balers and buck-rakes is becoming common. In recent years, the forage harvester and the barn drier have been introduced. 

Corn harvesting also has undergone considerable change. In the harvesting of corn for silage, corn binders have replaced the corn knife. Corn pickers have replaced much hand husking in the harvesting of corn for grain. Each year more small grain harvesting is being done with combines. 16

Numerous smaller mechanical devices such as electric milking machines, water heaters, refrigeration equipment, and animal clippers also have been introduced. Each of these devices has had an important effect on the methods and cost of performing operations and the quality of farm products. 

Numerous District farms are so small that the expenditure of funds for such equipment as tractors, pick-up balers, combines, and corn pickers cannot be justified on an economic basis. High investment, depreciation, and maintenance costs spread over a small acreage result in extremely high costs per acre. It appears that many of the smaller farms can do their farm jobs more efficiently on a cooperative or custom basis. Mechanization is providing an incentive for enlarging the size of commercial farms. 

Improvement in machines for the performance of agricultural tasks is certain to continue. Whenever this improvement is in the direction of larger machines, the small, irregular plots of ground common to western Pennsylvania farms and unadapted to the use of large scale machinery, are placed more and more at a disadvantage in agricultural production.

All of the five major breeds of dairy cows are found in the District. Guernsey and Holsteins are most popular with Guernseys slightly more numerous than Holsteins. Jerseys are third in importance but are only about one fourth as numerous as Guernseys. There are a few Brown Swiss herds, mostly in Somerset county. Very few 

Ayrshires are found on District farms. The prevalence of Jerseys and 

Guernseys is attributed to the fact that in earlier years much of the District's milk production was used in the manufacture of dairy products. Today most of the milk production is sold for fluid use. Generally the premium paid for butterfat in milk for fluid use has not been high enough to warrant emphasis on high fat content. As a result there appears to be a shift toward the Holstein breed. 

Poultry comprises the second most important livestock enterprise. 

Poultry and eggs sold in 1944 were valued at $14,849,250, an amount 20

larger than the combined value of cash receipts from beef cattle, hogs, goats, sheep and the sale of horses and miscellaneous livestock products. Cash receipts from the sale of poultry and eggs in 1944 accounted for about one fourth of the cash receipts from the sale of all livestock and livestock products.

Chickens are by far the most important poultry. In 1944 approximately 7,600,000 chickens were raised on 41,901 farms — 76 per cent of all farms in the District. 

Although the income from chickens is derived both from the sale of eggs and meat, egg production is the more important, and meat is primarily a by-product. In 1944 approximately two thirds of the cash receipts from poultry in Pennsylvania came from the sale of eggs. The situation is similar in the District. Many poultrymen buy straight-run chicks to raise replacements for the laying flocks. 

The cockerels, low producing birds, and old hens are sent to market as meat. There are very few specialized poultry meat production enterprises in the District.

On the basis of inventory values, hogs, beef cattle and sheep followed poultry in that order. Goats and bees are also found in the 

District but are of minor importance. Cash receipts from the sales of all livestock and livestock products other than dairy and poultry amounted to $11,740,663 in 1944 – approximately one fifth of the total value of livestock and livestock products sold.

Approximately 10,000 beef cows were kept on 2,300 farms - about 4.5 per cent of all District farms in 1940. Most of the beef cattle enterprises consist of breeding cows maintained for the production of calves for sale. There were few fattening operations. Although no statistics are available since 1940, observation indicates that the number of beef cows has increased somewhat since 1940. 

Approximately 265,000 hogs were raised on 26,910 farms - about half of all District farms - in 1944. There are few specialized hog farms in the District. The hog enterprise usually is supplementary on general farms. Forty two per cent of the hogs grown in 1944 were butchered at home. The number of farms raising hogs for home slaughter was more than two-and-one-half-times as large as the number of farms raising hogs for sale. 

In 1945 there were approximately 186,000 sheep on 4,535 farms - 8.2 per cent of all farms in the District. From these flocks 1,200,000 pounds of wool and 69,000 sheep and lambs were sold in 1944.

WHAT ARE THE TRENDS IN THE IMPORTANT INDIVIDUAL AGRICULTURAL ENTERPRISES 

Dairying, beef cattle and sheep. - Most farms of the Pittsburgh District are best adapted to the production of forage, in the form of hay and pasture. Dairy cows, beef cattle and sheep are alternative uses of the forage, and for this reason the trends in the numbers of these kinds of livestock may well be considered together. 

The dairy enterprise, as measured by the number of cows on farms, was an expanding industry in the District between 1850 and 1890, fig. 10a. From a peak of 247,133 head in 1890, the number of dairy cows declined to 198,707 in 1930. There was an increase in numbers between 1930 and 1940. Although census data are not available for years since 1940, estimates made by the Crop Reporting Service indicate that numbers continued upward to an all time high in January 1945 and leveled off between 1945 and 19477.

The trend in milk production in the District has been continuously upward since 1909, fig. 10a. Between 1909 and 1944 milk production increased 64 per cent. A major part of this increase is the result of increased production per cow. 

The long time trend in the number of beef cows on District farms has been downward. From a high of 34,000 in 1910, the number of beef cows had declined to 6,500 in 1930, fig. 10a. Between 1930 and 1940, the number of beef cows increased to almost 10,000 head. Although no statistics are available since 1940, observation in the District indicates that there are more beef cows on District farms at present than in 1940.

The statistics on which trends in the beef enterprise are based include only beef cows two years old and over. If data on the total number of beef animals were available over a period of years, it is likely that the downward trend would be more pronounced. At one time the fattening of steers was an important enterprise on many District farms. Carlots of western range steers were brought in to be 27

fattened during the winter. At present there are few fattening operations and they are more likely to be the grass fattening type than of the winter grain fattening type.

Sheep numbers in the District reached a peak in 1880 and have declined sharply since then, fig. 10b. The decline was from 1,325,647 in 1880 to 185,966 in 1945 or 86 per cent. Estimates of the Federal 

State Crop Reporting Service indicate that an all time low of 157,790 was reached in 19478. According to this same source the peak of sheep numbers in recent years was 353,860 in 1934. The decline between 1934 and 1947 was 55 per cent.

Greene and Washington counties are the center of sheep production in the Pittsburgh District. Prior to the census of 1920, Washington county had the most sheep. Since 1920 Greene county has been the most important sheep county in the District. The 

80,335 sheep on Greene county farms in 1945 represented almost half the District sheep numbers. In the same year, Washington county had about one third of the total. Every other county had less than 

9,000 sheep and most of them had only one or two thousand. 

The trends in numbers of dairy cows, beef cattle, and sheep summarize the livestock history of the District. Dairy cattle have largely won out in the competition among the forage consuming classes of livestock for the hay and pastures of the area. Dairy cows have held their own since the principal expansion of numbers ended more than 50 years ago, and milk production per cow has risen. 

Sheep, once very numerous in the area, have yielded to the dairy cow except in certain sections of especially rough terrain distant from city markets. Dairying has resisted the general contraction of agriculture in the District, including lower forage production, while sheep numbers have declined sharply. Beef cattle have done better than sheep but not so well as dairy cows; the beef enterprise has never been large in the area and has been irregularly declining since 1910. 

The great advantage the dairy cow has had in the Pittsburgh District is due to the perishability and bulkiness of fluid milk. The costs of shipping this perishable product, together with the necessity of inspecting its source, give production areas located close to market a great advantage over distant areas. This marketing factor has offset. any superiority of production conditions that more remote regions may have had.

The dairy enterprise also has certain advantages from the standpoint of individual farm management. A larger income is realized per animal from the dairy enterprise than from the beef enterprise. 

8 Census and Federal-State Crop Reporting Service statistics are not directly comparable. The latter have usually averaged higher. In 1945 the Crop Reporting Service figures were 5 per cent higher. 28

Consequently, for the family type farm prevalent in the District, a smaller investment is required to produce a given income from the dairy enterprise than from the beef enterprise. The limited investment available for most farm operations also provides more effective utilization of family labor in the dairy enterprise than in the beef enterprise. The dairy enterprise also provides a steadier income throughout the year.

Beef cattle have not had the protection that transportation and associated costs have afforded to dairying. Although fresh meat is perishable, techniques of refrigerated storage and transportation have been developed to a point where costs per unit are relatively low. 

Since meat is a more concentrated product than corn, grain-fattening of beef is more economically done in the surplus grain producing areas than in grain deficit areas. 

Beef production in the northeast has to compete with a highly specialized system in which cattle are started on the western ranges, shipped to the grain areas of the mid-west, fattened there, slaughtered, and shipped in the most concentrated form to population centers. The pasture season in most of the range area is longer than in the northeast, and in normal winters considerable feed can be obtained from grass cured on the stem. Grain finishing is done where grain is cheapest. There are no back-hauls in transportation; the product is always moving toward the great population centers. 

This has been and will continue to be keen competition for beef production in the Pittsburgh District. Beef cattle are not likely to offer a better alternative than dairy cows on most District farms. 

Many of the present beef herds of the District are owned by business men who farm as a sideline. Beef cattle are better adapted to absentee ownership than are dairy cattle. Beef cattle have been particularly attractive in recent years because beef cattle prices have been in the upper part of the long-term cycle to which these prices have been subject.

Sheep are at a distinct competitive disadvantage in most of the Pittsburgh District. Wool is a very concentrated product in an economic sense, and lamb and mutton also have low transportation costs in relation to value. Sheep can utilize the sparse range in the dry areas and high altitudes of the mountain states. Flocks can be moved from mountain ranges to the valley floors to obtain year-round grazing. Sheep numbers in the Pittsburgh District may recover somewhat from the extreme low reached recently, but the longer-term downward trend is likely to continue. 

Poultry. – Egg production in the District, as indicated by the dozens of eggs produced and the number of chickens on hand, increased continuously from 1880 to 1945, fig. 10a. Egg production has risen 29

more steadily and to a higher level than the number of chickens because of an increasing rate of production per bird.

On the basis of past trends it appears likely that the poultry enterprise, with emphasis on egg production, is likely to continue to increase in importance. There are several reasons why the poultry enterprise is important in the agriculture of the District. 

Because of nearness to market, local producers have an advantage in being able to market high quality eggs and obtain a price premium for this high quality. Any program designed to promote the development of the poultry enterprise should emphasize this factor. 

The cooperative organizations have stressed the importance of high quality in their marketing programs, and in all areas where they are operating the industry has expanded considerably. A program of consumer education on egg quality would be desirable. Additional information is also needed on consumer preferences and how these preferences can best be supplied by producers in the District.

The trend toward increased mechanization, which places the small irregular fields in the District at a disadvantage in crop production, has no direct adverse effect on the poultry enterprise since little heavy farm machinery is required for the poultry enterprise. 

The poultry enterprise also requires very little land.

The poultry enterprise is also well adapted to part-time farming. 

Although the larger poultry enterprises are more efficient in the use of labor, this is not a serious handicap to the smaller operations using spare-time family labor.

It appears unlikely that the production of poultry meat will be expanded beyond the state of being a by-product of egg production. 

Other areas appear to have advantages over the District in specialized broiler and turkey production. 

Hogs. The long time trend in the number of hogs on District farms. has been downward, hog numbers having been at their peak of four and one third million in 1890, fig. 10b. In that year a sharp downward trend began that extended through 1930. From 1930 through 1945, there was an increase. The estimated hog numbers on hand, by years, indicates that the highest point in the last 20 years, around 231,000, occurred in 19439. Between that year and 1947 the number of hogs on District farms declined more than 25 per cent. 

The long time downward trend in hog numbers indicates that hogs are relatively less profitable than other alternative agricultural enterprises in the District. Commercial pork production depends on concentrates in which the District is a deficit area. A few hogs will continue to be raised in connection with other farm enterprises but large scale hog production is not economically feasible in the District.

Hay. - Although still the most important crop in the District, hay acreage has declined from earlier years, fig. 11a. The peak acreage, slightly less than one million acres, was reached in 1889. From 1889 until 1919 there was a steady but slight downward trend. In the period from 1919 to 1924 there was an upswing followed by about a 35 per cent decline between 1924 and 1939. From 1939 to 1944 there was once again a slight upswing. 

Accompanying the long time downward trend in hay acreage, which has been less than the decline in the scale of agriculture as a whole in the District, has been a change in the kind of hay grown. 

In earlier years a large part of the hay was fed to horses and mules. 

This market was for timothy hay. Today the dairy cow is the hay market and the demand is for the higher yielding legumes of superior feeding value.

In view of the physical adaptation of the area and the importance of the dairy enterprise, it is probable that hay will continue to be the most important crop in the Pittsburgh District. There are indications that hay or grass crops may become even more important. Some interest in grassland farming is developing at present. 

The shift from low quality timothy to high quality legume hay is economically desirable. Roughage which can be produced in the area should be made to take care of as large a part of the nutritive requirements of dairy cows as possible. Grain supplements which must be purchased should be held to a minimum if District dairymen are to produce milk most economically. This shift from cash marketing of hay to utilization for milk production has been one of the most significant changes which has occurred in the agricultural development of the District.

A sharp upswing occurred between 1929 and 1934 followed by a leveling-off period lasting through 1944.

Since 1929 a reversal of the downward trend has occurred. Relatively high wheat prices since 1939 have been a factor in increasing the wheat acreage.

The long time downward trend in wheat acreage indicates that under more normal price relationships than have prevailed in recent years, less wheat will be grown as a cash crop and there will be a reduction in acreage from present levels. Buckwheat acreage on District farms rose from 1879 to 1909 and declined steadily through 1939, fig. 11b. Census data are not available for any crop year since 1939, but estimates of the Federal-State Crop Reporting Service indicate that the 1947 acreage of buckwheat was higher than the 1939 acreage. 

It is not likely that the downward trend in buckwheat acreage will be reversed. Buckwheat is not particularly valuable as a feed, is not used in the basic rotation, and is difficult to harvest with a grain binder. There is considerable land in the District, not suitable for other crops, on which farmers will probably continue to grow buckwheat. Buckwheat will also continue to be used as an emergency crop and for weed control.

Potatoes. - Potato acreage in the District has declined from a peak of 60,000 acres in 1909 to 27,000 acres in 1944, fig. 11b. In only one inter-census period since 1909, between 1929 and 1934, was there a reversal of the downward trend. The brief upswing terminated in 

1934 and was followed by a sharp decline through 1944. 

The decline in total acres of potatoes grown has been accompanied by some fundamental changes in the character of the potato enterprise on individual farms. Potato growing is becoming a more 34

specialized, commercialized operation. In earlier years, almost every 

District farm grew enough potatoes for family use plus a few to sell. One to five acre potato fields were common. Today, fields of 

10, 20 or 40 acres are becoming more common where the nature of the terrain permits. The average of 1.2 acres in 1944 indicates that numerous small operations still exist. However, the number of small growers is declining rapidly and the size of individual commercial operations is increasing.

In spite of the favorable market, the potato enterprise appears destined to become less important in the District. Small growers have accounted for a large part of District production in the past and it appears that they are going out of the potato business. The low and uncertain yields of potatoes on these farms do not return sufficient income to keep the land in potatoes rather than in alternative uses. Acreage used for potatoes can be used for corn or truck crops. 

Cabbage, peas, sweet corn, and tomatoes are possible alternatives which are being used by some farmers. 

Fruit. There were fewer bearing trees of all fruits and fewer grape vines on District farms in 1940 than there had been in 1900, fig. 11b. 

The number of apple trees had declined from four million to less than one million. Peach tree numbers exhibited an upward trend from 1890 through 1920. Between 1920 and 1940 the number of peach trees declined from 1.2 million to one quarter of a million.

Cherry tree numbers were at a maximum of 400,000 in 1910 but declined to less than 150,000 by 1940. The number of pear trees remained practically constant between 1900 and 1920 and declined relatively little between 1920 and 1935 but dropped sharply between 1935 and 1940. There were almost 350,000 plum trees in 1920 but only 100,000 in 1940. The number of grape vines was high in 1900, low in 1920, at an all time high in 1935, and at an all time low in 1940.

Throughout the District there is evidence that orcharding has passed its peak. Neglected farm orchards are numerous. Only 43 per cent of all farmers reported any kind of orchards in 1945 as compared with 69 per cent only 10 years earlier.

In recent years the disease and insect problem has made it impossible to produce marketable fruit without an intensive spraying program. This requires expensive equipment which cannot be provided economically for small operations. The downward trend in the size of the fruit enterprise in the District indicates that many of the small operations, which previously accounted for much of the fruit production in the District, have been going out of the fruit business. The remaining operations are becoming more and more specialized commercial operations. 35

There appears to be some possibilities for expansion of small fruit production. Strawberry or raspberry growing fits in well as an enterprise for the part-time farmer. Small fruit production does not require a large outlay for equipment, and a relatively large business can be conducted on a small acreage. The principal limiting factor in the production of small fruits is the large amount of labor required during the harvesting period. 

Vegetables. - Vegetable acreage has fluctuated considerably in the 

District with no definite long time trend, fig. 11b. The peak vegetable acreage occurred in 1934 when 21,501 acres were harvested. Between 1934 and 1939, vegetable acreage was reduced by half but by 1944 had increased again to 17,446 acres. 

The available census data on vegetable production are limited in value since all vegetables harvested for sale are included and no differentiation is made between vegetables for fresh market and for processing. The estimates of the Federal-State Crop Reporting Service differentiate between the two types of production but are available only in state totals. Consequently, it was not possible in this study to determine the trend in either type of vegetable production separately.

It appears unlikely that the production of vegetables will experience any considerable expansion in the District even though local production currently represents only a small proportion of total consumption. Much of the area is not physically well adapted to vegetable production as compared to competing vegetable areas. Before the advent of modern transportation and refrigeration facilities, nearness to market compensated for the relatively poor physical adaptation in the production of vegetables for fresh market. The development of the railroad refrigeration car and refrigerated trucks has modified the advantage of nearness to market. Sufficient data are not available to determine the trend and possible future development in the production of vegetables for processing. 

Horticultural specialties. — The production of horticultural specialties appears to be well adapted to the area for a small number of operators. However, the market for horticultural specialties is limited and does not appear to offer much possibility for any considerable expansion.

WHAT ABOUT THE FUTURE 

Looked at over several decades, the agriculture of the Pittsburgh District shows many striking changes. Out of these changes is emerging a more persistent farming pattern and a more stable agriculture than the past has known. Despite some contraction of acreage, the agriculture of the District is now producing products valued at ap- 36

proximately $120,000,00011 and the investment in agriculture in 1945 amounted to $373,000,000. About 50,000 persons are employed on the District farms. These are impressive figures even in one of the most industrialized areas of the United States, and an efficient and prosperous agriculture in the future will benefit not only thousands of farmers but also the economy of the District as a whole. 

This report can hardly attempt to consider the future changes in general economic conditions in the whole country that will so greatly influence both the agriculture and the industry of the District. But the agricultural trends of the past, and even more important the reasons for those trends, help us to evaluate the type of farming which will be best adapted to the District in the foreseeable future. Furthermore, study of the agriculture of the District indicates certain problems which arise from characteristics of the area and problems of efficiency on individual farms.

Programs for the improvement of agriculture in the District will yield the greatest returns if concentrated on those farm enterprises best adapted to the area. Farmers' experiences in the past are the soundest guide to the type of agriculture most suited to the District. 

Such experience, often painfully accumulated, should not be disregarded in favor of efforts to revolutionize agriculture by re-shaping the farm economy.

Fluid milk production will continue to dominate the agriculture of the District. It will be based upon the production of the hay and pasture to which the area naturally is adapted. Although dairymen of the District have been improving their businesses, considerable room for improvement still exists. Much remains to be done in increasing hay yields and in growing more leguminous hay. The carrying capacity of pastures can be improved by recommended management practices. Progress in forage production is basic to the improvement of dairying.

The average milk production per cow in the area is well below levels attainable under actual farm conditions with good management. 

Dairymen are not making full use of the Dairy Herd Improvement

Associations and Artificial Breeding Cooperatives in building up their herds. Labor efficiency in the dairy enterprise is low on many of the 

District's farms. Feeding practices are too often wasteful. Bang's disease is a serious problem in numerous herds, and less important diseases also make inroads into profits. Milk production on the majority of farms is considerably higher in the spring and early summer than in the fall and winter; such uneven production reduces profits of dairymen who are capable of better management, and it creates problems in the marketing of milk.

Poultry farming has achieved second place among the farm enterprises of the District, a position which it is likely to hold. Quality is one of the principal advantages resulting from nearness to market, and egg production and marketing methods will need to emphasize quality particularly. As in dairying, efficiency on many farms can be improved by increasing the rate of production and labor efficiency. 

Where flocks are too small for efficiency, the size of business should be enlarged. Similar problems of greater efficiency exist for the less important but still significant farm enterprises of the District. 

The need for widespread adoption of soil conserving and soil building practices will be, if anything, greater in the future than in the past. Despite the advances of recent years, farmers' awareness of the economic return to be realized from such practices lags behind expert knowledge in this field. Creating this awareness among individual farmers is perhaps the greatest of the conservation problems. 

Hive The close proximity of farmers to consumers in the Pittsburgh District will continue to mean considerable direct retailing by growers, especially those who need to enlarge small businesses by undertaking the selling function. The encouragement of direct retailing will benefit both farmers and consumers.

Another result of the close association of farming and industry is the importance of part-time farming. Many families who are farming part-time or which might do so are not fully aware of the extent to which they could use their agricultural resources to improve their living. They lack know-how in producing foods and in canning and freezing them. Such informational needs of part-time farmers have not been fully met.

Probably some sub-marginal land is still in farms and will eventually be abandoned. The development of abandoned farm land for forest and recreational uses is in the interests of the whole community.
