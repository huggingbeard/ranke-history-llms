FILRARY CHAPTER I. 

The Outermost Island 

Anna Karine Gaarden (Widtsoe) was born June 4, 1849, in the little fishing village of Titran (then Titteren) on the famous little island of Fröya (then Fröyen), the outermost island off the coast of Norway.

The island of Fröya is about thirty-two miles long by nine miles wide. Little villages, of two to a few dozen families, are located at considerable intervals along the shore, wherever an indentation of the coast line makes possible a small harbor of quiet water. 

The extreme southwest corner of the island, facing the unbounded Atlantic, juts out into the ocean. Almost on the very tip of this projection is located the village of Titran. 

Anna Gaarden was born into a display of nature such as few places offer, for on this island, naked nature walks from season to season in full majesty of beauty and power. The sensitive nature of the child was nurtured and moulded in the early impressionable years by a mighty environment.

The coast of Norway is skirted by thousands of little islands, called Skjaergaarden, meaning the home of rocky islets. The deep channels between them are few and irregular. Submarine reefs are everywhere. 

An accurate knowledge of this labyrinth of narrow, winding water passages, among countless little is- 13

lands, is so difficult to obtain that even the pilots, brought up to know a few miles of the straits intimately, fail at times to bring a vessel through safely. 

Shipwrecks are many. In recent years the government has made extensive soundings and has placed markers at dangerous points. When Anna grew up, the pilot with his incomplete chart and practical experience was still the chief dependence of the mariners. Yet through these straits came ships from all countries of earth to trade for Norwegian herring, cod, and salmon.

The islands are bare, rocky, with heavily indented coast lines. On those lying farthest away from the mainland, such as Fröya, Anna's home island, not a tree is to be found, and few shrubs more than three feet high. Decayed rock, washed into the hollows, has formed patches of soil; and moss clings to the sides of the rock; but everywhere clean-washed, gray-white domes of granite dominate the landscape. In some of the hollows, rain water has accumulated to form beautiful tjerns or small lakes. Anna's island was lowlying, treeless, rocky.

Covering the island, wherever the rocks are not exposed, growing on the patches of soil and amidst the moss, are heather and trailing plants yielding various delicious berries. The centuries have formed peat bogs which furnish ample fuel to the inhabitants. The gulf stream swings inward towards the island, ameliorating the climate, and prospering vegetation. 

When the long northern winter breaks, there can be no more glorious place than these islands. On moss, peat, soil and seemingly on the very rocks, in

15 every crevice, there appear a wealth and variety of flowers that compensate for the absence of trees and shrubs. Flowers bloom in rich color there, as nowhere else, in the midst of light, warmth, and moisture. In the spring the North Atlantic is still angry. It roars and threatens, and at times works itself into a passion. 

A visit to the flower-covered island, in the midst of the ruffled ocean, under the mild warmth of the northern sun, is an experience that lives long in memory. 

When summer comes, the ocean settles down to rest. The fishermen row, bravely, miles away from the islands. The birds come from the south, and on every high point, on every sunny hillside, are tens of thousands of snow-white or pure gray sea birds, resting in the sunshine. In the safe places the birds make nests, lay eggs, and hatch their young. The sun shines eighteen, twenty, or more hours in a day; and there is no darkness upon the land. The flowers last throughout the summer, for as the earlier ones disappear, others appear. Wild bees buzz about, gathering nectar for delicious honey. The flower garment of the island remains unchanged except for color. To the sailor, passing the island, it is a sign. of the season, as red changes to blue, blue to yellow, and yellow fades into colorless gray. Then, in late summer comes the harvest of berries, the nutritious, health-giving blueberry, the red, bitter tytteberry, near relative of the cranberry, and the delicious, semitransparent, pale-yellow multeberry, which melts in the mouth with unsurpassed delicacy of flavor. In late summer, also, the proud mother eider duck rides the slow swell of the sea, followed by her brood of

covers the island fledglings. Exceeding beauty covers throughout the summer.

Afterward come the barrenness and desolation of a rock standing alone and uncovered in mid ocean. 

The short summer is over. The flowers fade away. 

The dry berries fall to the ground. The birds, one kind at a time, gather into great flocks, and amidst much noisy cackling and flapping begin their journey to the south. The days grow shorter, the nights longer, the ocean rolls restlessly, and autumn is in the air.

In winter the islands are cheerless. The late fall storms are incessant, and frequently continue throughout the season. The waves run high and wash inward seventy-five to a hundred and fifty feet upon the shore. There are only four or five hours of daylight. Snow falls, but melts quickly, and running off the hillsides washes the rocks clean. Under the leaden winter sky the island looks a grayish brown with steelwhite spots where the washed granite appears. The fishermen stay at home or venture upon the sea at the peril of their lives. The place is sea-locked, for few vessels dare to brave the rough waters in the narrow straits. The winter passes in a forced and almost unnatural quiet, and in practically an absolute rest, except for the season when the herring crowds towards land.

At length winter breaks, spring reappears, and the scenes of the seasons are reenacted. 

The extremes of nature, on the outermost island, are appreciated with difficulty by those whose sheltered lives are spent among the dull monotony of the

17 mainland, away from the ocean. 

These varying phases of nature, from fierce intensity to easy gentleness, have reacted upon the people of the islands and made them a marked race.

Anna Karine Gaarden was born with the gift of vision and sensitive feelings. The tremendous natural manifestations of her native island thrilled her, taught her, helped her understand God and man, and fitted her finely tempered nature for noble endeavor. As she developed, her own nature seemed to reflect the strong wild winter tempest, and the flower-bedecked, sunlit summer beauty of the island.

CHAPTER II. 

The Island People 

Anna Karine Gaarden was island born and bred, and mainly of island ancestry, well-traced into the seventeenth century. Her forebears on both sides were intelligent, honest, honorable, God-fearing people. In the search of their history, no moral taint or blemish has been found. Anna was well-born, of goodly parents.

Her paternal ancestors had lived in the village of Titran for several generations-how long is not known. Her father, Peder Olsen Gaarden, was the "King's Pilot," that is, the official government pilot, as had been his father and grandfather before him.

Father to son, for three generations they had carried down the knowledge of channel turns and twists, of deep and shallow water, of submarine reefs and treacherous headlands. Their wives had been found in Titran or other villages of the island. On the paternal side, the island blood ran pure in Anna's veins.

Her maternal ancestors were partly from the island and partly from the mainland. People had lived on the island of Fröya from prehistoric days. An old church dating back to about 

1000 A. D. stood at Titran until about a century ago. 

Implements from the stone age have been uncovered there in considerable quantity. At least a thousand years before Christ, people lived upon the island. 

Where they came from is not known; perhaps many of them were of the northward moving tribes of Jacob at the time of the dispersion of Israel. Written island records go back a thousand years, showing already at that time vigorous commerce between the island and the Norwegian mainland and foreign countries. 

Scientific study has shown that since the stone age Norwegian culture has been continuous, with no evidence of new racial additions. Throughout their long history, they have been what they are today: farmers on the land and sailors and fishermen on the sea. 

The first settlers of Fröya came no doubt in search of good fishing grounds. Fishing is exceptionally fine about the islands that lie far away from the coast. The herring, cod, and salmon gather in stupendous shoals in the comparatively shallow ocean, invariably found in the neighborhood of the islands. 

It was soon discovered that to obtain the best fish and

largest catch, it was necessary to go out into the ocean, and the outlying islands became the nearest stations for food and fresh water. Titran has been famous from time immemorial as one of the finest fishing grounds off the coast of Norway. 

The islands may have attracted also adventurous spirits whose constant battle with nature had developed in them a love of places where nature shows her wildest, most dangerous moods, or the spring and summer flower-glory of the island may have created a sudden love within a visitor which led to permanent settlement. Who can tell why the human family have covered the earth from pole to pole? The children of the first settlers knew, of course, no other home, were content with their island life, its joys and terrors, and carried on throughout the centuries. 

Certainly, however, they preferred fishing to agriculture. Fair-sized areas of good soil lie inland, uncultivated to this day. Unless he can row to the farm, the fisherman has little interest in it. He is much like the rancher who cares to farm only from a horse's back. The small cultivated areas are all in the villages and near the water. Only trails lead from village to village. Visiting and business are done by boat.

The people in Titran did not live lives of isolation in Anna's day. The world visited them. In early fall, when the herring came down from the north in shoals of uncounted millions, fishermen from far away, from many lands, gathered in Titran to share in the catch, and buyers were present from many countries. It was not uncommon for five thousand persons to crowd

21 the little village of perhaps two hundred inhabitants. 

The visitors slept in their boats, spent the days in fishing, and the evenings in such recreation as the village afforded. Then there were song and dance and talk, and often drink. The views of many men and lands were aired among the gathered fishermen. 

The clear-eyed child Anna with the unquenchable thirst for knowledge learned much of the world from these annual gatherings.

The Titran herring fishery was picturesque and full of life: Hundreds of boats with as many nets, rise and fall with the rolling of the sea; shouts and laughter are passed from boat to boat, as the nets are lifted or lowered; smaller boats pass from one crew to another; herring is hoisted from the fishing boats to the purchasing ships; above all are the blue sky and the bright sun; the while seagulls flap their wings and screech at the sight of the fish drawn out of the water. Even more picturesque was the scene at night (for when a large shoal has been discovered, fishing goes on night and day), when the faint moonlight and the sleepy, rolling lanterns furnish a ghostly illumination of boats and nets and men, as unreal as the mirage of the thirsty desert.

Sometimes, when hundreds of boats and men are engaged in such fishing, a considerable distance from shore, the sun hides behind a cloud, the wind comes in from the west, the ocean grows unruly, and perhaps in an hour or two one of the fearful storms of the North Sea is raging upon the fishing ground. 

Then the herring is forgotten; the nets are cut loose; masts are taken down; and the God of the ocean, who

22 

has been forgotten in the excitement of the fishing, receives delayed petitions. Men prepare as best they can for the battle to come, which will end, they know, in death to some of their number. The attempt is made to reach the nearest land, but with the mad waves below, and the senseless wind above, the task is one of such infinite labor that many a brown-haired youngster reaches shore a white-haired man. After the ocean has calmed, the wrecked boats and the bodies of the lost drift towards shore, and are gathered in by the island dwellers. There is no laughter on the island that day. At one time or another every family on the island has been required to give a father, son, or sweetheart to the sea. None is spared.

Such storms are viewed calmly by the fishermen as part of the life that fate has willed that they shall lead. Nevertheless, though the first settlers must have been of exceptional courage, as familiarity with danger breeds contempt for it, yet the ceaseless gamble with the elements upon the North Atlantic has fixed upon the people, men and women, certain definite characteristics.

The Norwegian fisherman on the outermost island is large and strong of body. He has developed the sharp eye, careful thought, and sound judgment necessary to live in safety and to prosper amidst the tumultuous activity of the ocean. The memory of his lost nearest ones, carried away by the ocean, and the steady resigned dread of the time when he, too, shall be swallowed up, fill him with a sad sternness which is often mistaken for pride or gloom by those who do not understand the country. On the other hand, while

23 at fishing the day long in his little boat, miles from shore, he thinks dear thoughts of wife and children and other loved ones, who at home are anxiously examining the sky for signs of an approaching storm, and his heart grows warm and tender with love. The women, likewise, must steel themselves against possible disaster, and their loving thoughts go out to the men on the sea. There is a strange blending of sternness and tenderness in the island people. The man who unflinchingly will carry home the dead bodies of his friends, drowned during the latest storm, will weep at the sorrows of a child.

Naturally under such influences the people have developed an intensely religious outlook. Men cannot live in close communion with nature and witness her many moods without becoming certain that behind the colossal ocean waves, the snow-white breakers, the tearing wind, and the anxious mind; behind the gentle sunshine, the mirrored ocean, the island flowers, and the heart at peace, is a Master of Storm and Rest.

There are no infidels upon the outermost island. The people often break away from the rules of the established church, but they worship God as truly, a God they can better understand than the one defined by the priests. They keep the Sabbath day holy, read the Bible and other good books, keep up with the news of the day, and most of them can quote from memory, with an easy grace, much of the Bible and the best literature of the country. They must read to keep happy.

The people on the outermost island are far from perfect. They are independent, jealous of their

rights, and fiery of temper. The stranger is looked upon with suspicion, and if unworthy receives cold treatment. Though at times the men must battle for their lives, their labor is mainly light, and they are inclined to ease. They use few words, but mean a hundred times more than they express in words or actions. Above all, they love honesty; a thief loses complete standing; and truth is revered by them. 

Among such people Anna Karine Gaarden passed the greater part of her childhood and girlhood.

CHAPTER III. 

Childhood's Round 

The Gaarden family was small: Father, mother and two children, Anna Karine, the eldest, and Petroline Jörgine, four years younger. A brother had died at birth. At the time of Anna's birth her father's father, into the eighties, was living with the family. 

Anna's childhood was cast in pleasant places, according to the modest island standards. Her father was prosperous; she lived in the oldest and at that time the largest and best house in the village; her wants were amply satisfied; she had loving care from parents and adoring relatives and friends. 

The father, Peder Olsen Gaarden, of medium height, small of bone and delicate of feature, courteous of demeanor, was a head worker rather than a hand toiler; the type, in a different environment, of the scholar and the gentleman, a bit of a dandy in clothes and manner. His constant contact with men from many countries as he piloted foreign ships through the Norse fjords, had made him somewhat cosmopolitan in outlook; and his cordial, social disposition had made him very popular, up and down the coast. To qualify as a pilot he had secured rather more than the usual meagre education of the day. 

This also helped establish him as a leader in the community. He was a good money-maker, but spent freely, and not always wisely. His affections were 25

centered upon his little family; and he was very proud of his attractive daughters.

Daily at set hours he climbed the highest lookout and scanned the horizon with his spyglass. Were a ship in sight, signalling for the pilot, then nilly-willy, good or rough weather, off he must go and take charge of the wheel and pilot her to the safe harbor of her destination. Since the traffic was heavy past the outermost island, he was absent from home much of his time.

The mother, Beret Martha Haavig, was a tall, erect, dignified woman, of a kindly and generous disposition, who had inherited much power from her father. This man, Jörgen Arntsen Haavig, who, as said, had acquired single handed, ownership of the village of Hovik, must have been cast in a vigorous, determined mould. It is related that when his foot scraped upon the floor as he turned in his chair, the cat hid behind the cupboard! He had five sons and two daughters, all of whom he reared to stalwart and honorable maturity, and to all of whom he left a modest competence when he passed on to his fathers. 

Much of this man's gift of leadership had been passed on to Anna's mother.

There was need of a steady, governing hand in the Gaarden family. Anna's father, as pilot, was often away, sometimes weeks at a time. Besides, though the family was small, the household was large. 

Peder Olsen Gaarden not only acted as pilot, but he owned a fishing outfit, several boats, many nets and other fishing paraphernalia, and employed several men to look after his fishing venture. These men

There was other help as needed. Altogether it was a household requiring daily, wise management, such as the young wife and mother was eminently fitted to give. The father earned well and liked to spend freely; she placed a restraining hand upon his liberal arm. The family prospered under such cooperation. 

The mother, though precise and practical, was of tender feelings, and loved her two daughters devotedly. She set about to train them for happy womanhood. Play they must have under the summer sun or winter rain; but regular duties they must learn to perform also. Therefore they were trained from childhood in the round of tasks that measured the year. 

Every day the household must be fed; the house cleaned, with fresh, clean sand strewn on the living room floor; the cows fed and watered and milked, the sheep looked after, fish cleaned for the next meal; peat brought to the house for fuel; and a hundred other household tasks performed; but something must always be sent to the poor, for the mother believed that "he who giveth to the poor, lendeth to the Lord.”

In this daily round, the little girls were allowed to take part; and as they grew older were required to do their share. Nurse Engeborg, who had the girls in daily charge, let them bake playcakes in the long, dark oven, and boil fish of their own selection in little pots hung over the big grate fire; help carry the kitchen refuse, boiled and made tasty, to the cows-for, .

on the island, cows relish fish soup; and to roll and play in the clean sand to be spread over the floors where the heavy-booted fishermen lived. From infancy the claims of the day in service and labor were impressed upon the girls. 

In addition to the daily round, there were the seasonal demands, in which the girls participated or accompanied as spectators. 

In early summer, to provide fuel, the men went to the nearest peat bog, cut out small blocks, somewhat bigger than standard sized bricks, which the women and children built into loose, shapely cones for drying until fall when the light chunks of peat were carried to the village, usually by the women, children, and older men who were not out fishing. It was great fun for the girls on peat piling occasions to roam over the free country, to build houses with the peat blocks, and to hunt for berries among the heather.

The cash box was replenished at spare hours in summer by gathering boatloads of seaweed growing near the shore, and gently burning the dried seaweeds to secure the ash from which iodine was extracted in England. That was also a lesson in chemistry for the little girls.

In late fall, provision for light was made. The accumulated fat from the household, and the fat livers from small fish, not suitable for sale, were assembled, boiled in great kettles, and purified. Then long strips were dipped into the fat to make candles, or later poured into moulds, with the string held in the middle of the mould. That was fun for little girls, and old

29 ones too, especially since Nurse Engeborg allowed the children to make baby candles and Christmas candles, and all manner of things that the older folk were not allowed to do. So, a huge heap of newly-made candles was piled neatly upon a shelf of the storehouse, against the long winter nights.

The fat that was left over was mixed with alkali from the peat ashes and formed into soap for use in the household. That also was of interest to the watchful children, but Nurse was more particular, there. 

No tampering with hot soap, was the explicit order.

Nevertheless there was a chance to cut the warm soap into cakes, and to mould pieces into figures of men and cows and horses.

Then throughout the summer the garden had to be cared for. That was woman's work, or work for old men. Able-bodied men must be on the sea where fish are, for fish are money and the things that money can buy. But, digging the soil in spring, planting the seed, pulling weeds, and harvesting, were fun also. 

Since the plots were small, no one wearied of farming; and the women could always praise their own skill when white, mealy potatoes, and delicious yellow, vitamin-rich rutabagas were brought steaming upon the table.

In late summer and early fall came the indescribable delights of the berry excursions. All who were not fishing joined in. Miles and miles away, it seemed to the little tots, up among the rocks and the heather, lay the berry fields, blueberries, tytteberries, multeberries delicious to the taste. What little girls could be expected to fill their buckets when eating was so

good! After a long day, the company, blue with berry juice around the lips, and fingers stained, would return with big buckets full of berries. And the next day, and the next, when the fruit was made into mush or preserves, were days worth waiting for a whole year.

When the dark days of fall and winter came, the girls had yet more fun. Then, mother brought from the storehouse the wool of the spring clip, when the sheep, to the delight of the children, had squirmed and twisted under the shears of strong young men or women. The wool was washed, dried, and carded, all by hand. Even little girls could learn to card the wool into long fluffy rolls. Then the wool was spun into thread. Anna and Petroline had their own little spinning wheel, for Mother Gaarden believed that early training makes for a happy life. After spinning came weaving. The loom was set up in the large living room. The mother tried it out, sat queen-like before the loom, throwing the shuttles in and out, and pressing thread to thread, until she was certain that all was in order. After she had decided on patterns, colors, lengths, the more trustworthy were set to work, or an experienced old woman was employed to weave. No little girl was allowed to do more than to look on, except of course when the woman was out, then the shuttle was passed back and forth a few times, but not often for the loom was sacred, and irregular threads were soon discovered and sternly rebuked by mother. Still, in time, the girls were taught to weave. They took part in the joy of splashing and boiling white wool in a muddy liquor to have

31 it come out beautifully red or blue or yellow. At last came the sacred days of measuring, cutting, and sewing. Mother and the women took care of the underclothing and children's dresses; but a real tailor, sitting cross-legged on a big table, came in to make trousers and coats for the men. Of course, the girls had to learn to sew, and they were taught how to make their own patterns as they made clothes for their dolls.

There was so much to do that the year ended almost as soon as it began. 

The constant demand of the fishing industry, also consumed much of the time of the women, children, and old men.

The herring fishing in the fall and winter was the most exciting. Then many a person of importance "put up" with the Gaarden family; and the girls, quiet for a change, heard much big talk. 

From February to early April, after the herring season, the codfish sought shallow water to lay eggs, and the codfishing season was on. The cod, caught in nets, is cut open in a manner peculiar to fishermen, so that it will lie flat. The livers are put into large barrels for later extraction of the cod liver oil; the other refuse in other barrels for fertilizer and other uses. The cod is then salted and placed for some time in strong brine. When taken out, it is washed in sea water, laid on the clean granite rocks of the island, and turned twice a day. When sufficiently dry, the fish are piled into loose pyramids, which, to prevent rotting, are taken down and built up again every day

or two. At last, immaculately clean, almost snowwhite, the cod is dry enough for export. 

When the busy fishing season is over, the cod livers are heated in large iron kettles, surrounded by a hot water jacket, to extract the oil which is sold for medicinal purposes. The fishermen catch the cod, clean, open and salt it; but those on land have to attend to the drying. That means steady, regular attention over the summer months. 

During the summer and between the seasons, halibut, whitefish, salmon, flounders, and many other fish are caught, which make work and food for the community. Once in a while, a great whale will drive a large shoal of herring landward. Then there is real excitement. The little fish are caught; and usually at last the whale. Then there is rejoicing, for whale blubber has money value.

Such a life fastened upon the minds of the girls the activities by which men live temporally. It was invaluable training. 

There were many simple pleasures. Egg hunting in the spring, on the bare rocks sticking up out of the water near the island, and berry hunting in the fall, were among those most enjoyed. As the girls grew older, the five sturdy uncles at Hovik, would row around the island, and secure permission to take the girls for a visit to their grandparents. On such trips, on the swells of the waves, they began to understand the fear of the ocean. 

When Father Gaarden returned home from his trips, the children were hushed with eager expectancy. 

What had he brought them this time? Sometimes a

33 gem from Italy, a bit of lace from France, or a dress pattern from England. Sometimes he came with a strange fruit, yellow and fragrant. But always he came with the love light in his eyes. 

As Anna approached ten years of age, a new, glorious type of pleasure came to her. Her father began to take her with him on his trips to the mainland. She went to Kristiansund, a town with real streets; to Trondheim (formerly Trondhjem) the ancient Norwegian capital, and went inside the ageold cathedral, big enough, it seemed to her, to hold the thousands of fishermen in Titran at herring time. 

The parents saw to it that the children broadened in experience.

All this, the home life and that beyond, was permeated with religious influence. The old church on 

Titran had been destroyed; the new church had been built on the neighboring island of Hitra (at Dolm) several hours away by boat. On one or two Sundays a month, if the sea were quiet, the whole household rowed to church to attend the noon services. The day before, food was prepared, placed in the native fireless cookers packed in the boats. Early Sunday morning they would be off, rowing and singing as they rowed. The service in church was followed by lunch and an hour or two of visiting with friends. Then home again.

When the household did not row to church, which could not be done in foul weather, family services were held in the home, the master of the household leading the exercises.

In addition, a beautiful, old daily custom prevailed. Every evening, after the day's labor was over, the household, and many of the neighbors, gathered in the large dining-dwelling room of Gaarden. Father 

Gaarden, when at home, took his place at the head of the long dining table, otherwise Mother Gaarden. 

The audience partly sat and stood. A hymn was announced, usually from an old collection of songs written nearly two hundred years before by the quaint Norwegian fisherman-preacher-poet, Peder Dass. All joined in and sang with a will the tender words of devotion of the ancient poem. After the song, a chapter from the Bible was read; then another song was sung. After this preliminary service, the head of the house, or Mother Gaarden, or others called upon, recited poems and tales, from book and memory, of the rich legendary history of Norway. Sometimes, after the mails had arrived, the news of the day was given. At intervals a song was suggested or a selection played on the flute, violin, or guitar, the prevailing musical instruments on the island. About ten o'clock, Father Gaarden would arise quickly, and say, "It's time to go to bed." In five minutes the room was cleared. It was an ideal home evening, now as a tale of long passed days, which, repeated whenever work and weather permitted, stimulated the mind to good thoughts and nourished the intellect. 

The years of Anna's childhood were full and happy, and they laid secure foundations for the years to come.

CHAPTER IV. 

The Fishermaiden 

Infancy and childhood had been left behind. Anna 

Karine Gaarden, at twelve years of age, faced a radiant young womanhood.

She was pretty and gave promise of becoming a beautiful woman. Her hair was brown and wavy, her skin clear and fair, her features cast in classic mould, her dancing eyes dark blue and sparkling with intelligence. She walked erect, looking the world in the face. Her speech was natural and her manner sprightly. The passer-by looked at her twice. 

She had been endowed with notable qualities which the natural and human environment of the island, and the steady routine at home, could not overshadow, only direct and perfect. She was individualistic from childhood, knew her own mind, and made her own decisions. She was impatient with traditional practices, which her superb self-reliance enabled her to ignore. She fought her battles with courage, never counting the cost, if she felt herself to be in the right. 

Her temper flashed quickly at opposition, but subsided as promptly. She loved with intensity. She was as clean in mind and body as the white foam of the wind-driven waves of the ocean, and as firm for right and truth as the storm-washed granite of her native island. Her many-faceted nature, surcharged with 35

desire for freedom and truth, was as the seabird, winging his free, unhindered way over wave and reef. 

She had been blessed with a clear intelligence, coupled with sound judgment. This as she grew older, and the ways of childhood were passed, made her the easy leader of her group, and, in time, of the village. 

Her sympathy with those of her kind in sorrow or distress was poignant. Many a surreptitious basket of goodies did she carry from her mother's larder to the poor. And many a time did the mother, herself charitable but made of sterner stuff, explain that charity must be given wisely, and that little girls must ask permission before they break into household stores. Those in sorrow or distress recognized the genuine sympathy that radiated from their Anna. 

The poor and the broken in spirit sought her out to her last day.

Beauty she loved passionately. It found expression in the flower garden that she made as a child on the patch of soil in front of the house, the only one in the village. Her father had brought home packets of seed, which, carefully planted and tended by Anna, grew into new, strange plants. The villagers could not understand a child who would raise flowers on an island covered all summer long with wild flowers. 

Throughout her life, wherever she went, she had her flowers, in window pots, if no land were available, but, preferably, in a garden where the soil could be turned and seed planted.

The self-willed girl sat up through long sunlighted summer nights, reading and rereading sagas of the north, stories of the south, poetry and prose

From early girlhood she wrote her thoughts, mostly in the rhythm and rhyme of the poet. Forever she hungered for more knowledge, more truth, more progress, for greater vistas of the coming day. Therefore, she was never quite satisfied. There was always another step to take. She knew the meaning of "noble discontent."

She was full of pranks. She must have led the village a merry chase. The wise ones said, "Peder

Gaarden (Per Gaarn in the vernacular) has some job on his hands in that girl." But they loved her; she galvanized them into joy. After ninety years, she is still a bright memory there. That father and mother had to take her severely in hand at times was only to be expected. 

The world is organized for the average, not for the exceptional person. 

From her earliest days she had looked upward to

God. She was always religious, prayerful, and saw divine power in the flash of the elements and in the workings of human life. Orthodox she was not, for she laughed at the minister's ruffled collar, and thought that divine service at home was nearly as good as in church. But she was reverent of sacred things.

Education in those days, and therefore, on the outermost island, was scanty. Yet, for many years,

the island people had provided teachers for their children. The youth who did not accept the educational opportunities offered was held in disrepute. The villages were small, means were smaller, and the system of education was not perfected as it is today. One teacher was employed for two or three villages. He traveled from one to the other, spending, perhaps, a week in each village every other week, or once in three weeks. Surprisingly good results were obtained by these peripatetic teachers. Their pupils learned the fundamentals as well as do the modern youth with daily uninterrupted instruction. 

Anna attended these schools regularly and voraciously. She pounced upon new truth as a starved man upon food. Her mother and nurse had taught her to read and write very early, so that she made good use of the school whenever it came. 

When she was about twelve years old, a new teacher was employed for Titran and the neighboring villages. It was said that he was a young man who had come from the mainland some years ago, why, no one knew, and who had gone away for study at

Andersen Widtsoe, was also new to Titran people.

CHAPTER V. 

The Schoolmaster's Story 

The schoolmaster who won the heart and hand of the fishermaiden also had a story to be told. 

John Andersen Widtsoe was born June 9, 1840, in Öien, in the district of Hemne (then Hevne), some forty-five English miles south of Trondheim. 

The name Widtsoe was taken from a well-known estate or large farm, on which members of the family had lived.

His paternal ancestors, for many generations, had dwelt in the upland, fertile valleys called Örkedalen and Meldalen, not far from Trondheim. While they had moved about considerably, it had been within a relatively small area. They had been farmers, schoolteachers, and small public office holders. Several had held a position similar to probate judge (lensmand). 

They had prospered, had good reputations, and enjoyed the confidence of the community. They were inclined to office rather than field work. John's father, Anders Johnsen Widtsoe, an apprenticed carpenter, had broken away from the old family haunts, and had settled in Trondheim. His name appears as a carpenter in the old cathedral record. As time went on, he became a contractor and builder with much success. The house which he built for himself and family in Trondheim in 1869 still stands as an evidence of his technical skill and ambitious ideals. He was well 43

known and respected in Trondheim. He died of a fall from the old mission house and hospital in Bergen which he and another had contracted to build. He lies buried in Bergen.

John's maternal ancestors also came from the valleys south of Trondheim. Some years before John's birth, however, his mother's brothers and other members of the family had moved to Trondheim. That may have been why Anders Widtsoe moved there also. His mother's nephews became very prominent in the community. His cousin, Johan Peter Bye, was idolized by the common people; he was returned as the city's representative in the Storthing (Congress), until at last he refused to go again. Another cousin became a priest of much note. Other relatives were in the professional and artistic field. This branch of the family originated in Rönne, Denmark, where the family home still stands. The ancestor who came to 

Norway fled there to hide in the mountain valleys, for he had committed political offenses in Denmark. 

This ancestor was a man of much learning, whose memory has been handed down for two hundred years among the people.

The Anders Johnsen Widtsoe family consisted of the father and mother and three children, Johanna the eldest, John, and Sivert the youngest. When 

John was eleven years old, on November 11, 1851, his mother died, and the housekeeping duties fell upon his sister, then about sixteen years old. 

A few years later the father married again. Then trouble began. The children of the first wife could not agree with their stepmother. Especially was John

45 unwilling to accept the new mother of the house. He was not of the impetuous, fiery type, but determined and persistent. His mind was made up; he could not live happily in the new household. It is probable that his uncles and aunts, his mother's brothers and sisters, who had not approved of the woman chosen to take their beloved sister's place, encouraged the lad's discontent. He would leave home and stay for weeks with his downtown relatives, without any by your leave from either father or stepmother. The boy was beyond the father's control, which gave deep concern and sorrow to Anders Widtsoe who loved his eldest son.

A story from those days is illuminating. According to the custom of the Lutheran Church to which the family belonged, children when about fourteen years of age are confirmed into the church. This ceremony is preceded by six months' or a year's study with the priest, at regular weekly intervals. Confirmation depends upon whether the priest is satisfied that the child is able to answer certain questions concerning the faith. Then, on confirmation day the candidates are lined up in the church aisle, the boys on one side, the girls on another, and the ceremony of confirmation is consummated.

John was approaching the age of confirmation, and he was urged to attend the classes of the priest. 

However, he was indifferent to his father's pleadings. 

He might or might not attend the classes. His father was in real distress. John would probably disgrace himself and fail to be confirmed at the usual time.

Shortly before the confirmation day, John announced that he had seen the priest a few times and that he was to be confirmed. The happy father gladly provided the son with the requisite new suit. On confirmation day the father went to church to witness the ceremony, but with some misgiving. John had been wayward of late, seldom at home. The children were lined up in the aisle as usual. The father sat down rather wearily in a near pew and began to look for his son. The children were always arranged in order of the excellence of their knowledge. The father looked closely at the lower end of the line, where his run-away son should be. He was not there. The father's heart sank. Was it possible that John had failed at the last moment, and was not to be confirmed? That would be humiliation! The father's eyes wandered up the line, from one to the other; no 

John, no John! This was humiliation indeed! His gaze fell for an instant on the lad at the head of the class, the one of greatest excellence. He looked again. 

No, there was no mistake. There, at the head of the line, the most honored of all, stood John, stiff in a new suit and white collar. The scamp, bright as a new dollar, had kept his father in ignorance of his industrious study for confirmation. Anders Widtsoe looked at his son once more; then took off his glasses and wiped his eyes.

The newcomer was, however, able to shift for himself. He was strong of body, of quick intelligence, ready and willing to do anything required of him.

There were lecture halls and class rooms and a library and a small church, and a half-dozen venerable men, wise men, professors, who opened a new world, no, new worlds, to the group of picked young men. The coming leaders of the land were being trained there. Young John A. Widtsoe, as he usually wrote his name, revelled in the life. 

Soon the memory of his unhappy home was dulled. 

He began to understand that there were two sides to every question. Why worry about little things when there was so much to learn, mathematics, languages, drawing, surveying, methods of teaching, singing—yes, always singing- and all the other things a teacher had to learn?

After two years John was back in Fröya, sobered, enlightened, full of courage, filled with ideals for the future. He was about twenty-one years of age, and ready for the fray. There was only one fly in the ointment. To get the full certificate from the seminarium, he would have to attend two more years. He was certified as a teacher, but he wanted all that the school had to give. For that he had to save. 

One of his earliest assignments was to Fröya, where in his first class he met the clever little girl who was to become his wife. He developed into a splendid teacher. His fame became island-wide. 

There was no priest on the island. The schoolteacher became the advisor and helper of the people in matters involving deeds, wills, letter-writing, and all sorts

And he called upon the aged and the sick; and planned with parents for the welfare of their children. His quiet humor and wise suggestions made the family feel better for days. 

He had not been there long before he began the work of social reform which has become of lasting benefit to the community, and to its thousands of visitors, and which will be recounted later. 

Eight years he served that end of the island and grew in the affections of the people, until today, more than seventy years later, to be his relative is to find open doors in Titran and vicinity. 

Year after year he planned to go back to school to finish his work, but the people begged him to stay a little longer; he knew enough for them; and then the fishermaiden had not yet plighted her troth to him.

Eight years after his arrival, they became engaged; and began to plan for life together. She urged him, though the years would be lonely, to return to school and finish the work there. 

The fishermaiden was to be his! With a song on his lips and in his heart, he went away another two years. Rich years they were. He was ripened now. Powerful friendships were established. He had a definite objective: wife and home and family. His love letters to the fishermaiden, often in rhyme-for he, too, had the gift of the poet-reveal in beautiful words and rich imagery a nature of delicacy and no-

The young man must have been beloved by his fellows at the seminarium, for in his autograph album, as it would be called in our day, is a series of half a hundred tributes, implying a devotion and appreciation such as seldom comes from one man to another. 

He graduated with high honors, and came back to Fröya. Soon afterward he and Anna Gaarden were married.

CHAPTER VI. 

The Happy Years 

On December 29, 1870, Anna Karine Gaarden, then about twenty-one and a half years old, was marride to John Andersen Widtsoe, who was nine years older. The marriage ceremony was performed in 

Dolm Church, on the island of Hitra, the church at which Anna had worshipped during her whole life. 

The wedding party rowed over the sea and across the sound to the church where old Pastor Brodtkorb, who had baptized Anna as an infant, officiated at the ceremony. Afterwards the bridal party rowed back to 

Titran, and the whole village celebrated the occasion. 

Radiantly happy was the beautiful bride. The future was golden, touched with rose. But, her father, Peder 

Olsen Gaarden, thought sadly of bygone days. To him, a cloud hung over the future. He would now be more alone than ever. And sister Petroline wondered when her turn would come.

The schoolmaster had managed to build a large two-story house at Dalöe across the island, an hour's walk. It was built for the two-fold purpose of supplying his family with a home, and to furnish school rooms to which the neighboring children for some miles might come for instruction. He was breaking down the practice of the traveling teacher. Into this new house, in which none had lived before, and which the bride had furnished to suit herself, with her own 51

money, the schoolmaster took the fishermaiden to have and to hold forever. The happy years began. 

When the schoolmaster as a young man had come to Titran and witnessed the gathering of thousands of fishermen at various seasons of the year, he was stirred into deep thinking. The people themselves needed intellectual awakening; and the visitors needed social supervision. As it was, there were no opportunities for social relaxation or intellectual advancement, and card playing and drinking became the fishermen's chief amusements. He resolved to take a hand in the matter and bring about reform. The people must be lifted. That was his obligation as an educated man.

At that time an organization for popular education in Norway was being effected, The Society for the Promotion of Popular Enlightenment (Selskabet for Folkeoplysningens Fremme). With this movement he was associated and was in fact its representative on the islands. The society published choice books at cost prices, within the reach of the poorest. In fact, the society, became a great educational power in the land. The schoolmaster sought to place these inexpensive good books in the homes of the people. His own library was at public disposal. He would take a cherished volume of poems, and read throughout the evening to a group assembled at a family gathering. 

Thus he whetted their appetites for good reading. 

He soon found, however, that this was not enough. 

The houses were small, and he could as well speak to one or two hundred as to twenty or thirty. Therefore he conceived the idea of constructing a large hall on

THE HAPPY YEARS 

53 Titran, which on week days could be used as reading room and community center, in the evenings for lectures and musical events, and on Sunday for sacred services. He set about to secure the necessary funds, was successful in so doing, and finally had the satisfaction of seeing it built under his supervision. It was a duplicate of the assembly hall at the seminarium, eight-sided to get the maximum space with the least materials. It was expensive to ship timber and boards by boat from the mainland.

The experiment was a huge success. The village people, and the visitors in the herring and cod season, made use of the hall. The schoolmaster placed his books at the disposal of the readers; he gave lectures on various subjects in the evenings or conducted musical affairs; and on Sundays he read the service of the 

Lutheran Church. The institution became a powerful, reforming, upbuilding factor in the community. 

The building still stands, a silent monument to the eager, successful idealism of a young man who set out to help his fellow man. It was one of the many precursors of the numerous community centers now found in every country. The schoolmaster, in his corner of the world, was a forerunner of the modern social worker.

A contemporary, writing in these recent days, thus speaks of the service rendered by the schoolmaster to the people on the outermost island: "John 

A. Widtsoe created life, so to speak, on these islands, where life had almost ceased; he started a movement to lift the people, which even today shows the power

that was in him and his labor. He awakened the people as from a slumber." 

On February 23, 1871, Peder Olsen Gaarden died. His life had never been filled since his wife had passed away, eleven years before. When Anna married, it seemed that little remained to him in life. 

Petroline was then away attending school. Peder

Gaarden was tired, and he was taken home. 

The death of Anna's father pointed to a new life for the young couple. After the first two years of teaching on the island, the schoolmaster had begun to look towards new fields for the exercise of his talents. He wanted to go to one of the mainland cities, where the schools were graded and better professional opportunities were afforded. He had the urge and the gift to write, in prose and poetry, and wanted to be near places where papers and books were published. He also wanted to spend the year necessary to take a degree from the University of Oslo (then, Christiania), and this could be more easily done with the salary of the larger schools of the mainland. The pleadings of the people and the hope of winning Anna had kept him on the island, but always with the feeling that some day he must get away. When he married, he settled on the island because both he and 

Anna felt it was not quite right to leave her father alone. When he died, the situation changed. Anna herself wanted to try her wings. The world was big, and she really knew only the islands.

CHAPTER VII. 

The Schoolmaster Departs 

Early in February, 1878, when the baby Osborne was less than two months old, the schoolmaster was taken suddenly ill while in the classroom. His intense suffering was diagnosed as due to intussusception, or a telescoped intestine. The local profession did all it could for him; surgical aid, not then developed greatly, was not available. Inevitably the end crept upon him for three days. On February 14, 1878, the schoolmaster died. He was then nearly thirty-eight years of age; his wife was nearly twenty-nine.

There was lamentation throughout the city, for the schoolmaster had become greatly loved. He was buried from the schoolhouse which represented the ideals of his life, of his noble profession. The chapel and the streets outside were crowded, and hundreds followed the procession to the grave. The baby, of course, was left at home in the cradle, but the fishermaiden and her oldest son stood by the open grave while the cold words of the church funeral service were spoken, "Dust thou art, to dust returnest," with no promise of a future meeting in a happier place than man's earth. It was cold compared with the living fire of the ancient prophets; and at times she failed to reconcile the teachings of the priest with those that she found in the Bible. Her great sorrow had driven her to dig deep down near the foundations of truth. 

Thus her soul was prepared for the events to come.

CHAPTER VIII. 

The Shoemaker Bears His Testimony 

Soon after her arrival in Trondheim, Anna Widtsoe rented an apartment for herself and her two boys on Steensbakken (Steens Hill) which was then being invaded by residences. Above the dwelling, further up the hill, stood the old historic fortress; below, and far into the distance, lay the beautiful panorama of the old city, the thousand year old cathedral standing high, the harbor, the clustered ships, the fjord, and all around, except where the fjord zigzagged out toward the ocean, a background of fir-covered hills. It was beautiful to look upon at all times of the year. 

In these two rooms she proceeded to make a home and to look the future in the face. What was left of the schoolmaster's library, except for a half-hundred choice selections, she had stored in Grandfather Widtsoe's house lower down the hill. She had added a little to the treasured articles of furniture brought from Namsos. Equipment for dressmaking had been provided. She was kept busy enough with household cares, dressmaking, and reading and studying her Bible, and trying to reconstruct her shattered world.

CHAPTER IX. 

In the Gospel Net The widow was now well in the gospel net. The news that Anna Widtsoe had become a Mormon spread rapidly among her friends and relatives in Trondheim, and soon was common gossip in Fröya, her home island. She was not the type to hide her joy in the new-found truth. The inevitable happened. 

The schoolmaster's friends, though they pitied the widow and her boys, could not afford to become smudged by contact with Mormonism. All but a few heroic ones turned away from her. The priests threatened to have her pension cancelled unless she came back into the state church. 

Her husband's relatives, as had the friends, first reasoned with her, and pointed out her worldly folly; but pursued by her Biblical proofs and clear argument they left her, and tried to forget her. There was no more visiting between the little family and the relatives. The schoolmaster's oldest and only own sister, who had married and was living near Oslo, and who had deeply loved her brother John, pleaded with the widow to forget the insanity which possessed her. 

In sheer distress, the new convert took a trip to the island, to find peace among her own people. To her amazement, her dear ones there, who had heard of her scandalous behavior, would have nothing to do with her. Their hearts and houses were closed against 70

her. Those whom she had fed and clothed and nursed preferred not to speak to her. A few poor, humble folk gave her shelter and listened reservedly to her tale of God's restored gospel.

Heavy-hearted, slightly embittered, but more determined than ever to keep the commandments of the Lord, she returned to her rooms in Trondheim. Her faithful, devoted sister Petroline was then living with her, but even she could not understand the behavior of her sister. It seemed so foolish. Truly the fishermaiden was alone in the world. But, she had found the truth; she had entered God's earthly kingdom; she was preparing for a great eternal blessing; she trusted the Lord.

The perplexing thing was that the plain, simple principles of the gospel did not seem to be comprehended by her friends and relatives. It seemed to her that the truths of the gospel were so clear that no one with intelligence could fail to understand them. 

How could it be so? She had yet to learn that truth is neither comprehended nor accepted unless the man desires to know the truth and values it above all else. 

That is the law in all pursuits of truth, within and without religion.

Anna Widtsoe turned to the little branch of Latter-day Saints and took hold of its activities. Her fresh outlook on the gospel, her experience and quick intelligence, and especially her deep devotion to the Latter-day cause, helped to vitalize the branch. Soon, however, a restless spirit within her turned her thoughts, then her desires, and at last her hopes, to the land in the far west which her people-the Latter-day Saints-had wrested from the desert, and were now inhabiting as the headquarters of the Church. There was Zion, a promised land; there were thousands who like herself in love of truth had accepted the restored gospel; there were prophets and apostles, bearers of the eternal Priesthood of God. 

She must go there! The spirit of gathering was upon her.

This matter also required careful consideration. 

She loved her native land, the sea and the mountains, the sagas and the song, the sturdy people; and in this land rested her forefathers; and here she had thought to rest; and here lived her loved ones, albeit they disdained her religion. The far-distant land, with a new language and foreign customs would present new problems possibly beyond her power to conquer. She would lose her pension, that she had been told offi-

cially. Her boys? That was the real question. Would they have the chance in the new land that they would have here? Their futures were fairly well assured here, given reasonable intelligence and good behavior. 

The question of emigration, of gathering, was weighed and considered, thought about and discussed. 

Her decision was of course a foregone conclusion. 

She decided to break away from the past, and take a chance on the future, and to gather with her people.

Her one deep sorrow was that she would have to leave her only and beloved sister, who, of a thoroughly independent mind, could not yet convince herself that the gospel embraced by her sister was really true.

At length, in October, she set forth upon the journey. Never had Norway looked so beautiful to her.

Only those who have so left their native hearth can understand the tugging at the heartstrands, when the land of birth and home fades from view. It does not matter how much promise the new land offers. Human nature will have its way. It has been the lot of the Latter-day Saints ever to be torn from the moorings of home, whether in America or foreign lands--

but in the end they have rejoiced to sacrifice for truth and righteousness.

But the widow reached Oslo in safety with her two sons and three trunks.

The party embarked on the S. S. Wisconsin on October 27, 1883, and reached New York after a rough but otherwise uneventful voyage on November 7, 1883. The next day the long railroad trip from New York to Logan, Utah, was begun.

While the trip was long, it was interesting to the widow and her two sons. New countries, constant change, a new language, strange sights and sounds made time pass quickly. True, every once in a while there was a tug at the heart, the anticipation of the unknown, but it was not colored with fear or heaviness. Safe and sound, eager for the next experiences, the party arrived at their destination, the promised valleys of the Rocky Mountains.

CHAPTER X. 

In a New Land 

On Saturday, November 15, 1883, Anna Gaarden

Widtsoe and her two sons landed in Logan, Utah, their destination.

So this was Zion! This was the promised land, the new world! It was a bright, sunlit day. The mountains lay under a mild haze, but the valley air was clear. The widow looked long at the blue sky and the bare, high mountains. She was satisfied. Mountains, sky, and sunshine were as symbols of the strength and beauty and light of the message that had come to her. The love of the West begotten in her soul that day never died. Her heart was glad.

Here were the Saints of God, with whom she should spend her life. What more could she ask?

The first two or three days were spent under the hospitable roof of Bishop Anthon L. Skanchy and his family; after which she moved into a one-room house in a large yard, on the southern edge of the city. 

Bishop Skanchy who had brought her into the Church helped her secure the necessary furniture for meagre but comfortable living. The widow and her children playfully laid off the floor of their one room into four parts, living room, dining room, bedroom and kitchen. 

Blessed with the power of imagination, the little family soon saw the tiny house as a mansion. New life 78

began happily, though the little money remaining was fast melting away.

Within six weeks after arriving in Logan, the widow had a severe shock and trial. Her elder son 

John was set upon by a mad dog that bit and tore the flesh of legs and arms so seriously that the boy was kept to his bed for six weeks. February was well over before John was able to move about again in the open air. During this time, the mother was confined to the house as nurse, for there was no one to call upon for help, and the real beginning of the new life was somewhat delayed. But Anna Widtsoe knew that the evil one would gladly destroy her and hers, and defeat their righteous endeavors, so she rejoiced that Satan had been foiled once again.

That winter, which was a bitterly cold one, the little emigrant family tasted real poverty for the first time. As soon as John was well on his feet again, she set about to earn a living for herself and her children. 

She took up dressmaking, her old trade. Soon plenty of work came to her, for she sewed well and her artistic sense found its way into the clothes she made. 

But, under pioneer conditions, the pay was small (one dollar and a half was a good price for the making of a gown), and she had to work far into the night, every night, straining her eyes against the weak coal oil light, to earn enough for the support of the family. 

It was toil by day and night. The first year in Zion was well over before a hand-driven sewing machine was secured. That was a mighty stride into convenience and labor economy. When John's wounds were healed in March, he was employed for some time,

again through the help of Bishop Skanchy, in a sawmill, at a few dollars a week. Between them, mother and son earned enough for the needs of the family. 

Health waited upon them. The younger son, Osborne, grew and played and was the joy of the family and the neighbors.

Actual want there was not. The organization of the Church provided against suffering, should it occur, and kindly friends and neighbors were anxious to give the newcomers a helping hand. One evening during the first, bitter cold winter in Logan, the widow put her last stick of wood into the stove. Then she called her two boys to her side. Together they kneeled down and told the Lord of their condition, and asked him for fuel so that they might keep warm. 

Within five minutes after their prayer was over, there was a knock on the door, and Brother Larsen, living some distance up the street, and barely an acquaintance, came in with a big sack of coal upon his back, for the emigrant family. The prayer had been answered, almost before it was uttered. The luxury of that coal, the first they had in Zion, and the knowledge that the Lord was near, made the evening one of rare joy.

It was pride, foolish pride, perhaps, rather than necessity, that on several occasions led the little family in those early days to the brink of want. In the spring of 1884, Anna Widtsoe moved into a two-room house a noble rise in fortune-nearer the business centre and in another ward, so that her customers would not have so far to go. Collections were slow one month; John was out of work. At last the family

For three weeks, had only a sack of "shorts" left. they lived on "shorts" and water. Never before, in man's history, had "shorts" been served in so many different forms as the widow's ingenuity devised. 

Nevertheless, the diet was frightfully monotonous. 

They did not know, then, the high nutritive value of "shorts." One day, after nearly three weeks of rations on "shorts" and water, the Sixth Ward had a ward reunion. A Mormon ward reunion includes a feast of nearly all the good things of earth. When the meal was ended, there was food left over, and the goodhearted Bishop Skanchy-and no man was ever kinder to the poor-filled a large basket with roast chicken and lamb, vegetables and bread, cakes and desserts, and sent them by a messenger to the widow and her sons, who now lived in the First Ward. When the "shorts"-filled boys saw the well-filled basket of goodies uncovered upon their table there was eager, anticipatory swallowing, and much inward joy; all of which was changed to darkest despair when the widow drew herself up to her full height and said, "Brother, please take the basket back to the bishop with my thanks, and say to him that Sister Widtsoe is not a beggar, yet." To the boys, the departure of that basket with its contents was the darkest moment of their first years in Zion. It seemed a foolish pride, but perhaps it was better for the family to nurse a stubborn independence.

The language presented another problem, one to be overcome if progress was to be made. Sister Widtsoe knew only a few words of English; John had had a little in school. Osborne was not yet of school age.

22 However, they learned quickly, and in inverse proportions to age. Osborne picked up the language at once as it seemed; John, a little more slowly, and the mother, least rapidly. Spoken language, a matter of memory and imitation, is acquired most easily in youth. A copy of Leslies' Weekly became the first instructor, followed by the Book of Mormon and the Bible. By the time the first year in Zion was over, the little family had pretty well battered down the language wall. The mother, however, never overcame the accent inherited from the language of her childhood. But she learned to love the English language.

The most difficult phase of learning English for daily and constant use was the necessity of forsaking the mother tongue. Norwegian is an expressive and a beautiful language, well suited to clothe noble ideas in prose or poetry. It has a fine literature, reaching into the twilight of north European history. The thoughts and feelings, the romance and reality of life become interwoven with the language one speaks. 

Those who in mature years have not been obliged to adopt a new language for constant use cannot well comprehend the sacrifice that such a change entails. 

Sister Widtsoe accepted the requirement without hesitation, for it was part of her progress within the new truth; but she took good care that both of her boys retained their knowledge of Norwegian. Also, she yielded to the temptation of meeting with other Scandinavians to speak in the language that they knew best.

CHAPTER XI. 

The First Decade in Zion 

After the first year, life in Zion was much easier for the little immigrant family. They had found work; the barrier of the foreign tongue was breaking down; and they began to understand life as it was lived in Zion. The widow set about to plan for the future. Two things were uppermost in her mind: 

She and her children must learn to understand the principles of the gospel and obey its precepts, and she must educate her children. Her obligations to her God and to the schoolmaster, the father of her children, were ever before her.

She rejoiced in the gospel and in life within the Church. She was a regular attendant at meetings, no matter how busy she was. The Sunday afternoon sacrament meeting in the Logan Tabernacle was the favorite, though she delighted also in the less formal 

Sunday evening ward meeting. It was thrilling to her to sit under the voices of men who held the holy Priesthood, and faithful women who were as priestesses to her. But, she never forgot that they were but men and women through whom the Lord did His earthly work, and as such might make mistakes. Her northern canniness saved her from many a shock.

She joined the Relief Society when she first arrived and served regularly as a Relief Society teacher.

She had much to learn about the gospel. The two years of investigation had been devoted primarily to the fundamental principles leading to entrance into the Church. For example, she had not been taught the Word of Wisdom, except as it had been mentioned casually in gospel conversations. Now, she began to understand its real meaning and purpose and the necessity of obeying it since it was the desire of the Father that His children should heed it. Following the custom of most of her country people she had drunk coffee from her childhood and was an occasional user of tea. Alcoholic beverages she did not use. She set about to give up the use of tea and coffee but found it difficult. When she sewed every night far beyond midnight, the cup of coffee seemed to freshen her, she thought. After a two months' struggle, she came home one day, having given serious consideration to the Word of Wisdom problem. Her mind was made up. She stood in the middle of the room and said aloud, "Never again. Get behind me, Satan!" and, walking briskly to her cupboard, took out the packages of coffee and tea and threw them on the fire. light upon the whole meaning of the gospel as well as upon the nature of God. The day on which temple work was done for her father, mother, and husband, was the notable day of that year to the family.

Skanchy, she and her son John received tickets admitting them to the services. The spiritual enrichment of the day was ample reward for whatever sacrifices she had had to make for the gospel. It seemed to her as if the heavens had opened and the very voice of God had been heard by her. She had never been so near the heavenly hosts before. The same spirit was upon her when she received her own endowments on April 15, 1887.

Activity in the Church of God was a privilege, not a duty so she taught her children.

CHAPTER XII. 

The Wheel Turns 

The three years after the autumn of 1894 were happy ones. The widow kept house for her two sons. 

Her sister was in Salt Lake City, within easy reach. 

Finances were easier; a new kitchen was added, making the house a comfortable five-room cottage. John was well employed at the Utah State Agricultural College, and was giving satisfaction. Osborne was making a brilliant record as a student in the same school. It was with much gratitude in her heart that she heard her son Osborne deliver the valedictory address in behalf of his class, when he graduated in June 1897. The toil of the past was bringing its recompense.

During this time, as always, she was firm in her devotion to the gospel. The eternal truth restored through the Prophet Joseph Smith was the joy of her life. CHAPTER XIII. 

Preaching the Gospel 

The sisters left Salt Lake City for the mission field on June 24, 1903. As the train chugged through the canyons that day, the panorama of her life swept before the widow's eyes. She had reason to know that 

He "who is mighty to take, is great to give," and her heart swelled within her. Though she had at times rebelled at her trials, and questioned eternal justice, she was being taught in unmistakable terms that God is good-He had been good to her, and now He was giving her this great opportunity of bearing witness to the truth she had found, to her relatives, friends, and countrymen in Norway. June 24, 1903, was a memorable day for her. She scarcely foresaw, however, that four years and a half would elapse before she should again see the glow of the setting sun upon the mountains of the West.

The sisters kept a somewhat detailed diary of their missionary experiences, which with its naive, direct style is good reading. The indefatigable industry and selfless devotion displayed by these women in their journal is almost unbelievable. They certainly felt that they were on the Lord's errand, and could waste no time nor opportunity. Day after day for more than four years and a half, they labored with never a day of vacation or rest, except for such change and travel as accompanied the performance of their duties. 103

It is a marvelous record, an example to the host of younger men and women who go into the mission field.

Though both sisters were more than fifty years of age, they were eager for knowledge, keenly alive to every new experience, and responsive to the beauty, majesty or power about them. They confirmed the doctrine of this later day, that adults may learn as well as youth, and to the very end of life. 

On the outward journey they record the interesting places visited in Omaha, Chicago, and Boston. 

They were overawed with the power of the Niagara Falls, they stood gratefully at Cambridge, Massachusetts, under the old elm by which Washington took command of the American forces of the Revolution. 

Harvard University they inspected carefully for it was there that John and Osborne had studied and graduated. Likewise, across the ocean and in Europe they were interested in all that they saw. In spirit they were young.

Copenhagen, where they arrived July 13, 1903, was at that time the home of the administrative office of the Scandinavian Mission composed of Norway, Denmark, and Sweden. There the sisters met their old friend and father in the gospel, Bishop A. L. 

Skanchy, then president of the Scandinavian Mission.

There also they received their first assignment to labor.

The sisters were among the first regular women missionaries to Norway. Other women missionaries had usually accompanied their husbands. The slanderous word had gone forth into every Norwegian village that the Mormons were an immoral people, who sent out missionaries to gather girls to Utah for unlawful purposes. Tales of the wildest and vilest kind had been circulated about the Mormons and their practices. So widely and with such apparent authority had these false rumors spread that the population was up in arms against the Latter-day Saints. Under these conditions it was of great help to be able to present two women who had gone to Utah in their early maturity, had remained unmarried, and had come back to tell the truth about the Mormons. They awakened interest everywhere. Men might lie, said the people, but these women were likely to tell the

truth. The sisters told their story in every accessible village, they gave innumerable lectures on women among the Mormons, they traveled, visited friends and members, and often, because of their connections, had access to influential people. Perhaps the most important phase of their mission was to allay the prejudice created through untruthful tales about the Mormons. At any rate from one end of the land to the other was heralded the story of these women, of a respectable Norse family. The evidence that they produced, and they had a well-organized group of facts, figures, pictures, and personal experiences, tended to silence for the time at least the traducers of the Mormons. They entered into competition with one of these vendors of untruth, an apostate from the Church, silenced him, then visited him, and brought him to tearful acknowledgment of his error. 

The refutation of lies did not, however, bring Mormon popularity. The prejudice was too deep for that. In Trondheim, the home city of the widow's husband, the sisters became well known. There, they and the family had relatives. Trondheim is the ancient capital of Norway, where the king even now must go to be crowned, and it is the home of the finest cathedral in all northern Europe. Many distinguished men have claimed Trondheim as their home. Besides, the city lies at the finest fjord and harbor facilities in central Norway. These factors and others have given the people a foolish feeling of aristocracy. They, in the noted cathedral city, could have nothing to do with the Mormons! They could not put a Mormon

The priests as teachers of the people were often unwilling or unable to do even the duty of Christian men. At one time the sisters visited the church at 

Dolm on the island of Hitra (formerly Hitteren) in search of genealogy. It was the church in which both had been christened, in which both had been confirmed, and in which the widow was married to the schoolmaster. For generations the family of the sisters had supported the church. Yet, it was with extreme reluctance that the priest permitted them to examine the records to the use of which they were lawfully entitled. And when they asked if they might occupy a room during their stay, in the extensive buildings at the priest's disposal, they were refused, and he explained that he dared not give help or shelter to anyone of the Mormon faith. As a result, since the sisters were determined to secure all available family names, they had to cross day after day, morning and evening, an ugly, restless body of the ocean. 

So dangerous was the crossing that on several occasions they came near to losing their lives. The priest of course was fully informed of the predicament of the two women. Christian charity cannot remake the world, when they who profess to represent the Christ, show such intolerance towards those who may differ from them in matters of belief. 

The sisters also sought out persons of prominence in their campaign to allay the anti-Mormon prejudice. In these attempts they found helpers in the school friends of the departed schoolmaster. Many of

these friends had risen to prominence, and several were members of the National Congress (Storthing). 

The widow lost no time, but sought out these old friends, everyone of whom received her with courtesy and kindness, and listened with interest to her story of the restoration of the gospel and the organized Church of Christ in the valleys of western America. 

Another friend, a relative of the schoolmaster, of the Swedish aristocracy, but resident in Norway, opened his house to the sisters. Thus in the midst of opposition, there were oases of friendliness. 

Some of her powerful friends gave material assistance. Professor Hegstad, one of the schoolmaster's boyhood friends, now a congressman himself, presented a petition in congress for the restoration of the widow's pension (based upon her husband's service to the state) of which she had been deprived because she became a Mormon. The pension was restored, and continued to her death, and the sum due her for the years that the pension was unlawfully withheld from her, was also paid her. Such contacts of necessity assisted, however indirectly, in promoting the cause that she represented. 

Amidst the daily toil and battle for truth came now and then the keen reward of seeing some friend, to whom they had taught the gospel, go in faith and with a repentant heart into the waters of baptism. 

Likewise, there was joy in visiting among the Saints, teaching and comforting them, settling difficulties and leading the wayward into the path of safety. The journals of the sisters abound in kindly, loving references to the Saints among whom they labored. There

The sisters took great delight in their associations with their fellow missionaries, most of them young men. Rich spiritual experiences were theirs during the years in the mission field. On many occasions they witnessed the healing of the sick under the hands of the Priesthood of Almighty God. At various times the spirit of prophecy was present in their meetings, and they had the privilege of seeing the prophecies fulfilled. Once at least the gift of tongues was upon the widow to the comfort of herself and others. By the whisperings of the spirit they avoided dangers and often found unexpected success. They testified that their prayers had been answered time and again. 

Powerfully they bore witness, from personal experiences, that the day of miracles had not ended, that the

gifts of the spirit were again upon earth, and that the spiritual and temporal domains of life were often in communion.

Such experiences enriched their days and perfected their missionary joy. True happiness comes to those who serve God and who know of a certainty that God lives. They may commune with Him.

CHAPTER XIV. 

More Missionary Days 

The missionary experiences of the widow and her sister were varied and many. Upon their entrance into the mission (July 13, 1903) they spent a few days in visiting Copenhagen, the beautiful capital of Denmark. They then went on to Christiania (now 

Oslo) where they remained to witness the dedication of the new Christiania Chapel. On July 27, 1903, when the dedication occurred, there were present, in addition to the presidents of the European and Scandinavian missions, and the missionaries of the Norwegian districts, a large number of missionaries from the several missions in Europe. It was an occasion of great spiritual joy; and a splendid introduction to the work the two sisters were to do. They made their small contribution to the building fund, and had their names sealed in the iron memorial box.

On the last day of July, 1903, the sisters took train for Trondheim, where they had been assigned to labor. Early in the following December, they returned to Christiania to be present at the celebration on December 7, of the fiftieth anniversary of the organization of Christiania Branch. This was also a valuable spiritual experience to them. Then, also, on 

December 8, the memorial box was sealed. They were back in their field of labor on December 16. 111

Two years later, June 22, 1906, they witnessed the coronation of Haakon VII, King of Norway, in the ancient cathedral at Trondheim. They were not fond of kings but were glad that so fine a man had been selected to be ruler of their native land. They thought longingly of the inspired government of their adopted country, the United States of America. 

It was necessary to use intelligence in making headway among a people so prejudiced against Mormonism, or in any event not interested in it. One of the devices to this end, much enjoyed by the widow especially, was to give lectures on non-religious subjects, such as silk-raising in Utah, irrigation in America, the Scandinavians in western America. Many came to listen who would not have come to an address on Mormonism, and they often remained to hear the Mormon sequel to each of these lectures. Such talks were also interesting to the members in their Relief Society and Mutual Improvement meetings, in both of which organizations the sisters gave much valued help.

The widow's fine critical judgment and literary taste made her very useful in helping the young members of the auxiliaries of the Church. In fact she was in constant demand as "critic" at auxiliary programs, and usually filled the role to the satisfaction of all. 

During their long mission, the sisters traveled widely over Norway. The country is one of the most picturesque in the world, in nature, people, and customs. Among the most interesting of these missionary trips were the visits, beginning and ending at Trondheim, along the magnificent broken coastline

But, on these trips, even more attention was given to the people who needed the gospel. They preached to the passengers on the ship; they gathered the fishermen about them and spoke of revelation in our day; they stood in the northernmost city of the world and under the midnight sun bore their testimony of the truth of the restored gospel. Their journals make thrilling reading. 

Another missionary trip took them southward along the coast of Norway where they held meetings in all the principal cities, including Bergen, in which city they did very effective work. Other journeyings took them landward, to Röros, Tönsberg, Drammen, and numerous other places. Everywhere they did the usual missionary work, teaching the Saints, proselyt-

ing among strangers, always bearing testimony to the truth of the restored gospel. Occasionally they had harrowing experiences, often hard ones, but always there was a note of gratitude in their words and prayers.

The sisters felt that on their mission it was their duty to seek out and preach to their relatives. So, soon after their arrival in Trondheim, in the summer of 1903, they took the boat for the outermost island and Titran. As the boat laid to at the quay, they looked down the gangplank with some misgiving. 

More than twenty years earlier, the people had turned against the widow because she had become a Mormon, and the evil was aggravated when she betook herself, with her two boys, to the American home of Mormondom. It might have been a bit more decent, they reasoned, had she left the boys behind, but to inculcate Mormonism into the minds of the boys was unforgivable. Had she been forgiven? she wondered as she stepped ashore. Here her people had lived for generations; here she had been born and reared. Had the people forgiven her?

She soon discovered her standing in the community. The sisters introduced themselves. The word spread like wildfire among the homes: Anna Gaarden

Widtsoe and her sister had arrived, as Mormon missionaries! When the sisters sought lodging, every door was closed against them. After much persuasion, a simple-minded family, strangers on the island, who thought that to be charitable was to be Christlike, took them in, and there they remained, while the houses of their blood relatives were closed to them.

The sisters failed to make any converts among their relatives, but the violent prejudice of the past was removed. This was a great comfort to the sisters who felt that in bearing repeated testimonies they had freed themselves from responsibility. However, to the end of their days they kept in constant touch with their relatives in Norway, in Titran and elsewhere, and supplied them with Mormon literature. 

The last few months of their mission were devoted to an intensive search for genealogical information. 

Helpers were secured; they spent long weeks in the archive; they visited every locality in which members

of the family were supposed to have lived. They were moderately successful, and brought back a connected genealogy which became the foundation of the larger genealogy now possessed by the family. To the best of their ability these two faithful, devoted followers of the Lord Jesus Christ, sought to do their duty towards the living and the dead, towards relative and stranger.

It would fill a large volume to relate the many experiences that came into their missionary lives. It is so with every missionary story. One thing remains certain: that the devoted, never-ceasing, and intelligent labor of the sisters did result in much good. 

They were able to do work and to find access to opportunities not within the reach of male missionaries. 

There is a distinct, important place for women in the missionary system of the Church.

One journey the widow left almost to the end of the mission. At last, however, she took the journey to Namsos, eighty miles north of Trondheim. Here she visited old friends; the school in which she taught; those remaining of her husband's teaching colleagues. 

Then, with courage in her heart, she went on August 14, 1907, to the cemetery and stood long by the grave of the schoolmaster, her husband. "Memories of the past filled my heart and overflowed," she writes in her journal. Then she turned back to life on earth with its duties and blessings.

CHAPTER XV. 

Ripening Years 

The widow had made her second entrance into the "valleys of Zion." How would she find the home of the Saints after these years of absence? That was her question. The day after her arrival, in the full blaze of the western sun, the widow surveyed the valley, the mountains, and the city founded by her people. All looked good to her, though nature seemed parched and barren in contrast with the green luxuriance of Norway's mountain-sides. She was glad to be home again. This was home!

Then, as was her wont, she soberly weighed the issues of life. She had sought and found truth; she had taught it at home and abroad; henceforth it must be her stay, support, and guide. Through its power she must ripen her life into fitness for the eternal harvest. The reality of her good Heavenly Father was again borne in upon her, the self-sacrificing leadership of the Son of God, and the high commission of 

His truth-loving prophet, Joseph Smith. Her heart beat with warmth at the flood of grateful thoughts. 

Where the Saints are, there is home!

She set about at once to seek out those, chiefly immigrants but also many others, who had need of cheer and guidance. As in the past, her home became a gathering place for dreaming youth and disillusioned age. To both she gave strength and inspiration. She became an interpreter of life to the many who frequented her home. Ever she pointed her lessons with the truths of the gospel. Her quick resentment would rise against any aspersion cast upon the divinely inspired labors of Joseph Smith, who to her was the chosen interpreter to this age of Jesus, the Christ. Scores of men and women bear testimony to the spiritual comfort and sane practical advice received from "Sister Widtsoe."

One recently arrived convert who had not found things to his liking complained bitterly about the Church and the people. Promptly, the widow set him right, "We have come here to build up Zion, not to tear it down." That message changed the course of a life. 

A youth or maiden gifted with a spark of artistic genius won her immediate concern and support. Her own nature from girlhood had moved in the realm of spiritual things. Poets, painters, sculptors, all who possessed creative power, she cherished and encouraged, for she believed that they gave life to material existence. The young artist's hard struggle became easier when the widow said, "You have the gift; the world needs you; don't give up; you'll make it."

The poor in purse were her equal concern. The plea of the poor she could not resist. So she gave of her own small income, until she often had to go without. Yet, when she was found at her frugal meal, sometimes of sour milk and potatoes (an old-world wholesome dish), she used to say that it tasted sweet because she knew that the last visitor was having enough to eat. The poor loved her.

Meanwhile she was as active as she could be in church and civic affairs. Among the Scandinavians she was a ready committee worker. She took part in the work of the Relief Society to which she belonged.

121 she served one term as president. When the American-Norwegians celebrated the centennial of Norwegian independence, she and her sister were among the active workers. The widow was ever possessed of zest and an eager interest in new knowledge. 

She took a keen interest in civic affairs, for she believed that good government depended on the characters of the men and women elected to office. From childhood she and her sister had been trained in handwork, and always retained a feeling of appreciation for products of the skillful hand, especially if they had housewifely meanings. This appreciation was not merely theoretical, for the sisters were always engaged in some type of handwork which they occasionally exhibited. At one of the Utah State Fairs, Sister Widtsoe received a diploma for the best embroidery, and her sister for the best woodcarving exhibited. Their innate sense of beauty no doubt helped much in these home occupations. Philosophers of our day are urging the people of this machine age to occupy themselves with some handwork, both as a means of filling leisure time and as a provision against the social disorders which often follow exclusive machine production. Closely connected are the mind and the body. The healthiest man is he who uses, in proper balance, all of his faculties. The sisters lived normal lives.

Numerous appointments came and were filled, as the years rolled on. Her Church meetings in taber-

nacle and ward house were always attended and gave her greatest joy. She loved to hear the Priesthood discuss the principles of the gospel. She also gave attention to the dead of her line by doing temple work, though the burden of that work fell upon her sister Petroline.

One handicap overshadowed Sister Widtsoe's life. 

She was a mature woman thirty-four years of age, when she came to America, an age at which it is difficult to learn to speak a foreign language without accent. She acquired facility in reading and writing 

English, and understanding the spoken word. Yet, naturally, she was freer in the use of her native tongue. She always felt a certain inward embarrassment when, as she knew, she spoke English in her broken manner. Language is one of the most serious barriers among men. Failure to understand one another leads to misunderstanding and suspicion. Those who speak the dominant language of a country often fail to appreciate the virtues of those of foreign extraction who cannot acquire the native accent, or the ready use of the language. This was very evident in early Utah, peopled by Mormon converts from many foreign lands. In fact, the native American stock were not free from unkindness due to ignorance, in their views and treatment of the immigrant. In any event, the widow found or felt herself handicapped in her progress among her chosen people, because of her broken English accent, which was not improved by her years in the foreign mission field, and she was therefore led more than ever to use her energies among the people of her own national origin.

Current events fascinated her. She read in them the operation of human wills, bent to truth or untruth. When World War I broke out, before America had entered it, she cannily said, "Study the leaders, and let your sympathies move accordingly.

In the midst of this busy life-attending to her Church duties, waiting upon others, reading and writing-she made time to look after her garden. A home without a flower garden was impossible in her mind. 

She dug, planted, watered, and weeded, until a small flower paradise rewarded her efforts. To secure a slip of a new and better variety of a cherished plant, she would take a journey of miles. She would discuss for hours the best care of her beloved flowers. Her garden work she did alone; it was a favor to her sister to be allowed to take part in the gardening. "Flowers know those who love them," she said, "and the more you love them the better they grow." Her gardening no doubt contributed to her physical health.

She saw her family frequently; there were regular family gatherings, at which the ideals of the family were repeated for the benefit of young and old, with many a warning and counsel to all. The sons might grow in worldly position, but she was still their mother and under the obligation to teach and guide them. 

The family understood, and respected the wisdom of the mother and grandmother. At these affairs, the feasting was partly Norwegian, in honorable memory of the land of origin, and partly American, in honor of the country of adoption. The family were widely traveled, and an occasional dish from a far country tempted the palate. Christmas eve, true to north

European traditions, the family had ludfisk (cod brought to a jelly-like consistency through treatment with soda) and flödegröd (a mush made from sour cream) sprinkled with cinnamon and sugar. On birthdays, dainty, delicious fish puddings, served with browned butter, formed the piece de resistance. The daughters-in-law learned to make these dishes and others that have been perfected in long winter evenings of the lands of the midnight sun. And there was many another delightful celebration, of family interest only.

Thus, with labor and rest, writing and reading, serving God and man, the ripening years increased. 

She took frequent occasion to contemplate the future, to read its riddles.

CHAPTER XVI. 

Sunset In course of time Sister Widtsoe had the satisfaction of having her whole family settled near her in 

Salt Lake City. Osborne received in 1915, a call to enter the service of the University of Utah as professor of English and head of the department of English. John was called the following year to assume the presidency of the University of Utah. Both were active in Church work; both serving as members of the General Board of the Young Men's Mutual Im provement Association. She saw her boys in their professional and Church activity. She was happiest to know that both had a living faith in the gospel. 

Early in the spring of 1919, the widow was seized with an illness, diagnosed as of minor importance. An unexpected, accidental complication made the illness dangerous.

In the hope that complete rest would lead to recovery she was sent to the L. D. S. hospital, but was not happy away from her home. Therefore she was returned to her Wall Street residence. When she was laid upon her bed she looked up and said, "Do you think that this means death?" "No," was the answer, "not death, for we want to keep you a long time, yet.'

The wise eyes of the widow glowed as she said, “I'm not so sure of that. In case this is my last illness, I want to tell you that the most glorious thing that

came into my life, was the message delivered to me by Shoemaker Johnsen of Trondheim. The restored gospel has been the great joy of my life. Please bear that witness for me to all who will listen."

CHAPTER XVII. 

"Aunty" 

The saga of Anna Karine Gaarden Widtsoe can come to a close only with a chapter about her sister, Petroline Jörgine Gaarden-Aunty as she was known affectionately by the family. The lives of these two women were so intertwined and interwoven that they seemed as one. During most of the years of their lives they lived together under one roof or in very close proximity to each other. They met the issues of life together; they divided the labors of the day according to their special tastes and gifts; they shared the rewards of life's efforts. They were as Jonathan and David, but held together by the closest ties of blood as of spirit. They traveled life's journey, as it were, arm in arm. 

Petroline Gaarden was born July 3, 1853, and was therefore four years younger than her sister. She grew up with her sister in Titran. In her late 'teens she was sent away for a two years' course of study in a technical school on the Norwegian mainland. Suitors she had in plenty, but conforming to the family tradition, found it difficult to make a choice to which her heart would give assent. She never married, but in her late years was sealed in the temple to a good man long before deceased.

Aunty loved language and literature. She had an extensive vocabulary and a remarkable facility in 131

choosing and using words in her speech. Her training in household science during her school days kept her interested throughout life in household affairs. 

She left behind a large collection of clippings from numerous sources bearing upon her special interests. 

It was always profitable to converse with her, for her interests were wide, and she viewed men and affairs with a wise, clear judgment. It was not easy to deceive her.

Aunty was of a calm, unruffled disposition. She was never angry, seldom excited; her life moved onward smoothly. Thus she met easily the more active, urgent nature of her sister, who was quick to anger and as quick to forgive. Hers was the constructive, her sister's the creative temperament. She built as her sister originated. Aunty would express the need; her sister would devise the means of meeting it; and the two would set out to secure it. It was an astonishingly effective combination.

The Bible was taught to Aunty from babyhood. 

She knew it well. Her spiritual nature was well developed. Her faith in God and His Son was implicit. 

There were no lingering doubts. By temperament, however, she was inclined to cling to that which she had been taught to be right. Therefore, when the restored gospel came to the family she took several years longer than her sister to convince herself that the new message was really of the Lord. There was a stubborn determination about her which made it impossible to drive her into any belief or action. Therefore, when she at last had settled in her own mind and soul that Joseph Smith was divinely called, there

"AUNTY" 133 was no wavering, no changing, no compromisingthe gospel path was her path. 

She was baptized into the Church on September 23, 1883. Soon thereafter she emigrated to America, sailing on the S. S. Wisconsin from Liverpool on 

June 20, 1885, arriving in New York, July 1, 1885, and in Salt Lake City, July 7, 1885. On April 15, 1887, she received her endowments in the Logan Temple. The days of her baptism and endowment were great in the book of her life. 

When the sisters were on their long mission, Aunty was often obliged because of her frail health to remain home for a day or two, yet it was she who with unwavering fidelity and without one day's break, made the entries in their diaries of their doings. She gathered and formed, and her active sister fired the bullets that had been prepared. Her joy in teaching gospel truths was unbounded.

From her early womanhood, her health was uncertain. For many years she was unable to carry a full load of life's duties because of her physical infirmity. However, as she grew older, her health improved; though she was never robust, yet she outlived her more vigorous, healthy sister by twelve years. 

With more robust health, Aunty would undoubtedly have added to her many useful activities.

Since she had no family of her own, her sister's sons and grandchildren became the objects of her affection. She would not live with them; for that she was too independent; she scorned unnecessary assistance, and her life was after all her own. But when she was needed, she was always on hand. On holidays,

birthdays and all festive occasions, Aunty could always be depended on to provide something that all would enjoy. Both sisters were good cooks. On these special occasions, there would be an abundance of dainty dishes prepared with much labor and representing the highly developed culinary art of the north European countries. When Aunty arrived, everybody smacked his lips.

This lovely, peacemaking woman lived for others throughout her whole life. She was content to stand in the shadow and let others enjoy the limelight. Yet, there was no self-deception about her. She knew her rights.

She stood by her sister's deathbed. The parting left Aunty very much alone. She insisted wisely on living by herself. Temple work engaged her attention from that time as never before. She arranged the genealogical data gathered by her sister and herself. At the same time she attended to her Relief 

Society and other Church duties. She was a regular attendant at the Scandinavian and tabernacle meetings. Especially did the latter have an attraction for her. She read extensively. Her old Stars (Skandinaviens Stjerne, a missionary publication) were worn thin by frequent use. She filled her life with worthwhile duties. But, all the while she missed the association of her sister, the sister on whom she had leaned so long.

She was yet to endure another shock. Nearly two years after the death of her sister, her beloved nephew, Osborne, died suddenly. He was the younger of the widow's sons, and had crept into Aunty's heart as if

"AUNTY" 135 he were her own. She grieved greatly at his passing, but the gospel came to her rescue. Meanwhile her health declined rapidly.

She and John were now the only remaining ones of the emigrant family. They met often; and Aunty made frequent visits to the families of the two boys. 

John was called into the Council of the Twelve, which was a joy to her, but which also made their visits, because of his travels, less frequent. Nevertheless, the feast days were celebrated as of yore, with Aunty in the place of honor.

In the autumn of 1927, John was called to preside over the European Mission. Aunty did not feel herself strong enough to accompany him, and decided to remain at home. Thus in her old age and weakened by disease, she found herself the only remaining member of the original family, in the land of her adoption. 

Her letters betray her loneliness. John was the only remaining one who could talk with her about the things of the past, so dear to her. Yet, she rejoiced that John should have the privilege of teaching the gospel among European peoples.

Her health weakened. She was ready for the entrance into the other world. She received tender, watchful care from Lucy Gates Bowen and Albert E. Bowen, John's sister- and brother-in-law, who for several weeks took her into their home, and nursed her. Other friends helped her.

At last the body had no more resistance. The end was near. "Lord, let me go," she prayed. "I want to rejoin my sister." Death came to her while faith and

love were upon her lips, on April 11, 1929, at the age of seventy-five years and nine months.

Aunty's funeral services were held in the Seventeenth Ward meetinghouse on Sunday, April 14, 1929. A beautiful program, worthy of a lovable character was rendered. Among others, President Anthony W. Ivins, lover of mankind, was present to do her memory honor.

Beautiful singing was rendered by the Norwegian Choir (Silent Night); by a quartet consisting of 

Hyrum J. Christiansen, Agnes Olsen Thomas, and 

Brother and Sister Ole Gulbrandsen (O, My Father, and Shall We Meet Beyond the River), and interspersed among the addresses, were a vocal solo 

(Home So Blessed) by Hyrum J. Christiansen, and a violin solo by Alice Anderson, accompanied by 

Becky Almond.

Comforting remarks were made by Arnt Engh, Andrew Jenson, Albert E. Bowen, and Nicholas G. 

Smith, Aunty's ward bishop. President Ivins delivered a doctrinal sermon. 

The spirit of the gathering was sweet and peaceful, like Aunty's nature.

The grave was dedicated by Jacob F. Gates. 

Aunty could not have wished for a more beautiful service; and she, in her simple honesty, would have been grateful for whatever those left behind could do for her. Such characters, as was Aunty's, make the earth lovely, and give assurance that man is indeed in the image of God.

John was far away, across the ocean, when Aunty died; yet her sister Anna and nephew Osborne, long

"AUNTY" 137 in the spirit world, were probably near. The temporal and spiritual worlds are not far apart. There was a glorious meeting on the other side when the two sisters, long together on earth, clasped each other in close embrace at the beginning of an endless association.

Only one then remained on earth of the family which the widow's search for truth had brought to the western Zion. But he was not alone. He had his own family; his brother's family was near; he dwelt among friends, amidst the beloved people of his Church, who are as one great family, such as may nowhere else be found. He understood the eternity of life. He rejoiced with a warm gratitude that the widow and her family had been caught in the gospel net.

CHAPTER XVIII. 

A Summary Tribute At the funeral of Sister Petroline J. Gaarden, 

Elder Albert E. Bowen summarized the lives of the widow and her sister in touching, eloquent words: "More than forty years ago, there came to this state two women, leading by the hands two little, fatherless, immigrant boys. They had no wealth, no possessions. All these they had left behind in the 

Northland, whence they had come, but they were rich in purpose and in faith, and in those qualities which go to make manhood and womanhood. They were extraordinarily well endowed. And in this strange land among strange people with new ways and a new tongue, they set themselves down to fight out life's battle. "They had traditions, and one of those traditions was that the boys of that family must be schooled, must be educated, must be trained for usefulness; and the two lone women gave themselves the task of giving those two boys the preparation that would make them men of usefulness in this life. They worked with their needles, they toiled at whatever task lent them a recompense, in order that they might accomplish that one great task they had set themselves-and most richly was their work rewarded. Those boys used all the opportunities that were given them. They studied, they applied themselves with diligence, and it was not 138

A SUMMARY TRIBUTE 

139 long until they were marked men. Among their comrades they were looked upon as models of scholarship and achievement. "It was not enough that they should get the schooling which this state at that time offered, but they must go where the best in this land was to be obtained, and there perfect themselves further for useful lives. And while they were struggling to attain, these two women were struggling to support. 

But when that task was accomplished and these boys had achieved their goal, so far as the mere preparation for life is concerned, then it was their task to care for the women who had cared for them. And it is a most happy thing to be able to say that they prized what had been done for them and repaid it a hundredfold, because from the time when those boys set out upon their own careers these women never more had to toil or struggle. "Perhaps nothing could come to a human soul that would be more bitter than to see one for whom he had struggled turn away from him, and nothing is sweeter than to see fidelity rewarded with fidelity and appreciation. And that has been the lot of this little family which trudged into these valleys so many years ago. These two little boys, who were the butt of jests because they couldn't speak our tongue, have left a mark among the peoples of this state, of the surrounding states, and of this country, which anybody would be proud to have made. By their industry, by their intelligence, by their application, they have given to this land in which we live, knowledge that has blessed the land and the people who inhabit

it and will continue to bless it through the generations to come-nothing ephemeral or transitory, but an achievement which has blessed mankind. I would rather be the author of the books they have written, of the researches they have conducted, of the applied knowledge they have given to the world, than to be possessed of all the wealth of Croesus. "And this good woman, whose remains lie before us here today, is one of those women whose life's labors made possible the achievements of these boys. 

Shall anyone say her life has not been rich? And now she has grown weak, and her body is worn out, and most of her friends are on the other side. She has finished with life, and what a blessed thing it is that she could lie down to rest. This is not a day to mourn. 

It is a day to rejoice, that when people's bodies have grown frail and ailments have overcome their physical powers, and they are no longer able to go about the business of life, they can find release from it all by lying down in sleep. "May God help us with understanding hearts and with desires always to do our part as nobly and as well as this woman has done hers, I pray in the name of Jesus, Amen."

Epilogue 

The woman whose story has been told here was one of tens of thousands who in these latter days earnestly sought truth, happily found it, courageously accepted it, and were led into new places, where, despite many trials, they discovered new and greater joys in living.

The story herein told, may, therefore, be read as the saga of a glorious multitude who have been caught in the gospel net. 

Before this host of radiant men and women, we bow in admiration and gratitude. 

May the memory of their certain faith and diligent works ever rest in the hearts of the coming generations!
