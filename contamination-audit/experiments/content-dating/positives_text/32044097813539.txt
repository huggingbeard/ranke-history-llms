TEXAS YOUTH COUNCIL Statutory Responsibilities, Purposes, and Organization of the Texas Youth Council Article 5143a, V. C. S., enacted by the 55th Legislature, created the Texas Youth Council and charged it with the responsibility of administering the State's correctional facilities for delinquent children, providing a program of constructive care, treatment, education and training aimed at rehabilitation and re-establishment in society of children adjudged delinquent by the courts of Texas and committed to the Council, and providing active parole supervision of delinquent children released from the State Training Schools. The statute also vested in the 

Texas Youth Council administrative responsibility for the Waco State Home and the Corsicana State Home, and responsibility for the "coordination and combination of such facilities" and for "the creation of new facilities within the appropriations provided by the Legislature..." 

The statute directs the Council to report to the Governor and the Legislature its activities, accomplishments, findings and needs and provides that the Council's "report shall include specific recommendations for legislation, planned and drafted as part of an integrated, unified and consistent program to serve the best interest of the State and the youth committed to the 

Texas Youth Council. 

ון The Texas Youth Council consists of a three-member policy-making board appointed by the Governor of Texas with the consent of the State Senate. 

Members of the Texas Youth Council receive no pay for their services and must be "influential citizens in their respective communities who are recognized for their interest in youth". The Executive Director, appointed by the Council, is the administrative head of the agency, and all powers, duties and functions granted to or imposed on the Texas Youth Council by any provision of law may be delegated to and performed by the Executive Director.

Some Activities and Accomplishments of the Texas Youth Council for the Fiscal Year Ending August 31, 1964

The Texas Youth Council is pleased to report that appropriated funds have made possible considerable progress and improvement in physical plants, personnel and training programs at all institutions and facilities under its administrative jurisdiction. These "investments in programs offering benefits in terms of long-range economy and more productive, self-supporting citizens" could not have been achieved without the genuine understanding and concern of the Governor and the Legislature or without their continued support of the Council's long-range plans to meet these vital functions of State Government. 

The Council is especially pleased to report that these "investments in the future" have already begun to return dividends of remarkable proportions. 

Among the most notable examples of such dividends, during the period covered by this report, was the return to the public schools of 58% of all youngsters released on parole under the supervision of TYC Parole Officers while another 28% of the group were placed in gainful full-time employment utilizing the skills learned while in the training schools. Another significant dividend has been realized by the continued progressive reduction of broken paroles from the previous 42% to 26% in the areas where TYC Parole Officers were assigned. This is the lowest recidivism rate in the history of the State for these areas. These accomplishments are especially significant when compared with the virtually 100% "drop-out" and unemployment records of these youngsters prior to commitment to the 

Texas Youth Council. 

The Texas Youth Council has developed and established policies, procedures and long-range plans for the efficient and economical accomplishments of its statutory responsibilities. Effective administrative supervision and leadership have been provided in evaluating, improving, organizing and coordinating programs and operations of all institutions and facilities of the agency. 

This report attempts not only to make lucid Council activities and accomplishments with appropriated funds and other available resources but also to analyze the problems presented by the children and youth committed to the care and custody of the Council by the courts of the State. 

The Council has provided leadership for counties and communities of the State through consultation, conferences, workshops, and distribution of literature and has maintained a 

2

high level of coordination and cooperation with Juvenile Courts and local agencies whose efforts have been directed toward detection of child neglect and prevention of juvenile delinquency and youth crime.

The Council has encouraged local initiative and local leadership in action programs by communities, cities and counties in the following areas of service: (1) Local organization of active programs for the prevention of juvenile delinquency and youth crime, (2) local support of the Juvenile Courts, Juvenile Departments and 

Police Departments, (3) local provision of adequate staff and facilities to meet local needs in detection of child neglect, protective services, prevention of juvenile delinquency, and adequate detention and probation services for juvenile offenders.

It has been neither the wish nor the intention of the Texas Youth Council to impose its ideas upon any local government or community but rather to assist all communities in preventing and controlling juvenile delinquency to the fullest extent of local resources. One full time employee has been assigned to community organization work, and many communities have sought his assistance in finding solutions to local problems. 

During the period covered by this report, the Council distributed several thousand copies of its booklet entitled "Prevention of Juvenile Delinquency--A Proposed. Statewide. Program .Through

This booklet has been received enthusiastically by many county, city and community groups interested in organizing themselves to study, improve, extend and coordinate all local services to children and youth. The Council believes local leadership and initiative will result in communities (and counties) assuming more local responsibility and taking advantage of the opportunities to serve children at the local level prior to the time it becomes necessary for the courts to commit children to the State. - 3 -

STATE TRAINING SCHOOLS FOR DELINQUENT CHILDREN 

The Council has continued its careful study of the multitude of problems presented by delinquent youngsters committed to the State Training Schools, analyzed these problems and attempted to meet them with both immediate and long-range programs designed to meet the criteria of good practice and appropriate to the statutory responsibilities of the Agency and State government.

The Council has succeeded in providing institutional care and training programs considered by expert evaluations to be a credit to the State of Texas. The Council is convinced, however, that good institutional programs must be followed by adequate parole supervision if maximum benefits and economy are to be realized.

"This phase of the correctional process is often called "placement" when one refers to juvenile offenders, but some states, including Texas, realistically call it "parole". This is an appropriate term; whether used with juveniles or adults, parole is not considered a punitive method, but rather a method of helping institutional "graduates" to succeed in free life by giving them counseling, guidance, and firm but helpful supervision. The ultimate aim of parole services is to assist parolees to become not only law-abiding but also well-adjusted members of their families, neighborhood and communities. With juveniles, the parole officer must frequently help them adjust to difficult home situations and to public schools in which they have been retarded or disciplinary problems. "The success potentialities for offenders in all age groups of parole services that are well staffed have been conclusively demonstrated. In recent years, moreover, it has been proved that expansion and improvement of parole as well as probation services can reduce existing institution populations or counteract the increase that would occur proportionately as the state's population grows. This is an important consideration for taxpayers, since it costs ten to twenty times as much a year to keep a juvenile or an adult in an institution as it does to keep one on parole, • • "This is vividly illustrated by the recent experience of the Youth Council's juvenile parole program, even though it is only four years old and is woefully understaffed, with only 15 parole officers to cover this huge state. Effects of Parole Violations on Training School Programs 

The return of youngsters to the Training Schools for a second or third time due to parole violation was one of the serious problems faced by the Council during the period covered by this report. The Council has found through experience that rehabilitation of juvenile delinquents cannot be accomplished completely within the confines of a State Training School program. Such training must be followed by effective parole supervision in the community to which the child is released. Unless adequate and intensive supervision is provided for the children on parole, constructive progress made while in the Training School frequently is lost within the first few weeks after release, and both the money and effort expended are a total loss to the state as well as to the child.

The Council is pleased to report that substantial progress is being made toward providing essential supervisory services to parolees through the efforts of its Juvenile Parole Program. 

Also, the Council is confident of achieving marked success in providing improved parole supervision in years to come if future Legislative requests are granted to expand the parole program to its envisioned limits.

Out-of-State Parolees 

Reciprocal agreements between states make possible the courtesy supervision of parolees released from Training Schools of one state and placed in another state. These agreements work to the mutual benefit of all states and are cleared through certain legally authorized agencies before a child is placed, or received, from the state holding legal jurisdiction. The Texas Youth 

Council is the state-level agency which handles such matters in Texas. 

During the period covered by this report, the Council received 93 requests from 29 different states for pre-placement investigations in anticipation of placing children in Texas. Forty-six (46) of these children were placed in Texas and local supervision arranged. On August 31, 1964, there were fifty-one (51) parolees from fifteen (15) states residing in Texas. Sixteen (16) had - 13

been retained from previous years. Two children of this out-of-state group were committed to the 

Texas Youth Council and received into its facilities during the year, and thirteen (13) had been discharged,

Eighty-two (82) Texas wards were receiving courtesy supervision in 24 other states and two in Mexico on August 31, 1964. Eighty-four (84) children were placed during the current year; seventy-five (75) were carried over from previous years. During the period covered by this report, eleven Texas parolees, who had been placed out of state, broke parole and had to be returned to Texas training schools; nine children moved back into the state; and fifty-five (55) were discharged from TYC custody.

THE STATE HOMES FOR CHILDREN 

The Texas Youth Council is pleased to report that the child care programs in Texas State Homes for Children have improved to the degree that people of our state can take pride in comparing them favorably with any--public or private--in the nation. Funds appropriated by the 

Legislature have allowed the Council and its staff at the Children's Homes to plan, accomplish and maintain high standards of child care, essential services for education and training of children, and necessary placement services for children leaving these institutions when individual needs for group care have been alleviated. 

Waco State Home 

The Legislature, in accordance with long-range planning of the Texas Youth Council, has appropriated funds for the care, education and training of hundreds of dependent and neglected children at the Waco State Home. As of August 31, 1964, the multi-service program of the Home was serving three hundred and seventy-two (372) children with the funds provided. Two hundred and seventy-one (271) of this number were in residence at the Home; twenty-five (25) were in paid Foster Homes; and seventy-six (76) were with their own relatives in temporary home trial placements, pre-adoptive placements, free foster homes, college, or various job placements. 14 

---

Efforts to learn more about each child and his (or her) family have been greatly accelerated and every effort has been made to return each child to his (or her) own family, or other appropriate placement, as soon as practicable in the best interest of the child. 

All children of scholastic age at the Waco State Home attend Waco Public Schools on an annual contract basis, and the cost to the state is much less than it would be for the Home to operate its own school system. The children have made rapid and satisfying progress and adjustment in all public school activities, religious activities, athletic and recreational programs. 

Corsicana State Home 

The Legislature, in accordance with the planning of the Texas Youth Council has appropriated funds for the care, education and training of several hundred dependent and neglected children at the Corsicana State Home. On August 31, 1964, the Home was providing group care for three hundred and seven (307) children in a home-like atmosphere where each child could be cared for according to his (or her) individual needs and was providing a multi-service program of care outside the Home for seventy-one (71) more children, 14 of them in paid Foster Home placements. 

Many children were placed outside the Home during the period covered by this report and other children were accepted into the vacancies so created, thus allowing the Home to serve a maximum number of children with the funds provided. 

Children of scholastic age at the Corsicana State Home attend the Corsicana Public Schools on an annual contract basis, and the cost is much less than it would be for the Home to operate its own school system. The educational advantages and opportunities for community participation are considerably greater for children of the Home than would have been possible had the Home continued to operate its own schools. Public school authorities and teachers have assisted and encouraged children from the Home to participate in extra-curricular activities, and such interest and cooperation have all but eliminated the children's feelings of being set apart from the community. - 15 -
