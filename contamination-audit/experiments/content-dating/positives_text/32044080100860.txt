War and Peace 

We are met today in annual commemoration of the noble and valiant deeds of the nation's soldier dead.

By statute, in Minnesota, as in nearly all other northern states of the Union. appropriate observance of the day is provided for by law. A similar custom prevails in the southern states, but not generally on the same day. Cities and counties in

Minnesota are authorized by law to participate and assist in the commemoration; and upon clerks of cities and recorders of villages, upon the clerks of all towns, within the State. is laid the duty to supervise decoration of the graves of soldiers and sailors lying within their jurisdictions.

The custom so now made into law began, at first, during dark days of the Civil War. It gave comfort to those left at home to decorate, with early flowers, on a given day in spring, the graves of their loved ones, fallen in that mighty conflict, the conflict called in the North the War of the Rebellion and in the 

South the War Between the States. 

That was a fratricidal conflict, brother against brother of the same stock and sometimes of the same family, and dark and foreboding did it make the North, until, after nearly three years of bloodshed, Grant captured Vicksburg and General

Meade turned the tide at Gettysburg, in the year 1863. Just at the close of the Civil War, after the North had finally been successful and it was settled that the Union was to be preserved, the Grand Army of the Republic was formed. Remembrance of the graves of their fallen comrades was made one of the special objects of that organization. At first

generally called "Decoration Day," the Grand Army of the Republic, in 1882. at a national encampment, recommended that the name "Memorial Day" be universally used as the designation of the holiday as we now know it and observe it. 

It was in 1868 that General John A. Logan, first commander-in-chief of the Grand Army of the Republic, by his famous general order No. 

11, often read now as a part of the ceremonies of observance, designated May 30th, 1868, as the day of general observance of the memorial exercises, and crystallized the custom and the day which, under both names, has ever since been followed in the North. General Logan and the Grand Army of the Republic thus caused the institution of Memorial Day as we now know it. 

Nearly everywhere, the day has been made a holiday by law. It is meant to be a holy day in the life of our country. General Logan enjoined us to guard the graves of the nation's heroic dead with sacred vigilance. He said: "Let no wanton foot tread rudely on such hallowed grounds. Let pleasant paths invite the coming and going of reverent visitors and fond mourners. Let no vandalism or avarice or neglect, no ravages of time, testify to the present or to the coming generations, that we have forgotten as a people the cost of a free and undivided Republic."

The life of John A. Logan is an interesting study. He was born on the frontier. He was nurtured in the backwoods of early Illinois. He was a soldier of the Mexican War at the age of 22, honored by his neighbors with public office at 23. He was a member of the bar and rider of the circuit in the days when men like 

Abraham Lincoln and Stephen A. 

Douglas were the leaders of his

State, and he was sent to Congress at the age of 29. To him and to men like him, patriotism and loyalty to country were everyday realities by which to order the whole course of a man's life. He did so order his life, when decision on his country's side meant the severance of many beloved ties of friendship, and of kindred. an in In these days of 1937, when, in some quarters, patriotism and loyalty to the flag are ridiculed, when we hear of such organizations as the 

Veterans of Future Wars and of the people who proclaim that they will never fight for their country, it is refreshing to study the life of a man like John A. Logan. Mrs. Logan's book, "Reminiscences of a Soldier's 

Wife," published twenty-seven years after his death, is a treasure house of American history. Nobly, richly, to the full, he lived the life of 

American citizen, and when,

Washington, out of the secrecy and maze of the comings and goings of prominent leaders from the South who knew better, Logan learned that they intended to bring about secession, that they were resolved to dissolve the Union rather than submit to see their hold upon politics and the government lessened, he resigned his post in Congress, went back home to Illinois, and gathered his constituents together, men who were largely Douglas Democrats and sympathetic with the South, men bound to Kentucky and Tennessee by many ties of family and of friendship. It was a memorable occasion. The young Congressman spoke to his neighbors for three hours. Logan pictured to them the condition of affairs in Washington and the inevitable consequences of rebellion against the Government, should the theory of secession prevail; he told them at what cost of blood and treasure the Republic had been established, and how certainly liberty would be forfeited and anar-

chy reign were the Union once dissolved. He then said: "The time has come when a man must be for or against his country. The Union once dissolved, we should have innumerable confederacies and rebellions. I, for one, shall stand or fall for this 

Union, and shall this day enroll for the war. I want as many of you as will to come with me. If you say 'No', and see your best interests and the welfare of your homes and your children in another direction, may 

God protect you." An old comrade, a fifer in the Mexican War, was the first to enlist. That day 110 men of his neighbors and friends enrolled to form the first of ten companies of 

Logan's regiment, the famous 31st

Illinois, and it was that regiment, recruited by John A. Logan in southern Illinois, which he commanded until after the battle of Fort Donelson, in 1862, where Logan won his brigadier general's star for bravery in battle. The Illinois regiments were the backbone of Grant's campaign in the West. At Fort Donelson, Colonel John A. Logan, Lieutenant Colonel 

John H. White, four captains, and a great number of the men of the 31st Illinois, nearly all of whom were friends and neighbors, were reported among the killed or wounded. By their heroism Grant's great plan to drive a wedge from Cairo to New 

Orleans, to open up the Mississippi 

River and divide the Confederacy, was on its way to the success finally achieved when, on the Fourth Day of

July, 1863, General John A. Logan led the 31st Illinois into the Vicksburg stronghold which the Union 

Army had captured.

The fate of the mad purpose of 

Secession there and then was sealed, and Abraham Lincoln there found that he had at his command the fighting men who could and who did save the Union from ruin. In their generation these gallant men suffer-

ed the trials and the griefs of war that you and I of this generation might continue to enjoy the blessings of liberty, and so that government, as Lincoln said, “of the people, by the people, and for the people" should not perish from the earth. 

What would have happened had these men been too proud to fight? 

What would have been the issue had they loved peace and harmony at any price? Where or to whom would 

Lincoln have turned for aid in his extremity, had they told him that they were pacifists who believed in harmony and did not believe in fighting? Lincoln had to face a conspiracy of brilliant Southern leaders to wreck the Union. He knew what war would mean. His sensitive and kindly heart recoiled at the bloodshed, the hardships, the pain and suffering, the separation of families, the loneliness, destruction, death, and disease, which war inevitably brings. Lincoln's entry upon the scene at Washington meant long hours, and days and weeks, devoted to conciliation. He did everything that he could to induce the Southern leaders to see the utter folly of their position. But they were proud, bold, arrogant, and determined, and all of his efforts to avoid the conflict were in vain. No leader of men ever worked harder to keep his country out of war. When, at last, the issue of preservation of the Union was inevitable, he chose the way of honor: the Union, it must and shall be preserved, if it takes every man and every farthing of resource which we have at our command! The Union was preserved, but at a fearful cost of blood and treasure.

The same choice between war and dishonor was presented to Woodrow 

Wilson in 1917. No one can doubt that he earnestly and sincerely did all that he could to keep our country out of war. He, too, was a sensitive and kindly man, a scholar who abhorred war and all of its works.

He had deep sympathy for the German people and did his best to conciliate and to heal the wounded feelings of the belligerents. But he found that the masters of the greatest and most efficient war machine the world ever saw were proud and adamant. And then it was that he led this great, peace-loving nation into a European War and declared to all the world that force, and force to the uttermost, would be used by our country to thwart, to stop, the masters of that great war machine in their mad ambition to conquer the world and to impose their rule by force upon the liberty-loving nations who blocked their pathway to world domination. There, too, the integrity of liberty-loving nations was preserved against the onslaught of the policy of rule or ruin, but at what a cost of blood and treasure! A whole generation of the best young men of 

Europe was maimed and slaughtered before this latest attack of mad ambition for power upon liberty and freedom was stopped. men's

The lesson of history is clear and inescapable. Wars are not made by ethereal causes working, like some law of physics or of nature, toward destruction. Wars are brought about by men. Guilt is personal. It is the surging passions of dominant leaders, their likes and dislikes, their human ambitions and desires, the lust for power, the insane demand to rule or ruin, the refusal to be fair and just, which are the causes of war. The history of mankind is full of the awful consequences of man's attempts to rule by violence. Alexander tried it. So did Carthage and ancient Rome. The peace, imposed by Rome, the "Pax Romana," was a salutary thing for hundreds of years, for Rome proceeded to develop law, the rule of reason and of justice. But 

Rome, too, perished by the sword. In the last century, a little corporal

from Corsica drenched all of Europe in blood for twenty years and thereby made himself an emperor and his relatives kings. Peace was won only by meeting force with force, by defeating him in battle. Peace was maintained only by locking up this little man and his vaulting ambition.s upon a lonely island in the sea. The 

Napoleonic Wars drained the life blood of France and sacrificed the flower of her youth for generations. upon a mad project to rule all of 

Europe by force. ants

All through the ages mankind has sought to be free from this scourge of violence in the mass. The desire for peace is universal. It is error to assume that men who have worn their country's uniform and who have served their native land in army or navy are advocates of war. 

None know better than they the loneliness, the suffering, the hardships, which war visits upon combatand non-combatants alike. anyone think that it was easy for John A. Logan to give up his promising career in Congress, just as he had entered upon it, give up his law practice in Illinois, leave his young wife of 22, say goodbye to home and family, to spend his time, first in the swamps of camp at 

Cairo, Illinois, drilling men, and then in the saddle or on long marches, on the extensive expedition into the 

South? He was wounded, reported killed. It was almost by a miracle and his wife's tender nursing that his life was saved. He went back afterward into the fray and saw the great struggle through to the end with Grant and Lincoln. Later, when the war was over, the Grand Army of the Republic honored him by making him its Commander-in-Chief, in the year 1868. Previously, and in 

1866, he had been elected Congressman-at-large from Illinois. In 1871, and again in 1879, Logan was honor-

ed by election to the United States Senate from his native state. In 1884 he was the Republican nominee for vice-president, with James G. Blaine of Maine. But Logan died in 1886, at the age of sixty, and all through his lifetime, after the engagement of 

1862 at Fort Donelson, he suffered the effects of his nearly mortal wound, and of the disease which had been everywhere prevalent in the swamps of his first camp at Cairo.

Such men do not love war. They do not enter upon war for glory or to gratify ambition. None know better than they what war means in suffering and hardship. To say that our own veterans of the World War are enamored of militarism is to say what is not and cannot be true. They did not leave home and friends and kindred to serve in the trenches and mud of France to gratify ambition or to seek the rewards of glory. They went because their country needed them. And we may be sure that often, in the long watches of the night, they pondered upon war and its causes, and asked themselves why it was necessary for them to give up home and friends and loved ones and to risk death, suffering and disease in order to protect the nation they loved. Why did the nation need protection: protection from whom and what? We may be sure that none will ever know better than they what it will mean to some day renounce and outlaw war as an instrument of national policy, When, out of their experience, their thinking, their philosophy, our own veterans of the American

Legion tell us that we first must take all element of private financial profit out of war, that we must require wealth and labor to be subject to conscription just as the lives of our young men have been subject to it, we can trust them to know where-

of they speak. They make the proposal out of the crucible of their own lives and experiences. They believe that such a provision will retard and prevent the desire for war, if such there is ever likely to be, in quarters influenced by commercial or business interests. They tell us that property and labor are no better than human life and that both must be completely at the service of the nation in its hour of trial and need. The proposal grows out of the mature consideration on the part of our own veterans of the causes of war. It is a policy which they have evolved looking toward the day when war shall be outlawed and in its place we shall have the rule of reason and justice as between nation and nation, just as the arbitrament of orderly law founded upon reason and justice has long since taken the place of violence in determining the relations of individual men to each other. Is there any sound reason why the rule of reason as embodied in law, rather than the violence of warfare, should not determine the rights and govern the relationships of nations?

We know that the veterans of the 

World War wish to insure peace. 

They deserve all encouragement and support in their valiant efforts to deal realistically with the age-old scourge of war. Other sincere groups, likewise interested in the outlawry of war, will not lend themselves to propaganda to oppose the plans of the American Legion, but will, in offering their own plans, whatever they may be, gladly co-operate and assist in the adoption of this or any other plan which, evolved from the crucible of deep experience, is designed to remove from human affairs the mass violence which we call war and consign it to the realm of forgotten and discarded customs.

Is it not true that the cure for war is the cure of mankind? And when, in that better day of the future, the unruly passions of the individual shall have been curbed; when the intelligence of men, singly and in the group, shall have been trained to understand the eternal futility of violence; when the wills of leaders shall have been taught to seek the peaceful happiness of their people's firesides as the true objective of governments; and when the gratification of vaulting national ambition at the expense of others shall be seen in its true light, then shall wars and rumors of wars cease to plague the earth.

A Great Teacher, who walked among men more than nineteen centuries ago, counselled them to put away the sword: to love one another. And he laid down as a golden principle of life the rule: "Whatso ever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them." When the day shall come in which mankind shall understand the deep quality of these precepts as rules of life, and shall learn to live by them, and shall know that they are, in very truth, the guides to long life, to peace, and to happiness, among nations as among men, then, and probably not until then, will the scourge of war be gone. 

I look into the clear blue eyes of the young man of ten years who accompanied me on my journey here, and I hope, with the fond hope that only a Father or Mother can know, that never, in all of his lifetime, will the spectre of war take him away from the family circle and subject him to the loneliness, and suffering, and death of war. But I hope even more that if, in the course of his lifetime, and whether it be in a position of leadership or as just a plain good citizen of his country, he shall be confronted with the question which Abraham Lincoln, and John A.

Logan, and Woodrow Wilson had to answer, he will, like them, have the courage to choose the pathway of honor and will say, as did they: "this

Union, it must, and shall be, preserved."

Stanley S. Gillam 

May 31st, 1937.
