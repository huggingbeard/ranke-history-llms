FOREWORD 

U.S. dairy cattle breeders and exporters have shown an increasing interest in the export market for U.S. cattle. They are not only interested in maintaining their present markets but always alert to discover new ones. However, to appraise future possibilities, they need information on the worldwide marketing of dairy cattle. That information has not been available.

The Purebred Dairy Cattle Association expressed the need for such information and has requested the Administrator of the Foreign Agricultural Service to have this study made and the results published. 

In this rapidly changing world, markets can, and do, change almost overnight. These changes often come about, even in areas where the indigenous cattle population has been carefully developed through the use of high-quality imported breeding animals and post-sales services. 

Political and economic changes bring an end to thriving business relationships. Changes may be temporary or permanent. In either case, alternative markets are not only advisable but necessary, so that the export machinery can continue to function efficiently. 

This study attempts to show where opportunities lie for top-quality 

U.S. dairy stock.

PREFACE 

The countries included in this survey are so different that it was impossible to gather uniform information on each. 

The author originally sent a questionnaire in early 1959 to the agricultural attachés in all of the U.S. embassies in the world. The questions were an attempt to learn as much as possible about the trade in purebred dairy cattle for presentation in a short, concise manner. The author would like to express his appreciation to all of the attaches and their staffs for contributing the information that forms the backbone of this report. To the information submitted by the attaches, he has added his personal observations in the 47 countries he has visited in this type of work.

This first revision contains additions made to the number of countries reporting and changes in regulations and requirements of the countries represented in the original printing. 

Of the 52 countries or areas to which this questionnaire was sent, those that are neither importers nor exporters of dairy cattle are here listed separately, to show that they were not overlooked in this study. 

These are as follows: Burma, Indonesia, Iraq, Iran, Portugal, Thailand, and West Africa.

The author has made a sincere effort to obtain from each of the countries or areas the latest health and milk production requirements for imports and exports of dairy cattle. He welcomes notices of omissions or official changes in these regulations. 1018151

GUIDE FOR U.S. CATTLE EXPORTERS By Frederick M. Lege III Dairy and Poultry Division INTRODUCTION 

There have been noticeable changes in the marketing picture for dairy cattle since the end of World War II. 

Transportation costs have prevented U.S. cattle from being more competitive in world markets, but, in recent years, new airline equipment (DC-7 F) and 100-percent cattle boats have allowed the freight companies to lower their rates to more competitive levels. 

The greatest growth in foreign marketing by any country has been that of Denmark. Its exports have increased 1,700 percent since 1950. The sales policies outlined in this report explain, in part, the reason for this increase. 

Personal observation in Europe indicates that the most rapid advance since 1950 in the breeding of cattle for high production has taken place in Italy, Sweden, and Denmark. 

European buyers show a marked interest in U.S. dairy cattle, much more pronounced than the export figures would indicate at this time. The increases in milk production that farmers are getting by using U.S. bulls are the main selling factor. 

In general, however, Europe is the logical market for cattle from Denmark, Holland, and Sweden; Latin America, for U.S. and Canadian dairy cattle; and Asia the logical market for Australian and New Zealand dairy cattle. 

Nevertheless, more competitive freight rates and a more aggressive sales policy will see more and more U.S. cattle competing in Europe and Asia in the not-too-distant future their selling point being to furnish more production per dollar invested, both in the animal itself and in the feed available.

Trends and potential.--High exchange rates in 1958, 1959, 1960, and 1961 have hindered the importation of dairy cattle by Argentina. On October 22, 1962, the rate was 135 pesos per U.S. dollar (the 1957 rate was 40 pesos per U.S. dollar).

Transportation costs favor the United States among the competing suppliers. In spite of costs for air freight, ranging from $800 to $1,200 per head, this is the favored means of transportation. With the new airline equipment available, these rates should become cheaper in the near future.

Argentina's imports from the United States are not expected to increase beyond 20 bulls and 50 females per year until the structure of the milk industry becomes stronger.

Regulations. 

There are no production requirements on exported animals, but Argentina gives a guarantee that exported animals will meet the import requirements of the importing country, will be free from known inherited unsoundness and from tuberculosis, brucellosis, and trichomoniasis, and will have a guarantee of registration and transfer. 

Pedigreed animals are registered in the herd book of the Argentine Rural Society. The

Local Trends and Potential 

The purebred industry in Argentina is remaining almost static, though a slight growth is noticeable. There is a marked shift away from Milking Shorthorns in favor of Holsteins. The absolute potential for exports is not known but is believed to be in the range of 10,000 head a year, without damaging the local industry. This number would be an estimated 85 percent Pura por Cruza and 15 percent purebred.

Generally speaking, prices for breeding cattle have fluctuated only slightly since 1952; they were perhaps 10 percent higher in 1956-57 and 7 to 8 percent higher in 1958. There are no known production requirements for the exported animals, and Australia exports both pedigreed and grade cattle.

The potential for export is restricted chiefly by the limited demand for milk in the Far East.

Austria

Austria is basically an exporting country. Its exports are not broken down by breed or by use (such as beef and dairy); as a result, the statistics have little meaning. 

Customers and volume.--West Germany and Italy are the principal markets. Sales to Italy, Yugoslavia, Czechoslavakia, and Poland decreased in 1961, while sales to Greece, Turkey, Russia, and Bulgaria increased. Total exports in 1961 were 33,300 head, down 80 head from 1960. 

Trends and potential.--Prospects for exports of the Alpine varieties, i.e., Brown Swiss, identified by certificate of origin, are very favorable; such cattle may be shipped to EEC countries duty free.

Under an arrangement within the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, the EEC conceded to Austria a reduced tariff rate of 6 percent for an annual quota of 20,000 head of dairy and work stock. The Common External Tariff is 16 percent. The reduced tariff quota is thought to be about equal to Austria's exporting potential for these types of cattle for the next few years. Belgium

Regulations.--The cattle breeding industry in Belgium is based on the Royal Decree of 

February 10, 1954, relating to improvement of cattle breeds (amended by the Royal Decrees of

In spite of the fact that the Belgian cattle industry satisfies all the needs of the country, imports are authorized without quantitative restrictions, provided that the importation is approved by the technical services of the Department of Agriculture and provided that the imported cattle belong to one of the breeds officially recognized in Belgium: i.e., the Middle and High Belgium breed, the red heads of West Flanders, the red piebald breed of East Flanders, the red piebald breed Campinoise, and the black piebald breed. 

In addition, breeding animals that have satisfactorily met the conditions above must also comply with the following conditions regarding breeding, conformation, and production: 

1. At least a 3-generation pedigree of registrations in the Herd Books maintained by the breed. 

2. Freedom from hereditary defects. 

3. Conformation of bulls approved by a provincial commission of exports. 

4. A production record satisfying the norms listed below: (a) Animal should be by a sire that has been tested with favorable results, with no less than 25 daughters having production records in the first 305 days of their first lactation

showing an increased production, with a minimum of 8,377 pounds of milk containing 3.8 percent butterfat for the red piebald breed, and 8,818 pounds of milk containing 4 percent butterfat for the blackpiebald breed. (b) The dams must have been under daily milk-production testing, with an average of 27 pounds per day containing a minimum of 3.8 percent butterfat for the red piebald breed, and 30 pounds per day containing a minimum of 4.0 percent butterfat for the black piebald breed.

If the sire in question does not meet the requirements listed in part (a) above (i.e., has not been tested), the dam and the granddam should meet the requirements of part (b) above. 

Cows should meet the production standards outlined in part (b) above, and heifers should meet those of parts (a) and (b) above. 

Last of all, imported animals should be used in accordance with the regulations of February 10, 1954, and not taken to farms in a zone where the breed is not officially recognized. The imported animals may not be taken out of this designated zone for 2 years following their importation. 

The competent services of the Department of Agriculture may allow exemptions from the rules outlined above in authorizing the importation of animals that do not meet the established specifications, but importers must prove that these animals have certain qualities that will be useful in attaining the goals of the breeders and that are recognized as such by the service. 

Veterinary regulations regarding importation of breeding cattle are based on articles 44 and 51 of the Royal Decree of September 20, 1883, amended by the Royal Decree of December 5, 1952, and by the Ministerial decree of December 6, 1952, relative to the importation, transit, and exportation of domestic animals.

Each animal should be covered by a separate veterinary certificate made out by an officially recognized practicing veterinary of the country of origin, issued within 3 days of the animal's departure. 

This certificate should attest to the following: 

1. That this animal is free of the contagious diseases listed in article 44 of the Royal Decree of September 20, 1883, amended by the Royal Decree of December 5, 1952. 

2. That none of the contagious diseases contained in article 319 of the Penal Code exist in the place of export nor in the surrounding area. 

3. That in the last 15 days, and no more than 3 months prior to the date of the certificate, the animal has been vaccinated against type O, A, and C aftosa (foot-and-mouth disease viruses) with an inactive vaccine prepared with aluminum hydroxide, administered and controlled by the official veterinary services. 

Imported animals are subject to a quarantine period of not less than 5 days or more than 10 days. During this time they are subjected to blood agglutination tests for brucellosis and intradermal injection tests for tuberculosis. Animals showing a positive reaction to these tests are returned or slaughtered.

Importation must be made at the authorized ports of entry especially set up for this purpose. 

At the present time, there has been no importation of cattle from the United States because of the involved health procedures mentioned above. Brazil 

Brazil is an importing country only.

Sources and volume. -- The main countries that supply Brazil with dairy cattle for breeding purposes are Argentina, Uruguay, Canada, the United States, the Netherlands, Sweden, and the United Kingdom. Over 75 percent of the country's breeding cattle requirements are imported from Argentina and Uruguay. Import statistics issued by the Ministry of Finance do not specify breed, but give only the total number of head imported; and this total includes meat-type breeding cattle. Unofficial sources say, however, that 50 percent of all cattle imported are dairy breeds.

In 1959, imports totaled 5,136 head, and sources were as follows: Uruguay, 3,338 (mostly beef animals); Argentina, 1,471 (also mostly beef); the Netherlands, 124; the United Kingdom, 78; the United States, 62; Germany, 37; Canada, 18; Sweden, 4; Switzerland, 3; France, 1. 10

Breeds, ages, and prices. 

Brazilian breeders prefer Holstein cattle. There is no reason to believe the future trend will change away from this breed. 

The average age of the cattle that the Brazilians prefer to import is 12 to 18 months. They base this preference on lower shipping costs, easier acclimatization, and lower prices. The overall average for the dairy and beef imports was almost US$270 per head. Monetary exchange rates and transportation costs are the limiting factor for purchases in the United States. Shipments from Argentina and Uruguay are made by truck or ship, but almost all shipments from the United States and Canada are made by air.

British East Africa (Kenya and Tanganyika) 

These two countries are importers. Kenya, however, is also an exporter, whereas Tanganyika exports no cattle or only a few, mostly to Kenya. 

Imports 

Sources and volume. Main sources for importations into Kenya are the United Kingdom (major source), the Netherlands, South Africa, and Germany, which all supply Holsteins; India and Pakistan, Sahiwals; and the United States, for Holsteins, Guernseys, and Brown Swiss. Total imports seldom exceed 100 head. In 1958, Tanganyika imported 800 head from Kenya, but except for the Red Sindhi breed, very few of its imports originate outside East Africa. 

Breeds, ages, and prices. The popular breeds in Kenya, in order of preference, are as follows: Ayrshire, Holstein, Guernsey, Jersey, and, far behind, Red Polls, Shorthorns, and Brown

Swiss (one herd of which was recently started). The more advanced African stockmen who are located in suitable climatic areas are being encouraged to keep European grade stock, with partiality to Guernseys. They like the size, milk yield, and adaptability to local conditions. U.S. 

Friesians, Guernseys, and Brown Swiss have been imported in the past and have been found very satisfactory.

Air transport has affected the average age of animals imported. Mature animals are generally imported by sea, but young animals, 1 to 5 months, are transported by air. Anything 18 months or older is shipped in by ocean freight. 

Prices on the imported calves for AI studs average US$700 from Britain, and prices up to $1,700 are not uncommon.

Regulations. Heavy emphasis is put on the production records of dams, but no set rules have been established yet. For health, regulations are listed in the Animal Disease Ordinance of 1958, a summary of which follows.

Summary of Regulations Governing the Importation of Livestock Into Kenya 

Certificates as detailed below are required for every importation, whether by air, sea, or overland.

For imports from all countries: Certificate dated not more than 10 days before embarkation, showing that cattle have come from an area (a farm, in the case of the United 

Kingdom) free of infectious and contagious disease; a tuberculin test dated within 20 days of embarkation; an export license from the country of origin; certificates showing freedom from (a) Johne's disease, trichomoniasis, and vibriosis, dated not more than 10 days before export; (b) contagious abortion (agglutination test dated not more than 20 days prior to date of certificate); (c) for herd of origin, freedom for 2 years from above diseases, dated not more than 10 days prior to export.

For imports from Tanganyika and Uganda: Certificate showing freedom from trypanosomiasis (for female breeding stock only).

For imports from Australia: Certificate showing freedom from ticks. (N.B.: Australian authorities require certificate from D.V.S., Kenya, that importer is a bona fide farmer.)

Imports from South Africa and Southern Rhodesia may, on application to D.V.S., be permitted from mid-May to mid-August, by sea only, and by agreed route from farm to port of embarkation. Freedom from lumpskin disease and freedom from ticks must be proved.

On September 1, 1959, the Danish veterinary authorities declared brucellosis (Bang's disease) eradicated in Danish cattle herds.

The voluntary collective work of combatting brucellosis and bovine tuberculosis was terminated on the same date. Eradication of the two diseases from Danish cattle herds is the result of concerted efforts made by Danish farmers, veterinarians, and the Danish dairy industry. 

From September 1, 1959, on, the veterinary authorities of the government assumed the entire responsibility of keeping possible new outbreaks of the two diseases under control. 

To facilitate the control it was announced: (a) That all milk received at the dairies must be tested for brucellosis with 3 months' intervals and (b) that all herds from which milk is delivered to a dairy must be tested for bovine tuberculosis with intervals of approximately 3 years. 

The individual farmer has the responsibility of informing the veterinary authorities if he suspects his herd has been infected with either one of the two diseases. If the farmer neglects to do so, he is subject to punishment. Furthermore, he must follow the instructions of the veterinarian in regard to treatment or disposal of the infected herd. If he does not comply with the instructions, he will be penalized. 

The Federation of Danish Dairy Associations registers all herds free of tuberculosis and brucellosis. Milk from registered herds only must be delivered to the dairies. 

In other words, since September 1, 1959, all milk being commercially distributed or processed has come only from herds free of bovine tuberculosis and brucellosis. 

Production requirement on the dams of exported animals is that their yield be "not less than the average yield of the past 3 years for the breed in question." In this case, the source is "Survey of the Statistical Results of Danish Milk Recording Societies," the official milk recording organization of Denmark.

Export marketing techniques. -- While no specific terms for financing the purchase of Danish cattle are known, nevertheless the methods used in fulfilling a contract for 190 animals to Portuguese Angola in 1959 reflect the scope of services rendered foreign buyers by Danish exporters. 

The Danish National Committee on Exports of Breeding Cattle received an order from Portuguese Angola for 190 head of cattle. The order implied, however, that selection of the individual animals was to be made by the Danish exporter. The total consignment was made up of 150 bred heifers, 35 milk cows, and 5 bulls. All animals were sent by rail to Hamburg for shipment to Angola. The chairman and the board of directors of the export committee met in 

Hamburg to inspect each single animal before they were finally approved for export. Interviewed on this point, the chairman said, "When the buyers have so much confidence in us that they ask us to select the animals, we are obliged to be very critical in our selection.' Some heifers and one bull were rejected in Hamburg by the Danish Committee because of their appearance at that time. The animals were sent back to Denmark, and new and better animals were ordered and sent down to Hamburg by truck. 

The ship used for transportation had been especially equipped to accommodate animals. 

The sea journey took 28 days. A Danish 5-man team was sent along to attend and care for the cattle. The leader of the team was a Danish farmer who was formerly a farm manager in Kenya. 

He was acquainted, therefore, with tropical conditions, and he spoke fluent English and German. 

Another member of the team held a Bachelor's degree in both agricultural and veterinary science. 

Further, the team included another independent farmer and two young farmhands. 

The Danes thus could feel assured that they had done their utmost to provide the best products available and to have them shipped in a manner that would guarantee the satisfaction of the customer. The costs of transportation from Hamburg to Angola were reported at 400,000 kroner ($58,000), or $305.26 per head.

Trends of Local Industry 

Economic factors affecting Denmark's purebred cattle industry are the increasing costs of labor and difficulties in obtaining the necessary qualified hired herdsmen. These factors are the principal reason for the downward trend in the number of herds during the postwar period. 

The remaining herds have, however, displayed a gradually expanding trend. This is reflected in the fact that the overall number of cattle has remained rather stable. 

The local demand for breeding cattle may, therefore, be characterized as steady. This applies at least to the Red Danish Milkrace and the Black and White Danish Milkrace. In the case of Jersey, the number has been expanding and, accordingly, the local demand for breeding animals has increased. 

The position of the Danish dairy industry requires improvement, but relative to the dairy industries of other countries, the Danish industry may be characterized as sound. 

Industry leaders are of the opinion that the keen competition in dairy products on world markets can only be met and overcome by Denmark through more efficient operations and higher 23

quality products. They suggested that creameries be reduced in number and consolidated into larger units, and that large units of butter and churning plants be set up. A national committee of the dairy industry has been appointed to coordinate the solution of the problems involved.

Imports 

Sources and volume. India's imports of dairy cattle are very small. Known imports in recent years have included Friesians from the Netherlands; Brown Swiss from Switzerland; and

Most of the imported animals are yearling bulls, and a few, heifers. The average prices are not known, since most of the imports have been donations or have been made under aid programs.

Transportation by ship and air is expensive, but the rates from European countries are about half those from the United States or Oceanic countries. About 75 percent of the imported cattle are shipped by sea and 25 percent by air freight. Regulations. 

There are no production requirements on imported animals. Animals must be accompanied by certificates of origin and health and are subject to health inspection and quarantine periods imposed by designated veterinarians of the Government of India. No fertility guarantees are required. 

Trends and potential. It appears that Jerseys and Brown Swiss are preferred because of their tolerance to heat. On the other hand, Jersey crossbred bullocks are considered unsatisfactory, whereas Holstein and Brown Swiss cross bullocks have size but lack hump for acceptance by Indian cultivators. For milk purposes, production is increased two to four times on the first cross. 

Sexual maturity increased by 7 to 14 months in crossbred animals. 

U.S. imports are generally satisfactory, but there are many problems and complications, peculiar to India, connected with their use. Briefly, some of these are the lack of resistance to diseases, heat, and insects. India's low level of nutrition limits the use of U.S. breeds to a few breeding farms or institutions. Indians generally dislike an animal without a hump. They consider draft power and manure production more valuable than milk production. The buffalo is the preferred milk animal because of the high fat content of its milk. 

Imports from the United States to India will continue to face obstacles, since milk and dairy programs are at the botton of the list for import preference. Licenses for commercial imports are not available because of foreign exchange limitations. While imports are not prohibited, licensing limitations are applicable to all countries. For all practical purposes imports are limited to aid and gifts. Little opportunity for policy change will exist before 1962. 

Future import possibilities may increase if research underway on crossbreeding of U.S. breeds with native cattle is successful. Though the present status of India's dairy industry is not encouraging, there is some possibility that the dairy program may develop to the point that imports of bulls will be required. The very favorable results of the crossbreeding program to date may brighten the prospects for small sales in the future. Exports

VETERINARY INSPECTION OF LIVESTOCK INTENDED FOR EXPORT A. General requirements. 

The aim of the inspection of livestock intended for export is to give the greatest possible guarantee to foreign buyers as to the health of the animals concerned. 

In the Act for the Organisation of the Government Veterinary Service (26th March 1920), briefly called the Livestock Act (Veewet), provisions are made relating directly to the improvement of the general health of the livestock population. In addition, this Act contains regulations governing the inspections for export. 

By Royal Decree of 26th May 1922 (State Gazette 203) which enforces Sections 66 and 67 

of the Livestock Act, conditions have been laid down concerning livestock intended for export 45

that must be complied with before a health certificate can be issued. For the purpose of this 

Act "livestock" means ruminants, equines and pigs. 

It is prohibited to export livestock without a health certificate. 

Inspections can only be carried out between sunrise and sunset. All animals presented for inspection are examined individually. Livestock intended for export must be examined immediately prior to export by an official of the Veterinary Service. It must be evident at this examination that the animal does not suffer from any of the infectious diseases quoted in the Act, nor show any suspected signs of these. In addition, the animal may not show other symptoms or defects which would render it unsuitable for export. 

In this Royal Decree are also enumerated the ailments or defects rendering an animal unsuitable for export, even though not suffering from any of the quoted infectious diseases. 

These are as follows: a. b.

C. 

Any deviations, which might be mistaken for being one of the infectious diseases stated in the Act.

Obvious symptoms of an internal disease, whether or not accompanied by fever, even if it has not been possible to diagnose the exact nature of the disease. 

Any lameness or defect in the gait, which might be a hindrance during transport. d. Any considerable wounds or sub-cutaneous abcesses. e. f. 

Advanced emaciation of the animal, regardless of whether or not a disease can be diagnosed.

Any other symptoms or defects, which are considered to render the animals unsuitable for export.

The director of the Veterinary Service may recommend the enforcement of additional regulations. For instance, in case of an increasing number of outbreaks of one or more infectious diseases, the purchase at markets of animals intended for export may be prohibited. 

B. Specific requirements.

Apart from the requirements mentioned above, there are the following requirements with regard to contagious abortion, foot and mouth disease and bovine tuberculosis: 

1. Contagious abortion. 

Only animals which come from farms which are free from Bang's disease may be exported. Moreover they must have been subjected to a serological blood test for contagious abortion with negative result within one month before the date of exportation. 

2. Foot and mouth disease.

Cattle of more than 4 months old may only be exported if they have been inoculated against foot and mouth disease (types A, O and C) at least 14 days and not more than 4 months prior to the date of exportation. This does not apply to animals under 

4 months old on account of the immunity the young animal acquires from the regularly immunized dam. 

3. Bovine tuberculosis.

Only animals which have reacted negatively to a double tuberculine test with avian and bovine tuberculine, within one month before the date of shipment, may be exported. 

Ever since the end of the 5-year plan for the eradication of bovine tuberculosis on 1st May 1956 the Netherlands have been free from tuberculosis. 

The antigen which is used for the serological blood test for brucellosis, the vaccine against foot and mouth disease, and also the tuberculine are prepared at a Governmentcontrolled institute.

In this way a guarantee is obtained that only animals complying with very rigid health requirements are exported.

When an importing country however imposes special and more extensive conditions on the importation of livestock, the Veterinary Service will see to it that these requirements are complied with. 46

Imports 

Regulations.--A. General. The animals intended for importation should be presented for examination in the official animal quarantine stations to ascertain whether they should be admitted, or held in quarantine for observation. If their origin, sanitary conditions and the health certificate permit, importation is authorized (Article 77 of the Regulations on Epizootics). If the importer did not submit a health certificate on the animals that he wants to import, they will have to remain in quarantine and be submitted to a period of observation for a minimum of 8 days, at the end of which period importation will be authorized, if there have been no symptoms of any infectious or contagious disease (Article 79 of the Regulations on Epizootics). 

If it should be necessary to change the itinerary of the animals from a country free of infectious and contagious disease and in the process be necessary to route the shipment through countries or regions where there is widespread infectious and contagious disease, the abovementioned health certificate will not exempt the animals from the period of observation designated by the Director General of Livestock. 

When the Veterinary Inspector confirms the existence of an infectious or contagious disease in the imported animals, they will be reexported or slaughtered in the quarantine station at the importer's option.

The importers of breeding animals that would be classified as choice, in order to meet the procedure for obtaining the license from the Ministry of Commerce, ought to present an accredited certificate showing that the animals are of select quality, issued by the Director General of Livestock and should be accompanied by a commercial invoice containing the number of animals to be imported, the signature or symbol of the supplier, the breed, race, sex, age, and genealogical antecedents of the cattle, and their destination.

Bovine: (a) Dairy cattle. The female antecedents in the pedigree should have a minimum production of 5,000 kg. of milk in 305 days of production for Holstein-Friesian and 3,000 liters for 

Brown Swiss. These animals must be accompanied by certificates of registration in the official 

Herd Book of the country of origin. 

In addition to proof of freedom from infectious and contagious diseases, they should have proof of negative tests for tuberculosis and brucellosis, such tests being made less than a month before they were shipped from their point of origin. If these certificates do not accompany the cattle, tests will be made by the inspecting veterinarian of the Customs House, who will hold the cattle 3 days for the tuberculin test and 12 hours for the blood test for brucellosis, and gave the corresponding certificates in case of a negative result. Any positive reactors will be reexported or slaughtered (Article 89 of the Regulations of Epizootics).

There have been no imports of U.S. dairy cattle in the past few years. However, the author has seen excellent quality exports from Sweden that traced back to

The United Kingdom 

There are no significant imports of dairy cattle into the United Kingdom. As a February 1961 letter from the British Embassy explains, imports are governed by the legislation which in general prohibits imports of cattle, sheep, and goats and all other ruminating animals and swine, as a safeguard against the introduction and spread of animal diseases from abroad. The legislation in question is the Diseases of Animals Act, 1950, and the Animals (Importation) Order, 1930. Imports are, however, permitted under the following sections of the Diseases of Animals Act: 

Section 26 enables the Minister and the Secretary of State for Scotland - acting jointly, to make Special Orders for the landing, subject to such special conditions as they may think necessary, of pedigree cattle, sheep, goats or swine from the countries specified in subsection (2). 'The animals must be registered as pedigree stock in a herd or flock book recognised by the Minister and the Secretary of State after consultation with the Royal Agricultural Society of England and the Royal Highland Agricultural Society of Scotland; such animals have to be placed in quarantine. A separate Order is needed for each consignment of animals. These Orders must be laid before both Houses of Parliament. 

Section 27 enables the Minister and the Secretary of State to make Orders for allowing the landing of animals intended for exhibition or other exceptional purposes. Orders under this 'Her Majesty's Dominion, Republic of Ireland, India, any territory under Her Majesty's protection and any territory in respect of which a mandate is being exercised by the Government of any part of Her Majesty's Dominion. 63

Section must also be laid before both Houses of Parliament, and an Order is necessary for each consignment of animals for exceptional purposes. There is a Standing Order to cover the entry of animals for exhibition under which the animals (i) have to be placed in one of a number of urban Zoos approved by the two Agricultural Departments, (ii) must remain isolated there for at least 28 days, and (iii) must remain in the urban Zoo for another 11 months before they may be moved to other Zoos. There are a few importations of this kind annually and only very rarely are animals imported for other purposes.

Any cow accepted by the Friesian Breed Selection Commission may be eliminated by the experts, if the experts find in subsequent revisions that it does not meet the conditions required by the Friesian Breed Selection Commission.

In the matter of transportation costs, the United States has the advantage of proximity over its strongest competitor, Canada.

Yugoslavia Imports 

Sources and volume. Yugoslavia is actively carrying out its policy of importing dairy breeding cattle of leading breeds, in order to establish the breeds in Yugoslavia, thereby increasing milk production. The first interest in the Holstein breed was stimulated by a few head of 

U.S. Holstein stock that were imported under the UNRRA program. When it was found that these cattle were well adapted to Yugoslav conditions, many outstanding specimens of this breed (mostly high-producing cows and heifers, with about 5 bulls per thousand) were purchased from the Netherlands. There are now several thousand registered Dutch Black-and-White cows on state farms in Yugoslavia. Offspring are sold to other state farms, collectives, or to private individuals. 

Yugoslavia also has imported a few head of Dutch Red-and-White (Rotbund), considerable numbers of Danish Red dairy cattle from Denmark, and some Black and White cattle from West Germany. 

Simmental bulls and a few cows have been imported from Switzerland, for the purpose of improving the domestic Simmental breed, which is the most common in Yugoslavia.

(b) Accompanied with certificates stating that they are healthy and are not infected with Bang's disease and that they are from herds that are free of brucellosis. 

Yugoslav veterinary authorities do not permit imported cattle to be vaccinated with Bang's vaccine strain 19 virus before entry, as this makes it difficult to ascertain whether the animal was infected prior to vaccination. No guarantee of fertility is required. 

Trends and potential. 

Price and availability of foreign currency are important considerations in determining the country of origin of imports of breeding cattle. The Yugoslav decision to import Dutch and Danish cattle breeds was due to their fine qualities and their ability to produce high yields of milk in Yugoslavia under proper management. However, some Yugoslav livestock 71

experts have stated that Yugoslavia would import Black-and-White and Danish Red breeding cattle from other countries also, if they could be purchased at lower prices and if foreign currency were available for payment. These experts say dairy cattle from certain parts of the 

United States may be better suited to Yugoslav climatic conditions than the original breeds of the Netherlands and Denmark.

U.S. cattle have proved their worth in Yugoslavia, but imports are limited by lack of foreign exchange. Some U.S. dairy cattle were imported into Yugoslavia during 1945 under the UNRRA program. Although details of the imports are not readily available, it is known that some Holstein- 

Friesian cattle were imported. They reportedly have given excellent results in their new environment. Some were allocated to the Belje state farm near Osijek; others were given to the livestock institute at Zemun, which is connected with the University of Belgrade Faculty of Agriculture. 

One Yugoslav livestock expert has stated that an important factor leading authorities to choose to import and introduce the Black-and-White breed of dairy cattle was the high production of milk by U.S. Holstein cows. In 1950 and 1951, the livestock institute at Zemun carried out feeding and milking experiments with 28 Holstein-Friesian and 53 Simmental cows. During these two years, the Holstein-Friesian cows reportedly gave an average yield of 10,177 pounds of milk per lactation period, while the Simmental cows gave an average of only 7,700 pounds under the same conditions.
