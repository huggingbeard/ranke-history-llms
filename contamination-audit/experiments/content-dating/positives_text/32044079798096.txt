THE BEGINNINGS OF COORDINATION IN OKLAHOMA I. Introduction 

The following pages give the story of the beginnings of the coordination of higher education in Oklahoma from statehood to the present time. But chiefly they are the record of accomplishment of the present 

Coordinating Board, for the reason that this board is the first official body that has actually, consistently, and pointedly attacked the problem of an explicit higher educational policy for Oklahoma. These accomplishments, described in detail below, may be summed up as follows:

II. Preliminary Events 

At statehood, Oklahoma merged two very different territories. Indian 

Territory had been organized under tribal government and had only in a rudimentary way the institutions common to an American commonwealth. 

Oklahoma had been an organized territory, functioning through institutions typical of any of the federated states. At statehood, therefore, there existed the Territorial University at Norman, the Territorial A. & M. College at 

Stillwater, Territorial Normal Schools at Edmond, Alva, and Weatherford, and the Colored A. & N. University at Langston. These institutions were felt to be improperly located to serve the new State, and the years immediately following statehood saw the founding of new institutions on the Indian Territory side of the commonwealth. As institutions increased in number and kind, the number of boards of control also increased. 

By 1911 the State of Oklahoma had, through the process of accretion, acquired sixteen educational boards in control of its several educational institutions. In that year an attempt was made to centralize control through an act which extended and defined the powers of the State Board of Education by making it the legal successor of fourteen of the sixteen boards. 

An excerpt from this act will be found in Appendix A. 

The State Supreme Court had ruled in 1909 that the fifteenth board, The State Board of Agriculture governing the Agricultural and Mechanical Colleges, was a constitutional board whose powers could not, therefore, be superseded by the statute of 1911 or by any other statutory provision. This ruling will be found in Appendix B. The sixteenth, The State Board of 

Public Affairs, was a business rather than an educational board. However, the purpose of this Board was to coordinate and unify the business and fiscal affairs not only of the State-supported institutions of higher learning, but of all branches of State government. It was a result of the establishment of this board that the action of Trapp vs. Cook Construction 

Company arose with the result just stated. The significance of these two events for the coordination of higher education lies in the fact that the ruling of the Supreme Court that the State Board of Agriculture, acting as the Board of Regents of the Oklahoma Agricultural and Mechanical College, is vested constitutionally with certain powers in the management of the Agricultural and Mechanical Colleges, which fact precludes the possibility of any complete coordination of all institutions under one board without constitutional amendment.

In 1917 an act was passed increasing the powers of the State Board of Education by designating certain higher educational institutions to confer degrees and empowering the State Board of Education to approve other institutions for degree conferring purposes. (See Appendix C.) This act

Introduction was amended in 1919 by the extension of the list to include certain additional institutions, but the power to approve other colleges and universities was left with the State Board of Education. (See Appendix D. 

By 1919 it was urged that too many heterogeneous interests and activities, having little or no relation to others, had been thrown together under the same board of control. During March and April of 1919, a series of statutes was passed and approved creating separate Boards of Regents for 

Oklahoma University, Oklahoma College for Women, Oklahoma Colored A. & N. University, University Preparatory School, State School of Mines at Miami, State School of Mines at Wilburton, and the Military Academy at Claremore. (See Appendix E.) This left only the six state teachers colleges under the State Board of Education. It is probable that, if these institutions had requested it, each of them would also have been granted an individual board.

Thus in eight years the effort at centralization, begun by Governor Lee Cruce, ended in almost complete decentralization. Since 1919 the several state-supported institutions have gone their several ways except for their support, which has, in the main, been kept under control of the legislature. 

The policy of Oklahoma with respect to its higher institutions may best be characterized as a policy of institutional and educational anarchy. 

III. Creation of the Coordinating Board 

The first published study of coordination of higher education in Oklahoma appears to have been the dissertation of President Henry G. Bennett, offered in partial fulfillment of the requirements of the Doctor's Degree at 

Columbia University in 1926. 

The message of Governor William J. Holloway, delivered before the senate and house of representatives of Oklahoma in May, 1929 (see Appendix F), recommended a reduction in number of governing boards for state institutions of higher learning and the establishment by law of a central coordinating agency for the purpose of definitely unifying the taxsupported institutions into a state system of higher education. The following July a bill was enacted providing for a state coordinating board. The text of this act appears in Appendix G. During the two years of his incumbency, however, Governor Holloway never appointed the two members of the board who were to represent the public, nor did the State Superintendent ever call a meeting of the ex-officio members for the purpose of organization.

The next movement toward coordination of the institutions of higher learning occurred in the fall of 1929 when there was organized The Oklahoma College Association. The constitution of this body, which was approved the following April, is shown as Appendix H of this bulletin. In the spring of 1932, after some preliminary discussion with various educational leaders, the proposal of a closer federation of the public and private colleges of the State of Oklahoma was made by the Right Reverend Francis C.

IV. THE LEGISLATIVE ACT ESTABLISHING THE STATE COORDINATING BOARD 

Constitution, Powers, and Duties of the Board The complete text of the legislative act establishing the State Coordinating Board appears as Appendix L of this bulletin. In brief, this act created a central coordinating agency to be known as the Coordinating Board composed of 15 members appointed by the Governor and confirmed by the Senate with terms "co-terminus" with that of the Governor of the State. The members serve without pay but are compensated for expenses incurred in performing the Board's duties. The Board is empowered to formulate such rules and regulations as are necessary to carry out the provisions of the act.

Four duties of the Board are prescribed: Fact-finding and reporting, interpreting and defining the functions of each institution in order to check up-grading and expansion and eliminate unnecessary and undesirable duplications, securing harmonious working relationships among the institutions by the establishment of needed councils, and the unifying of the tax-supported institutions through a plan for their further development. 

Regulations, courses of study or standards set up by the Board, when approved by the Governor, are binding. All conflicting laws or parts of laws are repealed. An emergency is declared and the act made effective immediately.

V. Initial Activities 

The new Coordinating Board, after meeting and organizing, proceeded to a consideration of the problems involved in coordination and adopted the following guiding principles: 

Guiding Principles for the Coordination of 

Higher Education in Oklahoma 

1. That each state institution of higher education should be a part of a single state system and that all institutions of higher education within a state both public and private should be affiliated or coordinated for the common educational good. 

2. That the sole object of such coordinated system should be to serve the state economically and well. 

3. That no one institution should attempt to cover the entire field of education. 

4. That no institution should attempt to offer a particular course of study when it can be shown that greater benefit would accrue to the state if it were undertaken at another school. 

5. That the best service of the state can be accomplished by friendly cooperation better than by uncorrelated competition and that such friendly cooperation can be secured only by a unification of administration. 

6. That since an institution's only excuse for existence is the service it can render, the final test of its function, as well as of its right to survive, is to determine the way in which it can best serve the state. 

7. That it is not necessary for education of every character or type to be offered within the boundaries of every state, and that consequently those in control of the state's unified system should be given certain broad powers to coordinate the higher educational efforts of their state with those of surrounding states, in order that needless duplication of effort may be eliminated. 

8. That it is the duty and right of the state to determine for all state schools the educational functions and standards which will best serve the interests of that state.

Act Establishing the Board 

9. That this coordinating board shall arrive at fundamental decisions only after giving due consideration to all parties and interests involved. 

10. That no major decisions by this coordinating board affecting the life of an institution or department shall be made before that institution or department has had a chance to present its case. 

11. That this board shall be guided by the principles of cooperation of state and moral forces without compromising the principles of either of our constitutional system of separations of church and state, and that any independent or church related college may affiliate with this movement without loss of autonomy. 

12. That in the deliberations of this board every decision shall be measured in the light of its prospective effect upon the establishment and maintenance of the Greater University of Coordinated Colleges. 

Preliminary Plan of Procedure 

Several plans of organization and procedure were submitted to the Board. The text of these plans is given in full in Appendices. "M,' 'N,' 'O,' 'P,' and 'Q.' The plan given in Appendix 'O' and entitled "A

Preliminary Plan of Procedure for the Greater University of Oklahoma" 

was formally adopted on motion August 28, 1933. 

The adopted plan recites excerpts from the act establishing the Coordinating Board and suggests that the plan of unification of tax-supported institutions might be organized around a central structure to be called The Greater University of Oklahoma headed by a Chancellor and supported during the experimental period by the institutions concerned, interested foundations, and other friends of higher education. Later, the necessary funds were to be obtained by legislative appropriation or by a pro rata part of tuition, and other fees. 

The Chancellor would be the executive officer of the Coordinating Board and The Greater University would be its agency for carryng on the detailed work required in the performance of the duties of the Board. The plan of procedure further authorizes the organization of an institutional cabinet composed of the heads of all cooperating institutions in order to secure harmonious working relationships among the institutions and to afford the Coordinating Board authoritative council. A Board of Educational Consultants is authorized, composed of outstanding specialists and leaders of higher education in the United States, retained from time to time. Specialists consulted from time to time so far include Dr. O. D. Foster; Dr. E. C. Elliott, President of Purdue University; the late Dr. Suzzalo and some of his associates of the Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching; and Mr. Lloyd Morey, Comptroller of the University of 

Illinois and Chairman of the National Committee on Standard Reports for Institutions of Higher Education. An Office of Administrative Research and Statistics was authorized, charged with the duty of assembling exact information that will show actual costs of higher education and the immediate and future needs for each school, and of preparing an annual report of this data, as required by law. It was provided that the Director be

an experienced and professionally prepared specialist skilled in educational statistics and reporting. This office was authorized to set up a structure of uniform and comparable student and fiscal accounting for all institutions and to interpret the facts in all institutions of higher learning within the State that bear immediately upon the issue of institutional costs, needs, up-grading, and expansion. These include: 

1. Cost studies. 

2. Faculty and student personnel studies. 

3. Effectiveness of staff utilization. 

4. Effectiveness of floorspace utilization. 

5. Analysis of institutional carrying loads. 

6. Analysis of course of study relationships and trends. 

7. Preparation of the consolidated financial report. 

8. Preparation of the consolidated budget recommendations of the Co ordinating Board. 

Proposed Constitution and By-Laws 

In accord with the adopted plan, the Coordinating Board next moved to incorporate The Greater University of Oklahoma. To this end there was drawn up a Constitution and By-Laws of The Greater University of Oklahoma. These instruments are shown in Appendix 'R.' Articles of incorporation were denied by the Secretary of State upon advice of the Attorney General, who ruled that the provisions for fiscal support contained in the constitution of The Greater University were illegal. Copies of the two rulings of the Attorney General in point are exhibited in Appendix 'S.' Before articles of incorporation can be granted, therefore, further legal powers must be secured from the State Legislature. Therefore, the actual incorporation of The Greater University is being held in abeyance.

VII. THE UNIFICATION OF ACCOUNTING 

Since some system for gathering comparable data and treating it in a uniform manner must underlie all research which lays claim to the scientific attitude, the first action of the Division of Fact-finding and Statictics was to propose systems of student accounting, faculty service reporting, and fiscal accounting and reporting.

Uniform Student Accounting 

The uniform student accounting includes: a statistical information card for each student in all institutions of higher education, to be filed in the central office; a monthly report of enrollments, including additions and withdrawals for the month reported upon; and a distribution of the credithour loads of all students actually in attendance at the close of the period. 

From this data it will be possible to compute comparable carrying loads for all institutions, following in general the recommendations of Bulletin No. 6 of the National Committee on Standard Reports for Institutions of Higher 

Education, "Suggested Forms for Enrollment Reports of Colleges and Uni versities." This proposal was accepted by the Administrative Committee, adopted by the Council of Presidents, and at present writing has been in force nearly six months. Aside from the possibility of getting comparable notions of the carrying loads of the several institutions, and from furnishing interesting and valuable knowledge concerning their student bodies, this data is also fundamental in making a state-wide cost study. 

Uniform Faculty Service Reports 

The individual service report form furnishes a direct report of the amount and kind of service rendered by every college instructor in the State, checked and certified to by his Dean or President. These forms analyze the teaching load both as to course taught and persons taught. 

They also give basic data for a study of the efficiency of instructional floor space utilization, as well as for a study of the interrelations of the various curricula of an institution, size of classes, and similar problems concerning the organization of teaching. X. SUMMARY OF WHAT HAS BEEN ACCOMPLISHED 

Progress of the Coordinating Board thus far, then, includes: 

1. The setting up of a plan of procedure consisting of comprehensive factual studies of the problems involved in the coordination and economical administration of the institutions of higher education in the State. 

2. The putting into effect of such practices and such changes in the administration of these institutions as are justified by these studies. 

3. The establishment of needed councils to bring about harmony among institutions. 

4. The institution of the necessary procedure for the gathering, treatment, and interpretation of uniform data, the institution of uniform student accounting, faculty service reporting, and fiscal accounting and reporting. 

5. Institution of a definite program of research and the establishment of effective machinery, including the establishment of a Division of Factfinding and Statistics, the appointment of a Director of Studies, the employment of competent, disinterested advisors, and the establishment of close support between the office of the Board, the Presidents, the Registrars, and the Business Officers of the several institutions. 

It is believed that the systems of reporting will give adequate data for the objective consideration of most of the problems arising within the scope of the Coordinating Board's duties.

APPENDIX B AN EXCERPT FROM RULING OF STATE SUPREME COURT, 1909, IN RE. POWERS AND DUTIES OF STATE BOARD OF AGRICUL- TURE ACTING AS A BOARD OF REGENTS 

The question then is: Did the board of agriculture, being constituted the board of regents of the agricultural and mechanical college, thereby have fixed and vested in it, by the language used, the duties, powers, and authority of the established territorial board of regents whose place it took, as the same existed at the time of the adoption of the Constitution? If so, necessarily the Legislature could not divest it of these powers nor any part thereof, for, if it could lawfully take a part, it could from time to time take more and ultimately take all, and thereby defeat the will of the people who fixed the status of this board in the organic law of the state. As we view it, additional duties may be required, but none vested by the Constitution may be taken away by the Legislature.

Higher education-This is a subject which is becoming serious as well as the aid necessary for the weak school districts of Oklahoma-is in Oklahoma largely a state enterprise. We maintain eighteen tax-supported institutions for higher learning, which enroll approximately 80 percent of all college students in the State. These schools have been created and have developed with no definite plan and with no correlating or coordinating agency to promote cooperation and unity of function and to prevent needless duplication and competition. As a natural consequence of this unregulated growth, constantly multiplying problems involving duplication of curriculum and of effort have arisen. It is evident that there can be no permanent solution of the State's higher educational difficulties until the issues are squarely faced and the State assumes responsibility for determining an intelligent plan for the future development of its colleges. The acceptance of the following principles seems to be essential before a state system of higher education can in reality be established:

Message of Governor Holloway 

17 1. All state institutions for higher education are parts of a single state system. 

2. The sole object of the system is to serve the state economically and effectively. 

3. Where several state institutions have been established and their functions defined, no one institution should attempt to cover the entire field of higher education. 

4. No institution should attempt to offer a particular course of study. when it can be shown that greater benefit to the state would result if that particular curriculum were offered in another school. 

5. State service can better be accomplished by friendly cooperation than by competition and rivalry.

There is no excuse for a spirit of competition developing among the higher institutions of learning in the State of Oklahoma. The only thing we want to consider is the welfare of all of the people in this state; that is all! We are not trying to build up anything for any individual nor any town in this state, but we have the problem of unifying the policy for higher education in this state. 

6. Since an institution's only excuse for existence is the service it can render, the final test of its function is to determine the way in which it best can serve the state.

Now, I am not just going to offer you a lot of general suggestions and then leave you in the woods. I am going to offer you some practical suggestions as the basis for intelligent consideration, because you haven't the time, in four or five weeks, to make an exhaustive and intelligent study of these great problems. And, don't think this is entirely from my study either. I have had some of the ablest men in this state work on this for some seven or eight weeks, and here is what I am going to recommend: 

To vitalize these principles and thereby bring about a unified state system of higher education, legislation should be enacted incorporating the following proposals: 

1. The number of governing boards for state institutions for higher learning should be reduced from nine to three: (a) The Board of Regents of Oklahoma University should control the State University. This Board was established by the Eleventh Legislature, and should be made permanent by constitutional amendment. 

So that when a Board is appointed, members thereof cannot be removed except for cause, and serious cause at that. Agricultural and Mechanical College.

APPENDIX I ADDRESS TO THE COLLEGE PRESIDENTS OF THE STATE OF OKLAHOMA, APRIL 23, 1932 (By the Right Reverend Francis C. Kelley, Bishop of Doctors and Gentlemen: 

Oklahoma City and Tulsa)

I hold that success in education depends on success in implanting in the student a definite and true philosophy of life. I endorse what Chesterton wrote in his "Heretics":

Address of Bishop Kelley 

23 There are some people-and I am one of them-who think that the most practical and important thing about a man is still his view of the universe. We think that for a landlady considering a lodger it is important to know his income, but still more important to know his philosophy. We think that for a general about to fight an enemy it is important to know the enemy's numbers but still more important to know the enemy's philosophy. We think the question is not whether the theory of the cosmos affects matters, but whether in the long run, anything else affects them.

William James puts himself on record as agreeing with Mr. Chesterton. 

I hold then that the first obligation and the final objective of the educator is the formation of a Christian citizen. That ideal cannot be reached without the united efforts of the home, the church and the school. But the first is breaking down under the blows of our matrimonial laws. The second is shaking from the spread of indifferentism. The third practically closes its doors to character-forming influences. 

When the common schools fail to do what the home and the church are prevented from doing there is hope only in the colleges out of which will come the leaders of the future. But we feel the fact that the very existence of those colleges upon which we have been depending is threatened. One by one they plead for life but one by one they are doomed to die. The State seems about to absorb them, even though the best of our educators have come from them and are unwilling to lose such faithful helpers in the cause of education and culture. When the State with its restrictions "by law established" takes over education the coffin will be ready for Christian civilization. True influence of the past will not die quickly. All education is living today on the intellectual and ethical food religion fed us for centuries. But an unreplenished larder is soon empty. For me the fall of the privately supported colleges will mark the beginning of the end of all that is worth while in our national life. 

How are these institutions to continue to live? They cannot live without the assistance of the people. The demands made on the people who support them directly for equipment and endowment by the standardizing agencies are based, not upon the results of their work, but upon arbitrarily imposed standards that would in by-gone days have stamped the greatest of our national leaders as educationally unfit. Present day educational standards are mostly the guesses and experiments of those who think that in change alone is progress. Yet no age in the history of the world has been as devoid of efficient leadership as our own. We are not able to compete with the past in statecraft, in literary genius, in any leadership but the scientific and mechanical; and our machines have brought on a crisis that threatens social order and the democracy upon which we pin our hope for political perfection.

The present depression leaves the privately supported colleges in the worst position in their history, for the people who were loyally generous to them are no longer able to manifest their devotion by financial support. 

To rely on them now is only to postpone the day of the crash. Today they can and will do a little; but what of tomorrow? Prosperity will return?

But will it return before a long period of readjustment during which all but death and taxes will become hesitant and afraid?

A movement to guard that essential thing called religion in our taxsupported schools, The North American Conference on Higher Education and Religion, gave me hope and set my memory to work. I asked myself if schools of religion at tax-supported colleges would save the situation? I am convinced that they cannot. Why? Because by cutting out all defnite religious teaching they become houses without foundations. To be useful

we must have something to teach beyond platitudes. The Universities of 

Iowa and California have a better idea for such schools than the usual one. 

They leave to religious leaders the care of their own followers by trying "cooperation without compromise." Will that work? It may. but it can never fully replace the denominational college; for it is not actual instruction that chiefly counts but atmosphere. 

Suggestions for a discussion looking to a solution for the problem is to have the State University become what a University should be: An aid to the colleges, a Graduate and Professional School and an institution for research, leaving to the smaller institutions their proper functions and trusting them to preserve in their students not only tested standards of scholarship but also the religious atmosphere so necessary to preserve all that makes for Christian citizenship.

I am considering the situation in Oklahoma only. Each State has its own special problems which might make my plan unworkable for them in whole or in part. I propose that the State go as far as its legal limitations permit to keep up the small colleges and help them. The State cannot under our laws give the denominational colleges financial support, but it would become the duty of the State University, once made what it should be, to see that everything possible is done to keep them up to a proper standard of scholastic efficiency, leaving to themselves the teaching of religion and the maintaining of a religious atmosphere. I do not think that even those who planned our public school system ever dreamed that its policy of ignoring religion would later on be extended to higher education. 

This assistance from the University would come by adopting the plan which I barely outline, but which I shall be glad to discuss in detail in response to your questions.

FIRST: The education work of the State University to be confided to a Board of Regents headed by a Chancellor who would, ex officio, be the President of the State University; the members to be the Presidents of all affiliated Colleges, senior and junior, in the State; the former with a full vote, the latter with half a vote.

SECOND: All Degrees to be granted by the Regents on the recommendation of the affiliated Colleges. 

THIRD: A College of Liberal Arts restricted in the number of its stu dents to be kept at the University proper for the benefit of the Graduate School. All other undergraduates to be encouraged to go to the affiliated 

Colleges. The College or Liberal Arts at the University not to compete with affiliated Colleges in the matter of tuition fees. 

FOURTH: The Regents to aid affiliated Colleges by the loan of professors, books and laboratory equipment; all property thus loaned to be owned by the Regents but Taken Care of by the Colleges benefitted. 

FIFTH: The physical properties of State Educational Institutions to be taken care of by Boards of Trustees, one or many as found necessary, leaving education entirely to educators.

The advantages of the plan would be divided between (a) the people, (b) the University, (c) the Colleges, (d) the towns and counties in which the Colleges are located, and (e) the teachers. (a) The people. As the largest part of the burden of support for College education by this plan would fall upon the denominations (which would be only too glad to accept it) the taxpayer would profit by reductions in taxes. No new educational buildings, for example, would be needed by the State. (b) The University would take the place that history and tradition assigns to a University, instead of being as now half University and half 

College.

25 (c) The Colleges by giving their students a Regent's Degree would naturally keep those of their own denominations as well as attract local students who now go away from home. This plan would, I think, save such assets for the people most interested in retaining them. The plan I have outlined has little that is new in it. It is a composit of plans used by such old Universities as Oxford as well as by the newer Universities of Toronto, Laval and Montreal. APPENDIX K THE OKLAHOMA GREATER UNIVERSITY-COORDINATING ALL COLLEGES, PRIVATE AND PUBLIC, UNDER ONE HARMONIOUS SYSTEM 

After many discussions over the coordination of institutions of higher education in this state, I have come to the conclusion, after conferring with authorities on the subject representing both the tax-supported as well as the non-tax supported colleges, that the State owes something more to the latter than a mere "thank you." Going farther, I am convinced that, by bringing those institutions, which were our pioneers in the field of higher education without increasing the burdens of the taxpayers by a dollar, into the picture, and letting them help us solve our problems, we shall not only be rendering to them a belated recognition, but working for the best interests of education, and saving the State money. 

The Non-Tax Supported Colleges 

It may not have occurred to the average citizen to consider the debt that tax-supported higher education owes to the independent non-tax-supported colleges. The percentage of State educators who received all, or the greater part, of their training in these independent colleges is high, perhaps as high as seventy-five percent of the men and women who today are the backbone of State-supported higher education. These represent a contribution of ability and brains at no expense to the State. 

Now these independent colleges are becoming slowly done to death by thoughtless insistence on keeping them out of our planning. The State enters into what must be considered, in all fairness, active competition with them; but since the State in this is backed by the taxes, the battle is onesided. Yet, if the State allow these independent colleges to go to the wall, it is plain that the burden of the taxpayer must needs be increased. He will have to take care of thousands of students who now cost him nothing. 

If the tax burden cannot be increased, and I am quite sure it cannot and should not, we shall have to retrench still more, by cutting out courses in the State schools, or still further lowering teachers' salaries. We must bear in mind there is no especial virtue in tax-paid education over any other kind.

Two Subjects Studied

From all past experience we know that it is the product that tells the tale in education as in all other activities of life. 

Another advantage would lie in giving the independent colleges that are under religious control additional freedom to enforce their own tested ideas of discipline. As a matter of fact, too little attention is paid to character-forming influences in State education.

Such a plan of coordination would have another advantage, one now fully recognized by the Universities of Harvard and Chicago, that of permitting exceptionally brilliant students to graduate as soon as they are able to pass the academic tests. It has always seemed strange to me that such students are forced to the expense of a fixed number of years of college residence, during which they are held back to await the slower progress of others, and thus kept from their work in life.

A PLAN FOR THE COORDINATING OF INSTITUTIONS OF HIGHER LEARNING IN OKLAHOMA 

The time has come when higher education in Oklahoma must be coordinated. The State can not go on further with its policy of permitting its state-supported institutions to continue their independent and inchoate growth and expansion. The past twenty-five years has been a period of establishment and expansion; the next twenty-five must constitute a period of coordination and readjustment. The alternative is a disorganized system sapped into mediocrity by wasteful competition. In this movement for coordination the place of the church and independent college must not be omitted; provision for recognizing these institutions as integral parts of the 

State system, with commensurate duties and responsibilities, must be made. 

Definition of Coordination.-Probably the term "coordination of institutions of higher learning" had best be defined. Coordination in this respect means unification of higher educational effort in order to secure the most appropriate and effectve distribution of the burden. Coordination should be based upon an unbiased and accurate estimate of the State's needs, and should allocate duties and responsibilities only when such services are actually needed, and then in the light of the individual aims, physical equipment, faculty, student personnel, and curriculum of the institutions concerned.

General Underlying Principles. The acceptance of the following principles seems to be essential before a state system of higher education can in reality be established: 

1. That each state institution of higher education is a part of a single state system. 

2. That the sole object of the system is to serve the state economically and well. 

3. That the several state institutions have been established and their functions defined, and that no one institution should attempt to cover the entire field of education. 

4. That no institution should attempt to offer a particular course of study when it can be shown that greater benefit would accrue to the state if it were undertaken at another school. 

5. That state service can be accomplished by friendly cooperation better than by competition and rivalry; but that such friendly cooperation can only be secured by a unification of governing boards and administration. 

6. That since an institution's only excuse for existence is the service it can render, the final test of its function, as well as of its right to survive, is to determine the way in which it can best serve the state. 

7. That a well-considered and orderly coordination of the program of higher education will require the best thought and joint efforts both of consumers and patrons, as represented by laymen from diversified walks of life, and of producers and specialists, as represented by educationists. 

8. That it is not necessary for education of every kind and description to be offered within the boundaries of every state, and that consequently those in control of the state's unified system should be given certain broad powers to coordinate the higher educational efforts of their state with those of surrounding states, in order that needless duplication of effort may be eliminated. 

9. That the determination and administration of personnel and curriculum in institutions of higher learning is entirely a function of the state, and that consequently all Federal subsidies for education

A Plan for Coordination 

37 should be made to the state, to be appropriated for such purposes within the general field of education as the state alone may choose. 

10. That standardization is a duty of the state and not of extra-legal standardizing agencies, and that therefore all standardizing activities should be centered in and confined to the state coordinating agency.

Powers and Duties of Coordinating Board. To accomplish the fore going results, the state has provided by law a coordinating board, vested with the following powers: 

1. The assembling of exact information that will show the actual costs of education at the several institutions, together with immediate and future needs of each school, thereby giving the legislature a more accurate basis for the making of appropriations. 

2. The checking of the up-grading and expanding tendency by a clear interpretation and definition of function of the several institutions and the elimination of unnecessary and undesirable duplication. 

3. The bringing about of harmonious working relationships among the institutions by the establishment of needed councils. 

4. The unifying of the tax-supported institutions into a state system of higher education through the determination of an intelligent plan for their future development.

In addition, there are certain implied powers of the Board, such as the selection of officers, the carrying on of detailed work involved in performing its duties, and of organization. It is purposed that a permanent office be established in the State Capitol, and that it function through a federation of higher institutions of learning of the State, to be known as the "Oklahoma Greater University of Coordinated Colleges."

The Coordinating Board should employ a competent executive specialist to carry its policies into effect. This officer should be designated The 

Chancellor of Higher Education in Oklahoma, and should represent the Coordinating Board of the Oklahoma Greater University of Coordinated Colleges as its chief executive.

Analysis of the Higher Education Functions in Oklahoma Preliminary Statements.-"Form follows function." Once determine the function which an institution is to perform and the form of its organization follows as a matter of course. It is where complex functional aims are permitted to institutions that complex forms of organization results. 

It would seem that the sensible solution of Oklahoma's higher education difficulties would be to envisage the total job to be done, and on the basis of this analysis assign to the several colleges and the university the various well-defined functional unities that emerge. 

The following plan attempts to see higher education in the state as just such a total task, and to break that task into its functional unities in the most practicable manner, and to assign these units to the several institutions which now exist. Other functional plans are possible, of course; but it is suggested that a final plan which succeeds in achieving only a mixture of different plans will lack unity and, because it contains parts of plans directed toward different types of organization, will result in divergent trends of development therefrom.

It is all-important, whatever plan is finally adopted, that, first, the major functions of each different institution or group of similar institutions be decided; that, second, the organization of administrative and curricular divisions essential to carrying out these functions, and only these, be set up and all others eliminated; and third, that the straying of the institution

from the functional aim allotted be prevented. Such control implies a central coordinating board vested with appropriate and adequate powers, organization, and support, with the adequate way of carrying its rulings into effect.

Definition of Terms.-Major Fields: Major work as herein designated, is instruction in those fields allocated to an institution because they are parts of the functional unit of work assigned it. Designating a certain field as a major field of instruction for an institution means that the institution concerned is charged with developing the whole range of instruction on the college and graduate levels with reference to that field, except as hereinafter limited. The institution is expected to grant degrees, where degrees are appropriate, for proficiency in that field. In short, a major instructional field in an institution may be thought about as one which offers the student adequate work therein leading to a degree. No institution should be permitted to grant a degree in any but its major fields. 

Service Fields. Supporting each major field in every institution, many courses will be necessary for the well balanced preparation of students majoring in any particular field. It is agreed that service courses in literature, science, the social studies, and the fine arts shall be so developed as to interpret to the student the appreciative and social implications of the subject matter, rather than to inculcate specialized skills and knowledge. 

Noteworthy examples of such service work are foundation courses in English, in pure science, social problems, etc. Such courses should, for the most part, partake of the nature of survey courses designed to give the student a liberal conception of the social ramifications of the several problems and fields of knowledge. Then, too, there will be service courses not directly supporting the major subject, but designed to round out the personality of the student. These are exemplified by Music and the Arts, and other courses to familiarize the student with the more cultural phases of knowledge and of life. The designation of any field as a service field in an institution should mean, then that such courses as are necessary or advisable should be offered to support major courses and round out the personalities of the students. Under no circumstances should students be permitted to major in service courses for degrees. Herein lies the crux of most needless duplication of work and the source of institutional competition. 

The Real Source of Institutional Competition.-Institutions, or inter ested departments, are prone to argue that since the institution has the organization for instruction in service courses set up anyway, the various service departments should be permitted to admit major students and to offer major work since to do so does not expand the work of the institution. 

At first glance, this seems to be the case. The practice, however, of permitting service departments to grant degrees results in the expansion of those departments, first, in courses taught, and, finally, in size of staff, the diversion of the institution from its major functional aims, and competition for students between institutions with similar arrays of courses. It is said that there is little difference between offering four sections of the same course at one institution or dividing the four among several. This is quite fallacious, although most plausible. The statement evades the fact that two rival institutions offering similar subject matter will compete for students and will expand their arrays of courses and their organization unless the closest unified supervision is exercised over them. The result is that numerous students are probably stimulated to attempt higher education without having specific aims or adequate ability to insure probable success. 

Functional Fields Once Delimited, Must be Enforced.-It is the obligation of the state to offer adequate higher educational opportunities to all of its citizens who can, and will, benefit therefrom. This does not mean, how-

A Plan for Coordination 

39 ever, that every educational institution which the state founds is entitled to offer the whole range of differentiated preparation comprehended in the entire program of higher education for the state. The presence of more than one institution of higher learning in the state more properly means that the state has elected to divide the task of higher education by functional units among several different types of institutions. The only way in which these institutions can be kept to their functional obligations and prevented from needless and competitive expansion and up-grading is through the designation of functional fields for each, with major and service fields allocated, and the privilege of offering degrees, or even advanced work in service fields denied. Furthermore, the administration of such a functional allocation of work among the institutions will require a common administrative unit charged with the advancement of the whole program without prejudicing the interest of any one institution on the one hand, or permitting needless and unjustifiable expansion or duplication of functions on the other.

A Proposed Allocation of Functions 

There are six groups of institutions of higher learning in Oklahoma, namely, the University, the A. & M. College at Stillwater, the Oklahoma 

College for Women, the teachers colleges, State supported junior colleges and the denominational or independent colleges. This, of course, does not include the municipal junior colleges, which are more properly a part of the municipal public school systems of the state. To each of these institutions, or groups of institutions, should be assigned distinctive integral functions, with such limitations upon service courses as will prevent wasteful duplication.

The herein submitted plan for the coordination of higher education in Oklahoma, based as it is upon the concept of functional allocation of fields, implies that a degree of vocational or professional choice should be exercised by the high school graduate in selecting his college, and that those not yet prepared to make a vocational choice will elect a liberal arts or junior college. Clearly, the justification by an institution of any course, vocational or otherwise, on the grounds that many of its students want the work is not tenable if the principle of functional allocation is accepted. By the former method, all institutions can demonstrate need for any and all fields of instruction. In fact, this has been one of the time-honored methods of expansion. But the acceptance of the principle of the functional allocation of fields obviates the endless dilemmas constantly arising under the usual logical justification of need on the basis of numbers simply by the statement that if the subjects a student desires are not present in the currciulum in sufficient extent to suit his needs, he should change his institution. 

This principle is fundamental and explicit, and deviation from it will vitiate the entire program of coordination as herein conceived. 

It should be pointed out, however, that the provision of a uniform freshman year in all colleges would not seriously interfere with the programs even of highly specialized technical or vocational schools, and such a plan would afford sound integration of the state supported schools with the municipal and independent colleges, and would permit students to change schools readily after a year's trial of any institution. Such a uniform year should be endorsed and recommended.

The Function of the University. It is proposed that the University of 

Oklahoma concentrate its activities in the fields of the liberal arts and sciences, the learned professions (excepting theology), and the research upon the graduate level. Especially is there envisaged for the University of Oklahoma the development of research upon a graduate level in Arts, Letters, and Pure Science, which will give it a position of unquestioned and outstanding preeminence among the universities of the Southwest.

The Function of Oklahoma A. & M. College.--Complementary to the 

University, with its stress upon the learned professions, the arts and sciences, and the discovery of new knowledge, should be Oklahoma A. & M. 

College, devoted to the application of knowledge. The field reserved for the latter institution would be preparation for, and training in vocational, business, and technological work. Whereas the function of the University is to conserve, to discover, to organize, and to transmit the higher learning, it is the function of a land grant college to apply knowledge, through experimentation and instruction, to the practical needs of life. Unless this clear cut differentiation of function is made and accepted between the state's two largest institutions of higher learning, there can be no hope for the elimination of competition between them.

The Oklahoma College for Women.-The Oklahoma College for Women should be given its choice between going with the University as a liberal arts institution, or with the A. & M. College as a vocational institution. At present, without adequate funds, it strives to do both. Such practice affords a logical basis for endless expansion in every direction. If there is any justification of the segregation of sexes for education, such justification pertains to the education of young women in the arts and sciences. Certainly it is an anomalous argument to propose that young women roing to be stenographers or teachers should be segregated from contact with the opposite sex, for such contact will become no inconsiderable part of their vocational education.

The Teacher Training Function.-It should be the function of the teachers colleges to train elementary school teachers and to furnish lower division work in the training of high school teachers. Instruction in the upper division courses for the preparation of teachers in the several teaching fields of the high school would require that the teachers colleges provide adequate equipment and teaching staff in the fields of science, and adequate library facilities and teaching staff in the fields of social studies comparable to a curriculum of the liberal arts college. If such adequate provision for the training of teachers in subject matter fields should be undertaken, it would result in breaking down the whole scheme of allocation of functions on the basis of major courses and service courses with the result that the present tendency of the teachers colleges to develop into liberal arts colleges would be accentuated. 

A study of enrollment in the upper division courses will probably reveal that the classes are very small, and that the University, in the field of general education, and the A. & M. College, in the field of vocational education, are able to adequately meet the demands for the training of all high school teachers in the upper division. 

There is hardly a teachers college in the state adequately equipped to train teachers of pure science, and while possibly the libraries and achers may be adequate for the training of high school teachers in English and social studies, the need for new teachers in all high school fields can very amply be satisfied by the products of two institutions-The University and the A. & M. College. It is proposed, therefore, that the training of teachers for high schools be allocated to the State University because of superior subject matter facilities, both library and instructional, obtaining there, and more especially in the field of pure science the possession by the State University of adequate science laboratory facilities. Teachers for vocational high schools should be trained at the Oklahoma A. & M. College. Such teachers should have special certification.

It should be emphasized that if the teachers colleges are permitted, for the purpose of training high school teachers, to maintain major courses in all the fields which would constitute the liberal arts college, th whole concept of functional allocation of fields will fall down, and there will be no

The Independent and Denominational Colleges.-There should be, in any well conceived plan of the statewide coordination of higher education, a definite place recognized and provide for the independent and denominational colleges of the state. It will be a sorry day when these instituions are driven by a senseless institutional competition to close their doors. It is proposed here that the function of the liberal arts college be set aside for them. 

At present, they are having to meet competition in this field from every junior and senior college in the state. It is here proposed that the only state institution which has a right to that function is the State University. The 

Women's College is thought of rather as an adjunct to the University than as a different institution. If the state will reduce to those two institutions its competition with the struggling denominational colleges in the field of the arts and sciences, we may confidently predict a rescued hope and virility for the latter. Our denominational colleges cannot hope to withstand the unrestrained competition of seventeen state supported institutions of higher learning.

University Extension.—Extension service shall be construed to mean the making available of such instruction and training as will meet the needs of an individual or a group of individuals in relation to either their civic or vocational responsibilities, or cultural and leisure time interests. 

All extension service shall be known as University Extension. 

All University Extension shall be under the general direction of the Coordinating Board and shall, as far as possible, be detached from the institutions cooperating in the service. +4 The types of University Extension shall include, first, correspondence courses; second, campus courses; third, organized classes; fourth, individual service; and fifth, radio courses.

In the development of a complete program of University Extension, the idea should obtain that education is a continuing process and that in a rapidly changing civilization, one needs additional technical information in order to adapt himself to the changing standards, both in social relations and citizenship responsibilities and in occupations. Instead of conceiving the job of education to be confined to the period from birth to twenty-one years of age and attempting to give the individual, during these years, information which will function during later years, the effort should be to supply such information as may be required in the continuing process of

Supporting Argument No. 1-The Teacher Training Function 

In order that the teacher may effectively contribute to the program of liberal education, the teacher must be liberally educated; and by liberal education is meant such additional education as will enable the teacher to meet the specialized demands of professional service. The program for teacher training therefore is judged from two standpoints: (1) from the standpoint of its adequacy for liberal education and (2) from the standpoint of its adequacy for professional education. That is to say that the teacher must be a person conversant widely in the fields of human thought and at the same time one who has a large measure of command in some specialized field.

In view of the fact that the normal colleges are undergraduate schools with neither faculties, curriculum nor equipment commensurate for the needs of high school teacher training and the training of school administrators and further that these schools have a science equipment that is only adequate and not more than adequate to give service courses for the training of elementary teachers, it is apparent that the major function therefore should be the training of elementary school teachers. 

In the recent report of the National Society for the study of Education, 31st year book (1932), p. 325, it is stated: "The evidence in hand points rather clearly to the fact that in current practice in state teachers colleges, an adequate attainment of the standards for the education of teachers is not secured or even closely approached." 

Perhaps the greatest problem today for college administrators is the presenting of the methods of the teaching courses in science and other subjects to professional students; so that the teachers trained for the elementary and high school grades should obtain an education that should prepare them for work at these levels. More and more do we find local boards of education looking primarily to two sources for their high school facultiesto the State University and the A. & M. College the former because it represents the larger education of the humanities and the latter because it presents the more peculiar training along applied and vocational lines. 

This being the case, a better coordination of the state of Oklahoma's teacher training work should allocate to the Normal Colleges the field of elementary education, since these schools are undergraduate schools and only recently have arisen to college standing. From the necessity of the case their equipment in campus, buildings, and laboratories, together with smaller faculties, preclude all training other than the preparation of teachers for the elementary grades.

Because of lack of facilities such as the maintenance of a high school for teacher training, broader service courses and larger intellectual contacts, the field of the training of high school teachers and administrative officers should be allocated to the University and the A. & M. College; since the former with its Liberal Arts college can give major courses in all the teaching arts and sciences so necessary for the special training of high school teachers; and the latter can supply equal foundation opportunities in well rounded service courses in science and arts; in both cases giving sufficient subject matter to satisfy the needs of the well equipped high school teacher who has vocational as well as cultural responsibility in his or her teaching.

In the allocation of functions to the teachers colleges, in the preparation of elementary classroom teachers and in the allocation to the University and the A. & M. College of the function of the preparation of high school teachers in the fields of general education and vocational education, the Committee should have in mind the objectives set forth above and should forget as nearly as may be possible the functions which have pre-

viously obtained in these institutions and the ambitions which the several institutions may have had as future goals or objectives. 

The Committee should recognize, however, that an immediate transition from the present status to the program proposed will, of necessity, entail difficulties in the case of many individual pupils and groups of pupils which should, through further studies of the committee have careful consideration. For example, one who is completing just now Junior standing in an approved four-year teacher training institution, under the present status, having a definite objective attainable at the end of the next year, should probably not be interfered with. 

All such matters should be adjusted through conference with those now in charge of approved four-year teacher training institutions and the committee before final action is taken.

A PRELIMINARY PLAN OF PROCEDURE FOR THE GREATER UNIVERSITY OF COORDINATED COLLEGES OF OKLAHOMA I. The Duties and Powers of the Coordinating Board 

A. Excerpts from the Act.-The "Coordinating Board" established by act of the Oklahoma Legislature April 21, 1933, and approved by Governor Murray on April 26, is charged thereby with the following duties and powers: "(a). The assembling of exact information that will show the actual costs of education at the several institutions (of higher learning), together with the immediate and future needs of each school, and shall make a report of these annually to the Governor. "(b). The checking of the up-grading and expanding tendency by a clear interpretation and definition of the function of each of the several institutions and the elimination of unnecessary and undesirable duplications. "(c). The bringing about of harmonious relationships among the institutions by the establishing of needed councils. "(d). The unifying of the tax-supported institutions into a state system of higher education through the determination of an intelligent plan for their future development."

In order to make the work of the Coordinating Board effective, the law provides that "any regulations, courses of study, or standards set up by the Coordinating Board, when signed by the Governor, shall be in full force and effect in the administration of the affairs of the institution." 

These duties fall into four general divisions: 

1. Assembling and interpreting comparable facts; 

2. Setting up plans, policies, and regulations; 

3. Administering these; 

4. Reporting the needs and costs of the various institutions, presumably for legislative action.

B. The Legal Basis of the Greater University.-The fifteen men of the type who have been and should be appointed to the Coordinating Board do not themselves propose to carry on the detailed activities called for by the law-especially since they must work without pay. Under its fourth, or unifying function, however, the Coordinating Board clearly has authority to establish a central administrative structure for carrying on this detailed work. Such a central structure might well be called The Greater University of Coordinated Colleges of Oklahoma, and to it, subject to the approval of the Coordinating Board, under regulations previously approved by the Governor, the Board might delegate the detailed work of administering the unification of higher education. As its chief administrative agent, the Board could appoint a qualified educator, to bear the title of Chancellor. The powers and duties with which the Chancellor should be vested are given in Appendix A. The fiscal support of such an agency can be provided for during the experimental period by the institutions concerned, interested foundations, and other friends of higher education. Later the necessary funds can be obtained by legislative appropriation or by a pro rata part of tuition and other fees.

III. Immediate Steps 

The immediate task confronting the Greater University includes the 

1. Assembling facts upon which to base an allocation of functions to the several institutions; 

2. Prescribe uniform regulations governing the admission of students for such entrance and comprehensive examinations as the Board may wish to impose; 

3. Prescribe uniform forms of student and financial accounting and of institutional reporting. 

A. Considerations in the Allocation of Functions.-Allocation of functions to the several institutions involves three factors: the institutions themselves, a theory of allocation, and the facts of present practice. The

most difficult of these factors is the first. No theory of allocation is likely to survive if its application contravenes greatly the aims, traditions, and practices of the institutions concerned; such would probably result in coalitions to nullify the whole conception. Therefore an essential first step would be to analyze as concretely and directly as possible the aims of each institution in terms of general educational objectives and of special and specific vocational goals. It should be borne in mind that the subject of the coordinating movement is to help each institution to realize its aims most effectively by a constructive program of mutual adjustment. By no means should the idea be tolerated that the past activities of the several institutions have been ineffectual, lacking in moral significance, and generally wrong. The situation is simply that continuous and unregulated expansion among so many institutions has brought them all to an impasse; cooperative and coordinate action promises the best way out. But this action is not likely to come about of itself-hence the Coordinating Board; nor is it likely to come until all concerned get used to sitting around the common council board and acquire a mutual confidence born of mutual understanding.

Institutional aims having been clarified and the institutional cabinet organized and set functioning, the next immediate step will be to spread out the facts and view them comparatively, remembering that the same economic disaster threatens all institutions, and that unless all can find a common ground of coordinated activity, compromises greater than any need make will be forced upon all by the fact of a failing fiscal support. 

The Board should therefore take a next step in solving the problem of allocating functions, that of ascertaining what the present situation is with respect to curriculum, plant and equipment, faculty and student personnel, and efficiency. 

The Board needs to know not only what courses of study are offered in the catalog, but also what ones are actually taught, how many take them, what the qualifications of the instructors who present them are, what the actual relationships of each course to the total curricular organization is, and how much it costs to teach it. 

Especially important is the matter of the actual relationship of courses to the various curricula. For example, it has been suggested that this or that department be discountinued at an institution, with no knowledge of the fact as to how the courses in this department tie up with other curricula that would be left. Only through a clear-cut analysis of course-enrollments by major curricula classifications can the feasibility of eliminating a department, or even a school or college, be ascertained. On the other hand, such an analysis will reveal clearly the natural functional units of an institution, and the extent and importance of their interrelationships. 

Hardly less important is the analysis of course offerings. Hundreds if not thousands of courses are being offered in this country every year that might be eliminated. This is the most pernicious result of our departmental organization. Courses that might be offered occasionally as some professor was found with a light load take their place in the regular curriculum of the institution and eventually become evidence of a "crying need" for staff increases. Such courses usually have very small enrollments, and while no one could maintain successfully that size of enrollments alone is an adequate measure of the need for a course, certainly if there must be elimination, classes with such small enrollments as to raise a question about their justification should receive first consideration; and when the record of several years shows a course rather uniformly of value to very few, the question is quite definitely raised whether the subject-matter should ever have been isolated from more fundamental and more generally serviceable courses.

This might be the most effective place to state that there is no illusion here that statistical analyses can of themselves settle many problems. The viewpoint is rather that such analyses should precede any attempt to formulate plans and theories, and that the attempt to define institutional functions and to reorganize the institutions accordingly without careful analyses is likely to result in arbitrary and impracticable judgments. 

B. The Service Report.-The analyses of course offerings, enrollments and interrelationships, and of staff and student personnel being completed, the next step would be to set up the permanent system of fact-gathering, organization, and interpretation. The foregoing data will have required some form for its assembly. The type of permanent form may well have been agreed upon in the beginning and used for the preliminary work also. 

There are two sources of the data desired: (1) the Registrars of the various institutions, or (2) the individual staff members. For reasons of economy, superior accuracy, and greater directness, it is proposed that the data be gathered from the staff at regular intervals by means of a service report, like that exhibited in Appendix B. Such a report requires about thirty minutes of the professor's time at each reporting period and presents basic data that will give the facts on almost any conceivable question dealing with costs, teaching loads, class size, staff utilization, efficiency of space utilization, institutional up-grading, expansion or other shifts of curricular organization and content.

C. The Cost Study.—The next step will be to make a comparable cost study. Due to great differences in student and fiscal accounting, comparisons of cost among even similar institutions are liable to be quite erroneous, if not even unjust; especially is such the case when summer students are counted along with regular term students, and quarterly enrollments equated with those for semesters. The Board by now having established uniform student and fiscal accounting, is prepared next to proceed to cost accounting.

There are two important aspects of cost: (1) The amounts and proportion of each institutional budget that is spent for various activities, either directly or indirectly, and regardless of enrollment. Thus, it is important to know the cost of general administration at each institution as a unit, quite independently of the size of the institution; or the cost of maintaining and operating the physical plant, or of instructing lawyers, doctors, teachers, agricultural specialists, and so on. Then, (2) it is desirable to derive costs on various unit bases that will make some adjustment for the size of the task accomplished. This second procedure is open to grave misinterpretations, since it is bound to stress the economy of mass production; and since there is not being made simultaneously an adjustment for the quality of the product, unit costs based upon class size or teaching loads will likely create the impression that the most efficient college is one that overloads and underpays its professors. Yet, acknowledging this weakness of unit costs, such comparative data will be necessary for viewing the cost of education from institution to institution and passing upon the issue of relative need. Possibly an arbitrary standard can be set up, or new units derived that will be more satisfactory than any so far proposed. 

IV. Determining and Reporting Institutional Needs 

With the facts above provided, assembled and interpreted, the Board will be in position to receive from the several tax-supported institutions of higher learning their statements of future and immediate needs, to pass upon them with some degree of intelligence, and to report to the Governor the Board's judgment concerning them. 

In its essence, this procedure is nothing more nor less than making a consolidated budget, and would follow the procedures of any large uni-

versity, the Institutional Cabinet performing the function of the council of 

Deans. 

The facts assembled for study that are to be reported along with the budget to the Governor may best be published in four quarterly bulletins: 

(1) Aug. 1: Institutional Carrying Loads, and Course of Study Trends; (2) Nov. 1, Consolidated Report of Income and Expenditures, and Unit Costs; 

(3) Feb. 1, The Report of the Chancellor and the Institutional Heads; (4) April 1, Faculty Teaching Loads and Personnel Studies. 

Appendix A.-The Chancellor I. Relation of the Chancellor to Coordinating Board.-The Chancellor is the chief executive officer of the Greater University and as such he performs the duties set for him by that Board. He shall act as the medium of communication between the Board and the officers and organization of the University. II. Relation of the Chancellor to the Greater University.-The Chan cellor is a member of all legislative bodies set up within the Greater University for the purpose of representing the several institutions and coordinating their activities. He may refer any question of institutional or general policy to any council, faculty, committee, or to any member of the instructional or scientific staffs for investigation or report. He may call special meetings of any council, faculty, or committee set up under the authority of the Coordinating Board at any time. The Chancellor shall afford every opportunity consistent with sound administration and educational policy to every officer and member of the instructional and scientific staffs to present suggestions for the general welfare of the Greater University of any of its constitutent institutions. 

The Chancellor may veto any act of any council, committee, or other body set up under authority of the Coordinating Board, but in so doing he shall transmit with the veto a written statement of the reasons for such action. A copy of each veto statement shall be transmitted to the Coordinating Board. Any of the above-mentioned councils, committees, or other such bodies may appeal from a veto of the Chancellor to the Coordinating Board and may be represented before the Board by one of its members for this purpose.

Board the Greater University Budget. When approved by the Board and

the Governor, this budget shall govern all expenditures, subject to the provisions of the law.

IV. Incidental Powers of the Chancellor.-As the chief executive of the 

Greater University, the Chancellor is especially charged with the duty of securing harmony and cooperation among the institutions of the Greater University, and the economical coordination of their instructional and scientific work. To these ends he has such powers as may be definitely delegated to him by the Coordinating Board; and in addition such incidental powers as are necessary properly to perform the duties of his office.

The Oklahoma Tax Economy Association's Recommendations.—With reference to the Board's duties concerning cost studies, the Oklahoma Tax Economy Association states in its report under date of August, 1932, on pages 38 and 39, the following: "Whether one board or three separate boards are provided, or the higher institutions are left under present control, the taxpayers of Oklahoma should know the exact cost per student for each department, division, college or section of all the institutions of the state. They should know both the capital costs and the current costs of both teaching and research work. Some departments are obviously much more expensive than others for the reason that more costly building and labatory equipment are required. Data of this type will make possible a careful comparative study of the relative costs of different forms of education, which will be more valuable as a factual basis for an ecoromical and otherwise sound educational policy in case the institutions are placed under the control of one general board. "Service reports should be required in each institution, which, with other information regarding capital costs, will make possible a careful compilation of all data necessary through a thorough study of exact cost of higher education. 

Certain higher institutions of learning in other states have used this method with substantial savings and better educational service. * * * "In conclusion, the proper control of education is one of most responsible functions of the state. We should be guided by an impartial analysis of the essential facts."

Meetings. The State Council of Educational Planning and Coordi nation should meet four or five times a year to consider the state of the educational system, to determine what special problems require detailed consideration and the facts which need to be gathered to inform discussion, and to formulate constructive recommendations. From time to time it may invite qualified members of the State educational system, as well as representatives of privately controlled institutions, to sit with it and to contribute to its deliberations. 

Problems. among matters which might early be considered by the council the Commission mentions the following problems: the costs of all publicly-controlled institutions of higher education; the articulation of university work with work of the lower divisions, particularly in engineering, dentistry, and pharmacy; adult education; vocational education; the relation of teachers colleges to the university system; the quality and relationships of privately endowed colleges; problems connected with the granting and extending of charters of institutions of

higher education, both public and private; teacher tenure, and remuneration; the number, function, differentiation of function, duplication and specialization of various units of the higher educational system, especially the State teachers colleges, all of which should be examined into constantly; a study of professional needs and opportunities within the State. The council should inquire into the expediency of consolidating some of the State teachers colleges. In matters of controversy the council might serve somewhat in the nature of a court of arbitration. Conclusions and recommendations of the council should be transmitted promptly to the institutions involved, and such publicity should be given them as is desirable. The Commission anticipates that certain questions will be referred to the council by other administrative boards in the State educational system, but it points out that it is equally important that the council should upon its own initiative undertake such inquiries as it deems necessary or desirable. 

Fact-finding. The State Council for Educational Planning and 

Coordinating should have its own Division of Fact-finding and Analysis. 

This division should be in charge of a director of research, who also would act as executive secretary of the council and sit with it. A small, highly competent staff of secretaries and assistants is essential, and this staff should be so organized as to be capable of enlargement and contraction from time to time as the studies required by the Council expand and decrease. Much of the effectiveness of the council's work will be conditioned by the quality of the work done in the division of factfinding and analysis. The division will not deal in routine surveys or statistics which are adequatly provided by the State Department of Education and the university for their own purposes. APPENDIX P A SPECIAL REPORT OF THE CANADIAN EDUCATIONAL SYSTEM, WRITTEN BY BISHOP KELLEY FOR THE COMMITTEE FOR THE COORDINATING BOARD OF HIGHER EDUCA- TION IN OKLAHOMA TO THE MEMBERS OF THE EXECUTIVE COMMITTEE OF THE COORDINATING BOARD ON HIGHER EDUCATION IN THE STATE OF OKLAHOMA. 

Gentlemen: 

Before reporting the facts found and the impressions received in our study of institutions of higher education outside Oklahoma, which we undertook for the purpose of securing information for your Board, we wish to state clearly the reason why Canada was selected for our visit. Canada furnishes the most interesting and comprehensive examples of coordination in higher education, both state and private, known to us on this continent. Nowhere else in America has educational coordination been so long established that it can be seen and studied under different and varied aspects. We would have it well understood, however, that the Canadian system could not, under present conditions, be adopted in its entirety for Oklahoma; nevertheless, it has lessons to teach which we might profitably consider.

The Difference 

Higher education in Canada had a very modest beginning in a small French colony and grew with an increasing population, first of French and then of English pioneers. That period of growth covers well over a century. 

What Oklahoma had to provide almost at once in a new state, into which people were rapidly pouring, the Provinces of Ontario and Quebec started with simple and small foundations, allowing them to expand gradually into colleges and universities. The systems now used in these provinces have, therefore, comparatively long traditions behind them. It is no criticism of our own to say that we could not possess such traditions, for youth has none. Then the conditions under which these institutions in Canada grew were never present in Oklahoma. Canadians brought to the New World the educational deals of two different cultures and two different civilizations, which had to work and develop together. These represented, on one side, the ambitions of a people rich in all the favors that a victorious motherland could and did lavish on her pioneers, and on the other, those of a group of some thousands of merchants, farmers and woodmen deserted by their own country, and left apparently to be snuffed out as a race under alien rule. To complicate matters still more, these two peoples, destined to be fellow citizens, were divided both in language and religion. A clash was inevitable. It came. But, in the end, instead of flying again to arms, both peoples turned to education. They began with seminaries, since the products of clerical schools alone could in turn be relied upon at the time to preserve the culture of the two peoples, instruct them and keep them holding fast to their ideals. This situation had a close, but not an exact, analogy in colonial New England where higher learning got its start before the state itself was in educational action, through the foundation of Harvard as a theological school. The theological schools in Canada had to have preparatory classical schools. The colleges grew out of these. Universities grew out of the colleges. 

The contrast with our educational story in Oklahoma is plain. We had no war to fight before settling down to the making of a state. We had no internal struggles for the mastery of one people over another. confusion of tongues. We had little religious bitterness amongst our 

We had no

pioneers. There were educational institutions just outside our borders whose plans could be copied. We could take what others offered and build quickly. As a matter of fact, we built so quickly that we over-built. So we were ready with a system of higher education almost before the people themselves were ready to take advantage of it. In our zeal we even adopted the mistakes of the outside experimenter because they were new, and the very newness of them appealed to the citizens of a new state who were in a good mood for experimentation. Then we had the problem of attracting a population; so we had to offer what other states offered-older states than ours. If we consider intention, perhaps our mistakes might be thought as leaning toward virtues. At that we did not do so badly. With a bit of readjustment, much good-will, and a willingness to learn wherever we can, there will now be no need of imitating anyone in particular. An Oklahoma plan can be made to meet the present Oklahoma conditions. It is 

Oklahoma alone we are considering. We offer no suggestion to others, for their problems are their own. What may suit our situation may be unsuitable elsewhere.

Warning 

It might be well to note that it would be impossible to make a report on educational conditions in Canada without frequently mentioning church and religion. In both Ontario and Quebec religion plays a decidedly important part in the educational set-up. In Ontario, denominational colleges are an integral part of the state system of higher education. They take in the whole undergraduate body of students in Liberal Arts. In Quebec there are no tax-supported colleges of Liberal Arts. All are church colleges. In neither of these provinces is there any hesitation about admitting the influence of religion on higher education. The hesitation is found rather in suppressing the desire to ask us how it could be otherwise anywhere.

It is plain then that there is a well recognized principle vitalizing all education in both Ontario and Quebec with a cultural rather than a vocational emphasis. The religious and ethical invariably showed itself in every statement of their educational objectives. We are simply mentioning a condition without which no one can understand the Canadian systems. 

You may get a little amusement out of the fact that one of your investigators felt called upon to espouse the state side and the other the religious. 

So the job of getting up a report under these circumstances is not an easy one. The only safe thing to do is to set down the facts as they are. 

History 

It was quite natural that Canadian education should have a religious foundation. At the time of the conquest of Canada, England was strongly 

Protestant and the old universities in England were, and even today are to a great extent, directed and taught by a cultural clergy. So old Oxford and Cambridge peek out through the Gothic windows of the University of Toronto. The traditional Lycee system of France was enthroned on the rock of Quebec and has spread itself along the St. Lawrence. Both French and English thought of education rightly in terms of soul as well as of body and mind. Both were aiming at a culture predominantly religious. 

Both believed in the classic and philosophy. Those familiar with the history of education in our own colonial days will not be surprised at that. 

Quite recently the titles of theses defended in American colonial colleges, all Protestant, as well as many of the actual papers, have been dug out of their archives. It was a matter of wonder, and yet it should not have been a matter of great wonder, that they were in Latin. Our educational forefathers, as well as our statesmen, in the days when we had statesmen, were masters of the classics, as well as being decidedly religious minded. 

The co-ed of today may be surprised, but ought to be pleased, to know that

Theodosia Burr could speak fluently with her father and her father's friends in choice Latin.

In Canada, as mentioned before, this paradox occurred: Both elements, English and French, rushed to education to keep divided from each other. 

But education divided only to promote understanding. An English Canadian sums up what these systems of education have done. He spoke in 

French but we translate: "One nation, two cultures; one loyalty, two races; two glorious pasts, but only one future."

Convictions 

Since most men bring their own convictions into every act of life, and since these convictions more or less suggest their line of thinking and color their actions, it is only fair that we should mention the mental attitude we ourselves brought into our inquiry. On a definition of education and its main objectives, we were in virtual agreement. To us, education was and is the road by which intelligent beings reach, or try to reach, the goal of wisdom and a completely developed and well rounded personality. That road necessarily passes through the domain of study and training. Those who go far enough on it reach the gardens of culture. The completely developed personality we take as the ultimate goal, culture as a bi-product of attempting to reach it, knowledge as the ground covered and education as the road itself. The Canadian would express this same idea a bit more in detail, but he would agree.

Since man cannot take part of himself along the road, education must necessarily concern the whole of him. In American tax-supported education, the only kind with which the limitations of our laws permitted us to deal fully, the rule is to rely for certain necessary influences on the family and the church. The state, as a rule, maintains a sympathetic attitude to these others, and has confidence, alas too often misplaced, that they are equipped to do their share and are doing it. The state takes too much for granted. But it will easily be seen that, since your investigators were acting for a state, we had to work within the limitations made for us by the state. Nevertheless we were far from ignoring the necessity of influences other than the intellectual in the educational process. We could not ignore them in this particular inquiry, for, as stated, in Canada as a rule we found them recognized; in most cases highly exalted, certainly never ignored: 

Canada is, to a much greater degree than our own country, the home of deeply religious people, less affected by industrial problems than ours and, for the most part, rural. This applies to all creeds and to the people of both languages. One need not be surprised then, that in none of the institutions visited and studied by us did we find the culture of the spirit ignored. 

That situation is not looked upon as interference by church or state in each other's domain. It is taken for granted that each should recognize the need of the work done by the other. The state recognizes the need of religious courses and treats them with the same respect that the church colleges treat courses given by the universities in science, which they themselves do not provide. Ontario

The University of Toronto is the largest institution of higher education in the Province. It is well known to American educators and ranks high with them. Its graduates are well represented on the teaching staffs of many American colleges and universities. Since our problem of coordination chiefly concerned undergraduate work, our conferences here were with the heads of its constituent colleges. These colleges are four in number, three religious and one "neutral." Each gives instruction to its students in certain subjects, but the bulk of actual instruction is given by the 

University's teaching staff.

The annual examinations are conducted by the University, which also grants academic standing upon the results of these examinations, and confers degrees upon the successful completion of a prescribed course of study. 

Special exception is made for religion and philosophy (also for one college in history), which may be given in the colleges. Marking for such examinations, when the subjects are taught in the colleges, is done by the colleges and accepted by the University. 

The entrance and final examination system is in use. Examination papers are prepared by a Board, the members of which are selected from the constituent colleges. Pseudonyms are used on the papers to insure fairness in marking. There was unanimity amongst the heads of the colleges in commending the system and in testifying to its practical usefulness. 

The salaries of teachers in all subjects taken at the University are paid by the University. Those of teachers of subjects taught at the colleges are paid by the colleges. An annual tuition of $100 is charged all students in 

Arts. The fee belongs to the college in which the student matriculates. 

The four colleges of the University are not merely affiliated institutions. They are constituent parts of the University. But as stated, they do not give degrees (except in Divinity). Voluntarily the colleges have given up this right in all courses taught at the University, in exchange for other and very decided advantages. Discipline is the care and duty chiefly of the colleges.

The friendly relationship between the four constituent colleges of the University of Toronto deserves special mention. The spirit is one of helpfulness and mutual pride in the contribution of each and all to the making of a great University. We detected no sign of ill feeling of any kind, but there is healthy rivalry for success in examinations and standards. Each college makes an effort to contribute something special for the good of all. 

One, for example, has a school of medieval studies, specializing in philosophy, which follows the Oxford tutorial and lecture system. It boasts of paying two professors, brought from France, the highest salaries now being paid on the American continent for instruction. Indeed, its directors were very emphatic in stating that better work can be done by outstanding men of this type, working with instructors of a lower grade, than by a class of instructors somewhere between the two. This opinion did not, however, find favor at another Ontario institution, the fear being expressed that "big fish" had a habit of going hunting for deeper waters. 

While the University provides a great library for the use of all, each college maintains useful libraries covering the fields of their own offerings. 

Though, at Toronto, the constituent colleges are on or near one great campus, differing in this from the widely scattered units of the proposed Greater University of Oklahoma, the principles involved are the same. 

University College, for example, is on an equal footing with the religious constituent colleges. On entering the federation, the other universities-as the colleges were then called-relinquished all graduate and professional departments, with the exception of Divinity. Mutual exchange in instruction, use of equipment and academic planning, as was proposed for the Greater University of Oklahoma, is operating here successfully. 

At the University of Western Ontario we found another provincial (state) university. Ontario has three such institutions. To this one is allocated the higher educational needs of that block of counties which forms the western part of the Province. The University proper is located in London. Being a new institution, its chief work is still in Liberal Arts. While it has a medical school, it has as yet no other professional schools. Indeed we could see no need of them, all professions in Ontario, as with us, being already over-crowded.

Here we found in some respects a closer approximation to the Oklahoma situation. The colleges composing the Faculty of Liberal Arts of the 

University are not strictly speaking constituent, but affiliated. One is 10cated at a distance of 120 miles from the University, but distance does not trouble the relationship. Two are in the University City. 

The United (Protestant) Church of Canada plans to found an affiliated college here. The Preparatory Seminary for the Catholic Diocese of London registers its students for lectures and degrees through Brescia Hall, a fully afficiated college of the University. This University is really a development from an Anglican college, Huron, which remains in affiliation. 

The Toronto plan of examinations for matriculation and degrees is in force. 

The examining boards are made up of members from the affiliated colleges. even

This institution proved to be most interesting. Not a single complaint was heard about the practicability of its methods of affiliations, though its colleges are scattered. In fact the arrangement was pronounced not only workable, but working to the satisfaction of all concerned. 

The Dean of Arts and Registrar, a Ph. D. of Harvard and former professor at the University of Illinois, exceptionally well-informed on problems on both sides of the line and those of special interest to us, studied at length our general plan for Oklahoma. His rich experience in both countries made his observations very much worth while. To sum them up in a few words: He felt that, while our proposals in their entirety seem unprecedented, they were most commendable and in detail practical and within reach of realization. The actual articles of affiliation for his University and its affiliated colleges he submitted for our study. 

The University of Western Ontario grants all the degrees for its affiliated colleges. It even includes Divinity for the Protestant group. Like 

Toronto, it offers courses in religious knowledge, but through the colleges. 

It, like Toronto, also issues a catalog covering its own work as well as that of its affiliations. A single catalog for the whole system is both more economical and serviceable.

Here again are found exceptions made for Philosophy, Religion and History, but, as in Toronto, none of the colleges in London find the need of using the history exemption. One reason given way, that the always existing possibility of controversy made teachers more than ordinarily careful to explain both sides of such questions. Besides, it was added, true scholarship requires a knowledge of all the facts and their influence on the life of their times rather than the opinions of the teacher. The student is marked on his grasp of the subject matter, not on his decision as to the right or wrong side of the point in controversy. 

We did not visit Queens University in Kingston, but were informed that as yet it has no affiliated colleges. We call special attention, however, to the fact that Queen's as well as Toronto is not without endowment by private gifts and legacies, though both receive provincial grants of money annually as state institutions. In fact it is the policy of the Ontario Government to invite private gifts. This is interesting for Oklahoma, in view of the Governor's plans for a Greater University Foundation which may, sometime in the future, actually relieve the state of the necessity of making annual grants to its tax-supported institutions of higher learning. 

To Sum Up Some of the Things We Learned in Ontario 

1. The plan suggested for Oklahoma of drawing all institutions of higher education closer together on the educational side, without prejudice to their freedom and special missions, by coordination or affiliation, is actually working, and has been working in Ontario long enough to be judged by its results.

2. These results, on the testimony of the heads of the colleges, officers of the universities, religious and secular leaders, are in the main most satisfactory. 

3. On the educational side, the two tax-supported universities visited are fully up to the best educational standards. 

4. In no sense are the independent, private or religious colleges received on a basis of mere toleration. They are a valued and indispensable part of a state education plan, with the major emphasis of the colleges on the cultural and spiritual, and the university on the graduate, research and professional. The general principle of the relationship we would judge to be a recognition of the necessity of a cultural and spiritual foundation for advanced or professional studies. 

5. One result is a close bond of fellowship between all educators within the system. These aim to be mutually helpful to one another without sacrificing their own individual convictions. This includes cultured religious leaders affected by, but not an immediate part of the system itself. It is hard to express here what we experienced and felt, for it was the spirit that impressed us most. 

6. Higher education is taken in Ontario not alone as a state task, but rather as a common interest to be assisted by both church and state without in any way infringing on the rights of either. This is arrived at by insuring unity of general educational purpose on the highest level, which cannot fail, since it comes from the educators of future leaders, to reach the citizenship in the most natural and useful way. Religious and racial troubles seemed to us to be passing as a political problem in Canada, and the higher educational system is certainly a great influence to that end. 

7. The recognition of mutual benefits to be had through exchange is illustrated by the creation of a bilingual state normal school at the University of Ottawa to serve the people who speak both English and French. 

Another instance is the case of the Western University sending its students preparing to teach French to Quebec where they are instructed by professors from Laval University and by teaching Sisters from a religious women's college. 

8. In spite of the fact that the American credit system is in partial use in Ontario, the chief emphasis in matriculation and degree tests is on the examinations, which are prepared by the departmental faculties representing all institution contituent or affiliated. 

9. Though in the Ontario tax-supported universities elective courses are offered, we found many loath to depart from the traditional classical studies leading to the Bachelor of Arts degree. 

10. The spirit of mutual good-will and helpfulness between the different colleges on the part of the faculties is paralleled in the student body of the University of Toronto by a Students' Administrative Council. 

11. As an illustration of actual coordination the universities themselves bear the expense of all laboratory equipment as well as scientific instruction. Quebec

There is a vast difference between the whole educational system of Quebec and our own. Quebec has two coordinate educational systems in one. Its Board of Education is in two sections, Catholic and Protestant, each with its own secretary but with a common Superintendent of Public Instruction. On that point we have to remark that, while this fact refers to elementary and secondary education only, it affords a striking example of successful operation of an arrangement proposed in Oklahoma for a Chancellor's Educational Council, which is planned to have two sections, state and non-state. For example, in certain matters of interest only to

one section of the Quebec council there would be no occasion to convene the other. The Superintendent of Public Instruction presides over the Council as a whole, and is a member of both sections separately, but can vote only in the one belonging to the church of which he is a member. So in the proposed plan for Oklahoma, the Chancellor would preside over all meetings of the council as a whole and be a member of both sections, state and non-state, voting only in the state section. 

There are three universities in the Province of Quebec, none tax-supported. The oldest, founded in 1852 as a university, but over 325 years old as a college, is Laval, situated in the capital city of Quebec. The second in age is McGill. The third is Montreal. The latter really existed long before as a branch of Laval. McGill is looked upon as Protestant and 

English. Laval and Montreal are Catholic and French. The government recognizes all three. There is also one other Protestant college with a university charter, Bishop's (Anglican) in Lenoxville.

McGill has certain affiliations with the Universities of Oxford, Cambridge and Dublin, as well as with three Protestant theological colleges, and partial extra-provincial affiliations with Acadia (Protestant), Mount 

Allison (Protestant), Alberta (Provincial), and St. Francois Xavier (Catholic) Universities, and the Royal Military Academy (Federal). It has also two incorporated colleges: Macdonald (Agricultural) and the Royal Victoria 

(the Women's College of McGill). 

Quebec has been the scene of many racial and linguistic troubles in the past. Today one hears little more than echoes of them. The universities have had and are still having a large share in outgrowing them. In 

Montreal, where rivalry might perhaps be expected to hinder close friendly relations between the English and French universities, we heard nothing but words of good-will, praise and sympathy on the part of both for the work and problems of the other. There was general satisfaction expressed in the fact that the Provincial Government leaves them alone, for politics never enter their portals. They are as free as Harvard and Chicago to develop along their own lines and manage their own affairs. This situation they do not wish to change.

But one bit of government interference is welcomed by all, the arrival of a Provincial official with a cheque. Without committing itself to the grant as a regular policy, the Provincial Legislature makes to each classical college in the Province a gift of $10,000 a year. All are treated alike and there are no "strings" on the gifts. As to the universities, they receive now and then more generous gifts, usually divided equally between the three. 

Last year each received $25,000. Because Montreal University is new and struggling with a building program, it expects a large special grant; but no objection is being raised by the others. They know that Montreal will do as much for them if they find themselves in special need. The atmosphere of good-will was as apparent in the educational circles of Quebec as it was in those of Ontario.

The heart of the whole higher educational organization for the French in Quebec is a system of classical colleges. There are 25 scattered over the 

Province, with about 1,000 instructors and some 10,000 students. All are affiliated to one of the universities. A boy enters at 13 (one year before ours enter high school), and emerges after 8 years with a bachelor's degree.

That degree is his admission ticket to the professional schools. "The 

French college is most strictly regulated and regimented. Courses are prescribed and are to be mastered. Outside activities, such as football, dances, parties, etc., are practically unknown."

Professor Bovey of McGill, from whose book Canadian the above quotation is taken, points out that there are three characteristics of the general educational scheme in which Quebec differs so markedly from the average

English-Canadian and American practice. (1) The remarkable system of classical colleges among a people of so little financial resources is due to the fact that they are largely conducted by religious teachers who work for a mere living. (2) Organized athletics are conspicuous for their absence. (3) French-Canadian education is not necessarily democratic. (Note: But any boy or girl can enter college for as little as $200 a year for both board and tuition.) "While Laval," writes Prof. Bovey, "has an old-world atmosphere unique in North America, its younger sister, Montreal, is developing more in the general field, that is in the applied sciences and in vocational schools." But all Canadian universities recognize more than ours that "man and mind must be put ahead of greed and possessions." "A major principle in Quebec education is that education and religion are a unity." "To educate is to train all that are born to all that is human, and the state's only right in the matter is to see that the child does not get an education that might ultimately prove detrimental to the welfare of society." 

Ontario's three universities are supported by the tax-payers for all expenses not covered by endowment, tuition fees and actual services by their constituent colleges; but, as stated, the Budget of the Province of Quebec has no regular item of annual expense for higher education. Ontario avails itself of the willingness of the private colleges to help the Province, but supports the universities proper and thus indirectly helps the colleges. 

Quebec leaves the whole matter of higher education to private (principally church) initiative and steps in only to assist them. Both provinces seem quite satisfied. But Quebec gets off much better financially. Its legislators do not ignore the existence of the universities and colleges, but they do not interfere with them. In a sentence: Tax-maintained education in Quebec stops with primary education; tax-aided education goes as high as the uniersities.

To Sum Up Some of the Things We Learned in Quebec 

1. Since two distinct civilizations can live together, and together help develop a nation, the problem of mutual understanding and assistance between state and private colleges in the educational field alone ought not be a very difficult one in Oklahoma. 

2. Education in Quebec does not begin in the primary schools, but in the homes of the people, especially of the farmers. The home is both kindergarten and preparation for primary education. Parents are not only the child's first teachers but they go along as far as they can with every teacher. In the home the French-Canadian child is taught the story of its people-language, history, faith, legends, games, songs—everything that makes the traditions of the race. It thus imbibes a great love, not only for the country, but for the culture of which it is to be a part. Racial unity is made a major objective.

When the child goes to school then, it is not leaving home, since the school is really a continuation of the home. The teacher is not a hired servant of the school district to the pupil, but a member of its family, sharing the authority of its parents. Whatever fathers and mothers may think, what the child thinks they think is what the teacher thinks. So there is no premature advancement into grades beyond its capacity because the parents want it so. The understanding between parent and teacher is too intimate for that. As the child advances in the higher grades the bond between parent and teacher is stretched but never broken. 

All this explanation was necessary to an understanding of this statement: The French-Canadian system of education, full of sentiment in many things, is nevertheless utterly devoid of it in its tests for advancement. There results alone count. Up to a few years ago the college even cared little about teachers having degrees. What was wanted were teachers

who could teach. Some of the greatest educators in French-Canada taught through to ripe old age and died degreeless. That situation is changing, but, we thought, only in deference to outside opinion. The French- 

Canadian still believes that it is the result alone that should count, and he is a stubborn person.

Again, little reliance is placed on money endowment for the colleges. 

They have land; some have plenty of it. They feed its products to the students who board in the school, but, outside the cities, where there is some endowment in the form of rents, that is about as far as they go in the matter so seriously insisted upon by our standardizing agencies. Educators complain about this, but they cannot have their cake and eat it. 

French-Canadian families are large. The first responsibility of parents is for their children. When these are provided for economically, there is little left as a legacy to the college. So, many French-Canadian colleges need laboratory equipment, enlarged quarters, better libraries, etc. They must want patiently and get such necessary things slowly, but, too, they must keep carrying on. How do they do it? By an endowment of service. 

We found university presidents and professors working for their board and clothes. All the members of the teaching religious orders do that. 

You will understand now why we said that we could not copy much from Canada. We mean Quebec in particular. But its educational set-up is most interesting. One thing about it is especially well worth our thoughtful attention. It is its tenacity in holding to that test of results 

The examinations are searching without over-severity. 

3. In the after life of the student you have a real test of the value of the Quebec French system. The tradition of French-Canada calls for educational rather than vocational training, with emphasis on the Liberal Arts and special emphasis on Philosophy. The legislature, the government officials and the parliamentary representatives of Quebec, are superior men; usually equal to the demands of statesmanship on them, sanely conservative and extraordinarily ready and brilliant in debate. The professional class, out of which the legislators for the most part come, as a rule are men of considerable culture. There is a growing and accomplished body of writers. There is an atmosphere of quiet dignity in the Provincial Capitol building which speaks volumes. 

4. Proportionally, however, the French-Canadian is not as well represented in scientific careers as is the English-Canadian. More equipment is needed. Endowment of service does not count for so much here. It is money that is required, and it will take time to get it from a people of large families who are neither rich nor poor. The Government, which confines its educational activities to aiding instead of doing, has a wonderful opportunity, for, with the solid foundation of an excellent education in the humanities and philosophy, it ought to be no task to produce new Pasteurs and Fabres. The French-Canadian has had excellent results in every educational department he could conquer with small means and large devotion. 

He can teach us that credit hours and lectures, complicated by football, joy rides and dances, are a much less effective incentive to work than the knowledge that there is a Judgment Day coming, with the devil of an examination to appear as accuser of the lukewarm and lazy. 

5. French-Canadian education as far as it has gone is thorough. Each step thus far taken has proved to be a strong, safe lift upward. And each such step is closely cemented to those below and above it, from the home to the university. It is a homogeneous system only lacking the development of some, not all, of its scientific schools and in Montreal especially, more suitable housing. The University of Montreal is now erecting a model building for its purposes, but lack of funds makes for slow progress.

Other Schools in Canada 

We gave little attention anywhere to the Normal, Agricultural, Technical or Professional schools. In Ontario especially these are very similar to 

American schools of the same kind. Having so little time we thought it best to see only what was "different" enough to interest you. Besides, such schools could not offer us much in the vacation season, as we found when we visited one-especially urge to do so because it was "different." This was Ontario's Bilingual Normal already mentioned as located at the Uniersity of Ottawa. Its "difference" is in the fact that it prepares teachers for primary and secondary schools in which both the English and French languages are used on an equal footing by pupils and teachers. There is a surplus of trained teachers in Ontario and Quebec, as in Oklahoma, but no surplus of bi-lingual teachers. This school was the only Normal "different” enough to attract us in Ontario. In Quebec, however, we found some quite "different" enough to claim attention.

Quebec has more Normal Schools than Oklahoma-too many, we thought. But the Province is very large in territory, some pioneering sections are still poor, and there are hosts of children. Ninety-five percent of those of school age are attending school. Most of the other five percent might be accounted for by the constant push of settlers into the developing North.

The "different" thing about some of these Quebec Normals is that they were built by the churches, at their own expense. The Government only "helps" in supporting them. When it comes to saving money one wonders if these Canadians are not Scotchmen in disguise. No wonder we found

McDonalds, Blackburns, Warrens, even Murrays, who spoke French with ease and fluency and English with the timidity of learners. These Normals have, too, a very unique way of securing pupils for their model classes. 

They accept little boys as boarders at from twenty to twenty-five dollars per month. The best families try to get their youngsters into such favored schools. Some knowing Americans even have followed suit. Their boys came home, after a year or two, with a new language tripping on their tongues. But we mention these Normals only to call attention to the relation of the Government to them. Handicrafts

There is one other kind of Quebec school that should receive special attention. It is a sort of Teachers' School for household handicrafts, and pretty much of a Government venture, in which the English and the French population meet. Quebec thinks it quite necessary also to instruct her daughters how to be good housewives. There are twenty-one housekeeping schools in the Province with between 4,000 and 5,000 pupils. Nuns teach housekeeping to over 31,000 girls in addition. But the handicrafts school is quite another thing. "The Department of Agriculture has established at Quebec a school where young women and nuns are taught the traditional arts of spinning, weaving, and rugmaking. This is a kind of craft 

Normal School." Prof. Bovey of McGill University furnishes this information.

He called our attention a bit proudly to the suit he was wearing and told us that it was fashioned out of handmade "Canadian tweed." We did not yearn to wear one like it, but then one of us has to wear professional black and the other confesses that he has no taste for the English cut in clothes. We are willing to say that the material looked as good as the average Scotch tweed. The point is that the cloth was not made in a factory but in a school.

We spoke of this effort to develop home handicrafts to our Governor, asking if it might not be well to think of something of the kind for our own farm population. Many French-Canadian farmers' wives make winter-

earned money out of it. The Governor thought that our farmers first of all should be made owners, since such crafts develop best in independent farm homes. Doubtless he is right. The Quebec farmers are nearly all owners of the land they work.

This idea of fostering handicrafts is, however, one that should not be dismissed without study. Mr. E. L. Waldo, an Oklahoma artist, is an enthusiast about it because he believes in its economic value. He feels that the development of handicrafts by the small farmer would serve to make him independent. A thorough study of this matter might well be undertaken by our A. & M. College. (But we suspect that the A. & M. College has not missed it.) We already have buildings that could be used for handicraft schools. Quebec would turn such buildings into special schools, like her present handicraft venture or like a dairy school that we saw but did not visit. Mr. Waldo, in a letter, quoted a partner of the house of Morgan as saying: "Corporate enterprise is drying up private initiative and making a nation of hired men." It is admitted that present measures for the relief of unemployment are intended "to put industry back on its feet." In that it will in all probability succeed for a time. But after that what? If we are to go on standardizing, making one machine do the work of two, raising wages for the few and throwing the many out of work, what then? 

Little schools for handicrafts may not seem to offer much toward the solution of our major problem, but the lid of a tea-kettle had a great share in the discovery of the power of steam. It is worth noting, too, that there are many homes for dependent children in Oklahoma, all doing, at the expense of churches, clubs and lodges, what the state does not do or cannot do effectively. These children are sent to school, but they are not trained for the day when they must go out alone to face the working world. If we suggest that they are, in part at least,a responsibility of the state, will we be told that any help extended to train them would be too much like "a union of Church and State?" If we could only get it into our heads that cooperation is far from union, our children, especially the dependent, might fare better. Why should not the staff of the A. & M. College consider the need of training these dependent children in handicrafts, without taking them away from their proper guardians, as a challenge as well as a charity? 

That brings us to the Agricultural Schools, but only to say that we had to treat them no better than the Normals. They were closed for the summer vacations. Nowhere in Canada is there anything like the enrollment of students we have at Stillwater. Small agriculture school seem to be favored. But the Unievrsity of McGill has the beautifully located McDonald Agricultural College, while Montreal keeps the Institute Agricole of Oka under its wing and Laval does the same for the one of Ste. Anne. But even primary education in Quebec is given an agricultural twist in rural districts. Here is a quotation from a memorandum sent to such schools by the 

Department of Public Instruction in 1930: 

** ** "The primary school, like every other, must be practical in one sense of the word, it must adapt itself to the special needs of each district. The rural school has as its main objective to prepare the children who attend it to be good farmers or at least to love and value agricultural life. It is therefore essential to furnish an education which will develop the love of the land. 

In order that education may be 'ruralized' it must be permeated by ideas consonant with the social and economic needs of the country population. various subjects of study must be given an agricultural twist. 

Instead of asking the price of 42 yards of ribbon at 30 cents a yard, ask the price of 6½ pounds of clover seed at 112 cents a pound."

Neither did a Yale Ph. D. and a Bishop feel competent to report at length on the technical schools. But since we have drawn so generously from Professor Bovey, and are already "in bad" with him on the question

Correct Speaking 

Something of a more far-reaching character in Quebec education should be noticed. From the French universities and colleges goes a vast propaganda for the correct use of the language of the people. It is the "Parlar 

Franqais (Speak French)" movement, which is not anti-English but is anti- Anglicism. It aims, not to make the English speak French, but to make the French speak it with correctness and elegance. French newspapers have taken up the movement and the country pastors are deep in it. We drag this news into our report with joy. Why not have something like that come out of our Greater University, yes, out of our public and private schools? Why not try it on our grown-up selves, and learn to speak our language at least as well as it is spoken by the twelve-year-old youngsters of the cultured families of England? Simplicity

Higher education in Canada shows signs of moving, but slowly in Quebec, away from a simplicity. It might well, we think, decide to move slower. Too many experiments pull progressive steps and stages in education apart. Since American tax supported education has taken the field of instruction as its own, it might be well to remember that the process of instruction is not really a complicated one. Every day all American life is

being more and more complicated in the interest of place-making and career hunting. Someone has remarked that in business today a firm has to keep three sets of complicated books: one to show investors that it is making money; one to prove to the stockholders that it is soon going to make money; and one to assure the income tax collectors that it never has and never can make money. The business of teaching can and ought to be left uncomplicated with expensive fads and luxuries. There are a few indications, in some parts of Canada, of a departure from the simplicity that produces the self-reliant and disciplined scholar. We hope sincerely that the depression will kill them. At the Bi-lingual normal in Ottawa, when we asked for the catalog, we were given only a four-page folder. No long, expensive catalog here with syllabi of courses seldom or never given. Colleges might well begin the practice of economy by ceasing to pad their catalogues, and working out a solid and simple preparatory course for the university and professional schools, as well as for that long course of private study and spiritual formation out of which comes the leaders of men. 

Note Well

You would be far from the truth if you thought that we have been repeating in this report only what the Canadian educators said about themselves and their institutions. They were a remarkably frank body of selfcritics. But we expected that, especially after reading some of their public and private utterances. For example, take this from the pen of our McGill friend: "No one who looks over our University scene as a whole can fail to see that we have been affected by the spirit of materialism which permeates Western civilization. The teacher and the student alike have thought much of vocational efficiency, but," he adds, "to see errors like this is to begin to overcome them, and a different spirit-a turn from the materialist to the idealist viewpoint is apparent." This is from the cultured Rector of the 

University of Montreal: "It becomes difficult in these, our days, to uphold the rights of a general culture. We easily acquire the habit of under-estimating all superior knowledge in which we do not see a palpable and material return." But he said also, and here we quote from a personal letter: "We do all in our power to adapt our university teaching to the conditions of the present times, and to the just exigencies of new scientific developments. But we are convinced that towards them and before them it is necessary to keep the old classical culture called the Greco-Latin as the base of all serious formation of youth." Here is a still more significant sentence from the same letter: "You must know that, always accepting with great thankfulness the financial help of our public powers-civil and municipal governments—and of generous citizens, we aim to guard as a sacred thing the teaching and formation of our students." This is from the distinguished Rector of Laval (Dr. Flexner would like it): "Superior education wishes everywhere to find its place. Strictly professional education, to which it was too long confined, does not create in the University the atmosphere that belongs to it. 

Nothing favors more a false intellectual equality than the mediocrity of those who should be superior." He was there pleading as well as rebuking. One of the best things we found in the 

Canadian educators was a vigilant, "divine discontent." In that educationly happy and useful state they meet our Lowells, Flexners, Pritchetts, and 

Hutchins, exchange greetings and find kinship of ideas. * * * Decision We came away from Canada convinced that we can have a Greater University made up of coordinated colleges, tax-supported and independent, and thereby save money to the state, improve our educational product. make for unity, strengthen our citizenship and better our character. We can have a Greater University that will reach down into secondary and

primary instruction and force both to do the work that every passing year shows they are failing to do, for they send hosts of unprepared students into the colleges. We can do all that if we come to the task with open, unprejudiced minds, having no thought of a state monopoly of education and no desire but to have all our educational forces, while remaining as independent and free as they are today, unite in contributing their share to a common task.

PRELIMINARY PROPOSALS FOR THE COORDINATION OF THE GREATER UNIVERSITY OF OKLAHOMA, WRITTEN BY O. D. FOSTER. 

Realizing that all well motivated, guided, and planned education is constructed for the realization of a clearly defined and understood objective, an attempt is here made to propose in skeleton form an objective worthy of the expenditure of time, treasure and effort spent in its attainment. 

WE RECOMMEND that the objective of the Greater University of 

Oklahoma be understood to be as follows:

The highest development of the student's talents and personality along with a keen sense of responsibility to organized society realized through A symmetrical growth of body, mind, and heart

Physical health and vigor 

Mastery of fundamental processes 

Worthy membership in home and society

Proper perspective of vocational preparation Civic education and responsibility 

Constructive use of leisure 

Positive ethical character 

World mindedness and sense of human brotherhood

Thoroughly disciplined and cultivated mind Adequate philosophy of life and appreciation of values Or, in short, the quest of ultimate truth, and the proper fitting of qualified individuals for its attainment.

We recommend that a most careful inventory of the higher educational needs of the State of Oklahoma be taken at once that the program to be built may meet actual individual and social needs rather than to gratify institutional and local aspirations. 

While the various institutions now existing in the State of Oklahoma were organized to meet specific needs-real or imaginary-times have so changed their needs, purposes, relationships, bases of support and programs that these numerous independent and unrelated institutions must be viewed in the light of the central objective on the one hand and of the specific needs to be met on the other hand. In taking the proposed inventory, such questions as to the following may well be kept in mind: Just what are the needs of the state for an educated responsibile citizenry? Just what are the needs of the state for professional men? What needs, in the light of the central objective, are not now being met and what needs are over-provided for? How far is the state under obligation to provide higher education for all its population?

We recommend that the Chairman of the Steering Committee serve in the capacity of Chancellor until a permanent chancellor is appointed. 

This procedure will put into action the actual development of the plan at once as well as be training much needed leadership in carrying forward the plan when it is set up. For the permanent Chancllor to be as free as possible of prejudice and of jealousies he should not be related to any one institution more closely than to all. 

We recommend that the Vice-Chancellor chosen from the Presidents of the institutions in their respective groups be selected as soon as possible after the Chancellor, and be given specific tasks under the general direction of the Chancellor of developing the correlated interests of their respective groups along lines agreed upon by the Coordinating Board; and we further recommend that the present members of the Steering Committee, in addition to its chairman, serve in the capacities here designated for the Vice- Chancellors until such are officially named. 

Being presidents of institutions-whose offices will probably rotate, they will serve without pay except for reimbursement for expenses incurred in line of duty for the Board. 

We recommend that some one thoroughly acquainted with the problems involved be selected to assist the Chairman of the Steering Committee until the Chancellor is selected, and to give special attention and study to the section of the plan designated "Inter-Divisional Relationship" that he and his cabinet of Vice-Chancellors may have before them a simultaneous development for their consideration. 

When a full-time Chancellor is secured, he may be able to give sufficient personal attention to the development and supervision of this pioneering field of effort; but until then, perhaps, capable volunteer leadership may be found within the state. The relationships here proposed are so fundamental to the development of the Greater University that the study of this phase of the work should be simultaneous with the rest. 

We recommend that the Chancellor's Educational Council-composed of two sections-the Presidents of the State Colleges and the Presidents of the Non-State Colleges, be organized at once at the call of the Chairman of the Steering Committee and assigned tasks of educational coordination. 

For years the Educational Council of the Province of Quebec-composed of two sections-has been working successfully under a single head (the Superintendent of Public Instruction, corresponding to our proposed Chancellor) but with separate secretaries for the two sections, corresponding to

our proposed Vice-Chancellors. This arrangement permits close correlation with groups as well as assures the maximum correlation between groups. (See Appendix P.)

We recommend that the Chancellor's Council be at once set to the task of making coordinating and eliminations which their respective institutions along lines agreed upon among themselves in the interest of economy, effectiveness, and character.

While much has already been accomplished along this line, much more remains to be done; and with plans well devised and correlated excellent results will follow. General principles may be formulated in the central 

Council, the application of which will relieve local embarrassments and inhibitions.

We recommend that a committee under the direction of the Chairman of the Steering Committee be assigned to the task of determining the conditions for eligibility to membership in the Greater University. 

We recommend, since function follows need and objective, that allocations be worked out just as rapidly as is possible under the circumstances.

While this will be largely under the direction of the Chancellor's cabinet, the entire Council will provide invaluable assistance. The Vice-Chancellors under the advice of the Chancellor will assume leadership in suggesting methods, plans, and allocations of functions to institutions. 1 We recommend that the programs of all constituent units of the Greater University of Oklahoma be most carefully studied with the view to attaining the central objective and that the necessary changes be made as rapidly as possible to this end. 

This will mean eliminations, additions, shift of emphasis, conscious motivation, reorganization of curriculum and student activities with greater consideration for the part idealistic agencies have to play in the process. 

We recommend that in allocating functions and constructing programs greater distinction be drawn between "Education” and “Training.”

While Dr. Flexner has doubtless gone too far in his indictment of 

American education along this line, he has rendered a real service in forcing us to reevaluate and to classify as well as to allocate what we have been offering as a college education. 

We recommend that just recognition of the State be given those institutions of higher learning which are rendering invaluable service to the State free of cost to the State. 

Numerous ways and means may be found, as is abundantly illustrated in the Provinces and States, how this may be effectively done without in any way violating the intent of the framers of our laws. Furthermore, ordinary justice demands a thorough examination of our premises and laws in this respect. It is difficult to see why Oklahoma should provide, especially while in its present straits, higher education free of cost to student of much hicher states when these very states collect both fees and tuitions and, at the same time, see approximately one-third of its students desiring higher education go to other states where they pay well for the instruction they get. It may be good philanthropy but it is hardly justifiable practice. It must be further remembered that the practice is making it increasingly difficult for the independent schools, which have been rendering invaluable service free to the state, to survive. On the present plan, it will only be a matter of time when these schools can no longer operate. These students will be thrown upon the state and the added expense put upon the taxpayer; but worse yet, the people of the state will be denied the opportunity of educating their children in what many prize as religious institutions.

We recommend greater uniformity in the undergradate colleges in the interest of the central objective. 

This would work greatly in the interest of economy, effectiveness and the student's personal benefit. The cafeteria menu may provide a well balanced diet to the wise, but chiefly cake and bon-bons to children. 

We recommend for careful consideration the honors system with the view to introduction and prudent development. 

The best educational practice seems to be leading us further from the abuses of the credit system.

We recommend that the Chancellor and his advisers study the possibility of coordinating higher state education of Oklahoma with that of other states.

It is hardly wise for the poorer states, especially to attempt to provide every type of educational facility that might be desired on the part of any of its students. An agreement among states will lead to much greater effectiveness and much less cost by a coordinated system, especially in certain fields. For example, Oklahoma might well offer facilities for instruction in Petroleum Engineering in lieu of say Dental education. 

We recommend that professional experts be encouraged to take active part in the affairs of life. 

In the interest of vital teaching, society, economy, and good educational practice, this is advisable. Professional aloofness has brought results more serious than ridicule. If any group of citizens in a community is capable of leading, it should be those who are employed as specialists capable of training other specialists.

We recommend that in selecting personnel a hearty espousal of the central objective of the Greater University be required. 

Students are susceptible to leadership and form their philosophies of life largely from the attitude and instruction of their seniors who are to them authorities. The whole purpose of education may be vitiated by unworthy models for the imitative youth to follow. 

We recommend that athletics be in the interest of good health and physical vigor of all students rather than in the interest of mass amusement with its attendant evils. 

Interdepartment rivalries and organization add greatly to institutional interest and afford opportunity to the many, but too much reliance is put upon a few of the more spectular sports which draw gate fees and too little to developing the play life or the riches of its numerous forms. 

We recommend that interinstitutional departmental organizations of professors be organized as early as possible for the study of methods, standards, examinations, and exchange instruction. 

Conferences, for example, of the Latin professors of all the constituent colleges would soon lead to greater equality of merit on a high plane. Together they might well work out sets of examinations to be uniformly used in the various colleges. 

We recommend that uniform examinations be made as proposed above in given subjects and levels, and that pseudonyms be used by the student whose paper will go to a central board of examiners for reading. 

The evils of our present system are too apparent to merit discussion. 

Favoritism cannot be avoided in our present practice. Nor can outside pressure be escaped by the professor who happens to have the child of a powerful and influential citizen.

We recommend that the University of Oklahoma proper shall be composed of its graduate and professional schools and that only students enrolled in these schools shall be regarded as students in the University.

The University shall maintain a College of Liberal Arts, but students taking this work would no more be considered members of the University of Oklahoma than would students doing similar work in any of the constituent colleges of the Greater University. 

We recommend that students be encouraged to attend the constituent colleges until graduation or such time as distinctly professional work must be undertaken.

This practice would lessen the number of Arts students at both the University and the A. & M. College, but would enrich their specialties both in quality of student and in distinction. It would also enrich the local colleges, as well as permit the student to be nearer those who feel more responsible for his personal welfare. Many more would remain at home, thus getting an education at less cost, as well as under better influences, in most cases. 

We recommend that less attention in proportion be given to vocational courses and more to general preparation of citizenship and culture. 

With the overproduction of specialists in all lines, the unemployment problem is greatly accentuated. Highly trained specialists are the most helpless of all the educated when nothing in the line of their specialty is open. The moral obligation of the institution actively recruiting students for eventual disappointment cannot be condoned. With the raising of standards, this overproduction of mediocrity would be escaped, the student given a square deal, the state saved large sums of money, and the reputation of the instituion augmented. 

We recommend the council on educational and cultural planning to study educational integrations of all levels in addition to ways and means of mobilizing the general cultural influences of the state in a statewide plan in which the Greater University will have a major part. 

This Council will view the problem of education and culture in the State of Oklahoma as a whole. It will see the various phases in their proper perspective and from time to time make recommendations to the Superintendent of Public Instruction, the Chancellor of the University, and the Governor of the State. It will also take into consideration all those valuable cultural influences and agencies not now considered a part of the college planning and indicate ways in which these influences may be utilized in the general development of the intellectual and cultural life of the commonwealth.

We recommend that a Bureau of Research be created under the direction of the Chancellor for such uses as he may direct. 

Constant studies must be carried on in correlations, standardizations, personnel, finances, and programs. Such departments as the Council on 

Educational and Cultural Planning will need the assistance of the Bureau. 

While this implies an expert director, it should also imply that the vast bulk of the work may be secured through voluntary effort, once the importance of the work it proposes to do is understood. While there will be divisions of functions and allocations of responsibility, the entire development will be under the watchful eye of the Chancellor. 

We recommend that the Junior College period be considered as terminal rather than as preparatory to Senior College and that the program be arranged so as to give the maximum attainment of the objective of the Greater University to be had at this level: and that some recognition be given upon graduation in the form of a suitable degree or certificate. 

This procedure would produce a better equipped citizenry with less mediocrity in specialization. It would cultivate an appreciation of education as an end in itself rather than as a means of a livelihood and thus give room for the greatly needed idealism now lacking and at the same time lift the level of preparation for professional study.

We recommend that specialization for the professions be discouraged until at least Senior College. 

This will insure a broader cultural base, weed out much mediocrity, reduce the number to quality, now entering the professions, spare great numbers of students later disappointments, and greatly relieve the taxpayer. 

The last year of the Arts course or senior college might count both toward the arts and professional degrees. 

We recommend that students with early vocational learnings be directed away from the arts colleges to the vocational schools maintained to provide this training.

While this is assumed with an integrated state system where allocations of functions have been made, it may not be assumed that it will be easy to put into practice. It will require constant vigilance on the part of the 

Chancellor, his cabinet, and his Council to make this a reality. But when practiced, it will secure greater effectiveness, create a better spirit, and afford a wider field from which each institution may draw its clentele. 

We recommend the principle and practice of reciprocity among institutions be inaugurated in harmony with the plan of allocation of functions that the recommendation just preceding may be made more effective. 

There should be such esprit de corps among the constituent units of the Greater University that institutional student counsellors would without hesitation direct to other units of the system-and to other states if need be-students who would be better served by the change. 

We recommend that uniform application blanks be prepared to send prospective students, including such questions as will discover in a large degree in advance to what unit of the system the student should be advised to go.

This practice would lesson the number of misfits, lower the freshman mortality rate, permit better education, and materially reduce the cost to the supporters of the various institutions. 

We recommend that no unit of the system create graduate work which can better be provided by the University and A. & M. College. 

The temptation for colleges to imitate universities often makes of a successful college an unsuccessful university. 

We recommend that the dishonest practce of padding catalogues be forbidden in the entire system.

While this is no indictment of the institutions of the State of Oklahoma, the recommendation is made in the interest of precaution against general practice in all states. Its evils need not be pointed out. 

We recommend that courses in science requiring excessively expensive laboratory equipment be postponed-so far as practicable-till senior college and that the principles of reciprocity and exchange be invoked to meet this issue when the exigencies require. 

While this practice would reduce the cost of education, it would also encourage and strengthen the specific functions assigned to the various institutions. Without the load of expensive laboratories, many institutions could provide on their present budgets superior work in the arts and humanities.

We recommend that an interinstitutional commission be set up at once by the Chairman of the Steering Committee to study and recommend methods of improving the teaching in the various units of the system. 

Fortunately, Dean Dodge of the University of Oklahoma, for example, is especially well equipped to serve as chairman of such a commisson. His recent studies of teaching in so many institutions in all parts of the country render his expert leadership invaluable for the state as a whole.

We recommend that the number of students admitted for professional training be reviewed by the council on educational and cultural planning and that the number designated be selected on the basis of their quality of preparation and promise of success.

The reasons for this recommendation are obvious. The practice might be introduced in a small way as soon as the inventory of the needs for Higher Education in the State is made. This could be done by examination. 

We recommend that the number of students admitted for pedagogical training be determined by the actual knowledge of the needs and that the number accepted be chosen on the same principles as those recommended for the professional schools.

While this practice would greatly reduce the number of students attending pedagogical courses at Teachers Colleges, it would strengthen materially the courses in Arts and Humanities. It would tend to give great numbers of students better training and education for life, enrich the Arts Colleges, and eliminate certain of the teachers colleges as such. 

We recommend, in case the preceding proposal be put into operation, that at least one-half of the Teachers Colleges be discontinued as such and turned to other educational uses. 

Perhaps, such institutions could advisably be turned into junior colleges or to other specialized uses. Their function might be determined by the discovery in the inventory of needs of fields not now covered by the present program.

We recommend that graduate work in teacher training be done at the State University, except such vocational courses as must be taken at the 

A. & M. College. 

We recommend that ways and means be devised to release the administrative and teaching staffs of State institutions from political control and base their security entirely upon merit. 

The bane of politics has handicapped state education in America ever since its inauguration and will continue so long as political influence controls. No single reform in state education the nation over is more greatly needed.

We recommend that the Chancellor in conference with his advisers after a thoroughgoing study of the financial needs of higher education in conference with disinterested experts in his Board of Educational Counsellors present his budget to the people as a final and just statement of needs to be met.

The blame here is not all on one side. But that the present practice is a menace to the best interests of education and to state education is known by all acquainted with it. With a central officer to equate the needs of all institutions fairly and without preference, marked improvement will be experienced. Yet further reforms will need to be made. 

We recommend that the whole system of financing higher education in the state-whether public or private-be thoroughly studied and planned so as to make the costs to the state, the institution (or community of interest supporting it), and the parent or student equitable as to institutions and localities.* 

We recommend that the costs of education to the student be made more equitable for the same work in all units of the system. 

This practice will work for equity, fair play, better understanding, and easy coordinations. *See the studies of the Carnegie Commission in California on this point.

85 We recommend that all students pursuing higher education in the state of Oklahoma be charged tuition and fees commensurate with good practice in other states.

We recommend further that scholarships be provided out of funds collected and other sources for exceptionally worthy and capable students who might not be able to attend college without help. 

While self support is a fundamental part of the student's education and he should not be released entirely from the consciousness of this responsibility, scholarship funds will release more of the worthy student's time for study. 

We recommend that the proposed foundation for education be organized at an early date.

This foundation in time will accumulate such capital sums, the income of which will eventually greatly enrich institutional staffs, provide loan funds, scholarships, and prizes. It may be hoped that the foundation may offer competitive fellowships for foreign study. 

We recommend that series of well planned, coordinated, and prepared articles be released to the press of the state and such other means as may commend themselves from time to time to the Board to educate and to enlist the support of the public. 

A campaign of this kind, well directed along carefully thought out plans, will carry.public sentiment and secure the support of influential people who would otherwise be apathetic if not in opposition.

(An Association of Coordinated Colleges) We, the undersigned members of the Coordinating Board, in order to facilitate the work of coordinating and unifying the program of higher educaiton in Oklahoma, as provided for in House Bill No. 686, passed by the 

Fourteenth Legislature and approved by Governor Wm. H. Murray on the 

26th day of April, 1933, do ratify and approve the following constitution of The Greater University of Oklahoma (An Association of Coordinated Colleges):

First 

The name of this organization shall be "The Greater University of Oklahoma (An Association of Coordinated Colleges)". Second

The purpose for which this organization is formed is to provide a permanent central agency through which the Coordinating Board may discharge its duties of coordinating and unifying the system of higher education in Oklahoma, with which duties said Coordinating Board is charged by House Bill No. 686, passed by the Fourteenth Legislatutre, and approved by Governor Wm. H. Murray on the 26th day of April, 1933. This organization will carry out the orders and all the purposes of the organization known as the Coordinating Board, as provided by law in its creation. For such purpose, the organization shall be authorized to do and to perform the 

---

(a) To establish a permanent organization to carry on effectively the detailed work involved in the discharge of the Coordinating Board's duties; (b) To maintain a permanent office which shall be the seat of the 

Greater University of Oklahoma; (c) To receive and accept any properties, appropriated funds, donations or bequests, with other sources of income, adequately to support and maintain The Greater University of Oklahoma; (d) To employ, through the use of funds indicated above, and adequate staff for the successful prosecution of the purposes of the organization set forth herein; (e) To receive and disburse funds donated or bequeathed by any lawful corporation or honorable individual or group of individuals engaged in legitimate business; (f) To grant academic and honorary degrees to persons recommended therefor by any coordinated college under the jurisdiction of the Coordinating Board, or to persons of commensurate qualifications therefor whose candidacy shall be determined by methods to be prescribed by the Coordinating Board upon recommendations by the Institutional Cabinet; (g) To do any and all things authorized under existing law and to exercise all powers which may hereafter be provided by law necessary to carry out the objects and purposes of the Coordinating Board. 

The organization of The Greater University of Oklahoma, when adequate funds are available, shall consist of a Chancellor, an Institutional Cabinet, a Council of Educational Consultants, and an Office of Administrative Research, and such other officers and offices as the Coordinating Board may from time to time deem advisable to create. 

Fourth 

The Chancellor shall be the chief executive officer of The Greater University of Oklahoma, and as such shall perform duties determined upon by the Coordinating Board. (An Association of Coordinated Colleges) Purpose

ARTICLE I. This organization shall be known as "The Greater Uni versity of Oklahoma (An Association of Coordinated Colleges)".

Place of Business ARTICLE II. The principal place of business of The Greater Uni versity of Oklahoma shall be at the Capitol of the State of Oklahoma. Trustees

ARTICLE III. The trustees of The Greater University of Oklahoma shall consist of members of the Coordinating Board, provided for under the terms of House Bill 686 of the Fourteenth Legislature, approved by the Governor on the 26th day of April, 1933.

Section 1. Trustees shall hold their office according to the provisions of House Bill 686 of the Fourteenth Legislature, and shall continue to serve until their successors are selected and qualified. 

Section 2. The trustees of The Greater University of Oklahoma shall have general control of all matters relating to higher education, specially provided for in House Bill 686 of the Fourteenth Legislature. They shall elect from their number, the following officers, who shall serve until their successors are chosen and qualified: Chairman, Vice-President, and Secretary. 

Section 3. The trustees of The Greater University of Oklahoma shall meet in the State Capitol once each quarter of the school year on such dates as the Coordinating Board may, by motion or resolution, provide; special meetings may be held upon the call of the Chairman or written request of three members of the Board; provided notice thereof, in writing, shall be mailed to each Trustee at least five days preceding such meeting. 

Special meetings may be held at convenient places other than the State 

Capitol, when so designated.

A majority of the members of the Board of Trustees shall constitute a quorum, and the votes of a majority of those present shall constitute bind ing acts for The Greater University of Oklahoma. 

Section 4. 

The members of the Board of Trustees shall serve without compensation. Actual and necessary expenses incurred in attending meet-

ings of the Board shall be paid from such funds as are available for the support of The Greater University of Oklahoma.

Chairman or upon notice to all by any two members of their own number. 

During the intervals between meetings of the Board of Trustees, such committee shall advise with and aid the members of the staff of The Greater University of Oklahoma in all matters concerning its interests, management of its business; and perform such duties and exercise such powers as may be directed or delegated by the Board of Trustees from time to time. The 

Board of Trustees may delegate to the Executive Committee authority to exercise all the powers of the Board, except the power to amend the By- Laws, ad interim of its sessions. Vacancies in the membership of the Committee shall be filled by the Board of Trustees at any regular meeting, or at a special meeting called for that purpose. 

The Executive Committee shall keep regular minutes of its proceedings and report the same to the Board, when required.

Oklahoma may appoint any officers and agents as may, from time to time, become necessary. All officers shall hold their offices for such terms and upon such conditions as shall be determined by the Board.

Chairman ARTICLE VI. The Chairman shall be the chief executive officer of the 

Board of Trustees; he shall preside at all meetings of the Board; he shall have general oversight of the business of the Board; and shall see that all orders and resolutions of the Board are carried into effect.

Section 2. He shall be ex-officio a member of all standing committees and shall have the general powers and duties of supervision and management usually vested in the office of the president of an organization. 

Vice-Chairman ARTICLE VII. The Vice-Chairman shall, in the absence or disability of the Chairman, perform the duties and exercise the powers of the Chairman, and shall perform such other duties as the Board of Trustees shall prescribe. Secretary

The articles being somewhat out of the ordinary, I thought best to refer same to you for an opinion."

In reply you are advised that the House Bill above mentioned appears as Chapter 160, Okla. Sess. Laws 1933, the material part thereof being as follows: 

"Section 1. There is hereby created for the institutions of higher learning of the State of Oklahoma, a central co-ordinating agent to be known as the "co-ordinating Board"; to be composed of fifteen (15) members, appointed by the Governor and confirmed by the Senate; the terms of office shall be co-terminous with the Governor of the State. As soon as practicable after the passage and approval of this Act, the Board created shall be called together at a time and place designated by the Governor, for the purpose of organizing in conformity with this Act; and said Board is hereby empowered to formulate such rules and regulations as shall be necessary to carry out the provisions of this Act. 

"The duties of this Board shall be: 

(a) The assembling of exact information that will show the actual costs of education at the several institutions, together with the immediate and future needs of each school, and make a report thereof annually to the Governor. (b) The checking of the up-grading and expanding tendency by a clear interpretation and definition of the functions of each of the several institutions and the elimination of undesirable duplications. (c) The bringing about of harmonious working relationships among the institutions by the establishment of needed councils. (d) The unifying of the tax-supported institutions into a state system of higher education through the determination of an intelligent plan for their fututre development. "Section 2. Any regulations, courses of study, or standards set up by the "Co-ordinating Board," when approved by the Governor, shall be in full force and effect in the administration of the affairs of the institution. The said 'Co-ordinating Board' shall not draw any salary, but may be compensated for actual expenses while in performance of said functions and duties."

By an examination of the above quoted provisions, it will be found that same in no way authorize the persons who are appointed to fill the fifteen offices therein created and who compose said “Co-ordinating Board" to incorporate an organization to perform or to assist said Board in performing the duties enjoined there by said chapter. In fact, said Act expressly provides that said Board shall organize “in conformity with this Act" and that said Board, as such, is "empowered to formulate such rules and regulations as shall be necessary to carry out the provisions of this Act." By an examination of paragraphs “a,” “b,” “c,” and “d” of Section 1, supra, defining the duties of said Board, it will be found that same are to be performed by the Board, as such, and not by some organization to be incorporated by the fifteen officers who are members thereof. In fact, under the provisions of 

Section 9725, O. S. 1931, relating generally to the creation of corporations and Sections 9927 to 9933, idem, relating specifically to the incorporation of educational associations, etc., neither State officers nor members of State 

Boards or Commissions are authorized, as such, to be the incorporators of

Attorney General's Rulings 

93 a corporation; neither are individuals, who are State officers or members of 

State Boards or Commissions, authorized by law to form a corporation to assist them in performing their statutory official duties, or to perform, in whole or in part, said duties. In this connection, attention is called to the first paragraph of the proposed articles of incorporation in question, which provides: "That we, the undersigned citizens of the State of Oklahoma, members of the Coordinating Board, whose names are hereunto Subscribed, have associated ourselves together for the purpose of forming a corporation under the provisions of Chapter 46 of Article II, Oklahoma Statutes, 1931, and in keeping with this enactment, state:"

Inasmuch as there is no "Chapter 46 of Article II, Oklahoma Statutes, 1931" and as Article II of Chapter 46 of said statutes relates to the incorporation of educational associations, we assume that the incorporators of said proposed corporation intended to refer to said latter article, and in writing this opinion we will follow said assumption.

In this connection you are advised that the above quoted paragraph of said proposed articles of incorporation, taken in conjunction with the 11th numbered paragraph thereof and the manner in which said proposed articles is signed, clearly reveal that said proposed corporation is sought to be incorporated by the fifteen members of said "Co-ordinating Board," acting in their official capacity, and that said members, as such, are to be the Board of Trustees of the same. This being true, it necessarily follows that said members are without legal authority to incorporate the association in question.

Furthermore, attention is called to the second numbered paragraph of said proposed articles of incorporatiion, which provides in part as follows: "The purpose for which this corporation is formed is to provide a permanent central agency through which the Coordinating Board may discharge its duties of coordinating and unifying the systems of higher education in Oklahoma, with which duties said Coordinating Board is charged by House Bill No. 686, passed by the Fourteenth Legislature, and approved by Governor Wm. H. Murray on the 26th day of April, 1933. This corporation is to carry out the orders and all the purposes of the organization known as the Coordinating Board, as provided by law in its creation. For such purpose, the corporation shall be authorized to do and to perform the fol

"(c) To levy upon and collect from all cooperating institutions of higher learning in the State of Oklahoma, such fees as may be necessary, with other sources of income, adequately to support and maintain the Greater University of Oklahoma; 

"(f) To grant academic and honorary degrees to persons recommended therefor by any coordinated college under the jurisdiction of the Coordinating Board, or to persons of commensurate qualifications therefor whose candidacy shall be determined by methods to be prescribed by the Coordinating Board upon recommendation by the Institutional Cabinet;**"

In reference to the first above quoted portion of said paragraph you are advised that inasmuch as a corporation is a separate entity and is governed by its Board of Directors or Trustees acting pursuant to law, it can not, in the absence of an express statute to that effect, be made an agency through which state officers, boards or commissions perform their official statutory duties. For the same reason a corporation can not legally be formed which is required to carry out the orders of said Coordi-

nating Board, and of which the incorporators of the proposed corporation in question, are members.

In this connection attention is called to the case of In re Sunshine 

Steam Laundry, Inc., 47 Fed. (2)915, wherein the fourth paragraph of the syllabus is as follows: 

"Corporation is separate entity distinct from its organizers, acts independently, and is bound only by acts of representatives within scope of authority."

In reference to the above quoted subdivision "c" of said paragraph it should be noted that Section 2 of Chapter 160, supra, is in part as follows: "The said 'Co-ordinating Board' shall not draw any salary, but may may be compensated for actual expenses while in performance of said functions and duties."

As this is the only provision in said Chapter relating to the expenses of said Board, it was clearly the intention of the Legislature that no other expenses were to be incurred by said board in performing its statutory functions and duties. Inasmuch as a House bill, which was introduced in the Special Session of the Fourteenth Legislature, appropriating Three Thousand Dollars to pay the expenses of said board, was killed on the last day of said session, there are no funds now available to pay said expenses. 

However, we do not believe that such fact would authorize said Co-ordinating Board or said proposed corporation to levy and collect from the various "institutions of higher learning in the State of Oklahoma" or from the students thereof, the fees mentioned in subdivision "c," supra. 

In this connection, the Honorable Chas. W. Briles, one of the incorporators of said proposed corporation, advises this office that it is the intention of said Board, acting through said proposed corporation, and under the power which it seeks to have dedicated thereto by subdivision "C," supra, to levy a fifty cent fee against each student of the several State Institutions of higher learning in Oklahoma. In our opinion, no corporation can legally be incorporated in this State, in the absence of express legislative authority, which has for one of its purposes the levying and collecting of fees from said educational institutions or from the students thereof. 

In reference to the above quoted subdivision "f" of said paragraph, which attempts to give to said proposed corporation authority to grant academic and honorary degrees, your attention is called to Section 7161 to 7165, inclusive, Oklahoma Statutes 1931, wherein certain named educational institutions of this State and colleges and universities approved by the State Board of Education, have been given authority to grant academic and professional degrees. You will note that Section 7163, supra, expressly provides that: "No person, to whom Authority to grant degrees has not been granted in accordance with this act, shall grant, offer to grant, or advertise to grant, any such academic or professional degrees." we deem it unnecessary to quote or discuss the provisions contained in other subdivisions of said paragraph. However, you are advised that in the case of Sneed, Secretary of State v. Tippett, 114 Oklahoma 173, 245 Pac. 40, the fourth paragraph of the syllabus is as follows; "Our statutes do not make it the duty of the Secretary of State to issue a certificate of incorporation for purposes not authorized by law."

Attorney General's Rulings 

95 It is therefore, the opinion of the Attorney General, in consideration of the statutes above referred to and analyzed, that you should decline to issue a charter to said proposed corporation.

Reasons for using a plan of accounting and reporting: 

1. Internal purposes-a standard plan is likely to result in the best kind of financial reports and accounts. 

2. Makes it possible for reports to be compiled and issued that correspond with reports being compiled by other institutions and thus comparisons are readily made possible among and between institutions of similar scope. 

3. Greatly simplifies problems of reporting to central bodies such as 

United States Office of Education and United States Department of 

Agriculture and to state departments as well. 

There are a few fundamentals relating to financial accounting and student accounting. One of the essentials of college and university accounting is that the accounts be classified into fund groups. This is more vital in private than in public institutions but it applies to both because private institutions generally have endowment funds to administer. However, such funds will probably come into possession of public institutions. 

The fund groups that are common and essential in the usual college or university are: 

1. Current funds, including funds that are general or unrestricted and funds that are restricted, as gifts. 

2. Student loan funds. 

3. Endowment funds, the purpose of which is to produce income, and the capital or principal must be kept intact. 

4. Plant funds, including the building and other equipment and the capital represented by those buildings or equipment. 

Sometimes an additional group, called agency funds, is required. This is recommended when institution is handling student organization or activity funds to any large extent. 

Next problem involved is classification of income and expenditures in accordance with some reasonable, uniform plan. Income Classification is rather a simple matter but one fundamental that needs to be recognized is the segregation of funds for educational purposes from funds for auxiliary activities which go mainly to operation of such enterprises as residence halls, book stores, intercollegiate activities, etc. 

Recommend that income be classified, first, as to educational and general income, second, auxiliary enterprises and activities and, third, scholarships and annuities.

Classification of expenditures has a great variety of conditions at the present time. Needs particular care. In some cases has been placed upon classification by object, for salaries, wages, supplies, travel, etc. Another, even more important, classification is that by function which indicates scope of activities by institution. In this classification the Committee suggests the three major classifications by function. 

Practically every item in Oklahoma classification is found in Committee's classification by object. Attention is called to classification by function. Suggest that the Committee's classification by function be followed. 

Especially the budget, financial account and financial reports should follow a uniform plan. Often a lack of harmony among those different items, all of which deal with the same things. Dear Doctor Bennett: 

April

Twelfth 1934 In accordance with your instructions, I spent the period from April 2 to April 6 inclusive in Oklahoma with reference to the forms of accounts and financial reports of the colleges and universities of the state and the cost study being carried out under the direction of the State Coordinating 

Board of Higher Education. 

I made brief visits to the University of Oklahoma, the Oklahoma Agri-

cultural and Mechanical College, the University of Tulsa, and Phillips University. In each case I made a brief examination of the accounting system and forms of financial reports and offered suggestions for improvement. In some cases only minor details were involved and in one case, that of Phillips University, some major changes were found desirable. I submitted to the 

President of each institution a memorandum covering my suggestions and a copy of this memorandum was filed in the office of the Board at the State 

Capitol. 

A conference was held with the administrative committee in which problems relating to a uniform classification of accounts and problems relating to cost study procedure were discussed. Certain of the suggestions presented in that conference were submitted in later conferences to state officials and institutional representatives. 

Conferences were held with the State Examiner-Inspector and with the State Budget Officer relative to a classification of accounts uniform with that of institutions in other states. These officers expressed willingness to accept for the state supported institutions of higher education whatever classification of accounts would be agreed upon by the various institutions, having in mind the desirability of reasonable uniformity in certain respects with the general accounting classifications of the state. 

A conference of the Presidents of the various institutions, both public and private, was held, at which I reviewed the principles which should apply to financial reports and cost studies and submitted the following recommendations: (1) That the group adopt in principle as the basis for general uniformity in the accounts and reports of all institutions represented the recommendations of the National Committee on Standard Reports for Institutions of Higher Education. This committee, which is sponsored by the U. S. Office of Education and the General Education Board has worked out a uniform plan of accounting and reporting which has been adopted by the U. S. 

Office of Education for statistics of colleges and universities, the U. S. Department of Agriculture for reports of federal funds appropriated to state colleges and universities, several state governments for their institutions, and by the majority of colleges and universities over the country. It is now the recognized standard for accounts and reports of this kind. (2) That a general advisory committee of about five members to advise the director of the cost studies on technical matters relating to his work be appointed from among the faculties of the various institutions. (3) That in each institution a faculty committee be appointed by the executive head to review the report of the cost study for the purpose of submitting comments and recommendations to the executive head of each

Institution, this committee to be appointed in each case by the latter officer.

These recommendations were approved by the conference of Presidents. 

A conference of financial officers of the various institutions was also held at which I presented in some detail the uniform plan of accounting and reporting recommended by the National Committee and answered questions which were raised concerning these matters. In connection with that conference an organization of these officials was perfected and officers elected. This will enable the officials to exchange information from time to time and get the benefit of each other's experience. 

I reviewed in detail with Mr. Schiller Scroggs, the director of cost studies, the procedure for the studies. I found that this procedure is carefully and comprehensively laid out and that adequate information is being

111 assembled. At certain points I suggested minor modifications of the procedure intended to make the results more accurate and more truly comparable. It must be kept in mind that unit costs of higher education are subject to variation as result of many conditions. For that reason, it is important that the figures not only be very carefully compiled, but be carefully interpreted and analyzed when presented. Otherwise erroneous conclusions may result. It is my opinion that if these precautions are taken the data that will be assembled through the study as it is now planned will give the desired service.

In addition to the recommendations already mentioned, I offered the 

(1) That the representatives of the state supported institutions request the State Examiner-Inspector and the State Budget Officer to institute a uniform classification for the budgets and the reports of these institutions to be put into effect as soon as practicable. These classifications are of two types: (a) A classification of function (b) A classification by character and object the classification by function to follow the recommendations of the National Committee. I have left a preliminary outline of that classification with Mr. Scroggs and suggested that he confer with several of the institutions concerned, perfect it, after which he and I suggest that it be transmitted to the state officers for approval. It is my understanding that this procedure was acceptable to the Presidents at the time of the conference already mentioned. 

With respect to the classification by character and object, uniformity with the general state classification is desirable. Mr. Harrison, the 

Budget Officer, has asked that I submit any suggestions which I may have on the present classification to make it more satisfactory to the colleges and universities. This I will do in the near future and will let you know what I suggest in this respect. (2) That the private institutions of the state make arrangements for some plan of securing centralized advice on their accounts and reports similar to that which will be given state supported institutions by the state officers. It is my understanding that these institutions have already acted on this suggestion and have selected Mr. C. I. Duncan, Bursar of the University of Tulsa for this purpose. 

I trust that these suggestions will prove practicable and helpful. I shall continue at your service to answer questions which may arise in the course of your further progress and shall be glad to give further time on the ground at a later date if you should so desire.
