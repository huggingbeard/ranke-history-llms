FOREWORD The Rur 

Rural Life Association is very grateful to Dr. Fretz and the Mennonite Central Committee for permission to publish this pamphlet which is based on observation and first hand study of Mennonite communities in Canada.

Most people have had some vague knowledge of the Plain People, of whom the Mennonites are one group. Their sturdy qualities and way of life are not widely known and understood. 

However, everybody knows that they are good farmers. The houses, barns, landscape and soil of a Mennonite community bear pleasant witness for even the casual tourist's appreciation. 

Only the neighbors and associates know the full worth of the Mennonite families and homes and their quality as people. 

How have they managed, in spite of persecution, migration and hardship, to live such wholesome lives of simplicity, sufficiency and devotion? How may we learn from their experience?

In these troubled times and in the years of uncertainty just ahead, many thoughtful people will be seeking anew the eternal values. Where and how may our young people, in the stress of these times, work out useful and wholesome lives for themselves and their children, while helping to build the kind of a world that ought to be?

It is because we believe this study of Canadian Mennonite communities will be helpful that we are glad to publish this pamphlet.

RURAL COMMUNITY SETTLEMENT Case Studies in Canada 

This is a study of communities set up by Russian 

Mennonite settlers who came to Canada from 1923 to 1930.

It was thought important to study this immigration for a number of reasons. First of all it resulted in colonization on a sufficiently large scale to make it significant. More than 20,000 

Mennonites came to Canada in these years. In the second place the effort was so recent that many of the problems of colonization in the early stages can still be studied effectively and yet they are not too recent to prevent one from seeing trends. In the third place these Mennonite colonists, almost without exception, had little or no financial means and most of them had heavy transportation debts. This fact makes a study of their efforts significant for our investigation of Mennonite youth who will need help in establishing themselves following this war.

ONTARIO 

The Mennonite immigrants from Russia in the period 19231930 landed in Eastern Canada and many of them were employed for a time by the older Mennonites already established in Ontario. The period of employment lasted anywhere from three months to ten years. Most of these immigrants gradually shifted to the prairie provinces. Some, however, remained permanently in southeastern Ontario on the Niagara Peninsula. 

The problem of becoming established was difficult, due to the lack of funds, the heavy transportation debts and the prolonged period of economic depression which faced them in the years after they arrived. However, it may be said that the Mennonite settlers who came here since 1923 have made remarkable economic progress.

Vineland and Virgil 

Two settlements visited were at Vineland and at Virgil, Ontario. In each of these communities there are two Mennonite churches-a General Conference and a Mennonite Brethren congregation. There are also a number of Old Mennonite churches. There are about 240 families in the two churches in the Vineland community and approximately 300 families in the two churches of the Virgil community. In recent years from 40 to 50 families of the 1874 immigration moved east from 

Hepburn, Saskatchewan to Virgil.

The pattern of becoming established in both these areas is the same; the Mennonite immigrants worked for a time as hired men, saved what money they could, then bought land for themselves as quickly as possible.

It should be said that these Mennonite colonists changed the agricultural pattern by intensifying fruit growing. Many of the older Mennonite settlers while growing fruit also practiced general diversified farming on their farms ranging in acreage from forty to one-hundred acres. In the Virgil area grapes were the chief fruit crop, but the newcomers added a variety of other fruits and intensified farming methods. 

Intensified Farming 

The recent immigrants, partly from necessity, developed a way of living on a much smaller acreage. Large farms were purchased and divided into five, ten, and fifteen acre tracts. 

These were bought on what has come to be called "Mennonite Terms." This meant 10% down payment, which often had to be borrowed at a local bank with interest sometimes as high as 7%; the remainder was paid in annual installments. 

The purchasers were very eager to own their homes and were willing to start on a very low plane of living. The dwelling house was generally a small one or two room building of a temporary sort, often a cabin or a chicken house. Fruit trees and vegetables were immediately planted and while waiting for these to produce, the husband, and often the entire family would work out as day laborers in surrounding communities. 2

The combined income usually provided means to buy food and to meet the financial obligations incurred in the purchase of land. Through tremendous sacrifice, a great deal of hard work and an undaunted faith in God these people established homes on their own land.

Geographically the Ontario communities are ideally situated for fruit growing because the large annual rainfall and the long growing season enable fruit and vegetables of the highest quality to be produced.

Desirable Location 

The two Mennonite settlements under consideration lie along the shores of Lake Ontario. It is significant that in this area the producers of fruits and vegetables have a ready market. 

The roads are generally good so that farmers living off the highways are not easily mud bound. Railroad connections and truck facilities are also excellent. 

The religious outlook of the community is very good. The church is definitely the predominant institution. More than a place of public worship, it becomes the cultural center for the whole life of the people. A number of community problems, such as the health and medical care of the members, are organized around the church. The collection of the transportation debt has been arranged through the local church. 

Truly these people make religion a way of life. 

Economically the communities are becoming very successful. Virgil especially demonstrates a remarkable economic growth within the course of a single decade. 

Fruit Cooperative

Here the Mennonite immigrants faced considerable discrimination on the part of the earlier English settlers. This is illustrated in the case of the cooperative association which existed in the Virgil community. The Mennonites were discouraged from joining, so they organized a cooperative association of their own in 1937 known as the Niagara Township Fruit Cooperative. It now is so successful that they are reported to 3

be doing ten times the amount of business that the older cooperative is doing. This cooperative serves as a marketing agency for the Mennonite fruit and vegetable farmers. Prior to the present shortage in consumers goods it also provided groceries and other supplies to the members. It has a membership of 100 at the present time. Two branch cooperative associations have been started, one of them three miles from 

Virgil, and the other in Saint Catherine. 

Fruit and Vegetable Cannery

Another aspect of the economic situation in Virgil is the establishment of a cannery by Mr. Peter Wall who came to 

Canada as a penniless refugee. For a number of years he lived on a Saskatchewan farm but, becoming discouraged, he moved to Vineland where be began to farm. He has not only become financially prosperous but he has helped a great many other Mennonite settlers to establish themselves. He did this by purchasing large farms, one at a time, subdividing and selling them on "Mennonite Terms." After doing this in the 

Vineland community a number of times he sold all his holdings there and bought one large tract after another in the community of Virgil about twenty miles distant. 

An almost entirely new community has been built at Virgil which had consisted of little more than the post-office, store, and about one-half dozen private dwellings; today there are some 300 Mennonite families there, the first of which came in 1935. The school building has had to be enlarged on two different occasions to take care of the increase in children. 

Mr. Wall noticed that the problem of disposing of the increasing amount of produce was a major one even with the cooperative organization. Various Mennonites in the community urged him to establish a cannery. In a short time he had become sort of a legendary character to whom many looked for a solution of their problems. Since the majority of these people had no funds of their own and he had made an economic success they urged him to establish the cannery. 

After deliberation he undertook the venture, but not without

a great deal of anxiety as to its ultimate success. However, today the enterprise is on a very firm financial basis. At the present time there is a large, modernly equipped cannery located in the open country about two miles from Virgil. It started operating in 1940 and now represents a capital investment of $85,000. About 200 women and 50 men are employed for some five months out of the year.

An index to the growth and size of this business venture is given in the following figures: 

1940-$27,000 worth of goods manufactured $ 2,000 loss 1941-$ 97,000 worth of goods manufactured $ 7,000 loss 1942 $351,000, worth of goods manufactured $20,000 profit 

In 1942 the Mennonites in the vicinity of Virgil sold approximately $100,00 worth of goods to the cannery and earned about $40,000 in wages.

It is the plan to introduce new lines such as baby foods so the cannery can be operated throughout the year. The cooperative consumers' and marketing agency plus the cannery and the productive farms make this a stable economic area and the future looks very promising. The climate is favorable to annual crops and the community is located close to urban markets and has its own local marketing and processing agencies.

It is gratifying to notice that as the economic situation improves so do the homes. They are being remodeled, repainted, and landscaped to give the impression of permanency. This reflects love of the home and family life. The majority of the people with whom I spoke seemed optimistic and above all thankful to God for the blessings which they are enjoying after years of privation and suffering. I observed also that a number of the young people were being sent to schools and colleges to prepare for jobs as teachers. In the churches Bible Schools have been started to teach religion and the German language. 

My general conclusion is that the Mennonite colonization effort in Vineland and Virgil, Ontario is generally successful and promising for the future.

MANITOBА Historical Background 

The majority of Mennonites who settled in Manitoba are located in the southeastern section of the province within 150 miles of Winnipeg. The largest settlements are in what is known as the East and West Reserve, large blocks of land east and west of the Red River reserved by the Dominion government especially for the Mennonites.

The settling of the East Reserve represents the first coming of Mennonites in western Canada. These early settlements took place following the immigration of Mennonites from Russia in 1874. This area is all prairie land. The growing season is sufficiently long to permit the growth of all common varieties of grains and vegetables although the climate is somewhat unpredictable.

I visited in the communities of Steinbach, which is something of a Mennonite Capital for the Eastern Reserve, and in Winkler, Altona and Gretna in the West Reserve. 

Religious Situation 

In this area there are at least eight different branches of the Mennonites. There is some cooperation between the various bodies but on the whole there is little that is done in common. 

Beside this is the cultural conflict that has resulted from the different migrations. However, in all branches members accept the church as the predominant institution. 

One wing of the church is the Bible School. The well established school at Gretna is perhaps the best known; this is both a Bible School and an Academy. Winkler, Steinbach and some of the other communities have well established Bible Schools. These serve to promote the German language, the 

Mennonite faith and in general to preserve Mennonite culture. 

Economic Activities 

Of primary concern to me was the economic condition of these Mennonite communities, for from it I hoped to learn

some lessons which would throw light on establishing our own Mennonite young men after the war.

The Mennonite communities resemble the general pattern and character of rural communities in the middle west. While the majority of the farmers are grain growers and dairying and live stock are of secondary importance, in recent years there has been a growth of creameries and cheese factories. 

The towns range in size from small hamlets to the town of Steinbach with a population of 1800, which shows a growth, in the last two decades, of about 800 people. I was interested in the activities and characteristics of these communities which have particular significance for this study and I shall dwell at some length on these activities. One point of interest was the growth of truck gardening and small self-sufficient farming around the towns of Steinbach and Winkler. Steinbach had from 30 to 50 small homes on the outer edge of town erected within the last ten years by the owners who hoped to make a living as wage earners in the town or on surrounding farms. 

In Winkler I counted over a hundred such homesteads. On the whole these places reflect a much lower economic status than those in Steinbach. This indicated an excess population which had no place to establish itself. Dr. C. W. Wiebe, of 

Winkler, who knows the actual home conditions in the community showed me around and described the social and economic conditions.

Many who thus established themselves were young married couples with no funds to buy farms or start businesses of their own. That the desire to establish themselves on the land is strong is shown by those who start on so small and feeble a scale. 

Southeast of Winkler one may count as many as ten and twelve small homesteads within a single mile. In this area from fifty to one hundred families have established themselves on small farms ranging in acreage from five to twenty. These people started on an amazingly primitive level; one room frame houses of a very crude sort and barns which are made of posts and straw are not an uncommon sight; dried and pressed

manure is still a common fuel for the heating of the homes. 

House furnishings and facilities are extremely meager. 

An Experiment 

Related to this situation is the experiment in land settlement of the Old Colony Mennonite Church near Rhineland which bought 160 acres of land in 1940. Each man within the church who needed land but had no means to buy it was given ten acres of land on credit without interest. He was to repay this loan on the basis of one-fourth share of the crop. If he had no money the church helped him build his house and barn. He was expected to repay this building loan on a basis of onefourth of his cash earnings, the assumption being that he would work out as a day laborer. 

All of the homes thus established are very primitive. At the present time there are twelve farmers in the congregation. who started on this basis. The general practice is for the family to have three or four cows, a few pigs and chickens, and then for the man and the older children to work out either for cash or to earn feed or food by working for their neighbors. The wife and smaller children do most of the work at home. 

At the present time the cash income may run as high as $300 to $400 a year. The church has the understanding that if one of these farmers has paid for his ten acres and wishes to sell he does so only to the church. The money for the purchase of this 160 acre farm by the congregation was raised by about 25 of the more prosperous members. The price of the farm was $1800 and the minimum amount of each contributor $25.00. Some of the members contributed as much as $250.00. 

The money is to be repaid but there is no interest charged. 

When any member of the church needs help of this kind he appeals to the elder who arranges for him to get the piece of land. Mr. Klass Heide, one of the promoters of this scheme, said that the congregation would be willing to buy a second farm if necessary. The plan was still too new for me to make any predictions as to its ultimate outcome. 

Those families which we visited seemed to be fairly opti-

mistic but one must remember that these are unusual times and that prices for products and labor may not continue on this abnormally high level; therefore the economic basis of this experiment seems to be rather precarious.

It is interesting that most of these small scale farmers are still depending on grain crops for their cash income rather than some of the more highly preferred crops which would produce a larger cash income per acre. I would think that the agricultural pattern would have to be shifted from grain. farming to a more intensified row crop type of farming. 

New Industries 

There are several examples of promising new small town industries in these Manitoba Mennonite communities. In 

Steinbach some industries related to bee-keeping grew up around a lumber yard.

Another phase of the industry sprang up around the tinsmith and plumber who manufactures the metal supplies needed by bee keepers, such as smokers, honey extractors, etc. On a farm several miles from town a man has established a bees-wax factory with a considerable volume of business. Some thirty different items necesary to bee keeping are manufactured in this community. This has stimulated bee culture and has provided employment and cash income for about twenty-five men in the area. Instead of the local bee keepers shipping their equipment from eastern Canada it is manufactured locally and supplies not only the immediate locality but a large section of the market in Manitoba.

Another interesting illustration of local industry began near Steinbach where the Plett brothers started manufacturing cheese boxes. At present they claim to be making practically all of the cheese boxes used in Manitoba. This factory is located on their own farm which is operated in addition to the factory.

Around this cheese box industry have grown a number of other activities; a machine shop and manufacturing of various types of farm equipment; in fact, the Plett brothers have

something of a rural hardware store on their farm. The employment of the four Plett brothers plus their hired help has resulted in the creation of a little village on what was once a single farmstead.

A third illustration of a small town or rural industry is found several miles out of Winkler on the farm of J. A. Gunther. In 1935 he got the idea from a Saskatchewan Mennonite that wheat could be puffed successfully at home. After some experimenting which cost him close to $1,000 he bought a machine and began manufacturing puffed wheat. He bought a house-trailer and used it to market his product in the surrounding towns to a distance of about thirty miles. At the present time he consumes about 5,000 bushels of wheat annually; 1,000 bushels of this he grows on his own farm. He employs one man and two boys beside himself. This industry illustrates a way of disposing of a crop at a much greater income than could be realized if sold unprocessed. At the same time it provides a wholesome cereal for the people in the local area at a very nominal cost.

Cooperative Activities 

It is the general opinion of the recent Mennonite immigrants with whom I spoke that cooperative activities help tremendously in furthering their economic position. Their enthusiasm for cooperatives is revealed in the number of organizations which they have formed since settling in Manitoba.

Almost without exception in the villages where the recent Mennonite immigrants are established one or more cooperative activities can be found. Even the small villages, formerly occupied by the Old Colony people who went to Mexico and South America have their small cooperatives; usually a creamery, cheese factory, a store or a credit union. The older

Mennonite communities within the last ten years have been influenced by the recent immigrants, or by the growth of the general Rochdale Cooperative Movement, to organize cooperatives because most of them have been established within this recent period.

ΙΟ

Very large and successful cooperative stores have been organized in a number of the communities, the largest being the store at Altona which has stock worth $40,000 and current net assets of $64,000. Other cooperative stores are found in 

Gretna, Plum Coulee, Winkler, Horndean, Lowe Farm. There are cooperative creameries and cheese factories in eight of the small Mennonite communities in the East Reserve and as many or more in the Mennonite communities of the West Reserve. 

In addition there are credit unions and cooperative oil stations in several of the towns, and a cooperative hatchery in Altona. 

A cooperatively owned and operated machine shop was organized here in 1942 by a number of farmers. At the present time they have $13,000 invested in the project which is proving quite successful. It was organized because farmers could not get farm implements so they decided to improvise some of their old machines for modern use. They have converted seven binders into swathers and are now planning to change a number of sheaf loaders into sugar beet loaders.

This enterprise shows the creative thinking that is being done by some of the local leaders. The man who supervises this project was called from an aircraft plant in Winnipeg where he had been earning high war-time wages, but he saw the possibilities of this cooperative plan to help the farmers and decided to return to his home town in Altona. This would indicate that when a place is provided for a man to work he will prefer to stay in his home community. The machine shop now employs ten men full time. 

Federation of Cooperatives

These various cooperative activities of the Mennonite communities in southern Manitoba have been organized into the Federation of Southern Manitoba Cooperatives. This central agency permits certain economic and educational advantages. 

First of all, a greater purchasing power with resulting better prices; second, a greater accumulation of funds for expansion purposes; third, the elimination of duplicating service and fourth, a coordination of accounting and operating methods. 

II

As a fifth point, it promotes employment. Altogether there are 

82 employees in the cooperative activities under the direction of the Federation. In the cooperative store at Altona there are now fifteen employees where there were formerly two; in the machine shop there are ten where there were formerly none; in the hatcheries there are two where there were formerly none; in the old station there are five where there were formerly two; and the Cooperative Federation employs six men where there were formerly none.

It is significant that the Cooperative Federation provides employment for the young Mennonite men who have had college and university education. At least four young men with college degrees have been called back into their home community and given worthwhile work through this activity. 

The people in the West Reserve have formed an Agricultural Society and this Society has organized an Agricultural Institute. This organization trains young people to understand and love rural life and the Mennonite heritage. The Agricultural Society has taken the initiative in further experimentation and economic expansion. Plans are under way to erect an oil extraction plant for sun-flower, rape, flax, soy beans and other seeds. This organization is working with the Mennonite 

Agricultural Advisory Committee which was formed to give counsel to the young men who wish to take the position of conscientious objectors before the law.

Educationally this cooperative device promotes a greater loyalty and understanding on the part of the Mennonite people. It provides an excellent way for training members, managers, directors and leaders of the organization; it promotes the method of Christian mutual aid and in general binds people together into a more harmonious community organization. 

A Cooperative Farm 

The Mennonites in Southern Manitoba are wrestling with the problem of establishing young people on the land, as are the Mennonites in the United States.

An interesting illustration of the way a group in Altona and 12

Gretna are facing this problem is found in the Rhineland cooperative farm which was started and is being operated by eight Mennonite young men. Each was anxious to establish a home of his own but had no financial means. They decided to rent land and farm it cooperatively. This year they have 

31 acres of potatoes, 10 acres of table beets, 14 acres of flax, 16 acres of soy beans and 10 acres of other products. They started with $50.00 each and they operated the land cooperatively. 

Four of the boys live on the farm and work the land and the other four work outside to earn cash to supplement the farm income. The objectives of this group are to work out a way in which young people can establish themselves on farms without each individual requiring a large capital outlay for stock and equipment. They hope to demonstrate that this is a workable plan for other Mennonite young people in the post-war world and they believe that cooperative farming enriches their lives and improves their economic welfare. They feel that together they are able to meet the changing conditions more successfully than they could if they operated individually. 

The advantages of the plan are that it provides an opportunity for specialization, such as stock raising, seed growing, and mechanics. Furthermore, it provides the necessary labor to till the soil and improve the land and buildings; it enables capital to be raised for machinery and equipment and thus eliminates the high over-head cost per farm that is now so characteristic of farming everywhere. 

Teacherages 

An item of interest in the Mennonite communities was the custom of providing a teacherage for the local school teacher adjacent to the school ground. The teacherage usually had the house, a small barn and a few acres of land on which the teacher could keep his cow, pigs and chickens, raise his own vegetables and perhaps keep some bees. This is a good way to supplement the cash income.

This is significant because it encourages married men with families to accept school teaching positions and is an induce- 13

ment for them to remain in one locality for a longer period of time, and thus become a part of the community. 

My impression of the economic life of the Mennonite settlements in Manitoba was generally favorable. Leadership exercised by younger men in the communities is promising for the future. It is regrettable that the leadership in the economic and business realm is not in harmony with the leadership in the religious and social realms. There is a definite cleavage between these two phases of community activity. Some of the difficulties will undoubtedly be eliminated as the cultural conflicts, due to different periods of migration and different European backgrounds, disappear. The older generations are more aware of the conflicts than the younger ones.

COALDALE, ALBERTA 

Practically all of the Mennonite families in this community came from Russia to Canada in 1924 and 1925. Many of them at first worked in Ontario and Manitoba Mennonite communities. The Canadian Pacific Railway had tried to get Mennonites to settle in Coaldale but it could never persuade the Mennonite Colonization Board to support its proposal. The 

Board opposed settlement in irrigation districts because the immigrants had had no experience with or interest in this type of farming.

The first Mennonite settlers came in spite of the Colonization Board. A number of families were attracted by the high wages for workers in the beet fields. These families, after working in the beet fields for one season were persuaded to settle here permanently.

Four Enns brothers and a Mrs. Gerhard Janzen were the first Mennonite families to buy land here. At that time, 1926, land sold for $80.00 an acre. They were sold 320 acres on condition that 150 of it would be put in sugar beets. Other settlers were more easily induced to come to Coaldale after this start had been made. The C. P. R. offered the Mennonites eighty 14

acre farms on condition that the farmers would promise to plant and give ten acres of beets to the C. P. R. each year as principal and interest on the farms until they were paid for. 

The settlers were also granted $400 in cash for the erection of buildings by the C. P. R. Of the sixteen farms that were offered on these conditions fourteen of them were taken immediately by Mennonite settlers.

From 1926 to 1943 the community of Coaldale has gradually grown to a present total population of 267 families and 1,477 souls. There are two Mennonite churches in the community. 

A General Conference church with 150 members, or about 

45 families, was organized in 1930. Rev. Peter Schellenberg, the pastor said that only one-third of his members owned land, the other two-thirds rented or worked as day laborers. He himself rents 110 acres on a half share basis. The Mennonite 

Brethren church has approximately 200 families and 600 members. There are a number of Mennonite families in the area who do not belong to any church. 

In this community as in others the church is the predominant institution. The whole of Mennonite community life is in and around the church and most activities originate there. 

The local Bible School is situated on the premises of the Mennonite Brethren church and was organized in 1929. It is supported by both Mennonite churches in the community. 

The school has three teachers and a five month term, from 

November 1st to April 1st. The attendance runs from sixty to eighty.

The Orts Committee 

One of the significant community organizations is the Orts Committee, which is concerned with all local Mennonite cultural activities. It has officers and rules and keeps a record of 

Mennonite families, the number of children, dates of births and deaths, the number of people moving in and out of the community, whether they owe a transportation debt to the C. P. R. and other factors of general interest to church and community. 15

An illustration of the work they do can be found in the following figures taken from the annual report for 1942. Here we find that the occuptational distribution of the Mennonites in this area is 164 farmers, 81 day laborers, 11 in business, 3 apiarists, 3 teachers, and 28 retired people. Since the settlement in Coaldale began, ninety families have moved away; fifty-one of them went to British Columbia. Of the fifty-one, twentyone were farmers and twenty-six were day laborers. The Orts 

Committee transcends all branches within the Mennonite denomination and is a community cultural organization rather than a strictly sectarian one.

Mennonite Health Society 

A further aspect of the community solidarity is the organization for the promotion of health. The Coaldale Mennonite 

Health Society is a systematic plan whereby medical attention is provided for members of the community at rock bottom rates. The society now has a membership of three hundred. 

The fee was originally $9.25 per year per family or $6.00 for single people and $4.50 for widows and other economically dependent persons.

The origin of this health plan goes back to Russia where these people had their own medical doctors and hospital. When they came to Coaldale they found that hospital and medical expenses were prohibitive. The impoverished settlers were shocked to discover that a tonsillectomy cost $60 and a doctor's death certificate $10 to $15. I was told of numerous instances where people already in financial difficulties were completely submerged with debt when they encountered medical expenses. 

As a result of this situation the people studied the possibility of group medical protection.

At Lethbridge, a city ten miles distant, they were discouraged from such an effort at the local hospital. Arrangements were finally made with an individual doctor in the city on the basis of $12 per family on condition that the families would call at his office. This did not prove satisfactory and in a few 16

years the community called its own doctor. Dr. David L. Epp, a Mennonite, came from Saskatchewan and continues as resident physician. There is a set fee of $35 for major operations and $10 for all minor operations and $15 for confinement cases in the hospital. Home calls at night are $2.00 plus 35c mileage charge one way. The day mileage charge is 25c one way. This is in contrast to the standard charge of $1.00 per mile which the Lethbridge doctors had been charging the 

Coaldale residents.

When one considers the number of preventive cases and others where individuals were improved through medical attention in proper time, in the course of a year the saving is tremendous. The doctor's report for 1942 indicated that there were 1,936 office calls; 106 home calls; 28 major operations; 

71 minor operations; 390 tooth extractions; 33 fractures and dislocations; 57 confinement cases; and four deaths.

Another phase of the health program in Coaldale is the community hospital. Local citizens organized and continue to manage it.

The Burial Society 

The Alberta Mennonite Burial Society was organized by the Alberta Mennonite Provincial Committee in 1943. The plan is to provide help in the form of a fixed cash payment to the families visited by death. There is a 50c membership fee at the time a person joins the society and after the death of a member there is an additional assessment of 15c each. This protects the entire family. At the time of a death the bereaved family is paid $50.00 if an adult, and $25 if a child.

It is still the custom in this community for a local man to make the coffin. The more fancy hearses are unknown in funerals. Commercial trucks are used instead of hearses. This makes the ordinary funeral very inexpensive and indeed makes the doctrine of Mennonite simplicity a reality. 

Fire and storm insurance on the basis of Christian mutual aid is also provided to the members of the community who desire it. 17

Other Activities 

In the community one finds Mennonites operating two blacksmith shops; three general stores; two garages; one feed and flour mill; one shoe repair shop; one cheese factory. The latter enterprise is cooperatively organized and operated. It was started in 1937 when a group of Mennonite farmers decided to work as a unit to market their milk. At present there are 140 farmers shipping milk to this factory. Six thousand pounds of cheese are made a week, in addition about 5,000 pounds of milk are shipped to a German alien internment camp at Lethbridge about twelve miles away. The cooperative also crates and handles eggs for local farmers.

The bee keeping industry is another important local enterprise. The Willms brothers have one of three apiaries in the community and are operating on a rather large scale. One of the brothers has 200 hives which occupies his full time as well as that of two other individuals. Each hive produces about 150 pounds in a normal year.

Conclusion 

Practically all of the farming in this community is done by irrigation. Most of the farmers I talked to are not enthusiastic about this method. It is a hard and very confining way of farming but one which pretty definitely assures an annual crop. One of the difficulties that a number of the Mennonites face in Coaldale as in some other communities is that they were not farmers by training or experience. A number of those I met had formerly been school teachers, bankers, ministers and business men. On the whole one must say that the Coaldale settlement is quite a successful Mennonite colonization effort. 

The churches are strong and exercise a great deal of control over the majority of the people. This is illustrated in the collection of taxes for the support of activities. Each member is assessed 6oc a year for the support of patients in mental hospitals; 25c a year for the Orts Committee and 50c for the 

Mennonite relief work. Members are denied participation in the local health society and fire insurance society if they refuse 18

to pay these assessments. The Coaldale Mennonite community as late as 1942 had a forty-seven thousand dollar transportation debt to the C. P. R. By 1943 this debt was completely liquidated. It was only possible through the combined efforts of the total community. 

This community has been fortunate in having wise and devoted leaders in such persons as Rev. B. B. Janz, the minister of the Mennonite Brethren Church, and his brother J. B. Janz who is a successful farmer, active church man, local historian, and a man devoted to the best interests of his community. 

BRITISH COLUMBIA 

In British Columbia I visited four Mennonite communities; namely, Yarrow, Sardis, Abbotsford, and Coghlan, all within 

100 miles of Vancover.

Yarrow, Historical Background 

The first settlers came to Yarrow, the oldest and largest of the Mennonite settlements in British Columbia, in February 

1928. Upon their arrival from Manitoba, Saskatchewan and

Alberta they found hardly a suitable dwelling in what is now a flourishing community.

The early settlers in British Columbia left the prairie because of their unsuccessful effort to make a living on farms there. Most of them also disliked the long cold winters, being used to milder climates in southern Russia. 

The first settlers were attracted to Yarrow by an advertisement in the Mennonitische Rundschau which invited Mennonites to buy land in Fraser Valley. Yarrow is about sixty-five miles east of the city of Vancouver and is in the valley along the Vadder River.

Mr. Chauncy Eckert, the land agent who had come from 

South Dakota, induced the Mennonites to come here, and he has served as a benefactor to the impoverished refugees. 

The first eight families stayed in one house on the side of a mountain a mile from Yarrow. This house has six rooms 19

and there were altogether about fifty persons occupying this small and inadequate space for a period of three months. None of these first Mennonite settlers had any money. The men worked in the hop fields three or four miles away to earn sufficient cash to buy food and to provide essential clothing and shelter.

The land which the Mennonites settled was bought from Mr. Eckert at $150 an acre. It was part of a 700-acre plot which had been sold for taxes to Mr. Eckert and his business partner. 

The first plots of ground were purchased by the Mennonites on credit. The men started building their houses after regular working hours and on off days.

Most of the early settlers bought ten acres of unimproved land, thus obligating themselves immediately for $1500. Mr. 

Eckert required no down payments and in addition ordered the lumber for the houses and advanced other credit to the settlers. 

He bought three tractors and put them to work clearing and breaking the ground. The settlers later paid him in kind as they could. Mr. Eckert also gave some of the men cows to supply milk for their families.

After about four months some of the families moved into their improvised houses, many of them being small cabins and chicken houses. The dwelling houses were later improved as economic conditions permitted. In many cases this took five to ten years because of the severe economic depression which followed.

Married men with large families walked five miles to hoe potatoes and pick hops for fifteen cents an hour during the depression. At the same time they planted fruits and vegetables and rhubarb which provided the first cash income. Cows and chickens were gradually acquired as the settlers established themselves.

New Mennonite immigrants constantly kept coming to increase the total population, some of them bringing a little money with them. Mr. Eckert and other old timers were very much impressed with the willingness of the Mennonites to work at whatever jobs they could find. This demonstrated to 20

them the sincerity, industry and thrift of the settlers and went a long way toward establishing credit.

Generous Land Agent 

On one occasion Mr. Eckert bought a 700-pound hog, butchered it, and gave all the settlers a portion of it. He is an unusual land agent. Mr. Klassen and I had a profitable visit with Mr. Eckert, and could see why the Mennonite settlers continue to look to him as a friend, father and helper. He is not a church member, but contributed $100 toward the building of a new church in Yarrow. He continues to be interested in each new settler and his progress in the community. 

It should be said that Mr. Eckert was amply repaid for his investment, because previous to the coming of the Mennonites the land was not worth much more than the taxes. 

The present population of Yarrow consists of some 250 families or a total of 1250 individuals. The Mennonite Brethren church has a large new building which was erected in 1938 and has 660 members plus about 400 children. The General 

Conference church has about 90 members and 50 children.

The public school in Yarrow has an enrollment of about 

400. So rapidly has been the increase of Mennonites in the community that the school building has had to be enlarged on two different occasions. The Mennonites maintain their own 

Bible School and have approximately 140 in regular attendance. 

As is characteristic of all the recent immigrants the German language and religion are the two major subjects of emphasis in the Bible Schools. Yarrow reports a high degree of community consciousness.

Mennonite Government 

I found here that the Mennonites were organized as a community but were not legally incorporated. From a governmental standpoint they have developed much the same pattern that was so characteristic among the Mennonites in Russia. 

They have elected a chairman to look after the community affairs. This chairman is similar to the Schulz found in the 21

Mennonite villages in Russia. He, with a committee, represents the Mennonite community of Yarrow in all public matters in the municipality and in Chilliwack, the county seat. 

In such local affairs as road work, bridge building and relief this committee deals entirely with the outside agencies. During the depression when Mennonites opposed public relief for their members this committee went to the municipal officials and asked for permission to construct its own roads, build its own bridges, and in general take care of its public utilities and be paid its proper share out of the public treasury. This was accepted and the funds were used to provide employment for those Mennonites who most needed it.

During the war such matters as air raid work, legal matters and public relations due to antagonism to Mennonites and conscientious objectors and other such things, are all handled by this local committee. In political elections candidates deal only with the authorized Mennonite agents rather than solicit the support of individual Mennonite citizens. This is a unique 

Mennonite pattern in America.

The community has organized its own volunteer fire brigade, and has provided its own equipment. The water pump is mounted on a small truck and used with a power take-off. 

This is a completely local device. Water supply is provided by digging water holes in various parts of the town instead of the fire plugs usually seen. Each family is assessed for the community expenses that are incurred. 

The community boundary extends for nearly two miles in length and from three-fourths of a mile to two miles in width. 

This is definitely not a political boundary, but is merely for 

Mennonite convenience. 

Group Insurance' 

A further aspect of community organization is shown in the fire protection enjoyed by the residents. The committee to which we have referred investigated various insurance companies to find what rates would be possible if 80% of the insured properties could be underwritten by one company. 22

About 15% of the residents do not insure at all. When the reports were received from various companies a community meeting was called and the people decided with which company they wished to insure.

The Halifax Insurance Company was selected, and this company agreed to appoint as its agent for the Mennonite people in Yarrow, the Schulz or chairman of the group. The chairman has been authorized to assess the property and agrees to base the rates on his assessment. The insurance rate is $1.00 per hundred dollars of value for a three year period. This is one-third less than the price that is ordinarily paid to this company. This arrangement is an advantage both to the 

Yarrow residents and to the insurance company. The company claims that the Mennonites are better risks, because they would never set fire to their own buidings.

Health Society The Bethesda Mennonite Health Society was organized in 

1935 under the care of the Mennonite Provincial Committee. 

Dr. H. W. Epp, a Mennonite from Manitoba, came and established an office in the town of Sardis. The group health plan called for an annual assessment of $4.00 a year for a single person, $8.00 for a family without children and $10.00 per year for family regardless of the number of children. An unlimited number of calls is permitted under this contract. A flat charge of $5.00 was formerly made for small operations but the present rate is $10.00 and the annual fee has recently been raised to $12.00 a year per family. 

The churches, according to their size, buy one or two tickets in the health society. This money is taken from the deacon's fund and the membership cards used for poor people who are unable to pay for their own medical attention. The present membership in the Health Society is 250.

Cooperative Activity 

The most successful economic effort in the community is the Yarrow Cooperative Association. It had its beginning in 23

with five or six members who organized to provide themselves with groceries, feed and other equipment by group effort. A charter was taken out in 1937. 

In 1937 also, a Yarrow growers' cooperative union was organized by ten or fifteen farmers to market the fruit that was being produced locally. In 1943 these two organizations, which really had duplicating personnel, were merged into a single cooperative consumers' producing and marketing agency. At the present time the cooperative has about 300 members. Near the store is a berry packing and preserving plant. The cooperative organization collects the berries from the homes of the growers, brings them to the cleaning and packing plant at Yarrow, crates and ships them either as fresh berries or packed in barrels for preserving. 

Many of the berries are sold directly to England. By thus performing their own marketing service, these Mennonite fruit growers have been able to eliminate all middlemen's costs except a very nominal handling charge of about two cents per pound. During 1943 the growers were getting about 17c per pound gross for their berries and about 14½c to 15c net. 

The value of the cooperative business for 1943 was about $300,000. Out of the 300 families who are members only about twelve are non-Mennonites, although membership is open to anyone.

Besides providing cash income from the products and a low price for consumers' goods, this cooperative activity also provides employment for from ten to sixteen persons. Future plans call for the formation of a credit union, feed mixing and grinding mill, a cooperative egg grading and marketing plant, a creamery and cheese factory and a jam factory.

Without exception all of the members of the community are highly enthusiastic about this cooperative arrangement which has been working very much to the benefit of all participating. 

Raspberries, strawberries, boysenberries, currants, grapes, prunes, plums and apples are the chief fruits produced. Raspberries are by far the most important of the fruits grown. The 24

proximity to Vancouver, a market and seaport city, is of great economic significance to the Yarrow community. 

Private Industry 

An interesting development in Yarrow is the box factory which started in the garage of Henry Voth. He came from 

Brandon, Manitoba, in 1923 where he had worked in a sash, door and body shop for a number of years. Mr. Voth built several houses in Yarrow on contract during the first year of his residence, and in this work found it necessary to have a little shop in his garage. As the berry business began to grow there was need for berry boxes and he decided to meet this local demand.

The business grew rapidly and he was soon forced to build an addition to his garage. In 1938 he built a 30 x 40 foot building, and the next year an addition to this building was required. In 1939 he made 20,000 boxes and in 1943 his production was 60,000 boxes. Mr. Voth was thrifty and ingenious in being able to buy used machinery and fix it up for his own use.

He has about $10,000 invested in fixed capital at the present time and he has 15 steady employees. According to his present plans he expects to build a large new building to carry on his growing business. He is manufacturing practically all of the berry boxes for the Fraser valley at the present time. It should be said that he is perhaps benefiting from the anti-Japanese policy which the Canadian government has been carrying out in removing all the Japanese from the Fraser valley. This is entirely beyond Mr. Voth's control. This very profitable private industry is bringing money into Yarrow and providing employment for a number of local residents. There are a number of local businesses; two lumber yards, two bicycle shops, one meat market, one electric service and supply store, one general store, a service station, a feed mill, elevator and mixing station, a nursery, three barber shops and a shoemaker. This all illustrates the pioneering stage of a growing community. 25

SARDIS 

Background The community of Sardis, which is located about three miles across the Vadder River from Yarrow and about eight miles by road, started in 1930 as a result of an offer of 765 acres of land by the Northern Construction Company. 

The land was offered at $115 an acre. Three men, Aaron 

Rempel, J. D. Jantzen, and Jacob Krause, had an option on the land. They acted as an advisory committee. The Construction Company accepted their recommendation for any 

Mennonite buyers in place of a cash down payment.

The tract was divided and each plot valued by these three appraisers. The poor land was marked down and the good land marked up from the average price of $115 by the advisory committee which acted as an agent for the Northern Construction Company. The committee considered applications submitted by prospective settlers. Many could not afford the down payment of $10.00 per acre and this requirement was not strictly observed. The settlers were to have fifteen years to pay. 

The company was lenient with those who found they could not meet their regular payments with interest at 7%. Because of the severe depression the land owners in 1938 were persuaded to reduce the price of the land 15% below the original price and reduce the interest rate from 7% to 6% and extend the payment over twenty years instead of the original fifteen. 

Many of the original 28 lots were again subdivided so that the total number now is about 70 plots. The Northern 

Construction Company was quite well pleased with the Mennonite settlers as there had been no delinquents in taxes and payments in principal. The original agreement called for the full payment of the entire amount of all purchases before deeds would be granted to any individuals. This discouraged payment on the part of some individuals for fear of being denied their land in the end in case too many others could not 26

or would not pay. In 1938 this arrangement, too, was cancelled and each owner was made responsible for his own application. 

Some of those who had held back paid their amounts immediately.

The Northern Construction Company realized a very handsome profit as a result of the sale of this land to the hard-pressed Mennonite settlers. This company had acquired the 765 acres of land by paying delinquent taxes. The land for years had been a "white elephant" on the company's hands. When they found the Mennonites, who paid them on the average about $100 an acre, they realized over $75,000 plus an interest rate of 6% or 7% on the unpaid balance. The company had been renting the whole ranch for about $1,000 a year previous to this land sale. 

Mr. Rempel, one of the Mennonite commissioners, believed that if someone had offered the company $50 an acre for the whole ranch, they would have been happy to sell it because this was the top price for land in surrounding communities at that time. The Mennonites were willing to pay more because they wanted to have their own community where they could settle near each other and establish their own churches and schools. Since they had no money they were in no position to bargain. It is very likely that this high price was asked for the land in anticipation that a number of the settlers would default. However, not one of the purchasers has done so. The company can well afford to be pleased with the arrangement. 

There were no heavy administrative costs and not a single court case resulted from the sale.

There are two churches in the community of Sardis, a 

Mennonite Brethren church with 185 members plus about 250 children, and a General Conference church with 130 members and approximately 200 children. The Mennonite Brethren church erected a fine new building in 1938 and the General Conference church added to its building about the same time. 27

Cooperative Activity 

The community of Sardis is not as cohesively organized as is that of Yarrow, partly because the people are not settled so close to each other.

The Sardis Agricultural Association is a cooperative activity with an interesting history. It was started in 1937 as a partnership or buying club to provide goods and services to the needy families at the lowest possible rate. The settlers were too poor to pay for the goods they needed at the private stores and they discovered that by accepting credit they went still deeper in debt. From this small beginning developed the present cooperative store which now employs a man full time and has its own building erected by the members. 

Plans are under way to organize a berry growers' cooperative; poultry and egg cooperative; feed, fuel and grain cooperatives and other agencies needed to serve the community. 

Mr. Aaron Rempel, one of the leaders of this community, made the following statement: "The use of cooperating techniques in every line in starting a Mennonite settlement is 50% guarantee of its success." He also commented that it is not money that is necessary for new settlements but absolute honesty and perseverance.

It is significant to note that the Mennonite community of Sardis is several miles removed from the non-Mennonite town of Sardis where the post office and business center of the old village are found. The Mennonites are really forming a separate community. At the present time there are approximately 250 Mennonite families and 1,000 individuals. Most of these families came from the prairie provinces. 

The future of this community from an economic and social standpoint looks promising because the same general factors hold true for Sardis as for Yarrow. The community spirit seems to be excellent in both places. One senses a cooperative attitude and harmonious relations between the two church bodies. In fact, on the matter of collecting the transportation debt the two groups worked together harmoniously for a number of years. 28

ABBOTSFORD 

The third Mennonite community visited in British

Columbia was Abbotsford, about twenty miles southeast of Sardis. The first Mennonites settled here in January of 1931. 

Ten families came during the year, seeking the cheap land that was available.

Land could be bought here in 1931 for as low as $7.00 an acre. It was cut-over timber and bush land and it required considerable energy and expense in clearing before it became usable for farming. 

There are two Mennonite Brethren churches here; the

North Church and the South Church. There is also a General 

Conference church to the west of the town of Abbotsford. All told there are approximately 200 Mennonite families in the 

Abbotsford area or about 1,000 souls. A considerable number in the community, possibly 25%, are not members of any church. Some who were formerly Mennonites joined such. groups as the Seventh Day Adventists, Pentecostal, Holy 

Rollers, United Church and others.

There is a cooperative known as the South Poplar Cooperative Association, organized in 1939. It began in the private home of an interested cooperator. It now has about sixty members and new people are joining gradually. It has about $1,500 invested in a stock of consumer goods and all dividends remain to build up additional capital. The cooperative association bought a house and an acre of land for $400 in the center of the district in the open country which is the present headquarters.

This community, in contrast to Yarrow, is not densely populated and the land is more difficult to clear. Some think it is not as productive. My own feeling is that the future of this community is not as promising as is the future of a community like Yarrow. A threat to a portion of the Abbotsford settlers is the large, new and expanding airport to the west. The 

Mennonite farmers in the area told me of rumors that they would have to sell their land, which they had struggled hard to improve, to the government for the expanding airport. 29

COGHLAN 

The community of Coghlan is one of the newest and smallest of the Mennonite colonies in the Fraser Valley. It was settled in 1934 by Mennonites who came from Sardis and 

Abbotsford in search of cheaper land.

The first families could not afford to start dairy farming as most of the Mennonites in the Sardis community had done. A 

160 acre farm at Coghlan was purchased for $5.00 an acre and divided into eight lots ranging from ten to forty acres each. At the present time there are about thirty Mennonite families in the community. The local church has a membership of 77 and there are approximately 65 adult Mennonites who live in the area but who do not belong to any church. 

This congregation belongs to the General Conference. All these people are making a living on their own farms by growing strawberries, raspberries, and raising chickens for sale. 

Most of them, of course, raise cows and chickens and grow their own vegetables for family food. It is possible to make a fair living at the present time on the land after it is cleared, but it is very difficult to make one's living while clearing the land and getting it ready for production.

This settlement is still too small and too new to predict what its future will be. However, I observed that there is no cooperative economic activity whatsoever in this community. 

The church is the main center of community life and activity. 

CONCLUSIONS 

There is no single cause of success in Mennonite settlement, but here are listed the most important factors contributing to it over a long period of time and in a majority of cases. 

1. The first factor is religion. It is because of the sustaining power of religion that Mennonites have endured persecution and suffered privations and economic hardships. Religious conviction enables them to make the great sacrifices which generally accompany the early stages of colonization. 30

Religion acts as a solvent for personal and group conflict which is bound to arise in all human societies. An important characteristic of the Christian religion is its capacity to solve conflict situations through the exercise of love and forgiveness. Differences of opinion, jealousies and excessive personal ambition are often held in restrain or completely brought under control by means of a commitment of one's whole life to God and to the welfare of the brotherhood. (Editor's Note-The Mennonite home is the quiet, but radiant focal point of religion. It is here that the Mennonite way of life is demonstrated and confirmed.) 

2. A second cause of success in Mennonite colonization is the practice of Christian mutual aid. This is one of the fruits of religion and it is a demonstration of Christian love in everyday life. Mutual aid was practiced between various groups of Mennonites and within single groups. Sometimes whole villages migrated and in such cases the poor families were always taken along at the expense of the whole group. Christian mutual aid has been a major factor in successful Mennonite colonization, not merely in social and religious matters but in the economic and business activities of the local community as well. One may almost make the generalization that Mennonite colonies have prospered economically and spiritually in direct proportion to the degree to which they have practiced 

Christian mutual aid.

In the various settlements of recent Mennonites in Canada one finds co-operative creameries, cheese factories, stores, credit unions, marketing associations, producers' societies, hospitals, burial societies, and health associations.

All of these activities are carried on as community organizations. They are owned by the people who use them; profits are shared by the community and losses are borne by the community. This means that individuals have an opportunity to work together during the week as well as pray and worship together on the Sabbath. 

3. A third factor contributing to success is the willingness to work hard and to live simply. Industry and frugality helped 31

make possible the transformation of the barren plains of Western Canada into productive agricultural lands. After ten to fifteen years of hard work, these colonists have productive farms, large new church buildings and substantial houses. 

Mennonite colonists have believed in and demonstrated the dignity of labor. 

4. A fourth important factor is the location of the settlement with regard to geographic and economic conditions. The most successful colonies have the largest possible combination of these favorable economic and geographic characteristics. 

No matter, in the long run, how great the religious spirit or how strong the practice of mutual aid or how unstinting the human labor, if the soil is poor, the rainfall insufficient, the climatic conditions unfavorable and the markets inaccessible, a colony cannot succeed economically nor spiritually. 

5. A further factor in determining successful Mennonite colonization efforts is the matter of adequate financial credit. 

The colonies which have succeeded best are those which had some financial capital in addition to the social and spiritual assets mentioned above. Oftentimes settlements succeeded in spite of financial deficiencies but the struggle was long and hard and perhaps unnecessarily difficult. 

6. Another factor in successful settlement is that of adequate leadership. Two different groups of people may settle in approximately the same areas and be subject to the same general economic and social conditions and yet one group may fail where another succeeds. The quality of leadership found in each community largely accounts for such differences. In the successful community the leaders have the necessary vision as well as the loyalty of the members of the group. (Editor's Note-Another element in the strength and stability of the Mennonites is the fact that they have matched their education to their culture. It is no accident that Mennonites can and do cooperate. Their training and education are in that direction. They have been very cautious about adopting any educational system that tends to destroy the Mennonite culture.) 32
