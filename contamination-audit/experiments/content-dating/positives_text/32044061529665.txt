A. Developments in International Trade 

Continuing economic expansion, particularly strong in the industrialized countries, pushed free-world trade to record levels in 1969. Exports rose to around $245 billion for a 14 percent gain, the highest growth rate in the decade and second only to the 1951 increase over the past 20 years. Prices went up by 3-4 percent, however, after declining slightly the previous year, so that the total quantity of goods exchanged rose less rapidly in 1969 than in 1968. I. 

Developed Countries The developed countries continued to be responsible for the bulk of the expansion in free-world trade. With exports increasing by 15 percent to $196 billion, these nations accounted for 87 percent of the dollar increase as their share of the total edged past the four-fifths mark. Price rises of around 3 percent helped sustain the advance.

WORLD TRADE IN 1969 

On the strength of above-average growth in both the European Economic Community (EEC) and the European Free Trade Association (EFTA), exports from Western Europe advanced by 17 percent to $120 billion to account for nearly half of the free-world total in 1969. With intra-Common Market trade the main spur, shipments from the EEC reached $76 billion. Each of the member nations registered substantial gains. 

Belgium-Luxembourg led the group with a 23 percent increase, but the German gain of $4.2 billion was the largest not only among the Six but also in the free world. EFTA deliveries went up by $5 billion to $38.3 billion, although the rate of expansion was slightly lower than the EEC advance. The United Kingdom provided over two-fifths of the increase as British shipments climbed sharply, especially to non-EFTA countries. 

Imports into Western Europe grew at about the same rate as exports, rising to $130 billion. The sharp surge in EEC purchases accounted for more than 70 percent of the $19 billion gain. The West German and French markets were especially buoyant expanding by nearly one-fourth in each case. The more moderate advance in EFTA imports reflected the slowdown in UK purchases from foreign sources, especially Western Europe and the United States, as British efforts to dampen demand held arrivals to less than 6 percent.

In Canada, strikes in major mines and plants reduced shipments of iron ore, copper, nickel, and steel by more than a quarter of a billion dollars and limited the rise in overall exports to 9 percent for a gain of $1.2 billion. Meanwhile, Canadian imports grew by 15 percent, moderately faster than the free-world rate. 

For the second consecutive year, Japan recorded the steepest export growth rate among the major industrialized countries. Buoyed by stepped-up sales of iron and steel, especially to the EEC, and expanded deliveries of machinery, motor vehicles and rice, Japanese exports rose by nearly one-fourth to $16 billion. Imports, on the other hand, advanced at a considerably slower pace, increasing by $2 billion to $15 billion.

Australia and New Zealand each recorded export gains of slightly under one-fifth. 

Larger shipments of metals and meat contributed to the Australian export advance, while meat, particularly lamb, also accounted for a major portion of the New Zealand gain. 

Import growth in Australia, where arrivals of defense equipment dropped sharply, fell substantially below the free-world rate. Purchases by New Zealand advanced significantly. In South Africa, exports went up only marginally after several years of substantial growth, but imports rose by 14 percent as they rebounded from a decline the previous year. 

Developing Countries Although exports from the developing countries of the free world reached an all-time high of over $47 billion in 1969, the rate of expansion was considerably weaker than that recorded by the more economically advanced nations. 1

The increase was nevertheless substantial for the second year in a row. The inflow of goods climbed more slowly reaching about $48 billion, or a fifth of the free-world total. 

Shipments from South and East Asia expanded by nearly one-eighth to about $12.5 billion for the year, based on January-September data. Exports from Hong Kong cont ed their sharp upward trend with an advance of one-fourth to $2.2 billion, while Malaysian sales grew almost as rapidly to reach $1.7 billion. Trade from 

Singapore also advanced significantly, although a sizable portion of the increase may be attributable to transshipments. 

South and East Asia remained the largest and most vigorous of the developing import markets. The region's purchases rose by one-tenth to over $17 billion. The most pronounced gain was in Hong Kong where arrivals advanced by nearly one-fifth. 

Imports into Taiwan, Korea, and Singapore, all smaller markets, were even more expansive. In contrast, India sharply cut back its overseas purchases, largely due to continued stress on import substitution, a slow recovery in demand in many industries, and a good crop which reduced requirements for foreign foodgrains. 

Exports from the 19 Latin American Republics grew by about 8 percent, considerably faster than in 1968, to reach some $12.5 billion. Both Brazil and Argentina boosted deliveries by nearly one-fifth, while Mexico posted a less rapid rate of gain. 

Brazilian sales of cocoa beans and raw cotton were especially strong, but shipments of manufactures and iron ore were also up by more than a third. Argentine exports benefited from good crops and rising grain prices.

Imports by the American Republics advanced moderately to more than $12 billion. 

An upsurge in purchases by Argentina accounted for a large portion of the advance and stemmed from the demands of a generally expansive economy along with inventory accumulation as a hedge against possible devaluation or imposition of exchange controls. 

Brazil and Mexico, on the other hand, reported much more modest growth rates. 

Data for the first nine months of 1969 indicate that African exports rose less vigorously than in 1968, although probably exceeding $10 billion for the year. After declining for two successive years, Nigerian exports climbed by more than half. The normalization of the transport system and the resumption of agriculture, both disrupted by the civil war, were responsible for the recovery. Libyan deliveries went up by 16 percent to $2.2 billion for a solid but far less dramatic gain than in 1968. 

On the basis of statistics for the first half of the year only, Africa as a region boosted imports moderately to the neighborhood of $8.5 billion for the year. Algeria accounted for a third of the advance. Nigeria also stepped up imports sharply, but 

Libya and Morocco recorded only minor increases. Arrivals into Zambia were down significantly.

Predominantly on the strength of a large increase in Iranian shipments, exports from the Near East expanded to about $10 billion. Deliveries from Israel and Iraq grew only modestly, however, and Saudi Arabia showed little change from 1968. 

As a group, the Near Eastern countries recorded comparatively small import growth to around $7 billion, despite a boost of more than one-fifth in Israeli purchases abroad. Iraqi imports increased moderately, but Iran's leveled off. 

B. U.S. Foreign Trade 

The U.S. foreign trade performance in 1969 was characterized by an above average increase in exports and a growth in imports below the average rate of recent years. 

With shipments expanding faster than purchases of foreign products, our merchandise

trade surplus rose to $1,262 million from the nine-year low of $837 million recorded the year before. 1/ Exports, excluding military grant-aid but including other government-financed shipments, climbed to $37.3 billion, an increase of 9.5 percent over 1968. This rate of gain was roughly the same as in the preceding year but well above the 7.1 percent average annual advance achieved during the 1960-68 period. Imports rose to $36.0 billion, but the slowdown in U.S. economic activity and the absence of strikehedge buying of certain commodities reduced the rate of increase to 8.5 percent from 23.6 percent the year before. For 1960-68, the average rate of growth amounted to 10.4 percent in arrivals of foreign products. 

Commodity Exports 

The 10 percent expansion in U.S. exports in 1969 stemmed entirely from sharply higher shipments of nonagricultural products. Machinery exports were particularly buoyant. As a result of booming steel demand abroad, unusually large increases occurred in sales of iron and steel-mill products and associated materials such as coal, iron and steel scrap, and certain alloy metals. Exports of transport equipment and chemicals, both of which had been large gainers in 1968, increased at a much more subdued rate in 1969. Large stocks of farm products abroad and sales losses attributable to the dock strike caused a drop in exports of agricultural products for the third year in a row. 

Commodity Imports All of the 9 percent increases in U.S. imports resulted from larger receipts of consumer and capital goods. However, arrivals of consumer goods did not advance as rapidly as in 1968 primarily because of a slowdown in imports of automobiles and parts. Entries of foreign capital equipment recorded a strong advance which stemmed largely from accelerated deliveries of machinery. Purchases of industrial materials showed no change, as sharp declines in copper and steel receipts offset increases in some other commodities. Food and beverage imports dipped slightly due to reduced coffee arrivals. 

In 1969, the ratio of imports to the gross national product was 3.9 percent, a slight increase over the 3.8 percent recorded in the previous year. This ratio averaged 2.9 percent in the 1960-68 period.

Area Trade U.S. trade with Canada, our largest trading partner, continued to advance strongly. Exports rose by 13 percent, the same rate as in 1968. Although imports were less buoyant, a substantial 15 percent increase was recorded. Trade with 

Japan again climbed sharply, with exports doubling their rate of growth. Smaller increases were noted in purchases from that country, however. Shipments to Western 

Europe posted a sizable advance, largely as a result of much improved markets in West Germany. However, imports from Western Europe showed no change after a rapid advance in 1968.

Trade with developing areas moved to higher levels, but the rate of increase was smaller than in 1968. Exports to these countries were led by gains in shipments to the American Republics and the Near East. Deliveries to East and South Asia declined slightly. A steep rise in imports from East and South Asia contributed almost all of the advance in the inflow of goods from the developing nations.

The boom in foreign steel demand stimulated exports of complementary materials as well. Shipments of coal and iron and steel scrap in particular recorded large gains. Large gains were also recorded in sales of certain additive materials, such as molybdenum ores, used in the production of steel alloys. 

The marked fall off in growth of transport equipment exports reflected the decline in sales of commercial aircraft and the more modest expansion in shipments of automotive products to Canada. The drop in aircraft exports was expected as airlines awaited the introduction of the large jumbo jets. However, deliveries of military aircraft continued to advance.

The decline in shipments of fertilizers and insecticides to Asia and Latin America played an important role in reducing the rate of advance in exports of chemicals to 3 percent from 17 percent in 1968. Additional factors were the speed up of deliveries in late 1968 which curtailed the following year's totals, and some slackening of demand in Western Europe. The latter development had a moderating effect chiefly on exports of organic and inorganic chemicals and plastic materials. 

Continuing a downward trend that began in 1967, U.S. agricultural exports dropped by 5 percent to $6 billion. Considerable improvement, however, occurred in the final half when farm shipments were running at an annual rate of $6.5 billion. In addition to reduced world demand resulting from bigger crops in both exporting and importing areas, U.S. farm exports in 1969 were severely retarded by the dock strike. Wheat exports fell by more than $250 million, reflecting reduced loadings for India, Pakistan, and Brazil. Greater foreign supplies and a drop in the quantities available for export depressed shipments of cotton. Large declines were also noted in deliveries of grain sorghums.

Consumer Goods Again Pace Imports 

For the fourth consecutive year, arrivals of consumer goods contributed most to the overall import advance. Stimulated by another sizable rise in personal consumption expenditures, these purchases climbed to $11.1 billion. This was an increase of 

22 percent over 1968 when a much larger, 40 percent, advance was recorded. Imported consumer goods, excluding American model automobiles from Canada, accounted for 4.3 percent of personal consumption expenditures for goods. By contrast, this ratio stood at 3.9 percent in 1968 and averaged 2.9 percent in the period 1960-68.

The more moderate 1969 growth rate for imports of consumer goods reflected the slower pace of spending by U.S. consumers. Imports of durables, which account for nearly three-fourths of total U.S. purchases of foreign consumer products, advanced much less rapidly than the year before. 

Following an increase of nearly two-thirds in 1968, imports of foreign made autos climbed by only 7 percent in value last year. The unexpected decline in arrivals from 

Germany attributed by industry sources to delivery problems during the longshoremen's strike was the principal restraining factor. On the other hand, Japanese manufacturers continued to make inroads into the U.S. auto market. -1 

Sales of imported cars, excluding those from Canada, accounted for 10.9 percent of total automobile sales in this country last year. This ratio has increased steadily since 1961.

Large increases were again recorded in imports of clothing and footwear. Clothing purchases rose to $1.1 billion, an advance of 29 percent. Greater receipts from Japan and the developing countries of East Asia Hong Kong, Taiwan, and Korea for most of the rise. accounted

Spurred by the sharply stepped up rate of investment spending, imports of capital equipment climbed strongly to $4.1 billion. This 23 percent expansion, the same rate as that recorded in 1968, was the result of accelerated arrivals of machinery and the continued heavy inflow of trucks from Canada. The ratio of capital goods imports (excluding Canadian trucks) to U.S. outlays for producers durable equipment climbed to 5.1 percent from 4.7 percent in 1968 and 2.0 percent at the start of the decade.

Two-thirds of the $749 million advance in imports of foreign capital equipment consisted of greater receipts of machinery. In the forefront of this expansion were sizable increases in arrivals of office machines and parts, materials handling equipment, telecommunications apparatus, and electronic components. The value of civilian aircraft purchases was 5 percent below the 1968 level which, in turn, was inflated by the return of used commercial jetcraft from Canada. 

In contrast to the one-fifth increase recorded in 1968, imports of industrial supplies held level at $14.2 billion. By far the most important factor contributing to this development was the sharp decline in purchases of copper and iron and steelmill products. Copper imports dropped by nearly a half from abnormally high levels in the previous year when receipts of refined copper were swelled by a long strike in the industry. At $487 million, imports of copper were the lowest in four years. 

After a steep climb in 1968, induced by strike anticipatory stockbuilding, arrivals of steel fell by $238 million. Two special circumstances were important in

this regard. On the one hand, suppliers in the EEC and Japan agreed in late 1968 to limit their 1969 shipments to the United States to 5.75 million tons each. On the other hand, extremely heavy steel demand on the Continent limited export availabilities in the EEC. 

In 1969, imports of steel accounted for 13.7 percent of U.S. apparent consumption, down from 16.9 percent the year before. However, the ratio was higher than that recorded in 1967.

Imports of industrial and agricultural chemicals increased by only 3 percent following a one-fifth jump in the previous year. The fall off in the import pace reflected the slowdown in U.S. chemical production and weaker demand for crude fertilizers.

Advancing for the first time since 1966, imports of newsprint rose by 9 percent. 

A substantial step-up in U.S. consumption, related largely to increased advertising linage, provided the main stimulus to these imports. Crude petroleum and product imports totaled $2.6 billion, 9 percent above 1968 levels. 

The large $246 million drop in coffee arrivals held imports of foods and beverages close to 1968 levels, offsetting increased purchases of meat, fish, and cocoa beans. 

As a result of expanded purchases in late 1968 to avoid shipping delays in the event of a dock strike, coffee stocks had been built up to unusually high levels. To reduce inventories, U.S. importers cut back purchases in 1969.

The import bill for foods and beverages accounted for 4.4 percent of such U.S. expenditures. This ratio was less than the record 1968 level but exceeded those of other years of the decade.

Area Trade Developments 

Canada U.S. exports to Canada totaled $9.1 billion, a 13 percent rise over the 

1968 value. Although economic activity in that country slowed considerably in the second half, our exports benefited from high levels earlier in the year and in late 1968. Investment spending in Canada was particularly buoyant in 1969 after a downturn the preceding year, providing the major impetus for a strong acceleration in our shipments of machinery. Continued increases in industrial production north of the border led to larger deliveries of steel and chemicals. 

Shipments of automotive products rose by 15 percent, but the advance was substantially more moderate than in 1968. Most of the increase consisted of parts and accèssories for assembly at Canadian plants, where auto production increased strongly. A sharp rise was recorded in exports of agricultural products, particularly corn and soybeans.

Although U.S. imports from Canada rose by 15 percent to a total of $10.4 billion, the rate of increase was considerably below the 1968 advance of about one-fourth. 

This slowdown resulted from a decline in imports of metals and a smaller rate of advance in arrivals of automotive products. The large drop in the inflow of iron ore was attributed to strikes at Canadian facilities. Increased U.S. requirements for most other industrial materials and for machinery contributed importantly to the advance.

A. 

The Proposed "Trade Act of 1969" 

In November 1969, a proposed trade bill was submitted by the Administration to the Congress. Its major features provided: 

II. 

B.

MAJOR DEVELOPMENTS IN U.S. TRADE POLICY 

authority for the President to reduce tariffs by limited amounts, notably as compensation for future escape clause actions or statutory changes in tariff classifications, when failure to provide such compensation would expose the United States to retaliation by our trading partners. liberalization of the provisions governing aid to industries, firms and workers suffering from import growth. The test for escape clause relief would be modified to require a showing that increased imports were the primary cause of actual or threatened serious injury to an industry. Individual firms and groups of workers would be eligible for adjustment assistance when increased imports were found to be a substantial cause of actual or threatened serious injury. In both types of case, it would no longer be necessary for the petitioner to show a causal link between increased imports and trade agreement concessions. elimination of the American Selling Price (ASP) system of customs valuation, under which the value of imports for tariff purposes is set by the price of competitive American products rather than the actual price of the foreign product, which is the basis of valuation for most products. Under an agreement negotiated during the Kennedy 

Round, removal of ASP would bring not only reciprocal cuts in foreign duties on U.S. chemical exports, but also the reduction of several nontariff barriers significantly hampering U.S. trade. expansion of the President's authority to impose duties or other import restrictions on the products of any nation that places unjustifiable restrictions on American exports of agricultural products so that the authority granted in 1962 would henceforth comprehend exports of all types of products. New authority would also permit the President to take appropriate action against nations that give subsidies or other such incentives to their exporters, resulting in unfair competition against American trade in third country markets. authorization of annual appropriations to cover the U.S.' share of the expenses under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT). 

The President's Message accompanying the proposed bill underlined the importance he placed on initiating serious and sustained efforts to obtain reciprocal lowering of nontariff barriers and indicated that a clear statement of Congressional intent would be welcome. Close consultation with Congress was pledged and the stated purpose of requesting such an advance declaration was to strengthen the American position in future negotiations.

No hearings were held on the proposed trade legislation during 1969. 

Presidential Commission to Examine Trade and Related Policies 

The trade legislation proposed by the Administration in 1969 contained only the essential first steps in formulating a program geared to a new decade. To assist in the development of a comprehensive, long term program taking into account the farreaching economic changes underway both at home and abroad, Congress was informed in

the President's Message on the trade bill that a Commission would be established to examine the entire range of U.S. trade and related policies, and to prepare recommendations to meet the problems and opportunities of the 1970's. 

C. Nontariff Barriers 

In addition to participating in the GATT examination of nontariff barriers, the United States continued its bilateral efforts to obtain removal of such measures adversely affecting U.S. exports. 

Japan The United States made a particular effort to achieve reciprocity in the U.S.-Japanese trade situation, which is an important aspect of the bilateral relationship between the two countries. Two-way trade between the United States and Japan about $8.4 billion in 1969 is the largest overseas trade ever conducted by any two nations. 

Recognizing that other nontariff barriers could be used to vitiate any liberalization of import quotas, the most visible, of Japan's import restrictions, the United States also sought elimination or relaxation of such barriers as: a) the Automatic 

Import Quota system,under which import licenses are required for a number of products not subject to import quotas; b) the prior import deposit requirement; c) the "standard method of settlement", which requires that the Ministry of International Trade and Industry approve extended time payments for imports; and d) restrictions on foreign investment. 

The United States played an active role in a series of formal consultations aimed at accelerating across-the-board liberalization of Japan's remaining quantitative restrictions which are inconsistent with the GATT. Japanese trade liberalization was also discussed in four high-level bilateral meetings during 1969 and was the subject of numerous discussions between U.S. and Japanese officials.

Preferences for Developing Countries 

In October, the President announced that the United States would press in world trade forums for the establishment of a liberal system of generalized tariff preferences for all developing countries. In submitting its proposal to the OECD and to 

UNCTAD, the United States made clear that the extension of preferences would require Congressional action. The United States proposed to reduce duties on imports from developing countries to zero on manufactured and semimanufactured products (excepting only textiles, shoes, and petroleum and petroleum products) and on a selective list of agricultural products. The traditional escape clause and adjustment assistance would provide safeguards for domestic industries and workers. 

The proposal was designed to meet one of the world's major economic and political problems the struggle of the developing countries to achieve a satisfactory rate of economic growth and to meet their need to increase and diversify their export earnings in order to finance the imports required for development. It would also go a long way toward eliminating the discrimination arising from existing special preferential trading arrangements such as those between the European Community and its associated states in Africa and between the United Kingdom and developing countries of the Commonwealth. 

The additional earnings must come largely from exports of manufactures since the demand for most primary products grows slowly. Generalized tariff preferences would make the manufactured goods of developing countries more competitive with those of industrialized countries on world markets. 

Discussions with other prospective donor countries as well as the potential beneficiary countries were still in progress at the end of the year in an effort to reach an understanding on the principal outstanding issues. While these discussions had not reached the stage where it was possible to frame legislative proposals, Administration officials were in touch with key members of Congress to report on developments. 11

4. Regional Arrangements Among the Developing Countries 

Little progress was made during 1969 toward the broad goal of forming a Latin American Common Market (LACM), but studies were continuing, preparatory to a future merger of the Joint Latin American Free Trade Association (LAFTA) and the Central 

American Common Market (CACM). 

At the Ninth Ordinary LAFTA Conference held in Caracas in late 1969, the eleven LAFTA members significantly amended the Treaty of Montevideo by adopting the Protocol of Caracas. The basic feature of this Protocol, which must be ratified unanimously before it will enter into effect, is to postpone the terminal date for the completion of the free trade area from 1973 to 1980. In addition, the Protocol provides for studies of measures that could be taken to harmonize economic policies leading to an eventual formation of a Latin American Common Market, and the countries agreed to review these studies in 1974 and to act upon them. The hope is that these studies will lead to measures which will preserve what has been achieved and regain the momentum toward LAFTA's long-range goals.

B. Related Aspects of International Monetary Policy 

The continued growth of international trade requires a well-functioning international monetary system. This involves sufficient liquidity to finance short term imbalances and secondly, exchange rates of major currencies must be stable enough to maintain traders' confidence without being so rigid as to prevent the necessary adjustment to changing international trade patterns and terms of trade. 

The system established under the International Monetary Fund as part of the Bretton Woods Agreements after World War II has served remarkably well in helping to underpin the unprecedented postwar growth of international transactions. However, during 1969 there was some concern that the system may have become rather rigid, and the IMF examined the desirability of introducing limited flexibility for exchange 14

rate change. Such examination was not designed to alter the present system radically, but rather to see whether what has worked well might be made to function even better in terms of the international balance-of-payments process. 

A major step toward providing a new source of liquidity was completed early in 1969 with the coming into force of Special Drawing Rights (SDRs). Member countries of the IMF now jointly determine the need for and amount of additional reserve assets (SDRs) and distribute them in accordance with each country's quota in the IMF. It was agreed to create about $9.5 billion of SDRS over the three-year period 1970-72. 

The functioning of the international monetary system during 1969 was also assisted by the successful operation of the two-tier gold system introduced in 1968, designed to separate the private gold market from monetary gold. This system has conserved monetary gold stocks and made speculation in gold considerably less attractive. the end of 1969, the free market price of gold was very close to the official price of $35 an ounce.

By The year also saw constructive parity changes of major currencies which brought the pattern of exchange rates more closely in line with international competitive positions. Devaluation of the French franc in August and revaluation of the German deutschemark in November contributed to the smoother functioning of the international monetary system, and were a significant step toward bringing the merchandise accounts of these countries into better adjustment with the requirements of the world trading system. 15

The GATT Council THE GENERAL AGREEMENT ON TARIFFS AND TRADE (GATT) 

The Council of Representatives acts as the executive body of the GATT between sessions of the Contracting Parties and meets periodically to consider matters notified by individual contracting parties, to take action on the reports of GATT committees, working parties and other bodies, and to guide the work of the secretariat. Fifty countries and the Commission of the European Communities were members of the Council in 1969. 

For some matters, action is taken directly by the Council (e.g. reports on waivers), but in most cases the Council carries out its responsibilities by establishing subgroups whose work is thereafter reviewed and decisions made on appropriate further steps. Many matters formerly reserved for detailed consideration by the Contracting 

Parties were treated by the Council in 1969 and are included in the activities reported in the following sections. While the Contracting Parties have normally met each year, the 26th Session was postponed until February 1970 to allow the necessary time for key subgroups to complete reports on their progress. 

B. The Program on Expansion of International Trade 

1. 

Work continued during 1969 on the program adopted by the GATT Contracting Parties to consolidate the gains of the Kennedy Round of trade negotiations and to lay the groundwork for further progress in trade liberalization and expansion. The activities of the two principal committees carrying out this mandate under the supervision of the 

Council are summarized below.

Industrial Products 

The Committee on Trade in Industrial Products (CTIP), in a series of meetings starting in December 1968, completed the first factual examination of some 800 measures that countries had notified as constituting nontariff barriers to trade in industrial products. These measures applied by various GATT contracting parties, including the 

United States, had been notified by one or more other countries as having a restrictive effect on their own trade.

By the fall of 1969 the CTIP had gathered substantial information on how the various measures were applied and a general indication of their trade effects. At that time the Committee took stock of its work and turned to consideration of how to organize the next phase of its activities.

The Committee concluded that the purely fact-finding and confrontation stage of its work was completed and that it should look toward solutions of the problems reflected in the nontariff notifications. For this purpose five working groups were established to consider the problems arising from: 1) government participation in trade; 2) customs and administrative entry procedures; 3) standards; 4) specific limitations on trade (quantitative restrictions and licensing); and 5) charges on imports (taxes, surcharges, import deposits). This exploration of possible solutions was not to involve any commitment at the time by participating countries to take any of the actions discussed.

The Committee suggested that when these working groups started functioning in 1970 they might consider dealing with some types of barriers multilaterally by proposing new rules or additional interpretations of existing rules, or by establishing more effective consultative machinery. Other barriers might be dealt with either multilaterally or bilaterally through the bargaining process. Still other measures might be given lower priority because work was already well advanced elsewhere or because of limited interest in them. 16

The CTIP also discussed a proposal that contracting parties consider subscribing to a Declaration of Intent that would underline the importance of a multilateral reduction of nontariff barriers and constitute an undertaking to avoid introduction of new nontariff measures or the reinforcement of existing ones. The question of a possible declaration was referred to the GATT Council. 

In addition to its program on nontariff barriers, a major study of tariffs was in process. Under the guidance of a Group of Technical Experts in which the United States participated and the general supervision of the Committee on Industrial Products, the Secretariat continued work on tabulations of trade and tariff data for an objective analysis by the CTIP of what the tariff situation will be when all Kennedy Round concessions have been fully implemented. The Secretariat obtained detailed import and tariff data for the years 1967, 1968, or 1969 from ten industrial countries which will enable comparisons of tariff levels and trade flows by fairly detailed tariff headings, product categories, and stages of processing. The tariff study, in conjunction with the material assembled on nontariff barriers, is intended to help the Committee discharge its general mandate to explore opportunities for further progress in trade liberalization.

Agricultural Products 

The Agriculture Committee largely completed its examination of factual and statistical material accumulated earlier on eight key product groups (dairy products; grains; beef and veal; poultry; pork and lamb; fruits and vegetables; vegetable oilseeds and oils; unmanufactured tobacco; and wine), and identified the main problems affecting international trade in these products. Actions Taken Under Articles XVIII, XIX, XXII, XXIII, and XXVIII

Articles XVIII and XXVIII of GATT contain the rules under which a contracting party may modify or withdraw concessions in its GATT Schedule. A country that takes such action is expected to negotiate or consult with the countries with which the concessions were initially negotiated and with other contracting parties having a substantial trade interest in the concessions. In such negotiations, the countries concerned are to endeavor (ordinarily by making new tariff reductions) to maintain a general level of reciprocal and mutually advantageous concessions not less favorable to trade than the level existing prior to the negotiations. Article XIX, under which the negotiating procedures are basically the same as those under Article XXVIII, is concerned with escape clause actions. 

Article XXII is the general consultation provision, and when differences cannot be resolved in such consultation, further action may be taken under Article XXIII which deals with complaints of nullification and impairment of benefits accruing to contracting parties under the GATT. 19

The special GATT Working Party on Border Tax Adjustments, established on request of the United States, completed its examination of the tax structures of major countries and their tax adjustment practices at the border, and proceeded to a consideration of trade effects which might result from border adjustments.

Consultations with Individual Contracting Parties 1. Balance of Payments Consultations 

Article XII of the General Agreement permits any contracting party to impose value or quantity restrictions on imports to safeguard its external financial position and its balance of payments. The degree of restriction permitted is limited to that required to forestall a serious decline in monetary reserves or, if monetary reserves are very low, to permit a reasonable rate of accumulation of reserves. Yearly consultations are required between the Contracting Parties and the countries imposing restrictions for these reasons. The consultations review the progress made by the country and its case for trade restrictions that remain in effect for balance of payments reasons. In 1969 such consultations were held with New Zealand, Israel, Iceland, and Spain. South Africa announced that it would no longer invoke Article XII for certain of its import restrictions and that a further announcement would be made later regarding these and other trade measures. 

Article XVIII permits contracting parties, "the economies of which can only support low standards of living and are in the early stages of development", to restrict imports temporarily to establish a particular industry or to protect the balance of payments. A contracting party invoking the right granted by this Article is required to consult with the Contracting Parties every two years to evaluate the progress toward establishing the particular industry or the improvement of the balance of payments, and additionally, to evaluate the progress toward the removal of restrictions on trade. Countries consulting in 1969 were Chile, Ceylon, Brazil, India, the Republic of Korea, Pakistan, Tunisia and Turkey. 

2. United Kingdom Import Deposit Scheme 

A Working Party established by the Council to consider the import deposit scheme introduced by the United Kingdom consulted on the matter with the International Monetary Fund. Taking into account this finding, the

At the GATT Council meeting of October 1969 the United Kingdom announced its decision to extend the scheme for a year beyond its original termination date of December 5, 1969, but reduced the rate of the deposit from 50 percent to 40 percent. 

The Council decided to keep the matter under review.

IMF/IBRD Report on Commodity Problems 

A comprehensive IMF and IBRD staff study on the problems of commodity trade and their possible solutions was completed in mid-1969. The report, together with the conclusions of the Executive Boards on the scope for assistance, was formally submitted to the Boards of Governors of the two institutions at their fall meeting. 

The chief potential significance of the decisions concerning assistance in dealing with commodity problems of developing countries appears to be the greater emphasis to be given by the World Bank Group to diversification aimed at reducing or restraining overproduction of particular products, to assistance in strengthening the competitive position of primary products exported by developing countries, and to other measures aimed at the root causes of their commodity trade problems. In addition, provision is made for certain types of support of international commodity arrangements, including assistance in financing buffer stocks, principally by the IMF. 

The absence of a source of international financing for buffer stocks had become a major issue within UNCTAD, and the Bank and Fund had been requested to address themselves to the problem. The reports of both institutions concluded that there are likely to be few serious candidates for an international buffer stock operation. They recognized, however, that where a buffer stock can play a constructive role, assistance may be appropriate, particularly short-term credit assistance from the Fund. Possibilities were found for providing assistance within the terms of their charters and their normal standards and procedures, but both institutions have framed the authority for such assistance most cautiously. In addition, national stocks were recognized as a generally constructive and more common type of stockholding, which can normally be financed with domestic resources, but may entail some indirect foreign exchange costs. 

Accordingly, the Fund and Bank agreed to take account of these requirements in their credit operations.

The Fund decision, in brief, authorizes 3 to 5 year credits to help countries which are in balance-of-payments difficulties make a required contribution to an international buffer stock that meets certain criteria (e.g., producer-consumer participation, sound objectives, and good prospects for financial success). The buffer-stock financing facility is closely linked in its concept and procedures to the Fund's compensatory financing facility, which provides balance-of-payments assistance to offset severe shortfalls in export earnings such as result from commodity price declines. The Fund will also be prepared to consider in its normal credit operations the indirect foreign exchange costs of national stocking operations which it finds to be constructive in terms of international commodity policy (e.g., consistent with the objectives of approved commodity agreements and serving the general interests of the international community). Such support of national stocks is not a marked departure from present Fund credit operations, but the intent is to give more attention to the financial requirements of stocking and to the "appropriateness" of such stocks in terms of their international effects. 

The Bank has authorized no more than a contingent, residual role for itself in the financing of stocks. In part this is because it believes that the holding of stocks for a long term is, in general, not sound and should not be facilitated. 30

International Trade in Cotton Textiles The Long-Term Arrangement Regarding International Trade in Cotton Textiles (LTA) 

was negotiated in 1962 by the GATT Cotton Textiles Committee. The United States proposed the LTA as a means of insuring a more orderly development of trade in cotton textiles than had occurred in the 1950's when the United States, as one of the few open markets, bore the brunt of sharply rising exports from new suppliers. 

The Arrangement provides for growing access to world markets for cotton textiles from exporting countries, while providing for avoidance of disruption of established markets in importing countries. Specifically, it requires developed countries with import quota restrictions inconsistent with GATT to reduce such restraints on disruptive imports of particular products from particular countries. Unilateral action to control imports may be taken under Article 3, which provides minimum restraint levels determined by a formula based on recent performance. The levels of these restraints must be increased by at least 5 percent in succeeding years, but in special circumstances, may be increased by less in the first year of renewal. 

Under Article 4, the LTA also permits bilateral agreements between exporting and importing countries. They have become the principal means for regulating imports of cotton textiles into the United States, which had 23 bilateral agreements in effect at the end of 1969. Such agreements benefit exporting countries by providing increased flexibility, assured access, and greater control over exports. Importing countries benefit by the comprehensive coverage of the agreements. 

The LTA came into effect on October 1, 1962, for an initial period of 5 years. 

Prior to its expiration it was extended for three years, beginning October 1, 1967. 

The GATT Cotton Textile Committee (CTC) met in October and December of 1969 to consider another extension of the Arrangement.

During 1969 the Tariff Commission completed six investigations instuted pursuant to the provisions of Title III. For the first time since the TEA went into effect in 1962, the Commission reached an affirmative finding on petitions for both tariff relief and adjustment assistance.

Three investigations were instituted at the request of industries seeking increased import restrictions on pianos, canned sardines and flat glass (rolled, plate, float, tempered and sheet). The Commission found that the statutory requirements had not been satisfied in its investigations of canned sardines and rolled, plate, float and tempered glass and consequently that the domestic industries producing such products did not qualify for tariff adjustment or other remedial action. With respect to sheet glass, the members of the Commission were equally divided in their findings, and in such circumstances the President is empowered to accept the finding of either 33

group of Commissioners as the finding of the Commission. In the piano investigation, the Commission found that the domestic industry was threatened with serious injury. 

The Commission's report of its findings in the canned sardine investigation was issued in July 1969, and those on pianos and flat glass in December 1969. Decisions by the 

President as to the disposition of the piano and glass cases were pending at the end of the year.

The other three investigations, instituted at the request of groups of workers employed in separate plants producing buttwelt pipe and transmission towers, resulted in Commission findings that the statutory requirements had been satisfied, and, as a result, the workers were eligible to apply for adjustment assistance. The Commission's separate reports were issued in November 1969.

Two Title III investigations were in process at the end of 1969. One was instituted at the request of a firm and groups of workers seeking tariff adjustment for the barbers' chairs industry, and the other by a firm in the barbers' chair industry seeking adjustment assistance. 

Escape clause actions are subject to review pursuant to provisions of the Trade Expansion Act. Section 351(c) provides that escape clause actions will terminate within a specified period of time, unless the President determines that an extension in whole or in part for an additional period up to four years would be in the national interest. Under this provision, two escape clause actions, namely Wilton and velvet carpets and sheet glass, which had previously been extended until December 31, 1969, were subject to termination on that date unless further extended. 

Pursuant to requests made by the producing industries concerned under Section 351 (d) (3), the Tariff Commission instituted investigations on the probable economic effect of the termination of the respective escape clause tariff increases. After receiving the Commission's reports, the President issued separate proclamations, one temporarily extending the existing escape clause tariffs on sheet glass to permit consideration of this matter in conjunction with the Commission's report which would be made on completion of an escape clause investigation then in progress on all types of flat glass, and the other, lowering the duties on carpets of Oriental design but extending the existing escape clause tariff increase on other Wilton and velvet carpets. 

National Security The national security provision of the Trade Expansion Act empowers the President to adjust imports if, following an investigation, the Director of the Office of Emergency Prepardness (OEP) advises that imports of the products involved are threatening to impair the national security. At the year's end, investigations by the OEP on textiles and textile products, ferroalloys and related products, miniature and instrument precision ball bearings (initiated in January) were continuing. Implementation of Section 22 of the Agricultural Adjustment Act 

Emergency import controls imposed on certain dairy products in September 1968 were made permanent on January 6, 1969. At the same time, the quota on other cheeses not specifically provided for was increased by 7.5 million pounds annually, and restrictions were added for milk chocolate crumb and Italian-type cheese in other than original loaves. World trade in dairy products continued to be characterized by excess supplies, export subsidization and severe price competition, and most importing countries maintained controls on foreign access to their markets. At 1.6 billion pounds, milk equivalent, U.S. dairy imports in 1969 amounted to 1.4 percent of U.S. production.

In addition to most manufactured dairy products, U.S. import quotas were maintained on wheat and wheat products; cotton and certain cotton waste and products; and peanuts. 

The legal basis for such controls derives from Section 22 of the Agricultural Adjustment 34

Act, as amended, which directs the President to impose import fees or quotas whenever he finds, on the basis of an investigation and report by the Tariff Commission, that imports are entering in such quantities as to render or tend to render ineffective, or materially interfere with, agricultural price support or other programs of the 

Department of Agriculture. 

G.

Investigation of Imports Under Tariff 

Sections 807.00 and 806.30

In recent years there has been a significant growth in imports entering the United States under tariff item 807.00 the provision under which the dutiable value of imported goods incorporating American-made components is determined by subtracting the value of the American components. Another item, 806.30, permits American goods made of metal to be sent abroad for processing and returned for further processing with payment of duty on only the value of the foreign processing. Strong, conflicting claims have been advanced regarding the effects of these tariff provisions on business, labor, consumers and the balance of payments. 

Before formulating a position on retaining, modifying, or repealing these tariff items, the President on August 18 requested the Tariff Commission to undertake an investigation and to report on all relevant economic factors affecting the use of items 807.00 and 806.30. Public hearings were scheduled to begin the following spring, with the report to be submitted by the end of August, 1970. 35
