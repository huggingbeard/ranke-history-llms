OPENING STATEMENT BY MR. MURRAY STEIN MR. STEIN: The conference is open. 

This session of the conference in the matter of pollution of the navigable waters of the South Platte River Basin within the State of Colorado is being held under the provisions of Section 10 of the Federal Water Pollution Control Act, as amended. 

The Secretary of the Interior is authorized to call a conference of this type when requested to do so by the Governor of a State. Governor Love requested a conference on July 18, 1963, and conference sessions have been held on October 29, 1963, and April 27-28, 1966.

The purpose of the conference is to bring together representatives of the State water pollution control agency, representatives of the Federal Water Pollution Control Administration, and other interested parties, to review the existing situation, the progress which has been made, to lay a basis for future action by all parties con-

State and local law.

The conference technique is rather an old one. 

It is used by many States informally in the normal conduct of their business in the field of water pollution control. 

The concept of the conference was proposed by the United States Supreme Court as long ago as 1921 in the famous case of New York against New Jersey involving interstate pollution. opinion:

I would like to quote briefly from the Court's "We cannot withhold the suggestion, inspired by the consideration of this case, that the grave problem of sewage disposal presented by the large and growing population living on the shores of New York Bay is one more readily to be most wisely solved by cooperative study and by conference and mutual concession on the part of representatives of the States so vitally interested in it than by proceedings in any court however constituted."

Before we start that, Mr. Bud Bauer, who will make this review for us, has suggested that I review with you some of the technical terms that will be used by him, and, I am sure, by other speakers today.

Many of you may have this sheet defining certain technical terms that will be used, but it isn't very long, and let me read it to be sure that each of you have some idea of what they are.

Biochemical oxygen demand is abbreviated as BOD and is analyzed as five-day, and 20°C BOD is a measure of the oxygen demand of sewage and industrial wastes. BOD in the stream is a measure of oxygen used by living organisms in decomposing these wastes. 

Total suspended solids, referred to as TSS, are solids found in waste water or in the stream which can be removed by filtration.

The origin of suspended matter may be man-made wastes or natural sources such as silt from erosion.

Population equivalents, often referred to as P.E., describe the pollutional effect of various waste discharges in terms of a corresponding effect of discharging raw sewage from an equivalent number of human population. 

P.E. represents the waste contributed by one person in a Each

single day.

Total coliform bacteria represent a diverse group of microorganisms whose presence have been used as indication of sewage pollution in water supplies. They are always present in the intestinal tract of man and other warm-blooded animals and are excreted in large numbers in fecal wastes. It must be noted that under some circumstances, coliform bacteria may be present which are derived from sources other than fecal excreta. 

Fecal coliform bacteria eliminate coliforms derived from sources other than the gut of warm-blooded animals. Enumeration of this group yields an index for that portion of the coliform group which is of fecal origin. 

As the numbers of fecal coliform increase, the relative probability of hazard from enteric diseases increases.

STATEMENT OF ROBERT W. GARDNER, ASSISTANT REGIONAL FORESTER, UNITED STATES FOREST SERVICE, DENVER, COLORADO MR. GARDNER: Mr. Chairman, ladies and gentlemen: 

I am glad to have this chance to discuss water quality as it pertains to National Forest lands in Colorado in which the South Platte River rises. 

Headquarters of the South Platte form principally

R. W. Gardner in three National Forests 67 the Roosevelt, Arapaho and Pike. 

The streamflow contribution from approximately 1,700,000 acres of National Forest land, or 14 percent of the area, is estimated at more than 80 percent of the total flow of the South Platte. Most of these lands are above 7,000 feet in the snowpack zone.

This proportion of total streamflow is not unusual as related to National Forests 

particularly in the West.

This was, in fact, one of the primary reasons for establishment of the National Forests.

The Act of June 4, 1897, creating the forest reserves, cited "securing favorable conditions of water flows" as a principal purpose. 

Because those "favorable conditions of water flow" include quantity and timing, in addition to quality, a great responsibility lies with the Forest Service in the use, management, and administration of these key lands. 

Very basically, the Forest Service believes its place in the water yield picture is to provide for full production and efficient transmission of water from the time moisture hits National Forest lands, either as snow or rain, until it leaves the forest. It is our job, then, to deliver you the raw material in as great a quantity as possible, without sacrificing needed quality.

R. W. Gardner 

In accepting this responsibility, we have developed four basic Forest Service policies related to water quality: 1. 2. 

Ensure that return flows, particularly those associated with recreation and other domestic use, do not impair streamflow for other purposes the water is expected to serve. 

Make certain that National Forest land management practices are conducted in a manner which will ensure a quality of water yield suitable for its intended purposes.

Maintain a water quality satisfactory for other National Forest resource uses, such as livestock and wildlife-watering and fish habitat, whenever it is within the capability of the Forest 

Service to do so.

Ensure a biologically safe and otherwise suitable supply of drinking water for the use of the public. 

Compliance with these guidelines is no simple task. About 21 percent of the gross area within National 

Forest boundaries of the South Platte is held in private ownership. We have no jurisdiction over these private lands. A large proportion of this private land is located along major stream bottoms, constituting a potential pollution

R. W. Gardner source over which the Forest Service has no control. 

About two-thirds of the area within the Forests are of granitic origin soils 11 they are highly erosive. 

This requires particular care in resource management to minimize stream sedimentation. 

And, while water production is the prime factor in any land management decision, other resource uses of these lands are, and must be, made. Timber is harvested, cattle and sheep graze the slopes, recreationists make year-round visits, and wildlife, large and small, makes its home there.

Contracts 

Under contract to commercial timber operators, there were 18 million board feet of timber cut in 1965 in the National Forest of the South Platte drainage. awarded to highest bidders include numerous clauses, many of which are directed toward prevention of stream sedimentation and other pollution. These timber sales are closely supervised by the District Forest Ranger and members of his staff. While a few small sawmills are situated on National 

Forest lands, this practice is uncommon. Most logs are hauled to sawmills in nearby communities. 

Silvicultural, or regeneration requirements of Engelmann spruce and lodgepole pine, our principal commercial species, generally require cutting in patches. We believe

R. W. Gardner managed timber harvesting contributes very little to stream sedimentation or other pollution. Where sufficient natural regeneration does not occur in the cut-over areas, they are satisfactorily stocked by planting. 

Last year nearly 13,000 cattle and 10,000 sheep grazed the South Platte area under permit. Livestock numbers and season of use are regulated in accordance with the forage supply and the herds or bands allotted specific areas of use.

Individual range management plans are prepared for each allotment designed to maintain and improve watershed and forage conditions. One objective of the plan is to prescribe practices which increase livestock distribution and minimize concentrations in stream bottoms. We continue to have some problems, but range management practices are continually being improved.

As it is throughout the United States, outdoor recreation use is rapidly increasing in the South Platte. 

Some 4-million recreation visits were made in 1965. A1though our recreation facility development has been accelerated, we still have a difficult time keeping pace with demand.

All sanitation facilities in developed camp and picnic areas accessible by road are now of the vault type. 

Sewage is pumped out under contract to commercial disposal

companies.

R. W. Gardner 

Sewage disposal from pickup campers and trailers continues to be a problem. Camp and picnic grounds with disposal facilities for trailer use are being developed in areas of heavy concentration. 

Use in undeveloped areas is checked as much as possible by recreation aids. Where a specific pollution hazard is found to exist, the area is posted. 

Loggers, stockgrowers, and recreationists are not the only users of the South Platte Forests. A great number of major resource development projects in the South Platte area have just been completed, are under construction, or are planned by other Federal agencies or private companies. 

To mention a few: The Urad Mine development in Woods Creek; the Public Service Company's Cabin Creek development above 

Georgetown; Interstate Highway in Clear Creek Canyon and

Straight Creek tunnel; the Malta-Waterton primary transmission line of the Public Service Company of Colorado; and the proposed Two Forks reservoir.

Before such projects are constructed, the Forest Service makes an intensive survey of the impact on forest resources, including water quality. From this analysis, stipulations are written and included in the permit or agreement.

During construction a Forest Service liaison

R. W. Gardner officer is assigned to the project to see that the stipulations are carried out, and to make on-the-ground decisions as may be needed.

Although the construction agencies and companies are most cooperative, it is impossible to construct developments of this magnitude without some adverse effect Such adverse effects are generally on stream quality. temporary.

National Forests are subject to natural catastrophes, such as fires and floods, which may have a serious effect on water quality. Local high intensity flood-producing storms are not infrequent in our high watersheds. Forest fires caused by man or lightning may denude large areas in a few hours, leaving the soil unprotected from rainstorms.

When fires occur, technicians are immediately assigned to the task of determining rehabilitation requirements and costs, and they often begin such work before the fire is completely under control. Emergency funds are usually available and reseeding is done immediately to hold the soil. There is always risk, however, that a heavy rainstorm will occur before ground cover is established, and wash the loose ash and soil into streams. 

This has been a very brief and far from complete sketch of our objectives, policies, resource uses, and management activities as related to water quality. It

R. W. Gardner should be recognized that while our objectives are to maintain water of a high quality in the National Forest watersheds, the quality level must be somewhat less than that of pristine purity.

We believe that with careful management, the development and use of these natural resources which are essential to Colorado's economy can be continued and expanded, and still result in an acceptable standard of water quality.

In closing, I wish to emphasize that we will be happy to work with the conferees in any way we can.

Since the last water pollution conference held in April of this year, the City and County of Denver has continued to progress in its water pollution control program. 

Because water pollution abatement is a joint effort in Denver by the Department of Public Works and the Department of Public Health, a joint statement has been prepared for this conference.

The greatest source of water pollution on the South Platte River within the City and County of Denver, has

F. Rozich now been corrected with the discharge of sewage effluent from the North Side Treatment Plant to the new Metro facilities for secondary treatment. This took place on October 17, 1966. The East Side Primary Treatment Plant will soon be abandoned when treatment at the Metro Plant becomes available. 

The ordinance covering the control of sewage wastes in storm and sanitary sewers and water courses passed City Council and was put into effect July 1, 1966. Corrective steps are being taken to correct the discharge of paunch manure into the sewer systems as provided in this ordinance. (See attached ordinance and rules and regulations.) 

A study is now under way by a consulting engineering firm at Stapleton Airport. A recommended solution to the waste pollution problem associated with this operation is expected by the end of this year. When this report is received, appropriate measures will be taken with respect to administration and the facilities needed at the airport. 

To control industrial water pollution, a coordinated effort is being made by both Departments to connect industrial wastes which now discharge into the river to the sanitary sewer system. Efforts are also being made to discharge unpolluted waters which flow to the sanitary sewer into the storm sewer or into the river to prevent possible overloading of the sewage plant.

F. Rozich 

At the present time, 112 outlets on the South Platte River have been established as routine sampling stations to evaluate all wastes discharging to the river. 

These sampling stations include outlets from industries and storm sewers which discharge water to the river under dry weather conditions. Five sampling points have also been selected along the South Platte River within the City and County of Denver to collect samples twice monthly. samples will give us information on the pollutional condition of the stream as it flows through Denver. These

As a result of this water pollution program, many industries have been pinpointed as causing water pollution and orders have been written to correct their pollution problems. To date, six industrial water pollution sources have been corrected, eight corrections are under way, three are waiting for a sanitary sewer line which will be programed by the city soon, and seven have shown very little response to the orders which have been written. The ones not responding are all trucking type operations which discharge wash waters into the river. The plan at the present time is to take legal action against these industries in the near future to obtain compliance.

At the present time, 16 storm sewers are causing minor pollution to the South Platte River. Our efforts have

The City of Denver plans to continue its water pollution control program in the future and it is anticipated that our efforts will result in eventual abatement of pollution in both the South Platte River and Cherry Creek within the boundaries of the City of Denver. October 27, 1966

ESTABLISHMENT OF STRENGTH INDEXES. 

The Manager of Public Works is charged with the responsibility of establishing strength indexes of B.O.D. and S.S. for the purpose of determining the surcharge to be levied against Industrial Waste Class 

Customers and Outside Surcharge Customers. The following Rules and Regulations will apply relative to these determinations: a. 

Methods. 

One of the following methods will be used for a

F. Rozich specific customer depending upon the arrangement of the private sewer and water facilities of that customer, and upon the judgment of the Manager: 

Sampling and Testing Comparison 

Best Judgment. 

(1) Procedures: 

(a) Sampling and Testing. 

This method involves the collection of samples of the sewage discharged by the customer and the conducting of laboratory tests of that sample to determine the B.O.D. and S.S. contents, which are the respective strength indexes. 

(b) Comparison. 

This method involves the comparison of the operation, business, product, etc., of a customer that cannot be sampled or has not been sampled with a similar customer that has had its strength index established by sampling. The strength indexes of the sampled and tested customer are then established for the customer that has not been sampled.

F. Rozich (c) Best Judgment:

In those cases where sampling and testing or comparison methods cannot be utilized or where it is impractical for either of these methods to be used, the Manager may use his best judgment in the establishment of strength indexes. 

(a) Sampling and Testing. 

This method is limited to those customers that have inspection chambers or control manholes on the private sewer connection that can be used by the 

Manager for sampling the sewage discharged from the building under consideration. 

The Manager may determine that it is not economically feasible to sample and test even if the private sewerage facilities lend themselves to this method. 

(b) Comparison. 

This method is dependent upon test data that is available from another customer that due to its operation, business, product, etc., it would be proper to

This method is dependent upon knowledge of the characteristics of a sewage discharged from a specific type of operation, business, process, etc.

We have pointed out repeatedly that changes are occurring in the Nation's aquatic environment 

changes that seriously threaten fishery resources and tend to deface the value and utility of this promised land. No single group or individual is responsible for these changes. It is the small addition contributed by one, compounded with those contributed by others, that results in creeping ruination of a stream, a lake, or a major river. 

Frequently, water quality becomes degraded and competing water uses become detrimental so gradually but so positively that fishes and those who use them are in trouble long before the more obvious issues of human safety

and welfare are threatened. 

Before the "public is aroused" valuable fisheries and the waters that produce them are lost or so deteriorated that great effort and expense are necessary for rehabilitation. We urge, therefore, a broad concept of pollution; a definition not necessarily restricted to bacterial counts, excesses of dissolved solids, gross deficiencies in dissolved oxygen or easily detectable changes in bottom fauna composition. We urge the establishment of water quality standards that have prevention of pollution as a goal; not a set of limits that will define the degree of deterioration to be tolerated or to which clean water can be defiled before action is taken to avert a national disgrace. We propose the establishment of acceptable and adjustable limits on quantities of phosphorus, nitrogen, and other basic nutrients or trace elements that trigger cycles of biological productivity frequently as damaging to natural waters as the original sources of so-called pollution. Of course, this proposal calls for research and development in chemical engineering and the techniques treatment, a challenge any nation like ours should accept Given such facility, we envision the enthusiastically. of deliberate environmental adjustments in the aquatic realm that have made agriculture in this country the envy of the world. We must and can learn to manipulate our "outfalls, 11

effluents, or whatever we may call them to serve our everincreasing population.

We must abandon the concept of "waste discharge" in favor of the positive approach of "by-product use" to improve our capabilities in aquatic as well as terrestrial management. 

As directed and authorized by Congress, the Bureau of Commercial Fisheries will continue its examination of lake and stream environments for water quality and for ecological appraisal. Experience has taught us that, frequently, troubles within one portion of a drainage have their origins in other, often remote, portions. Therefore, we must become aware and remain cognizant of the occurrences and practices in all portions of a drainage as large as that of the Missouri River that may threaten fish and wildlife.
