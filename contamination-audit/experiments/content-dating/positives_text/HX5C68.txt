TO THE READER : 

This story in rhyme is dedicated to the patriotism of the seven Bowmans and the other brave men who met the British soldiers on Lexington Green in the early morning of April 19, 1775; and also to the twenty-seven other Bowmans who served in the war for the Independence of the Colonies. Hoping that posterity may appreciate their valor and service, and that warfare among men may forever cease.

I. P. Bowman 

March 28, 1933.

THADDEUS BOWMAN AT LEXINGTON By I. P. BOWMAN 

THADDEUS BOWMAN, forget the name; 

He fought for freedom and not for fame; 

A Colonial soldier, true to his king, Alert and eager as a day in Spring,

He fought with the English against her foes When war with the Indians and French arose; 

He followed the colors thru shot and shell 

And kept on going while comrades fell;

No truer soldier e'er served the Crown; 

Nor sought he honor or renown,

But sought his home when war was o'er, His neighbors, friends, to fight no more.

But, alas, for the limitations of man; 

He knows not fate nor the Infinite plan; 

He glides on a sea in calm repose 

And awakens on billows the hurricane blows;

So Thaddeus Bowman found it when 

Made Captain of the Minute Men.

The story is old, has often been told, How England intrigued in ways manifold

Colonial rights to flout into dust, The rights of freemen, sacred and just; 

Freemen who knew how to rule and be ruled; 

Men of high principles, not to be fooled

By Acts of the Crown simply and plain, The forging of a tyrant's chain.

So, when the Redcoats to Boston came, Resentment like a rising flame 

Flew all along the Atlantic coast, A challenge to an English boast; 

And hearts that loved began to hate, And friends of peace to hesitate;

The English colors, once a charm, Were now a signal for alarm; 

As love for the Lion waned and died

The love for the Eagle was glorified; 

And men would die, and men would fight 

For love of truth and freedom's right. 

And when the stars with anxious eyes

The British beheld on a march of surprise It was an omen foreboding of ire, A conflagration bursting in fire; 

For Hancock and Adams the king had defied 

And must be captured, for treason be tried; 

And Colonial stores must be taken too; 

And they posted pickets the country thru 

To guard the roads from prying eyes 

And keep their purpose a surprise.

But even then, the lights in the tower Had already proclaimed the fatal hour When boats had carried the soldiers away To march in the Lexington-Concord way; 

And Paul Revere was speeding along

Spreading the news the homes among,- The Minute Men waking, and on, and on, Until he came safely to Lexington; 

And Captain Thaddeus Bowman then 

Went wakening up the Minute Men.

Arousing the country from cellar to roof 

With shout and shot and galloping hoof;

With ringing of bells and blowing of horn, With cheers for the hope of a nation new-born; 

Speeding his steed over valley and hill,

Close by the pickets, eluding them still; 

Close to the British in uniform gay,

Thus to make sure they were on the way; 

Back then, to tell them on Lexington Green The Redcoats were coming and soon could be seen; 

And still onward and on to call the men back 

Who waited for hours the British attack

And went to their homes to be notified In case they should come ere morning, betide. 

And then returning at rise of the sun 

Saw England coming, ten against one- "Disperse you scoundrels," its Leader said, They fired, and Minute Men fell dead; 

They fired again, and others fell—

A crime as black as the deepest hell; 

Then Thaddeus mad, his wrath unfurled, And fired the shot heard round the world. 

And as the smoke to heaven curled, And touched the God of all the world, Fate snatched from England the Colonies then,

For Freedom had spoken by blood of men. 

And one of the noble American dead

Had borne the colors of England ahead 

Over battle field, fortress and trench,

The colors he loved, in the war with the French.

But the British cheered for victory won, (The time was the rising of the sun), And marching to Concord seized the stores,

While Minute Men gathered by the scores; 

Then came a clash, the guns rang out, The British quickly turning about,

The Hall of Justice wantonly burned. 

Felling the Liberty Pole they spurned,

Then with the work of destruction complete, Started at noon on their sad retreat; 

For the blood of the Concord Minute Men

Cried out for vegeance there and then. 

And such a retreat! Can it be said 'Twas all revenge for the American dead? 

Not so, the Minute Men were there 

To strike a blow for freedom fair;

They followed and flanked the British troops, Fought like Indians, omitting the whoops,

Came from behind fences and stumps, Walls and trees, bushes and clumps, And hunted and drove the British on

Like beasts of prey to Lexington 

Into the Square Lord Percy made 

Of men and cannon, a fresh brigade; 

And there, exhausted and panting lay; 

Brave men, but beaten in the fray.

The news was carried far and wide, And men came like a rising tide;

And guns rang out, and Redcoats ran; 

Each hiding place held a Minute Man, And cannon were useless as the breeze 'Gainst men wrought up like rolling seas.

The British, retreating, were hounded and flanked; 

Riven and driven, pounded and spanked; 

And bleeding, receding and hastening for

A place 'neath the guns of their men-of-war, Fell prostrate there, completely outdone; 

The time was the setting of the sun. 

And while the stars looked down upon

The burning homes of Lexington, The dead and wounded, and widows' tears, The blood of war, its hates and sneers; 

A brighter star appeared in the sky,

The hope of a nation born, never to die; 

And it beamed on Thaddeus Bowman then, A star among the Minute Men;

And while his work for the day was done His work for the years had just begun; 

The shot he fired around the world

A phalanx of duties upon him hurled; 

From the blood and fire a flag unfurled; 

Beneath its stars fierce battles whirled 

Till tyranny was hell-ward hurled 

That Freedom's Flag might lift the world.
