CABIN IN THE PINES CHAPTER I THE MAN NOBODY WANTED 

RAHAM BOONE, coming out of the Schultz

Building, noticed a peculiar sensation in his hands; and glancing down at them he saw that they were trembling. This had never happened to them before. They were square, strong, firm hands, almost as steady as feet. He clenched them and put them into his pockets, but that did not keep them still; their tremor came from deep inside him. He felt like a hard, tight shell, enclosing something that fluttered like a bird.

If it happens to me once more (he said to himself, with a sudden sure knowledge), something's going to break - and when it does, I'll never be the same again.

There was a small, cindery park opposite the Schultz Building (also named Schultz, in honor of a defunct citizen who had amassed a million dollars in the service of his city), and Graham crossed to it and sat down on a bench. Before long, he was suddenly grimly aware, a park bench might come to mean a good deal more than a resting-place for a spare minute; if things went on like this, park benches and spare minutes would be all he had. The refusal he had just met with was his fifty-seventh. — He hadn't meant to keep count; in fact after the twenty-fifth he had carefully avoided doing so. But some inexorable bookeeper in his (alleged) unconscious had gone on relentlessly ticking them off - fifty-five, fifty-six, fifty-seven. — Me and

Mr. Heinz, he thought. The only difference between us is that they want his products; but they don't want me, fifty-seven times. Like a battering-ram pounding a wall, blow by blow the rebuffs pounded at his self-confidence and hope. When they made a breach, his courage would slip out through it. One more blow would be enough.

"I want to go there!" he exclaimed aloud. 

And why not? Why not go there? Nobody wanted him here. His old world had disintegrated, and he did not know where to look for a new one. He needed time to pull himself together and gather courage for a fresh start; and down there among the pines was a little spot of ground, a little chunk of house, that was all his own.

Suddenly a spurt of resolution tingled through his veins. The inner trembling ceased; an impression (entirely erroneous) that the sun always shone in Chapel

Hill made him feel warm all over. Walking fast, he set out for the station where he had left his suitcase. 

He meant to get to his cabin before his courage gave out altogether. If he were really beaten, at least he could meet defeat there in decent privacy.
