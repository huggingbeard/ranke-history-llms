PREFACE 

This bulletin deals with leisure time activities of junior high school boys. A like bulletin has been prepared on leisure time activities of junior high school girls. 

The information was obtained from a community survey. For a number of years a study of community life in Beloit, insofar as it affects the school curriculum, has been under way. 

As will be noted this study analyzes radio, hobby, organization, movie, party, library and music activities of boys in the 

7th, 8th and 9th grades of the Beloit Public Schools. A study of the charts and other materials found within the covers of the bulletin offers much light on leisure time activities of the age group under discussion. Such an analysis should help quite materially in locating needs of boys of the junior high school age insofar as leisure time activities are concerned. Teachers will also find many curriculum implications bound up in the information set forth. 

From these implications should come school and classroom curriculum reorganization and pupil activity reorganization of vital importance to junior high school boys. . The data contained on the following pages were organized by 

George Roth of the Lincoln Junior High School faculty. While these data had already been collected, much painstaking effort was required to organize them in a logical and presentable manner. Mr. Roth also spent much time and effort in constructing the pictographs and graphs. 

The Beloit schools, indeed, owe an expression of gratitude to Mr. 

Roth for his painstaking work. 

Respectfully submitted,

Victor F. Dawald Superintendent of Schools

I. INTRODUCTION 

It was felt that in order to develop a more functional curriculum in the Beloit, Wisconsin public schools, an understanding of the community was necessary. With this objective in mind, a group of teachers working with the city superintendent and one of the professors from the University of Wisconsin, formulated a community survey which attempted to determine the working opportunities, the pupil's home conditions, and the way in which pupils used their leisure time in Beloit. This questionnaire was filled out in May 1939. A section was taken from the recreation questionnaire and worked out according to the purposes stated below. No attempt has been made to exhaust the subject, but rather, a small section of the data is compiled and interpreted in such a way that it may be included along with similar analysis of this survey and distributed among the members of the teaching staff of the Beloit public schools. 

A few facts about the City of Beloit may give a background which will aid in understanding the following survey.

Beloit is a small city with a population of about twenty-six thousand. 

The southern boundary of Beloit is the Wisconsin-Illinois state line. 

The part of the city on the south side of the line is a separate city, South Beloit, Illinois. Beloit is chiefly dependent upon a few factories to maintain its prosperity. Recreational facilities consist chiefly of a municipal swimming pool, golf course, several playgrounds, and in the sumer a number of school grounds are supervised by young men and women. In the winter a number of school grounds are flooded and maintained for skating. The lagoon and river furnish more skating area. There is a modern Y.M.C.A. and the usual number of boys' organizations, including the Boy Scouts with the executive's headquarters for scouting in this area. The Scout camp is about seven miles south of the city, but is used very little at present.

There are two junior high schools in Beloit, one on the east side of the river, and one on the west side. The junior high schools are not used as social centers, but the vocational school and several grade schools have recreational activities. 

II, PURPOSES OF THE STUDY 

1. To graphically present the data on the leisure time activities of the junior high school boys in Beloit, Wisconsin, as it is recorded in the community survey taken in the month of May 1939. 

2. To interpret these graphs in terms of leisure time activities. 

3. To point out problems of educational significance indicated by the results. 

4. The purpose of this study is not to attempt to solve these problems, nor to 

Compare the Beloit boys with any "standard" boy shown by more extensive tests, but to bring these problems to the attention of the teaching staff. 

It is hoped that these data may be found useful in creating a more functional curriculum. (These data were gathered from approximately 600 boys in both junior high schools.)

III. DIFFICULTIES AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY 

While the facts presented by the following report are very revealing, it must be pointed out that there are a number of definite difficulties and limitations involved. These are stated below. If these are kept in mind while examining the material, the data will be more significant in revealing a clearer picture of the facts. 

1. This data was obtained by the questionnaire method, and is therefore subject to the errors common to a subjective measure. 

2. This recreation questionnaire was filled out along with another on the home, consequently it had to be brief, and could not analyze some problems as completely as was desired. 

3. The definition of "hobby" was left to the student or his home room teacher. 

4. The tendency is to list activities most appropriate to the season. Since this survey was taken in the spring, outdoor activities and summer sports may have been given undue emphasis. 

5. No record of race is available at this point in the refinement of the data. (Beloit has a considerable number of negroes.) 

6. "Loafing" in the form of valking about town, window shopping, sitting on the porch watching traffic, etc., was not suggested as a possible way of spending leisure time. It is obvious that this activity occupios a significant amount of time with some boys, yet no one mentioned this activity. 

7. Difficulty arose in the classification of some radio programs as being either entertaining or educational, and as to whether hobbies were vocational or purely avocational; in some cases they could be classified as both. 

8. Hours per week spent on activities is subject to error as the pupil may give the amount of time that he wishes to spend, or what he had done in the past few months. 

9. Activities which the pupil believes that the teacher and schools prefer may be emphasized, and less desirable activities minimized.

In the following portion of this study, the difficulties and limitations characteristic of each set of data, the activities participated in, and the educational problems raised will be discussed. At the end a brief summary will be given.

A. Radio 

Pictograph 1 divides the type of radio programs listened to as being educational or entertaining. Drama was classified as entertaining, as a further analysis of the type of drama listened to is not possible until the data is further refined to show actual programs listened to. Human interest was likewise a "border line case", but was called educational because of the biographical element, or possibly because these programs may reveal desirable human traits and give a better understanding of human nature. 

Quick inspection of Pictograph 1 shows that the entertaining type program is the overwhelming favorite. There is a slight shift from entertaining to educational programs from 7th to 9th grade. It must be remembered that these are the favorite types of programs, second choices were not given. A pupil could still listen to the news every day, and still not have this be his favorite program. 

The evidence points to the fact that our schools either have not attempted to interest pupils in educational programs, or their efforts have met with failure. There is also a need for a more complete analysis of these entertaining programs to determine what holds the child's interest. This would enable the teacher to know if there was any element in these programs which might be utilized in the classroom, or whether parents should be discouraged in allowing their children to listen to these. 

If it is felt that drama programs and human interest programs were wrongly classified, it can be seen that by changing one or both of these little difference can be made in the proportions desiring the two types of programs. 

The favorite type of program is shown in more detail by Pictograph 2. 

Thrillers hold first place among the 7th grade boys. However, by the time that 8th grade is reached, thrillers have dropped down in importance, although they still rate first choice, with comedy, drama, and swing becoming more important. In the 9th grade, thrillers have dropped to third place, comedy has moved up to first place, with swing only 6 per cent behind. The change from 

7th to 9th grade is from a narrower to a wider choice of type of radio program. 

The shift away from thrillers to such a strong desire for swing suggests that the boy is getting away from hero worship of a "he-man" of mighty action, and is substituting a less boisterous program suggesting the dance floor. This brings in the opposite sex and a social situation. It is a change from a homosexual to a heterosexual interest, although in a modified form. With so many boys preferring thrillers one wonders what the psychological effect of these programs is upon the boy. Many of these programs which are available are the type in which blood flows freely and the hero is left between the "devil and the deep blue sea" until "tomorrow, same time, same station".

Each episode of some programs is a complete story and leaves the child under no tension when finished. Others are filled with action but have not enough action to greatly stir the emotions of the listeners. Opinion seems to be divided as to the psychological effect of these programs. It is conceivable that one may become completely calloused by listening to so many thrillers. What is the moral effect of crime stories; do they teach the boy to hate crime, or do they school him in crime? Such questions can only be answered by extensive study.

The student's estimate of the hours a week that he spends listening to the radio is shown in Pictograph 3. Of the 7th graders, 29 per cent spend less than five hours a week, and 32 per cent spend five to ten hours a week; slightly less than two-thirds spending ten hours or less listening to the radio every week. 

Six per cent of the 7th graders report spending over 30 hours a week listening to the radio. If this is accurate, they must be the group who have radio background to everything done in the home, except sleeping, such as eating, studying, working, etc. It is difficult to conceive that any healthy youngster would be able to give his full attention to the radio for an average of more than four hours a day.

Among the 8th grade boys there is a slightly larger group, about one-third, who spend less than five hours a week listening to the radio, but 31 per cent spend 10 to 20 hours. This is an increase over the 7th grade. 

The largest group in any class, 35 per cent, occurs in the group of 9th graders who are entertained by radio programs from 5 to 10 hours a week. 

Combining this group with the 10 to 20 hour percentages, it is found that 62 per cent of the 9th grade boys spend 5 to 20 hours a week listening to the radio, compared to 54 per cent of the 8th and 55 per cent of the 7th grade. 

The "fiends" who allow radio to occupy over 30 hours a week drop to one per cent in the 9th grade.

There exists more homogenity among the 9th graders than the 7th or 8th graders as to the amount of time spent listening to the radio. 

Referring back to Pictograph 2, one can now get the full significance of the place of the radio. For example, 62 per cent of the 9th grade boys spend between five and 20 hours a week listening to radio programs, with comedy, swing, and thrillers as their first three preferences.

With radio such a time-consuming occupation of the adolescent boy, a number of problems of educational value arise. For example, is it possible for some of the time spent listening to the radio to be diverted into some other worthwhile channel? How much of the information gained from the radio programs is retained? Can the boy be interested in educational programs? If an attempt is made to interest the adolescent in more worthwhile radio programs, are there other valuable and interesting programs toward which teachers may direct the boy's attention? Since some boys spend a great deal of time listening to radio programs, is it possible to correlate any of these with class work?

It would be worthwhile to determine the effect of listening to the radio while doing school work. We should like to discover what factors make the radio such a force in the boy's life, as woll as why thero is such a diversity between the amount of time that some boys spend on radio. 

It is also significant to note that opera and organ programs are conspicuous by their absence. Not one boy listed either as his favorite program! 

In fact, not one boy, and only two girls, in the whole school system listed either opera or organ programs!

-- B• Hobbies Question eight of the recreational survey reads, "Name your hobby and give the number of hours a week that you spend on it". This gives the boy a very wide choice of what he wishes to call a "hobby".

Table I shows the various hobbies participated in. It can be seen that quite a wide list of hobbies are included in the interests of junior high school boys.

This list was then grouped taking those shown listed with percentages and classifying them as vocational, avocational, and those with no hobby. While sports may be a vocation to a few, it will be only a leisure time activity to most. Pets were classified as avocational, but it could be called vocational. 

However, the probability is small that the care for a dog, canary, goldfish or rabbit will lead into a life's work. Reading was another "problem child", but it was placed in the classification under the assumption that detective, western, and the like make up the major part of boys' reading. Further study is necessary to clarify this point. 

The following classification was used: Sports included general sports, baseball, swimming, golf, archery, and boating. Avocational consisted of collecting match folders, pets, fishing, hunting (these last two could be called sports, but the term was used to imply physical exercise and competitive games), reading, and miscellaneous collections. The vocational hobbies included model planes, woodwork, gardening, radio, photography, and drawing. 

Whether or not these classifications are accepted, the significance of the graphs is changed little. (Pictographs 4, 5, 6.)

The avocational type hobbies hold the interests of 35 per cent of the 7th grade boys, with vocational types trailing close behind, and sports a weak third.

In the 8th grade, avocational hobbies move ahead with 42 per cent at the expense of vocational hobbies. In the 9th grade, vocational hobbies take the lead with 35 per cent, and the avocational and sports trailing with 27 per cent each. It is significant to note that sports now tie for second place. The percentage of boys with no hobby remains under 16 per cent with little change. 

The division of hobbies into vocational and avocational carries the implication that it is desirable to turn hobbies into one's life work, which is open to debate.

The number of boys with no hobby should provide a challenge to the extra-curricular organizations of the schools. The worthwhileness of hobbies engaged in is probably as important as the permanency of the hobby. 

Such hobbies as model planes have high vocational value, but probably will not remain as a diversion into adult life as will stamp collections, in some cases. 

Graph 1 indicates that the boys of 7th grade who spend 5 to 10 hours a week on hobbies total 24 per cent, compared to 20 per cent of the 8th graders, and 16 per cent of the 9th graders. Forty-seven per cent of the 7th grade, 54 per cent of the 8th grade, and 46 per cent of the 9th grade spend five or less hours a week on their hobbies. C. Membership in Organizations 

The data presented did not distinguish between the cases where a boy's membership in two or more organizations occurred, so this is not taken into account in determining Pictographs 7, 8, and 9. 

Neither does this survey distinguish between active and inactive members.

These three pictographs point out that in the 7th grade, the Boy Scouts lead, claiming 48 per cent of the memberships, with the Y.M.C.A. following with 39 per cent, and the sport and hobby clubs trailing way behind. 

In the 8th grade, the "y" comes forward with the Scouts dropping behind. 

Church organizations rate third with a total percentage of 18, counting both C.Y.O. and other church organizations. 

A change is seen by the time 9th grade is reached, as Scouting has regained first place, the "Y" is second with church and sports as a weak third. Hobbies appear a little stronger. The 4-H club makes its appearance as rural boys come to the city for grades 9 to 12. The Sons of the Veterans of Foreign Wars claim 6 per cent of the memberships. It will be noted that in the 9th grade there is a wider range of clubs participated in. 

Since the Boy Scouts and Y.M.C.A. occupy the time of many boys, would it not be possible to correlate some of the work in these organizations with school? All of the organizations belonged to are adult supervised and should offer the adolescent good moral guidance. Would it be possible for the school 

Parent-Teacher organizations to sponsor more of the same type of boys' clubs as the Scouts?

About 48 per cent of junior high school boys belong to no organizations at all.

More 7th grade boys belong to no clubs than do 9th grade boys. This shows a trend toward a more socialized attitude. More 8th and 9th graders belong to two organizations than do 7th graders. It can be seen that no more than 4 per cent of all classes belong to the groups joining three or more clubs. (Graph 2)

D. Attendance at the Movies 

The motion picture theater is frequented by boys of all three grades as shown in this survey. Pictograph 10 estimates that approximately 2 per cent of the boys in each class never attend the movies. The most interested group of movie fans who attend more than once a week make up 15 per cent of the 7th graders, 17 per cent of the 8th graders, and 19 per cent of the 9th graders.

Attendance once a week makes up 39 per cent of the 7th grade total, the same for the 8th grade, and drops down to 34 per cent of the 9th grade. Slightly less than one-fourth of each grade attend once every two weeks, the largest group being 24 per cent in the 9th grade, and the smallest 18 per cent in the 8th grade. If those attending the movies once a month or less are counted as attending seldom, it is found that about one-fourth of each grade attend in this group. Only one or two per cent of junior high school boys never attend motion pictures. It is obvious that there is little difference between classes in the frequency of attendanco at movies, with about three-fourths attending more than once a month.

Psychological opinion states that movies aid students in social adjustment and give them harmless vicarious experiences. On the other hand they may school and motivate boys in crime, cause the child to feel that life is made up of thrills and that he is not getting his share so that he sets about by improper means to find them, and to allow him to escape the real problems of life and live in a world of fantasy. If these contentions are true, is not the boy who attends more than once a week in need of guidance? Is his time being spent to the best advantage?

Since pupils do attend the movies regularly, would it not be possible for the school to advise him of pictures which are to some extent educational? 

Could not some of these be discussed in class at the appropriate time? 

An investigation of the research in the field of the psychological affect of movies, as well as the retention of information and attitudes learned there, would be worthwhile, as much has been written on the subject.

E. Attendance at Parties 

Pictograph 11 showing the frequency of attendance at parties reveals little difference in the size of the group in all classes who attend parties more than once a week. There are more 9th grade boys, however, who attend parties from once a week to once a month, and fewer who never attend. 

The one to three per cent who attend more than once a week are undoubtedly as much in need of guidance as those who never attend. The implication should not be that party attendance is so desirable, but rather that it is a symptom of normal social growth.

F. Type of School Party Preferred 

"What kind of school party do you prefer" brought the responses in Table II.

This is the student's opinion, and is not influenced by having suggestions given to him. Since "no choice", "any kind", and "none" all appear, it will be necessary to distinguish between these terms. "None" is quite clear-cut in that it simply means that the boy does not like parties, or at least school parties. "Any kind" is also self-explanatory. "No choice" means that the boy has left the question unanswered; he meant either "none" or "any kind", which is not very helpful. Comparison with Pictograph 12 reveals that approximately 

18 per cent do not prefer any school parties; however, 28 boys in this grade left this question unanswered and were rated as "no choice". This seems to imply that the "no choice" group was split in their opinion. At any rate, they were not strong enough in their desires to make them known. 

Seventh grade boys give a strong preference to games as their favorite party activity, with the "no choice" enigma being second, and dancing as third.

In 8th grade the "no choice" group has moved up to first place, games second and any kind of party third. Dances are the definite first choice of the older boys, with games dropping off to third place for 9th grade. It is significant to note that only a very small percentage are definitely against parties. The trend is toward heterosexual and social partios as the boys mature.

25G. Desired Frequency of School Parties 

The frequency of school parties desired, if a small charge of about ten cents should be made, is given in Pictograph 12. Whereas, only about one to 

3 per cent of the junior high school boys actually attend school parties more than once a week, 7 to 13 per cent state that they want more than one school party a week. H. Books from the Library 

This question assumes that the reported frequency is quite constant for the whole year. Pupils may have been influenced by the season, or what they have read during the past school year.

An inspection of Pictograph 13 presents the fact that there is a slightly larger percentage of 7th graders who read more intensely than those in both 8th and 9th grade. This is the group who read more than a book a week. 

The boys who do not read any books form a smaller percentage in 9th grade than in other grades. This is an encouraging sign to the English teachers who encourage pupils to go to the library. 

The question arises, is not the group reading more than one book a week over emphasizing this activity too much at the expense of other worthwhile activities? Does this group substitute reading almost entirely for athletics and social recreation? If this is true, there is a need for guidance. 

The decrease in intense reading is understood by noting the wider range of activities in which the older adolescents participate. 

The trend to road more books from 7th to 9th grade is an encouraging sign, as it is generally conceded that the books in the library represent better typos of roading. Many of these books have a definite educational value and those which do not may aid in developing a worthwhile leisure time activity in later life. Is it not possible for teachers to treat the boy who never reads a library book as a guidance problem? Perhaps a slightly different selection of books would induce these boys to participate in this activity. The fact that good magazines and the daily newspaper are also excellent materials to read, and may even take the place of library books in some instances, must be kept in mind.

Table III presents the type of musical instrument played by junior high school boys. The number of participants is influenced by the accommodations of the band and orchestra in school. The lowering of the number of participants from 8th to 9th grade may be explained by the larger field of electives from which to choose in 9th grade. (Pictograph 14)

It is interesting to note that about one-fifth of the boys playing instruments in 7th grade have no affiliations with the school band and orchestra, while 43 per cent of the 8th grade have no school affiliations. The present refinement of the data makes it impossible to distinguish between those playing one and those playing more than one instrument. 

This also holds true for Pictograph 14.
