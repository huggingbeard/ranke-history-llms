FOREWORD 

We have been given the opportunity by our good friend, Dr. George S. Foster, to write a foreword for this very interesting book. We are glad to have the privilege of testifying to the interest and the time that he has devoted from a busy life to the cause of wild life conservation, particularly birds.

The past decade has seen a tremendous change in public sentiment towards all forms of wild life. The spirit of destruction has lessened and that of conservation has greatly increased the country over, and the impetus received from certain governmental activities inaugurated by President Roosevelt, well illustrated by the work of the C. C. C. camps, has speeded up the public mind and made it much more "conservation conscious." 

If only more busy men like Dr. Foster would take up their avocation the work of conservation and enjoy what goes with it, our wild life would be greatly benefitted and the individual, himself, would find that life is much more worth living.

NEW HAMPSHIRE! THE HOME OF SONG-BIRDS 

NEW HAMPSHIRE with its hills and mountains, its singing brooks, and beautiful lakes and rivers, winding paths and wooded roadways bounding those open areas of field and meadow make the Land of 

Scenic Splendor a natural habitat for song birds in their season.

Here in New Hampshire the natural growth of trees and shrubbery furnishes for our little feathered friends a most suitable territory for both food supply and nesting grounds.

The melody of notes afford a choral of music pleasing, restful and recreative to tourist, resident or summer visitor. Even in winter the winter sports seeker will find the winter resident birds in abundance sounding a welcome in musical strain.

Not a few larger and smaller bird sanctuaries are found within the bounds of New Hampshire and here the bird lover is afforded an unusual opportunity to study and observe these songsters amid their natural surroundings. 

The experiences cited in this volume were all gleaned within the bounds of this wonderful State. 

With these large expanses of natural feeding grounds and breeding areas New Hampshire can welcome all bird lovers with this message: 

"A Center For Song-Birds in Their Season."

INTRODUCTION 

This little work is brought forth with two main objectives in mind. The first of these is that to those who are already interested in our song-birds may be given added zest and to those who have never given any special thought to our little feathered friends may come an inspiration to learn something about our songbirds. Secondly it is hoped that every community large and small may bring forth a sufficient number of citizens from these two sources of interest so that there may be organized in each of these communities, "A Bird Club" of their own.

Without our song-birds our vegetation would soon pass into the oblivion and our entire country would become a desert and a broad expanse of wasteland. 

This illustrates the tremendous importance of our song birds as an economic factor.

Then there is the aesthetic and musical side of our every day life. What a silent spectre of monotony every community would be without the beautiful, natural colors of our numerous song-birds! How lonesome everyone would be if it were not for those varied, musical notes emanating from the throats of these little feathered friends.

All children acquire joy and added education in the study and observation of birds. The grown-ups learn by the observation of our song-birds that Nature sends to us every season throughout the year through these birds practical lessons of how to live better and be more cheerful and helpful. 11

Birds and Bird Clubs CHAPTER I General Outline 

Organizing of active bird clubs was never more vital and important than it is at the present time. With the 

Migration Bird Act in force and a fairly good understanding between the Canadian Government and our own it remains for groups of individuals interested in song-birds to take advantage of these improvements. 

Ernest Harold Baynes perhaps has done more to increase the number of bird clubs in this country than any other man. His words of encouragement and his personal interest have helped many a club. Mr. 

Baynes, in his work at Meriden, New Hampshire, has shown what can be done by one willing to give a little time to the matter. At Meriden, Mr. Baynes has established a bird sanctuary, a bird conservation museum and he has personally conducted the pageant made famous by him. To-day in every section of our country, Meriden, New Hampshire is known as the "Bird Village." Here is established a home and breeding place for our song-birds protected from destruction by cats, squirrels, snakes and the like. 

Mr. Baynes has told me that within the bounds of 13

Meriden not an English sparrow was to be found. 

The residents have become so well trained and interested in the subject that now and then when an English sparrow wanders in from some distant point the people get out their guns and Mr. Quarrelsome either falls a victim to them or is driven away. Trespass by a cat into the Bird Sanctuary means a cat funeral and burial the following day in the cat cemetery. Close watch over the song-birds and continual protection by all of the townspeople has accomplished this. Because of this awakening, Meriden has many birds the year round, both summer and winter residents that would otherwise find resting places elsewhere. This grand and noble, worthwhile work should be taken up in all communities for there was never a better time than now for every city, village and hamlet to have its own Bird Club. 

Winthrop Packard, who is a true lover of birds and active in their earnest protection, not long ago told me that he thought the future held out more for real active work on the part of bird lovers than ever before. As secretary of the Massachusetts Audubon Society he has accomplished things of which few others have even dreamed. The example set by him and his methods could well be copied by every state in the Union. Mr. 

Packard is always willing and ready to render assistance to new or old workers in the field. 

We can put forth as the greatest argument in favor of the birds, when we consider the amount of good our feathered friends do and the mass of insects, moths, harmful worms and creeping creatures devoured each year as well as the tons of noctuous seeds eaten by them. The various points in favor of the birds are taken up by numerous writers and do not need any

space here. Again the aesthetic side of bird study favors the presence of all birds the country over. The stand taken by the National Association of Audubon Societies has placed within reach of all several methods of bird study and observation. All of these things add to the interest in learning more of our song-birds. 

What seems to be most needed to-day is a system whereby anyone interested in birds can feel they have a proper basis for promoting the organization of a Bird Club in their locality. This is essentially the purpose of this little work. Many are those who are willing to go ahead but need a little encouragement as to some systematic method.

Once a Bird Club is started and a moderate amount of interest taken in its work the future is rather easy. 

It might be likened to a growing baby. First he comes into being and crows and kicks with glee and earnestness. He is regularly nourished and thus gains weight and strength and soon begins to sit up and take notice of things going on about him. He gathers others about him as he later creeps and all this time he is extending the limits of his activities. Time goes on and he walks and talks and handles various objects with increasing interest and acuteness. To us all this is an old story as we have seen it so often repeated. No less, however, may this example apply to a Bird Club. 

Those who join Bird Clubs are on the whole not equally informed about birds. On the contrary there are generally two or three who know birds fairly well while the remainder really know little about birds but are interested and willing to learn. 

Did you ever think how few people could name the birds which they see almost any day in a short walk? 

What is the reason they do not know these birds?

There are two reasons. They have never taken the time to observe them carefully enough and they have neglected to look them up or have no friend or neighbor who knows and will tell them what this or that bird is. 

Of course all of us know the robin and bluebird, because they are so common about our lawns, gardens, orchards and residences. The time is now at hand when all should extend their knowledge of birds and learn to name a greater number at sight or from their song or call notes. This is just what is most desired as a result of a Bird Club organization. 

To be sure all Bird Clubs should necessarily have among its members those in the community who al ready know more or less about birds, for I would not belittle the most important role such members play in the activities of any club. No club would have a fitting start without them.

However, this may be readily understood, it is the person who knows little about birds but can become enthusiastic by a little personal attention that is truly desired in such organizations. It is such persons who make up the rank and file and can become real workers. 

Herbert K. Job once told me that he felt there were thousands of people who went about their daily work with little regard for the birds, yet, if they were shown the advantages gained from spending a little time in observing our song-birds, they would in a large measure become ardent and enthusiastic bird lovers.

All bird lovers must feel the same about this. In all of us there is a dominant, inert, natural desire to know more about the things of nature. All most of us need is to have this inert desire awakened and our eyes opened to a new life of added interests.

Let us for the moment review the various things which mean a countrywide interest in bird life, the things which when used as weapons for instruction and observation will yield results fourfold. First of these is some one or two people in every community, no matter how small that community may be, who will spend a little time in organizing a Bird Club. This cannot be done without some thought and planning, but if given a little special attention generally works out well.

There was a time when just a few, gathering now and then for bird study, might be called a Bird Club.

The times have so changed things and the laws in favor of our song-birds have become so well molded that the matter of just how one should work along definite lines in formulating a club is worth special attention.

This does not mean that the duties are arduous because, if a well outlined system is followed, just the opposite is true. CHAPTER II ORGANIZING A BIRD CLUB 

When one starts to organize a bird club in a community where one has not previously existed it will be found not a harder, but a little more complicated task, than in a community where a club has once existed and died out.

By this is meant that if a bird club has existed some time in the past there are to be found those who have been interested and for one reason or another have lost interest and kindled no new fires. If such are to be found, it is a comparatively easy task to renew the old interest and renew it with additional enthusiasm.

Where such are not the circumstances one must expect to preach the bird gospel with a louder voice and greater force. There are those who are naturally more or less interested in bird life because they have made some personal observations of a few birds they may see about their own homes, even the English sparrow.

These previous remarks only lead us to certain generalities. Possibly the pioneer essential factor in making a start toward organizing a Bird Club is a person full of enthusiasm. One individual with full vigor and determination can generally make a beginning but this does not mean that any Bird Club should be a 18

one-person organization. That is just what it should not be. Any club to be successful and make the most headway from every angle must be democratic from the start.

However, the essential starting point is an individual who is filled with enthusiasm about bird life, bird conservation and bird spirit. With such an individual to start the ball rolling, the future is made more certain. Such an individual need not necessarily know a great deal about birds. In fact, he need know but very little and possibly only one thing, that an organized group in any community supporting the conservation of our song-birds will do much good and can never do any harm. A person with this enthusiasm should first make up his mind that he is going ahead and organize a Bird Club no matter how small the number of charter members. The next move is for this individual to get some good sound information regarding those in the community who have previously taken some personal interest in bird life and bird welfare. One does not need a large number of names. A few of the right kind will serve better than a larger number, only a few of whom will really work. 

Having decided upon the selected individuals, and this selection should be as broad as possible to include those in all walks of life, these should next be approached in a rather informal way and invited to meet and discuss the matter. The day and time for such a meeting should be selected so as not to interfere with the usual labors of those invited. At this first meeting it is well that the person who has done the inviting has prepared a little outline of what his ideas are. 

Following this brief opening all present should be called upon to express their views and proper atten-

tion paid to these individual suggestions. Possibly an hour will be consumed in this preliminary exchange of ideas. If the ideas of the majority favor going ahead, a formal vote should be put as to whether or not a Bird Club should be organized at this time. Of course, if those who are present should have deep enough interest to be present, an affirmative vote will generally be the result.

Next a motion nominating a temporary chairman and temporary secretary should be made, seconded and carried. The one selected as temporary chairman should then outline briefly the details necessary in order that the essential preliminaries be completed. 

Possibly a good way is for the temporary chairman to conclude his remarks by suggesting that a committee of three or more be named as a temporary organizing committee, to have full power.

This should be considered a very important part of the preliminary proceedings and whether the committee be named by the chair or nominated from the floor, some thought should be given the personnel of this group. For instance one prominent business man, a school teacher, some lady prominent in social circles, a newspaper editor or his wife, a naturalist if there be one in the community and one or two of the older individuals should go to make up this committee. In other words a well selected committee on temporary organization will be very important for the immediate future. Assuming that such a committee is named a further and very important step forward has been made.

Following this action the temporary chairman would act wisely by requesting any of those present to make helpful suggestions before adjournment of this meet-

ing. In this way very often many helpful ideas are brought forward which will assist the committee in its deliberations. A few moments thus spent will help much and tend to broaden the scope of further activities. This should conclude the first meeting and adjournment should be made or a motion made setting a definite date for the next meeting when the committee on temporary organization will submit a report. Following such an adjournment it is often well to informally exchange ideas and have a social chat of a few moments for the reason that any attempt at success in a new move is aided by social intercourse. 

The next few days following this first meeting and previous to a formal meeting of the committee on temporary organization, some member, possibly the chairman, should outline a plan of procedure. First, a suitable name for the new club should be found. It can be called the name of the town, Audubon Society, if one wishes. Many times, however, it seems more fitting and possibly more timely and suggestive if another title be used. At times a name coinciding with some historical event of local interest, or the name of a familiar stream, mountain road or trail serves well. 

Again some rural or primeval sounding name will serve best. A half dozen names could be thought up by members of the committee and be ready for presentation when this committee meets.

Next a short, simple, concise constitution and bylaws should be drawn up. The more brief and to the point these are, the better. Lengthy constitution and by-laws are not necessary. Suitable forms are suggested here that may be useful. The name of the club, its purposes, membership, dues, time of annual meet-

ing, order of business, officers and their duties are about all that is necessary to include. 

It is always rather wise to have several types of membership such as active, intermediate, junior, life and benefactor. This will enable all who wish to join to give the greatest financial help to the club. 

It is understood that the active, intermediate and junior memberships are sustained by annual payments of a moderate fee. For instance the active membership may be one dollar, the intermediate fifty cents and the junior twenty-five cents payable annually.

Active members are those who have reached the age of twenty-one. Intermediate members all those from fifteen to twenty-one years and junior members all under fifteen years of age.

Perhaps in the beginning the intermediate and junior memberships need not be considered as they can be organized later on after the adults have been enrolled. 

The amount for a life membership should be such that when deposited in any savings bank at four per cent it would yield the amount of the annual dues of an active membership. For example if the active membership be one dollar which is four per cent of twentyfive dollars, the life members fee would be twenty-five dollars. It is always wise to deposit all income from membership fees other than the three lowest in classification, as a sinking fund. In other words, all income from fees above one dollar should be deposited in a savings account and those from one dollar down on a national bank account. The former deposit is left for accumulation, only the interest to be used, while the latter can be used for running expenses. In this way the club will always have a reserve fund which grows larger with the years. All dues from the active, in-

termediate and junior members should care for the ordinary running expenses.

A benefactor may be one who wishes to contribute a hundred dollars. These are persons who know the worth of birds and who at the same time feel that they cannot place a hundred dollars to better advantage than in support of this work. 

In some localities it may be wise to go a step further and have a patron list which is open to those wishing to give a thousand dollars. This may seem extreme and unwise yet time and experience have shown that it is this sort of membership that enables a Bird Club to establish a sanctuary, purchase a building for a bird conservation museum, publish instructive literature, etc. Without such very material gifts much of the good work of Bird Clubs and individuals in the past would not have been accomplished. In other words the work in behalf of birds is limited only by the amount of the contributions which the few interested wealthy people give. So much for the consideration of fees. 

Regarding officers, a president, vice-president, secretary, treasurer and an executive committee of five with the officers as ex-officio members of this committee will be all that is necessary. The duties of these officers should be briefly outlined in the constitution and bylaws. Additional special committees can be taken care of later on. Two or three special committees are very essential; such as a membership committee and a program committee. The former committee works most profitably after the permanent organization is completed. They endeavor to arouse interest and add constantly to the membership. This can be accomplished best in a methodical way by taking a list of

the citizens and getting the names of those likely to be interested, and personally interviewing these individuals. 

A program committee is very necessary after permanent organization is completed. They engage the speakers for the various meetings and set the time and place of these meetings. This will be gone into more in detail in a later discussion. 

Following the compilation of these details the special committee on temporary organization should have a meeting and the matters above cited should be presented. The various names suggested for the club, should be brought forward and the best of these selected. It should not be forgotten that any action. taken by this committee is merely in the form of suggestions to be presented later before the general meeting as a committee report for discussion, alteration and final adoption by a majority vote.

Following the selection of a name for the club the outlined constitution and by-laws should be read and discussed. They should be so altered as to meet the varied ideas of the majority of the committee and then so accepted by a majority vote of the committee. The matter of officers, classification of membership, dues and the like should be fully discussed and should be accepted by a majority vote of the committee. Next the work of this special committee on temporary organization should be put into proper form for presentation before the general meeting.

After this is finally brought into proper form a general meeting should again be called for the purpose of considering this special committee's report. First the report should be read in full and following this each part should be taken up separately for dis-

cussion. For example, following the first reading, the name should be brought up for discussion and accepted by a formal motion and majority vote. Next each section of the constitution and by-laws should be read and discussed and accepted by formal motion and majority vote. After the complete report of the committee has thus been accepted the general meeting is then prepared to formally organize as a permanent body.

Nominations should then be opened for the various officers and the officers elected by a majority vote. 

With this work completed the Bird Club is then and there a permanent organization. The meeting should then be adjourned by a formal vote and await the future actions of the officers. The progress and activities of any Bird Club depends much upon the work and energy put forth by the officers. If the officers are competent, they will put their shoulders to the wheel and push with might and main.

There is now a permanent organization and much work has been done. Yet a good deal remains to be accomplished if this Bird Club is to be a real body serving the best interests of the songsters.

CHAPTER III PERMANENT ORGANIZATION AND PROGRESS 

Two things are vitally essential in order that a Bird 

Club be successful and fulfill its purposes. You must have a supporting membership and during the cooler parts of the year monthly meetings should be held with a good speaker for each meeting. That these two things may be successfully accomplished it is quite necessary that special committees be appointed. The 

Executive Committee is the business committee of the club. They should meet at this time and appoint a membership committee and a program committee.

The personnel of these committees should be carefully selected with a good chairman for each, and all the other members good workers. The number making up these committees will be governed by the anticipated size of the membership. Committees of three or five seem to work better than those of larger numbers. 

Duties of the Membership Committee-Following the appointment of this committee the chairman should be notified and given the names of the committee. 

Each member should also be sent a communication informing him of his appointment, the name of the chairman, and a full list of the personnel of the committee. In this communication it is well to outline a tentative plan of work for this committee. It would 26

seem a rather wise move in the beginning to name all who are then members to some place on this committee. In other words make the entire club, as it then stands, a membership committee. The results obtained by this method will very often prove a success. 

Later this committee may be reduced to three or five members. An active campaign should then be launched to obtain new recruits. Now a Bird Club is somewhat different from many other organizations. No one should ever be forced or over urged to join the club. 

Very obviously, of course, as large a membership as possible is desired, because the more members, the greater interest taken in the birds, yet every member should be personally interested in the song-birds. It is not necessary that one should know birds well to become a member. As a matter of fact many Bird Clubs have a membership made up of people who make no strong claim to knowing all of the birds. They are as a rule people who wish to learn more about birds and who wish to lend a little help in behalf of bird conservation. 

Of course some members do know the song-birds very well and have studied them for sometime. While these are in the minority they can serve in a very helpful capacity in encouraging the other members in the work. Therefore in the beginning a well worded application card should be formulated and printed. Have this card headed with the name of the club, the name of the community, the date and then below a list of the classes of members. Opposite each class have the fee and a little blank square. This blank square is left for the purpose of permitting the applicant to make a cross in the square opposite the membership classification he chooses. Somewhere on the application card

it is well to make a brief announcement that all applications should be accompanied by payment of the first fee. In addition it is perhaps not amiss to word a short slogan and place this at the very bottom of the card. Very often a simple worded, well sounding slogan helps to properly finish off such a card. 

When these application cards are printed every member should fill one out for himself and mail it to the treasurer with the fee. The treasurer will make record of this and mail a properly worded receipt to the applicant. Following this the treasurer will mail these applications to the secretary who will index them for future reference. Thus both treasurer and secretary will have a complete list of the membership for future use.

Presuming that all who first joined the Club have now properly filled out their application cards and sent them in it is well to supply each member with a few extra cards that they may obtain new members. 

Right here is a time to make a rather important announcement. It is always an honor to be on the charter membership list. For this reason publicly announce that the charter membership list will remain open for one month or until a specified date. This in itself will encourage others to join early. During the time that the list remains open for the charter membership all who are at this time members should endeavor to get as many new applicants as possible. 

Make out a systematic list of those who would most likely be interested. While conversing with anybody on membership make two points quite emphatic: that they will learn new worthwhile things about birds and that their membership will very materially assist in

the work of the club. Both moral and financial support count for much in this work for the song-birds. 

It is surprising as well as pleasing to see how materially the list will increase and the general interest in our song birds be enhanced. Those who previously have never shown any special interest in our bird friends will come forward and at times even become enthusiastic. A little chat on birds with a neighbor over the garden fence, on the way downtown, or in the street car, while motoring or talking over the phone will yield quite surprising results. There are many who are simply waiting to be shown how really important this work is and how educational it can become. We are sure that through such a procedure, ere the charter membership time limit is reached, the list will have increased very materially. 

There are many people who daily see birds in their garden or in other places but who do not realize the satisfaction that might be gained from this observation. It is these people who will inquire just what they will get from membership in the Bird Club. Here is where the program committee can act cooperatively and assist the membership committee.

Duties of the Program Committee 

Much careful planning and forethought should be put into the work by the members of this committee. 

All those who become members of the Bird Club will appreciate a well formulated program. To make this part of the work a real success, two things seem necessary: First, good talks by those who know their subject matter and second, a well constructed booklet for reference, this containing proper announcement of the

season's activities. Of course the most essential of these two things is a list of good lecturers, as well as a neat, clear and carefully compiled booklet with proper illustrations attractive in itself which should always be sent each member at the beginning of the season's activities.

The Program Committee should meet and get ideas as to who may be secured as speakers. Through the various speaking bureaus of the larger cities, by securing the private announcement pamphlets of many who give bird talks, from those who have had previous experience and a chance to know of some good speaker, by communicating with the officers of Bird Clubs in larger centres and by several other avenues, the program committee can become well informed of the various sources from which to obtain dates and terms.

There are always a few who join every Bird Club who are able and perfectly willing to give up an evening for the benefit of the club. From such members, much can be learned that is worth while and a very pleasant evening can be passed. 

Bird Clubs of adjacent communities often have among their members those who will be very glad to give a talk to the club. In this way a list of tentative speakers can be secured from which you can make a definite selection and secure their services for specific dates.

State Audubon Society secretaries are always willing and glad to speak and assist in promoting the bird welfare work in other ways. 

There is one thing which should be carefully consid ered by the program committee and that is the expense. 

Securing the services of lecturers of national repute,

those whose time is sought by many, is a greater expense than some Bird Clubs can afford at the very beginning, for no Bird Club should exist which cannot pay its way. Merely because it is in a large measure an educational organization and more of an uplift to the community is no reason why it cannot and should not be run on a good business basis. Every Bird Club should give dollar for dollar in return for the investment made by the members in the form of dues. Bird 

Clubs above all others should be self sustaining because if they are not, harm to the real worth while part of bird conservation teaching will result and impair the work of similar clubs both local and national in scope.

The first year of the work of the program committee will prove quite difficult in the average club, for the reason that the committee feels duty bound to have as good a program as possible this first season. This is true for two very obvious reasons; first, they wish to have all who are members feel that they have received a just return from the lectures and secondly, by putting forth a good program, that others, who are not already members will wish to join.

Right here a very important point comes to mind, namely; to have the members bring a guest to each of the meetings. By so doing you will find that new members will be coming in continually for the reason that those who have been guests will realize that the work is fully worthy of the support accorded by one year's dues. Here too, it can clearly be seen how very closely the work of the membership and program committees co-operate and dovetail one with the other.

To again return to the program of the first year. 

For this year's activities try to secure as qualified lec- 1

turers as possible, at minimum expense. If you will but look about it is often quite possible to secure the services of those who will give excellent talks and are very glad to do so for no more than their bare expenses, as for example car fare and lodging. It is often possible to put forward a whole season of lectures on this basis, but one thing must always be guarded against and that is not to lessen the standing of the gatherings. In the first year you may feel that you can well afford to secure the services of one or two real bird talkers of more or less established repute. The program committee should consider this from every point of view, being conservative enough to keep well within the bounds of the financial resources at hand yet not faltering in the courage needed to reach out in order to secure the new members who will be gained by such a step. It is a good committee in any club which is able to grasp the situation and govern themselves accordingly. It is a good plan to have these talks illustrated and for this reason the club should secure the loan of a projection lantern if it does not already have one. Illustrations in the form of lantern slides add much to the interest and also to the educational value of the talks. A point can always be the more clearly made and augmented with lantern slides. 

Every community has individuals who possibly belong to the retired-class. These same individuals however very often may become so interested in the club that they will offer to personally meet the expense of one of the lecturers. Such a thing is not to be solicited yet when it does come, as it sometimes will, by voluntary action, it is always to be accepted.

monthly meetings the program committee should now arrange for the publication of a booklet announcing these meetings and the sending of a copy to each member. In arranging this booklet several things should be kept in mind.

First of all secure the one member of your program committee who seems best suited artistically to get these things together. There will always be one member who will take an exceptional interest in this part of the work and will submit a plan to the whole committee for approval.

Nearly every community has some individual who can draw. In the towns where the school system engages the paid services of an instructor in drawing this is the individual to approach. If this person is approached in the correct manner and the educational side of Bird Club work is explained, he will very gladly make a few drawings for the benefit of the work. Small drawings of different birds in various attitudes, at rest, in flight or perched on a wire, pole or limb are preferable. The artist will be able to bring out the distinctive characteristics of the birds with one or two additional drawings of bird houses and a nest or two.

Having secured these drawings take them to a newspaper editor and he will inform you where to send them to have the proper cuts made. Remember these cuts can be used over and over again so that while the first expense is the greatest it should not be beyond attainment. You will doubtless find some member who will gladly meet this expense and in this way lend a helping hand.

Here is a suggestion for the artist who makes these drawings. One should be a trifle larger than the others, such as possibly a drawing of some well known

bird, the robin, blue bird, swallow or the like. This to be the cover drawing, while the others should be smaller, so that they may be arranged in different places for example the top, bottom or lateral margin. of the pages or elsewhere. The arrangement of these cuts in your booklet adds much to its attractiveness, and increases the interest taken by the members in looking over it. CHAPTER IV WHAT THE BIRD CLUB COULD DO FOR THE SONG- BIRDS IN AND ABOUT THE COMMUNITY 

When the Bird Club of your community is in its third year, a resumé of the activities of this club may not be amiss. Your club was organized for the purpose of arousing interest in the song-birds that come to your community in their seasons. The protection, shelter, supplying food and the study of the habits of our song-birds are some of the activities of the club. 

It is only through an organization of this kind that the real worth of song-birds can be fully realized. Any

Bird Club has as a part of its duty the educating of the public as to what a single little feathered friend means to a community.

Protection an Asset 

Each year we all realize the fact that our fruit trees, timber growths, shrubbery, flower gardens, berry bearing vines and lawns are infected with one pest or another which is detrimental to the particular tree or plant and, if left unhampered, would in time kill them. 

Today the extermination of certain species of trees, shrubs and other growths has become a vital factor in the economics of gardening and forestry. The United 37

States government, through the Department of Agr culture and the American Forestry Association through its magazine have pointed out these facts and brought them squarely before the public. Careful study and observation on the part of scientists has brought forth the fact that should our song birds be taken away, in five years time our forests, gardens, lawns and orchards would be a stretch of waste and barren land. This is an alarming situation and is one that warrants renewed activity in behalf of our bird friends. If it were not for the activities of the birds our cultivated lands, would yield nothing but weeds and mongrel, useless growths.

Practical Saving by Song-Birds 

For illustration many will recall the great black cricket pest that invaded Salt Lake City and made the farmer expect a hayless year. Millions upon millions of these pests were very rapidly killing off all the grasslands. That year by some good turn of an unseen Higher power great flocks of gulls came to the rescue and exterminated the crickets by feeding upon them. To the heroism of these gulls a large memorial has been erected by the grateful farmers and citizens' of that locality.

The annual loss to the farmers of this country from insects and rodent pests is about one billion dollars.

This is a tremendous waste and if it was not for the number of birds we now have it would soon be tenfold greater. Just think what this means to the agriculturist and indirectly to us all! What the farmer loses by insects or over-growth of weeds, we ourselves

as individuals must lose and bear the extra cost thus created when we purchase.

It has been shown that in but two states in the Union where the season's consumption by birds was studied, that one kind of birds alone, namely the Bobwhite eats in a single season one thousand three hundred and forty-one tons of weed seeds and three hundred and forty tons of insects. Multiply this by twenty-four and see what this one specie alone does for the country. The figures are simply astounding, yes! staggering. From the stomach of a single bird there has been taken no less than seven thousand five hundred seeds and insects. If one bird can devour this number in a day, think of the limitless good the birds the country over are doing each day. Birds feed continuously from sunrise to sunset in an average day.

They are busy all of the time and busy doing what? 

Making it possible for human beings to live. This is a true fact.

Beneficial Efforts of the Bluebird 

The little bluebird that each spring comes to your garden and flies about so cheerily is a most artful preserver of your gardens and orchards. Bluebirds feed on insects. Let us suppose one bluebird will eat thirty insects a day, a most conservative figure. What does it mean? It means just this, that one hundred of these pretty little birds right here in your community during a residence of eight months, would devour six hundred and seventy thousand insects. Think of the vast amount of service the bluebird renders to you each year. Pages could be written on such examples. The few cited are enough to warrant any effort put forward in behalf of our bird friends.

Educational Phase 

Thus it is seen that any study of our song-birds by organized education is a work well worth while. One of the purposes of your Bird Club is to place these facts before the general public and thus encourage every one to protect the birds, shelter them, feed them attractive morsels and so bring them about your homes and into your orchards and gardens. The song of most birds is an enchantment in itself, the colors and movements of birds are always entertaining but the real economic value of song-birds is after all the important part of their annual visit to us. 

The activities of your Bird Club during the past three years have brought some of these facts to the minds of many who have previously been uninterested in this important question. Your club has several live wire committees, and all credit should be given to these committees for what has been accomplished during this short space of time. 

Attracting Birds about our Homes

If all who have gardens, orchards, lawns or even limited space about their homes would endeavor to attract the song-birds, they would soon appreciate their real value. There are many ways in which this can be done, such as placing a little feeding and bath shelf outside a window, on the porch, on the lawn or in the garden; placing bird homes on a corner of the dwelling or in the trees about the home; planting shrubbery and trees about the home, for birds enjoy the berries of the shrubbery and nesting advantages of good trees.

SOME HOME-MADE BIRD HOMES

What has Been Done in a Practical Way 

During the past summer the secretary of your Bird Club has received scores of reports from members of the club and others who have placed bird homes, feeding stations, and bird baths and planted berry bearing shrubbery about their homes.

There are several members of your club who, on a comparatively small city home lot, have established small bird sanctuaries. It requires not more than a space twenty-five or fifty feet square to do this. The results of the efforts have been most gratifying. To one of these smaller sanctuaries during the past year, the chickadee, bluebird, house-wren, goldfinch, humming-bird, blue jay, blackbird, catbird, song-sparrow, chipping sparrow, fox-sparrow, junco, cuckoo, purplefinch, yellow warbler, white-breasted nut-hatch, flicker and other birds have been attracted. 

In two of these small lot sanctuaries, house-wrens chose homes for raising a brood and after the little ones learned to fly, the parents immediately went to another wren house and promptly raised another brood.

A pair of bluebirds came to a home in another of these places and raised a brood most successfully. 

This home was located just outside the dining room window and the progress of this little family was closely watched by the children.

The energetic mayor of our city erected a very attractive and spacious purple martin house in his garden and early last Spring, he reported that the purple martins had visited this home but remained only a few days. It is hoped that this bird, once a yearly visitor may return to this same home and remain. The pur-

ple martin is a very beautiful as well as useful bird, and while for several years has not been seen about this community to remain, he promises to return if given any encouragement. More citizens should place a martin home on their grounds and watch the results. 

Not long ago the writer chanced to walk home of an evening with a prominent business man. During the course of the conversation he stated that this summer he had placed about his home several bluebird, wren, flicker and also two martin houses and had erected a substantial bird bath on his lawn. At that time pairs of bluebirds and house-wrens were occupying two of the homes, purple martins had visited the martin houses and daily, birds came to the bird bath and enjoyed the coolness of the water. The numbers of new birds seen about his home and the frequency with which they came and cooled themselves in the bath was a source of daily entertainment for his family and himself. Such an effort, not expensive in itself, if it has proven its worth to this successful business man, certainly would warrant many others to do likewise. The little ones growing up enjoy the birds, and what better pastime can they have than that of taking an active part in the protection and encouragement of song-birds? 

Planting Shrubbery and Trees that Yield Bird Food

Many decorative vines and shrubs as well as trees are planted each year about city homes. Your Bird 

Club, through its educational activities, has endeavored to encourage the selection of such growths as will yield berries for the winter food of birds. The

barberry, bitter-sweet vines, trumpet-vine, mulberry tree, Virginia creeper, wild cherry, black alder, cork tree, frost grape, flowering dogwood, hollyhock, bachelor's button, sunflower, nasturtium, larkspur, butterfly tree, sumac, silver thorn, strawberry tree, and many others may be selected and will be sure to attract birds to the locality.

Reports have come to the Secretary that much has been done along this line. In one of the small sanctuaries, mentioned above, nearly all of these have been planted and the birds are attracted in great numbers. 

The Possibilities on the Larger Estates Within the bounds of one of the show estates in the centre of the residential district those already mentioned and many other species of trees, shrubs and flowers are found. Here in these spacious grounds daily can be seen a large variety of song-birds and the chorus that is heard at almost any time is one to which listening is worth while. The mistress of the estate is a bird lover and fully realizes the economic good that comes from attracting these little feathered friends. Numbers of bird homes are seen everywhere within the bounds of this rather large sanctuary and the babbling brook and prolific growth of large and small trees and shrubs invite hundreds of birds to nest and raise broods annually. Probably no other estate within the residential section of the community has more favorable advantages for the birds and the large variety of birds seen during the various seasons of the year is rather surprising.

The Bird Sanctuary at St. Anselm's College 

Possibly few residents of the community are aware of the extensive bird sanctuary established at St. Anselm's college, Goffstown, New Hampshire. Here one of the hard working members of the faculty has developed a model sanctuary in the two hundred and sixty acres at his disposal. This large tract of land is a veritable haunt for birds the year through. 

Berry bearing shrubs and trees have been set out. 

Winding paths lead one in and out among these high and low growths and midst beds of flowers attractive to numerous birds. Situated as this tract of land is, on an elevation overlooking the Merrimack valley, containing within its bounds an artificial pond, small babbling streams, spaces of marshy-areas and an abundance of pines, hemlocks, spruces, poplars and birches, the birds may dwell here and raise their young unmolested.

More well made bird homes are placed within this ideal sanctuary than in any other area within many miles radius. The birds are fed with regularity and enjoy the bird baths, homes and natural surroundings to their fullest extent. During the past summer hundreds of birds nested in the trees, shrubbery, on the ground and in the bird homes. The rental of the homes costs the birds nothing but the furnishing, and they are very alert in taking advantage of this fact and the protection afforded them. 

The curator of this sanctuary is not alone in his work for the birds. Many of the student body have become ardent bird students and render assistance in the work. When Ernest Harold Baynes, the naturalist, visited this sanctuary several years ago he offered a

prize to the first boy who would be photographed feeding a bird in his hand. Promptly a student at the college got busy and was so photographed, feeding a chickadee from his hand. He, at once, submitted the proof to Mr. Baynes and was awarded a prize. No prouder boy could be found and his prize was well earned.

The writer visited this sanctuary one cold day last winter and in company with the curator went the rounds. As we walked along the paths, he would call the chickadees all about him at will. With one flock of these little acrobats there always came a downy woodpecker. The chickadees would come to the call of the thin whistling notes and alight all about my companion and feed from his hands. They would perch on his shoulders and head and seem to understand that full protection was theirs. At the same time the downy woodpecker would feed from the suet crates. This was a spectacle worth observing. The work being done at St. Anselm's college in behalf of our song-birds is an exemplary one, to say the least. 

What a lasting impression such work will make upon the student body and how far reaching is its beneficial influence.

Field Observation and Study 

During this same week the teachers of the public schools took the pupils for bird walks and gave them valuable instruction about our song-birds and their value to us all. Members of the club went out on a field observation hike guided by an expert in the work. 

This proved to be a very enjoyable part of the program.

Organized Effort 

Organized work in behalf of our song-birds is receiving support from masters of industry, financiers, professional men and women, and others, the country over. The club is endeavoring to do its part to promote the work and create renewed interest in the community. This organization is yet in its infancy. 

However, the active committees are busy and their work is bearing fruit. 

Bird Homes in the Parks and Cemeteries 

Never before have so many bird homes been seen about the gardens and orchards in the city. The The past

summer has seen more song-birds in and about your community than for several years past. This worth while work should warrant the support of all who have gardens, orchards, and decorated lawns. In time the hope is entertained that the community officials will fully realize the economic value of the songbirds and we shall see bird homes in the trees of your beautiful parks and cemeteries. Such an idea is well worth serious consideration as a means of protecting our beautiful shade trees and lawns. Much is yet to be done, but a start has been made and the practical results thus far attained shows human interest, and renders encouragement. We all enjoy the song-birds and should do all we can to invite them to the city and give them protection. Let us make your community a Centre for Song-Birds in Their Season.

CHAPTER VI SONG-BIRD WEEK IN MANCHESTER, NEW HAMPSHIRE 

The week of May 21 to 27, 1922 was celebrated by the Manchester Bird Club as Song-Bird Week. At this time the migration of the song-birds was at its height and the bird talks before the club members during the previous winter had brought about a very considerable renewed interest in the songsters. 

The mayor of the city and the superintendent of schools entered into the spirit of the idea. Mayor 

Trudel issued a proclamation pronouncing the week as "Song-Bird Week," which very materially assisted in starting the movement. Superintendent Taylor wrote a letter which caused added enthusiasm among the school children. On the Sunday which began the week several of the pastors of the churches spoke in their sermons of the song-birds. Thus the week became a real week for study of the songsters. The members of the Manchester Bird Club took charge of the work vigorously, so that it went forward with much impetus.

In all grades of the public schools the individual teachers were permitted, by the principles, to take their children on a walk each afternoon after school hours to study and observe the habits of the birds. 

This proved to be one of the most striking features of 54

the work. The teachers took their classes out into the surrounding fields about the city and instructed them in the ways of song-birds, their habits in nest building, in how to recognize any special bird and their individual habits. This added many names to the roster of the junior membership, while the pupils learned much about the birds and were awakened to renewed interest in the importance of protecting our song-birds. The Manchester Bird Club offered prizes, in the form of bird books, to the pupils of the public schools. In one instance a prize was offered for the display of home-built bird houses. The pupil submitting the bird home that seemed most practical in every way to the judge received a bird book. A prize was also offered to the pupil presenting the best essay of a thousand words on our song-birds. One special part of the program for the school children was an afternoon meeting at which Arthur Edward Wilson of 

Boston imitated the songs and calls of some forty odd birds. Mr. Wilson showed remarkable ability in giving the songs of these birds, and his part of the program appealed to the younger element. The pupils greeted him with a packed house.

P. M. to 9 P. M. each day, was held in one of the spacious rooms of the Carpenter Memorial Library.

This proved to be a very attractive part of the exhibit. Colored plates of all the birds found in this vicinity adorned the walls with a full and detailed description of each bird beneath the picture. Many books of birds contributed by one of the local booksellers were shown. 

The National Association of Audubon Societies fur nished a large quantity of literature, in leaflet form, on birds. These were distributed gratuitously to all of the visitors. CHAPTER VII SONG-BIRD WEEK How to Attract Birds About Our Homes 

On arriving in a strange city or town to live, the first thing one seeks is a home. This is just what happens to our hundreds of birds when they come north in the Spring.

Little houses very roughly constructed and properly placed about our homes, in our gardens or among the fruit trees of the orchards serve to assist the birds in making up their minds to remain with us for the summer. 

If we can but offer a fairly good tenement for a pair of birds we will all soon learn how readily they will occupy it. A serviceable bird house is very easily constructed and practically without cost.

Every house has in the cellar or garrett numbers of small wooden boxes, clean tin cans or bits of roofing material which can very quickly and easily be made into a nice, neat, comfortable home for a pair of birds. 

Also practically every home has an industrious boy or girl who is only too glad to occupy their spare time in modeling such homes.

An ordinary small butter box with an opening near the top perhaps one or two inches in diameter serves very well.

After this modest home is made select a 58

No gar place on the outer shed wall or in an apple tree and with a hook or nail fasten it up at a height of ten to fourteen feet and watch the results. Soon you will notice such little homes will be occupied. den or orchard should be without at least two of these little bird homes. An easy matter to accomplish without cost. Only a few moments of your time is needed. Another thing which very materially attracts birds about your home is food. Every day families throw into the garbage can morsels of food for which. our little songsters would be very grateful.

A very substantial feeding tray can easily be constructed out of a small butter box tacked up on the side of the shed, on the wall of the house outside a chamber window or in an apple tree. Just leave one side of the box open and place bread crumbs, doughnut crumbs or the like in this home made food box and see the birds enjoy it. No cost to this construction and maintenance, yet how appreciable the saving to the gardens and fruit trees by thus attracting the birds. 

Another method of attracting birds about our homes is to plant little shrubs, trees and plants which bear the berries and flowers our birds feed on. A bitter sweet vine, elderberry bush, barberry shrub, sumac tree, larkspur plant or the like will serve to attract many birds to our gardens and any or all of these can be secured at minimum cost or in many instances for nothing except a walk out in the open woods and the digging necessary to secure a good root. 

A little time and proper watering is really all that is necessary. 

Another little particular which will serve to attract birds in the warmer weather is fresh water. 

A shallow basin or old discarded pan with a little clean sand sprinkled in the bottom and kept covered

with an inch or two of water serves the birds well. Here they will come to drink and bathe. The hot days of summer will be made more comfortable for them and such a pleasure to see old and young of our feathered flock gather about such a watering place. Merely take the necessary precaution to place the bird bath out of the reach of cats. Again we have constructed an additional attraction for our song-birds and without a cent of cost. Merely a little time most pleasantly spent.

In the cold winter days hang or tack a piece or two of suet in the apple tree. Birds like this winter feed and it serves to keep them warm. Meat scraps from the table can be served to birds in this way and without cost.

If we are a little more particular about the way bird houses are made, bird baths look or feeding stations appear, one can purchase ready made bird contrivances from the manufacturer.

However, the more rough appearing the better the bird enjoys it and the more homelike it is for them. 

Any industrious boy or girl can do well with what they have laid aside at home.

How simple these little things are for us to do yet how very important to the song-birds. Song-Bird 

Week should see many of these bird attracting devices, either home made or secured from the manufacturer, placed in our gardens, orchards or about our homes.

CHAPTER VIII MANCHESTER, NEW HAMPSHIRE- A CENTRE FOR SONG-BIRDS 

Manchester, New Hampshire, has become a real centre for song-birds. During the past summer far more of the feathered songsters were seen here than for many years. Of course there must be a good reason for this as the past year, in so far as climatic conditions are concerned, has been no more favorable for the song birds than previous years.

Song-birds will go where they find an environment to their liking. Such an environment either naturally or artificially arranged to attract the numerous songbirds will bring about the results. This stands out as one of the great reasons for the tremendous influx of songsters in the past summer, for Manchester has many natural and especially arranged resources that attract the feathered flock. These with the increased interest which the citizens are taking in bird life seem to account for the past fruitful season.

The Manchester Bird Club is aware of the situation and the members of this club mean to put forth every possible effort that each year may see more and more song-birds come here and make their home. If we look back five years we find very few things were done to attract birds to the city. One could go about the 61

city and find scarcely a bird home, bath, feeding station, or nesting supply station about any of the residences. 

To-day one may see, in contrast, in a great many gardens or orchards, about the homes and on the lawns these things, which when added to the natural conditions, make a most inviting spot for the numberless birds on their Spring migration.

It is a well known fact that the reflection of the water in a bird bath will attract birds while in flight even at a somewhat great height for birds enjoy fresh water and a daily dip. When such water reflection has attracted them and they have had a morning bath in the tub they learn to know this is a comfortable spot for them and they remain as a resident for the season. 

Again birds come North early in the Spring, sometimes so early that the late snowfalls cover the feeding grounds, and they seek some spot for food. Coming upon a well-filled feeding station they remember it and will remain in the locality for they know that they can find food at any time, snow or no snow. 

Thus they also will remain for the season and undoubtedly, if alive, will return to this spot the following year.

There are a great many of our birds who can stand cold weather very nicely if they have proper food; such as the robin, bluebird, chipping sparrow, songsparrow; all of whom have been known to occasionally winter about these parts.

Birds, like most human beings, are peculiar in some of their rather personal habits and methods of selfpreservation. For one thing, birds like to build and raise broods in an area where the proper material for nesting is obtainable in fairly good amounts. A well

placed nesting supply station filled with horse hair, cotton, wool, string, moss and lichen will very quickly be found by the birds. If these necessary nesting materials are at hand in the garden or orchard the birds spread the news and quickly decide to build near such a gratuitous supply. This alone adds very much to the pleasure of the industrious birds and one will immediately note the great influx of new birds nesting in and about the area where such a supply station is placed.

Looking further for a reason why birds will choose to come to a special area and remain for the season, we must not overlook the housing problem. For several years past the housing problem for human beings. has been a grave one in the cities all over New England. In no way is it different with birds. The old haunts for birds in many cities have been cleared and used for residential or commercial purposes which necessarily forced our little feathered friends to seek housing quarters elsewhere. 

Most anyone can recall a plot of land near or adjacent to their own home which for years has been vacant and permitted to grow up in underbrush, birch, or similar trees. Suddenly you have seen the factory man or residential contractor come along and cut down this fine bird area and build upon the site a factory or numerous homes. This in itself is necessary and all right but the next Spring the old as well as new birds come to this spot to build as in previous years, and, lo! they must seek another spot. Now if a few bird homes had been placed about the factory or new residences these same birds would find them and immediately begin housekeeping as in times past. Thus the housing problem for our song-birds in the city has become a

more or less economic problem for factory and home builders and owners. Neatly constructed bird homes add to the architectural beauty of our gardens, orchards and lawns and the placing of them for the otherwise disappointed bird is a small and inexpensive thing worth doing.

The Manchester Bird Club members have early recognized these vital factors necessary for the continued intimate friendship of our song-birds. A few years ago every member of the Club was strongly encouraged to place at least two bird homes in the garden or orchard, on the barn or ell, or in some other convenient spot about the home. As a result of this invitation scores of bird homes have been erected all over the city. The school children took an active part in the plan. Those who could not afford to purchase ready made bird homes of some manufacturer just simply became busy and built a few home-made bird houses and put them up. As a matter of fact birds like the more crude and inexpensive homes best. All that was necessary to assist the birds in this way was a very small personal expense and a few moments of time on the part of each member.

The Bird Club members individually reported to the secretary what they had done along these lines, that an accurate record might be kept. They went a step further and kept an accurate record of the number of homes occupied, what kind of birds used them, how many broods were raised and the number in each brood. The results were most satisfactory and this bird tenement work had very much to do with the great numbers of birds seen in Manchester in the past few summers. In addition, take into account the numbers of broods successfully raised and think of the

added numbers that we hope will return next summer. 

Soon the club will start a campaign among its members in order that each may put up two additional homes this coming Spring and to accommodate the now matured, birds raised this summer who, it is hoped, will return with their parents in the Spring.

In addition to the erecting of bird homes many reported that bird baths, feeding stations, nesting supply stations, etc., had been placed about the grounds. This has added much to the comfort of the birds while raising their broods. As a result of this effort numerous birds not seen here for several years were counted in the list of residents. House-wrens were found to have nested in many parts of the city. Purple martins, who had not been here for years, visited homes erected for them and while there is no record of these birds remaining for the brooding, they very carefully inspected the new tenements, it is hoped, with the idea of occupying them another summer. 

The citizens of Manchester have come to a full realization of the truly economic value of our song-birds.

They realize how much the song-bird has to do in the way of permitting us to have a successful vegetable garden, flower garden, fruit orchard, green lawn and well protected, decorative shrubbery. Club. Since that time this biologist has become a most ardent student of our song-birds. His interest in the activities of the Manchester Bird Club has increased and there is hardly a meeting when he cannot be found in the audience.

St. Anselm's College is situated on the Western rise bordering the Merrimac Valley and overlooks the great textile mills of a busy city. This college, some three miles out, is surrounded by fields, and woodland comprising some two hundred and sixty acres. The

The flower gardens, the many colored leaves of the numberless varieties of trees and shrubs, the natural rolling surface, the grass plots and in fact every detail of this wonderful beginning of a rather large private bird sanctuary is a cause for wonder.

The large tract of wooded land bordering the village of bird homes, protects the birds from storms and high winds. It also serves as an admirable feeding ground for the birds and an inexhaustible source of supply for their nesting material. The many birds not using homes for nesting have here the tree, bush or satisfactory ground from which to choose. Undoubtedly hundreds of birds nest and raise their broods within the bounds of this wooded area each year. Every detail has been gone into by Father Damion in order that the birds may feel contented and formally accept the invitation to become a tenant.

CHAPTER X SMALL, PRIVATE BIRD SANCTUARIES 

All club members are deeply interested in observing birds raise their broods, for this is one of the things that all people interested in birds dearly love to see. 

Some bright, warm Spring morning when you are starting out for your day's work, or possibly sweeping off the porch or side walk, a robin flies across your path and you observe that it has a piece of straw or dried grass or a string in its bill. At once your curiosity is aroused and you wish to learn the place where this robin is to build that new home. Again you may be cleaning the porch near a vine trailing up along the trellis when you observe a few twigs and straw skillfully woven in among the branches of this vine in some rather secluded corner. You have made an interesting discovery and as time goes by you observe the birds at work. Finally when a complete nest is made and the lining finished, the mother bird begins to rest for laying the eggs. The male bird perches himself nearby and sings his melody in a very proud and joyful tone.

In the course of time the young are hatched and both birds begin the feeding of these fledglings. They grow very rapidly, and some morning, as if the whole 77

nest had exploded, the young are gone and the nest is vacated.

Such an opportunity for observation is certainly to be encouraged. A vine proves a good site for nest building for some birds that would not use a nesting box. These birds such as the song-sparrow and chipping sparrow, you will have with you if you have the vine. Some birds prefer a tree to build in and, if you have a tree that suits their fancy, they will likely select it for a nest. The presence of the tree ensures the remaining of the bird with you for the time necessary to raise one or two broods. Such birds are the robins, purple finches, cedar-waxwings, yellow throated vireos and many others. A cherry tree, towering maple, lofty elms and graceful lilac will serve to encourage the birds to nest about your home.

While this is all fine and much to be encouraged, yet the work of encouraging the birds to come about your home is only started. The bluebird, house-wren, chickadee, tree-swallow, phoebe and the like demand some home of special construction. Therefore the establishing of small bird sanctuaries by members of a club is of great import.

The Executive Committee of a Bird Club should encourage members to erect such bird homes and thus materially increase the number of birds about each residence. Not only will such a movement encourage the birds to come and remain with you, but it will also serve to properly protect the fledglings until they are matured sufficiently to take wing and support themselves.

There are very few people who cannot afford to give up some space for the erection of a few bird houses. This is rather a small expense and a matter

requiring nothing other than a few leisure moments, yet what a large amount of real pleasure one gets from observing the birds that occupy these houses. 

A properly compiled form letter to each Bird Club member encouraging the erection of two or more bird houses on the grounds about his home will bring results. The Executive Committee will be very much encouraged by the reports received from members who have placed such homes for birds about their own grounds.

When sending out these form letters, a request should be made that the members make a simple record on a postal card of the bird homes they have erected and mail it to the secretary of the club. In this way a record can be kept of the work accomplished. At this time several firms making bird homes should be notified of the matter and a full list of the members forwarded to each firm, with the request that they send proper literature to the members. Thus you will place before all members numerous designs of bird homes at various prices. It is surprising what an interest the members will take in looking over these illustrated pamphlets and in selecting the homes they wish to place about their grounds. If these little details are gone into with the proper spirit, the move will certainly yield most encouraging results and reap a harvest of benefit for the birds and a world of pleasure and enjoyment for the members. Such a move will also arouse the interest of other than club members, for it is surprising how this news spreads. Recently in the Manchester Bird Club the Executive Committee sent out such form letters and several bird house manufactures forwarded literature. Soon many inquiries were coming in as to which were the better houses to

get and just where should they be placed. One evening soon afterward I was walking home with a friend who was not a member of the Bird Club. During our conversation he told me that recently he had erected on the grounds about his home two purple martin houses each containing twenty-eight apartments and several houses for bluebirds, wrens and flickers. After telling me about the bird homes he had put up he remarked, "I suppose I should join the Bird Club and thereby be a real member." Already he was encouraged in the work of assisting the birds. You may rest assured it was not long before his morning mail contained an application blank for all in his family. This is one illustration of the work that can be encouraged in behalf of birds, if the club has a capable Executive Committee.

Some members of the club may feel that they cannot afford even the small expense necessary to purchase bird homes ready made. The United States

Government under the department of Agriculture publishes a pamphlet on bird house construction. These pamphlets are obtained at a very slight expense, five or ten cents. From a very casual study of this government literature any boy can learn to make a bird house that will satisfy the several varieties of birds. Such homes, can be constructed out of wasteboards and made to look very neat and be very satisfactory to the birds. The form letter sent out should contain mention of this government pamphlet and how to obtain it. In this way no one will feel that they cannot place two or more bird homes in their garden. All that is necessary is a little time spent in studying the plans outlined in the government pamphlet and the use of waste boards, saw, hammer and nails.

Of course all kinds of birds will not be attracted by two or more bird homes. However the placing of bird homes in the garden or orchard will give many birds an opportunity to begin house-keeping if they desire. Attract birds to your garden or orchard and you will soon learn that they will take advantage of the homes you put up.

One of the best methods of attracting birds to any garden or orchard is to place a feeding station in some well selected spot and keep it well supplied with food. 

This does not necessarily mean that one must purchase high priced grain, sunflower or bird seed for such a station. Of course there are many birds such as the purple finch, goldfinch, rose-breasted grosbeak, blue jay, etc., that are particularly fond of sunflower seeds.

There are also many birds that enjoy quite as much, crumbs from the table, stale bread either white or brown, doughnuts, apples, bananas, cracker crumbs and the like. In cold weather, meat scraps, such as would be discarded, are excellent food for birds in the cold, stormy days of our winters. These can be collected in a proper receptacle and once a week ground or chopped up and put into the feeding station. 

A few simple rules in making and placing a feeding station should be observed. A spot should be selected which will be protected from the North and West winds and near some small tree or low shrubbery. It should have a weather-proof roof and the feeding shelf be protected from the rain, snow and sleet. The weather-cock feeding station outlined in a pamphlet published by the Government serves very suitably and can be easily made at home. There are many of these stations on the market procurable at a nominal price. By thus attracting birds to your little private

sanctuary you will soon find the bird homes you put up will be occupied. Birds figure things out in their own bird way, and, if they can readily find food supply for their young and themselves, they are pretty sure to remain with you. You will also find them returning from season to season.

Possibly one of the best means of attracting birds to the spot you have set aside for the sanctuary is to have a bird bath. It is a well known fact that the water in a bird bath will carry its reflection so that birds in flight have learned to very readily locate watering places. Once a bird acquaints itself with the location of a well-kept bath you may be sure daily and regular visitors will be the result of your effort. 

A bird bath is very easily constructed from a shallow basin, metal sink, hollow stone or wooden basin. for birds seem to enjoy a bath more when it is rather roughly constructed. The only thing necessary is that it be kept well supplied with fresh water which should not be too deep. A pool of water from one to two inches in depth serves best. A little clean sand put in the bottom makes it more attractive. The bird bath should be placed in the open and protected from the cats and a few bright shells placed about the edges. 

Another little thing which will attract birds is to place thread, wool, bits of cotton, étc., about the garden which will supply the birds with plenty of nesting material. This little attention will invite many birds to build nests in the trees and bird homes. There are nesting supply stations made by bird home manufacturers which serve as an additional attraction to the garden and home. Birds readily use the nesting materials put out for them and they seem to appreciate the selection made for them. It is not necessary to

have any special supply station as little bundles of hair, wool, and cotton hung about the limbs of trees serve very well. Of course a protected supply station keeps the material dry and, if desired, one of these little covered nesting supply stations can be made very inexpensively. 

A competent Executive Committee will bring all of these matters before the members so that they may put them into practical use.

Here in Manchester at the present time, we have several well planned Bird Sanctuaries. Members of the Manchester Bird Club have taken up the practical suggestions made and these private sanctuaries are proving a great rendezvous for the song-birds. I have several neighbors who have gone rather extensively into the small sanctuary idea. The personal interest these people take in observing the various birds that visit these attractive sites is most gratifying. 

For several years the house-wren has not been a frequent visitor here in the city. However, since these various private sanctuaries have been completed, these active, joyous little songsters are returning and this year they are quite plentiful. No one can be made happier than by listening to the full, vigorous, cheerful song of the active house-wren.

Small private bird sanctuaries constructed on a small city lot, in the garden or orchard will yield compound interest. Here the birds are attracted, they learn that they are protected, they build and raise their young and return to the same place from year to year. 

How gratifying to any one to see these little songsters come to your window, perch on the wire running across the garden, singing from the fruit trees, etc.!

Again what a pleasure one can find in helping the birds

to gain a good place for the raising of new families each year!

Last but not least the small, private bird sanctuary attracts those birds to the orchard and garden where they will help to protect the fruit trees, shrubs and flowers. The sanctuary may be small but once tried, will surely always be continued, and now and then an addition to the appointments will give pleasure to the builder and to the songster.

CHAPTER XI PLANNING THE HOME GARDEN AND LAWN 

An average city, residential lot has a fifty foot frontage with a depth of one hundred feet. This gives considerable space on one side of the house and in the rear between the residence and the garage. 

Probably the lawn would occupy the greater part of the front and south space while the flower garden and a few fruit trees would take up the space in the rear. 

Of course there would probably be some shrubbery bordering the front and possibly part of the south edges of the lot. This shrubbery not only adds to the attractiveness of a home but also furnishes a good resting area for song-birds.

In planning for a bird sanctuary about a house located on a lot of this size, a ground plan should be drawn. On such a plan, location for each part of the equipment for a sanctuary may be sketched.

The ordinary residence would be expected to have at least two shade trees bordering the street which would probably be some kind of maple, ash or elm.

Such trees have very good trunks for the placement of bird homes. On the lawn in front of the home could easily be placed a white birch on one side of the entrance walk with a blue spruce or similar evergreen tree on the other side. About the edge of the veranda could be placed numerous shrubs. 85

On the south side, where the lawn stretches back to the rear could be placed a white flowering dogwood, a tamarack, an ash and a red flowering dogwood tree.

These can be so arranged so as to be decorative, produce shade and furnish a very good surface on the trunks where bird homes can be placed. The branches and foliage would afford perching and nesting shade areas for the song-birds. It is a good plan to erect a trellis for a bitter-sweet and Dutchman-pipe vines, one at each end of the veranda.

At various points about the rear section of the lot could be placed one or two apple trees, a cherry tree, a pear tree and a Russian mulberry tree. These could be so arranged as to give ample room for a good flower garden. It is a good plan to have also a corner of evergreens such as red cedar, dwarf pine and some Japanese styrax, black alder and a sumac tree. This would be attractive for the birds.

At the rear of the lot, running parallel with the line, can be built a high, wooden trellis of neat, architectural design. The upright posts of this trellis should be set in cement to prevent decay. About the base of this trellis can be planted bitter-sweet and trumpet-vine which will eventually weave itself around the woodwork and make a very good boundary running parallel with the side of the garage, a very attractive nesting and perching area for the song-birds.

To complete the setting the entire remaining North and South boundary lines could be margined with a barberry hedge along one and an ibota hedge along the other. These hedges also furnish very good nesting sites for the song-sparrows, catbirds and other birds.

Parallel with the underpinning of the rear of the

home, a little plot can be set aside for a butterfly-vine, a small pine tree, a fleur-de-lis area and possibly one other small running vine.

With this work done the next thing would be to place the equipment for the sanctuary. For a sanctuary of this size such equipment would include a bird bath, feeding station, nesting supply station, one or two smaller mural feeding stations, possibly in addition a rock-away bird bath and half a dozen bird homes.

Every part of this equipment must be placed in a carefully selected spot. The bird bath should be set in the centre of the lawn on the south side of the house. 

It is a good plan to have one or two of the trees near, at a distance of eight or ten feet. This affords a resting place for the birds before and after bathing. The bath should always be well filled and a few bright colored shells and stones placed in the bottom will attract attention. This bath should be graded in depth so as to accommodate birds of all sizes. 

The feeding station is best in the rear, possibly near the flower garden and it is a good plan to have one or two evergreen trees at each corner to attract the birds. A sheltered or wind-less feeding station is the best type. The sheltered ones are rimmed with glass, thus creating heat from the sun's rays on cold, windy days. Keep this feeding station well supplied with millet seed, sunflower seed, meat and suet cones and bread and meat crumbs. The daily table waste is very good when run through the meat chopper. Such food affords both nourishment and warmth. It is well to set the post supporting the feeding station in cement, thus preventing decay.

The nesting supply station should be placed twenty

feet or so to one side of the feeding station and during the winter months could be used for an additional feeding station. During the nesting season keep it well supplied with hemp, horse hair, wool, etc. The birds will come to it and it will be surprising how much of this material will be used. Set the supporting post in cement to insure longevity. 

On the south side of the house near one of the windows, place a small wall-set feeding station. The selffeeding kind with points of attachment for suet cones serves best and will attract many birds for close observation and also bring the song notes and melody near. It is also a good plan to place one of these mural feeding stations on the end of the vineway at the rear of the lot. Here the birds will enjoy the food, shelter and rest in the vine. 

A very good idea is to gather some old cobblestones and at one corner of the vineway build a circular, rockaway bird bath. Divide the top into three triangular sections with cement bottom. These sections are so arranged as to have one two inches, another one inch, and a third half or three-quarters of an inch in depth thus affording good baths for birds of all sizes. The shade afforded by the vine will keep the water clear and cool. 

Half a dozen bird homes can be put at various points, a wren home near the end of the veranda, on the south side of the house, a second home could be placed at the south east, and a third at the east and toward the north of the house. If possible, face these homes south. Two of these homes could be for bluebirds and finches. 

One or two tree-swallow or flicker homes can be placed on the south side of the garage. This is a quiet

In the spot and will soon be occupied in the season. centre of the rear part of the lot place a bluebird home eight to fifteen feet above the ground on a metal post set in cement. About the base of this pole plant a bitter-sweet or ivy vine and soon the post will be covered with the entwining vine covered with foliage. 

The only care necessary is to keep the vine trimmed back from the bird home at the top. Birds will not occupy homes set among foliage and covered in, for they prefer and select homes that are placed in the open.

If this plan is carried out, perhaps not exactly to the letter, but approximately as suggested, the results obtained will be most gratifying.

It may take a season or two to get this small sanctuary well occupied, but once the birds learn of the protection afforded with food and water at hand and the comforts awaiting them they will come year after year and occupy the same homes. 

Of course larger residential lots afford a wider range of action and the expansion is only limited by the area at hand. However, the small residential lot as above outlined will give very gratifying results and returns in interest reckoned in the high degree of pleasure given to the owner and his family. 

Every bird lover can have a sanctuary no matter how small the area. Every city residence as well as all country sites should have at least a small sanctuary established. Thus will song, melody and real nature be brought to one's door and even to one's breakfast table.

CHAPTER XII SONG-BIRD WEEK 

The annual celebration of Song-Bird Week, seems to be one of the very best ways to keep up the enthusiasm, not only in the local Bird Club, but also in the community. Song-Bird Week is an innovation which had its birth here in Manchester, New Hampshire, yet the idea has been adopted by several Bird Clubs in the past two or three years and the idea is rapidly spreading. 

To set aside one week each year during which a fully, detailed program furthering the welfare and conservation of song birds is most fitting. Here in 

Manchester the Bird Club has for the past six years set aside the second week in May each year. All those interested in song-birds, and in fact many of the citizens of the city, look forward to this annual event with pride.

One thing of great import which has been shown during Song-Bird Week is the renewed and added interest taken by the school children of all ages. The juvenile membership always take on added interests and this seems to carry through for the entire summer vacation.

For proper observance of this week early and properly outlined plans must be formulated. The special 90

committee having this in charge must begin early in the year and have interesting matters ready for release each day during the week. Every day should have something significant and important as a Head Liner. 

First of all in the preparation the committee must outline publicity to be released to the Press at regular intervals during the two weeks previous to opening of the Song-Bird Week. This publicity should be in the form of announcements outlining the idea and what it stands for, publishing in full the general program prepared for the week and having appear each day's program in minute detail.

The Superintendent of Schools is interviewed and of him is obtained a written endorsement of the idea and this endorsement is published in the public press. This is very helpful in furthering interest in the idea. The mayor of the city is asked for a proclamation and thus by an official act the week is set aside as Song-Bird Week. This proclamation is generally published in the local papers the latter part of the previous week. 

The public learns what is really meant by this week and the public officials of high standing fully recognize its worth as an educational feature. The principals of all the schools are sent letters encouraging them to request their various subordinates to spend ten or fifteen minutes a day during the week in song bird instruction. It is a good idea, when the local club treasury will permit, to send to the National Audubon Society and secure charts of birds to place in each school, or even better, one in each school room. 

All public school teachers are asked to take the children of their room on a thirty minute or one hour field walk at the end of the afternoon session, each day dur-

ing the week. Here in Manchester this has proven to be an educational move of more than ordinary, practical value. Through the medium of these open field and observation walks, the school children of all ages learn to recognize the various song-birds when they see them and also learn the song of each one. We have found that the school children get a great deal of pleasure out of this hour of recreation and come to the point where they miss it when the week is over. 

In many of our schools the teachers continue these walks at various periods for a month or more. Those who previously had not been especially interested have become ardent enthusiasts in song-bird study. Where a teacher requires this enthusiasm, we find the pupils follow with the zest and vigor of their youth. 

In addition to this teaching of the song-birds and their habits to the school children, a fitting prize is offered for the best essay of not less than five hundred words covering some phase of song-bird life. This prize is usually in the form of a five dollar bill or some good, well illustrated book on birds by a well known author, written in such a style as children of school age enjoy. This has proven its worth during the past five years and many very creditable essays have been written. It is a good plan to have three judges of these essays; perhaps the Mayor, Superintendent of Schools and an Editor of a local paper. 

This will augment their personal interest in the work. 

For the past three years the Manchester Bird Club has also offered an additional prize to the school children for the best home made bird home built by either boy or girl. No adult assistance is permitted in the construction of these homes except general advice in drawing the plans. The construction itself must be

done by the boy or girl submitting the home for the contest. One thing we have found of importance for this bird home building contest to be a real success, is that the homes must be turned in by Wednesday of the week designated as Song-Bird Week and that these homes become the property of the local Bird Club. We have found this quite necessary for two reasons, 1. they can be exhibited in a group in the window of some local merchant, marked with the name of the builder thus engaging additional public attention; 2. by having these homes become the property of the local Bird Club, this club can later on use these homes as a very material part of the bird sanctuary movement. 

Each year the Manchester Bird Club will offer a third prize for the best poster designed by members of the drawing classes of these various schools. These posters are of course to illustrate some special songbird, group of birds, sanctuary plan or some other special phase of song-bird life. It is hoped that this added feature will draw forth many good posters which will be collected and displayed in a window of one of the local merchants. All of these facts will be given publicity in the local press, far enough in advance to permit time for their construction.

An outline such as Song-Bird Week necessitates would be as follows:

Sunday, the Clergy of the community are asked to include in their sermons of that day, something having reference to the aesthetic, musical, educational or economic value of song-birds.

Monday morning in the local papers an announcement of the Sunday sermons will appear and also three or four short paragraphs from nationally known ornithologists.

In addition to offering the prizes, previously mentioned, to the school children, it is a good plan to extend this offer to the Boys' Club, Girls' Club, Boy

Scouts, Girl Scouts and Junior Y. M. C. A. and 

Y. W. C. A. members. By doing so, added competition of a friendly kind is injected into the whole idea and much more good can be accomplished. Most 

Boys' Clubs have a carpenter-shop and here we have found the boys will, through the interest created, carefully study bird home construction and be ready in the 

Spring to exhibit some very fine, well modeled bird homes. 

Tuesday is a good day to have some imitator of song-bird notes go through the various schools giving short talks on our song-birds, and also imitating their various songs and call notes. This we have found to be of very marked assistance in furthering the study of our song-birds and teaching the children how to recognize the songs of the various common birds. This man should be engaged for the entire day and during that time cover as many schools as he can. To close this day properly this same person should give an illustrated talk on our song-birds in some spacious hall in the afternoon when school is over, for the school children only. No charge is made for admission, but tickets are distributed in all of the schools for the pupils to use at the door.

Wednesday is the day generally set aside for an open field observation walk for all members of the Bird Club and their friends. This walk is guided by a club member well qualified to take the lead and to explain things as they appear. The field observation walks have proven to be very popular and as the school children are invited to participate, if accom-

panied by their schoolroom teachers, it is very satisfactory from an educational point of view. 

As a general thing all should be instructed to bring a box lunch and at the proper time the group gather and enjoy an open air supper. This is very pleasant as the place chosen is in a wooded area and the songs of many birds add to the pleasure. There is always something new for the experienced bird student to learn, and many previously unseen things for the newer members. The children always find much to learn about our little feathered friends. Those who possess them, should always bring their cameras and take pictures of the birds.

Thursday is set aside for the annual meeting of the club and election of officers for the ensuing year. The annual reports are read and committees named for the work for the coming year. All points of bird study are gone over at this meeting and many things come up for discussion and clarification.

Friday is the day set for selecting the prize winners in the various contests. The judges go through the material offered and select the best. 

Saturday these winners are announced in the papers and the prizes awarded. For the winners and those worthy of special mention this is a very real day. 

Here in Manchester, a great many of the public school teachers are ardent song-bird lovers. Many of the schools maintain feeding shelves outside the schoolroom windows. It has been found that these feeding stations attract many of our most beautiful song-birds and a full record of the kind and number of feeders is kept. Not a few of our public schools maintain bird baths as well. Such a real interest in song-bird conservation deserves much commendation.

The Manchester Bird Club maintains permanent quarters very kindly given them by the trustees of the Carpenter Memorial Library. Here is found a well kept library of bird books, collections of birds, eggs and nests for close-up study for the members of the club. The walls are decorated with framed pictures of real bird scenes as taken by the club members. Some of these pictures cover the winter feeding of the birds showing them as they are feeding from the trays and scratch grounds. Some members are seen feeding birds from their hands and in many other poses entertaining the birds out in the open. There also can be found photograph records of the various exhibits and of groups of members during the field observation walks.

Here in this room, during Song-Bird Week, can be seen the bird homes and posters, presented for prizes, by the various contestants. This is kept open each day for inspection by the public, during the entire week. 

In addition to this, many groups of bird homes are shown by the various bird home manufacturers. The type and price of each home is clearly marked and the name of the manufacturer clearly printed so that all can have an opportunity of ordering homes where they desire.

This exhibit is one of the strong features of the week and is made as natural as possible with various types of birds properly arranged in the woodland scenery. During the week photographs of this exhibit are taken and displayed in the daily paper. 

The Librarian, an ardent song-bird lover, takes much personal pride in decorating the interior of the library by displaying several song-bird posters about the halls and reading rooms. A printed announcement

is placed at the entrance announcing that Song-Bird Week is made a special feature, and special bird books and magazines are ready for distribution and reading. 

Everything relative to song-bird life is correlated and properly displayed about the building thus adding much to the interest and educational work of this week. 

During the week, the Librarian has published in the daily paper, a full list of the books on birds to be found at the library. Not a little of our success during 

Song-Bird Week can be placed at the door of the Librarian for her earnest endeavor to make this a real worthwhile work.

In special rooms about the library can be found a large display of books on birds placed on sale by one of the local book sellers. In another room will be found special displays made by the Boys' Club, Boy Scouts and other organizations of a like nature. Here can be viewed these homes, properly marked with the type and name of the maker.

A careful record of the number of visitors is kept and it is surprising what a real interest the public takes in this work. All in all here in Manchester the club members have found that the observation of Song- Bird Week, when properly organized, has proven to be very entertaining, attractive and educational. We have practiced the observation of this week for six years and the results would warrant all other Bird 

Clubs following such an example.

CHAPTER XIII PLANTING OF TREES AND SHRUBBERY FAVORABLE TO BIRD LIFE 

Many of our song-birds seek special forms of foliage especially when the nesting season is at hand.

Some birds like to nest near the ground, others at a medium height while there are those who prefer rather high points for nesting sites. Some birds will select open foliage and seem to prefer to have the nest in the semi-open leafing sections. On the other hand there are those that seek the very dense and inaccessible foliage for the home coming of the young. While some song-birds prefer trees to build in, others have a strong preference for hedges and there are some that always seem to select a vine. Song-birds are quite as peculiar as human beings when it comes to selecting a site for a home.

There are trees, shrubbery and vines that yield fruit and berries. Here a bird may not elect to build its nest, but it will come daily for food, and because of this special attraction it will build in some nearby location to be near the source of supply of the fresh fruit and berries that it likes.

These are the facts and if one will make a little study of the desires of our song-birds in the way of foliage, fruits and berries, the increase in numbers of 98

the birds about the home will repay for the amount of time given to this study. Not only is the study of the various kinds of attractive foliage necessary but quite as important is the knowledge of properly placing these trees, shrubs and vines. Birds enjoy good perching sites and they soon learn and communicate to each other the locations of the good, protected perching places which are combined with the ones yielding the food they enjoy. As an illustration, the bitter-sweet vine grows very nicely over a trellis or about the corner of the veranda and gives forth abundant shade and affords a good shelter for song-birds in hot summer days. This vine will weave its way in and out among the cross bars of a trellis and thus give very good support as a base structure. It also yields an abundance of berries that in the Fall turn yellow as they ripen, and afford much food for the winter birds and the early Spring migrants. 

A border hedge of barberry will bring forth an abundance of red, ripe berries for the winter birds. 

This hedge is thick, and serves as a very good nesting site for the song-sparrow, chipping sparrow and catbird. An ibota hedge, while not thick, yielding only a blossom spud for food, will nevertheless attract many song-birds and keep them for the season.

The trumpet vine always has its place about the corner of the garage or veranda. While it is not as important for shade and shelter it yields the sweet nectar of its blossoms so pleasing to the hummingbirds. The nasturtium and larkspur which are also very decorative in the garden plot, will be sure to attract the humming-birds every year. 

A few elderberry bushes should be set out in some exclusive corner of the home lot. These, although not

affording special nesting facilities, will give the Winter and Fall birds ample food. Elderberries are enjoyed by many birds who are especially fond of this fruit. 

There should be at least one good cherry tree on the premises. The robins, rose-breasted grosbeaks and orioles are, as well as the cedar-waxwings, especially attracted by this tree. Then in the Spring time the cherry blossoms supply beautiful flowers, charming to the eye.

Here and there should be placed either a single black alder, Japanese styrax, smooth sumac, scarlet haw, bird cherry (black and red), beach plum, Canada plum, a service berry tree, blackberry, sheepberry and honey locust, or place these in a group. These will serve to attract many very happy songsters who, because of the fruit available, will remain the season through and nest near at hand.

Somewhere on the lawn should be placed a blue spruce tree. This attractive, decorative growth affords a very thick protective area for the nesting site of song-sparrows, chipping sparrows, cedar-waxwings or the catbird.

The red, and other forms of cedar trees, while not yielding any food supply are very decorative and when placed near the feeding stations will serve as admirable perching areas for our little song friends. This can be said also regarding the tamarack tree, a very decorative tree, which, when placed within easy access of a feeding station and bird bath affords the bird shelter and a drying stand, following the food taking and the bath.

There are three very important trees which should not be overlooked the mountain ash, and red and

white mulberry trees. The former yields its great clusters of red berries in the late summer and early fall, affording an ample supply of food for the congregating robins and cedar-waxwings. This tree is practically indispensable about any home. The two mulberry trees attract all birds in great numbers; more particularly so the cedar-waxwings and purple finches.

The purple finches will gather in great flocks in the white, sweet, or Russian mulberry. The fruit seems to be especially appetizing to them.

Somewhere about the grounds should be placed a fir balsam. The chickadee enjoy these trees which are very handsome in appearance in winter. A horse chestnut tree seems to afford very special home making and housekeeping advantages for the waxwings and also for the yellow-throated vireo.

The white ash, great laurel and sassafras trees afford very good nesting sites for the robin. The fine large branches give off well moulded forks in which these birds can place their nests and feel safe far above the moving masses and wandering cat. Many such trees, shrubs and vines could be mentioned but be sure to have at least six or eight different kinds of trees about the home.

The usefulness of the various types of maples, elms and other larger trees must not be forgotten. Whereever is found a fine, large elm there is sure to be located a pair of orioles. Robins like the maples, and of course there should be one white birch tree somewhere about for no home seems complete without one of these trees, and the birds enjoy the shelter. 

Border the garden walks, protect the feeding stations and bird baths, and decorate the lawn and other open spaces by carefully placing these foliage, flower

and food producing growths always combining beauty, placement and economy as the main features when placing these trees and shrubs. Give these growths a few years to develop and take good care to fertilize them well and your full reward will be gained by the melody of song close at hand during the warm season and the gathering of cheery, active, invigorating bird life in the cold days of the winter months.

CHAPTER XIV THE ENGLISH SPARROW 

This rodent of the feathered tribe is always a troublesome, discourteous, gruff, boisterous bully, ever belligerent. Wherever the English sparrow is found there is also noted fewer of the other more welcome birds, sometimes even none at all. If you will watch these sparrows you will notice how they quarrel among themselves and fight all the other birds, regardless of size. If the English sparrow had one good trait, we might forgive him for the other sins he commits, but it really is a well founded fact that this vagrant fellow has never been accredited with even one good deed. 

Selfish, jealous, strong, self willed, this little bird seems to ever play the part of, "the dog in the manger. What he cannot use he is not willing that others should enjoy. It is true that at times he acts as a scavenger if it is necessary for his self sustenance. 99 Years ago the English sparrow might have served in the cities as a feeder from the waste of horses, thus helping to keep the streets clean, but this fast disappearing need has been his only act of any real economic value.

The biological survey of the United States Government has made a very careful study of the English sparrow and has unequivocally declared this bird to be of no particular economical value. 103

Years ago a few pairs of these birds were brought over from England and liberated. From these few pairs millions of birds have been brought to life and have spread all over this great country. This bird, being very hardy, could subsist where other birds would perish. Coming from a relatively colder climate they could withstand the hardest winter and thus have increased very greatly in numbers. 

Any other variety of bird accorded the chance would have done as well if given an opportunity to have food the year through. This trait of being able to exist on little or nothing of value, has given the English sparrow its strong grip in numbers, yet it returns practically nothing for this wonderful strength Nature has bestowed upon it. It will build its nest anywhere and use almost any material. Destroy this nest one day and another will appear in its place by the next sunrise.

Thousands upon thousands of other birds are driven from house gardens, parks, commons, country sites and other natural nesting and breeding areas by this pugnacious culprit. There is hardly any other bird, with possibly one or two exceptions, that does not possess a marked economic value. These exceptions might possibly include the English starling and the purple grackle. This in itself would warrant the extermination of the English Sparrow, because if we leave one single pair soon hundreds of others of this bird will be bred.

It has been found that one English sparrow will devour half a pint of grain in one day. He is not willing to forage for food as he is lazy and will not work for the community good. Then again beside being lazy he is absolutely dirty. No other word ex-

presses it quite as vividly for he will smear paint, daub windows and mess things up generally. 

Just prove this for yourselves if you so desire. 

Place half a dozen bird homes about your residence, maintain a feeding station and bird bath and see how quickly the English sparrow will dominate the entire place if you give him half a show. The only way to have these homes occupied by worth while birds, birds of real proven and economical value, is to entirely, once and for all, drown out the English sparrow. 

They will not become easily discouraged and the only way is by trapping them and doing away with them. 

The trapping method seems the safest as the use of poisoned food may be very disastrous to other birds of value.

The best practical illustrations of what can be accomplished is found at the Bird Sanctuary in Meriden, New Hampshire which stands as a monument to that wonderful man, now gone to his noble reward, Ernest 

Harold Baynes.

Meriden boasts of not having one English sparrow in the whole town. If by chance one does appear he is immediately shot. CHAPTER XV BIRD OBSERVATION AWAY FROM HOME 

This Easter Sunday, in the middle of the day, I strolled out through Druid Hill Park in Baltimore and was greatly impressed by the number of resident and migrating song-birds in evidence.

Druid Hill Park is one of the finest natural parks in this country and its wooded hills, natural streams and springs and variable foliage and small growth affords a real paradise for birds.

While sauntering along, I came across thirteen different kinds of birds. Of these I saw great numbers of certain kinds and only a few of others. For bird lovers a walk through this park at just this time of year is of more than ordinary interest. 

The English starling is everywhere in evidence and, I am told, is multiplying so rapidly that it is becoming rather a nuisance. Great numbers of them are seen singly and in flocks. Every tree, having a hollow in the trunk or limbs, offers housing for this bird. 

The purple grackle is also very prevalent and is seen flying about in small groups. Both the grackle and starling I am told are permanent residents. 

On both sides of the pathways, a variety of shrubbery is growing. Most of this is natural to the location. Hearing the feeble lisp of some small bird, I 106

paused and peering through the underbrush, I saw numbers of the ruby-crowned kinglets. These beauti ful little fellows were enjoying themselves immensely. 

Their presence added much to the interest of such a stroll. In the four or five hours I spent in this park 

I saw many of these little fellows. Bright, clean, smooth, active and cheerful always and seemingly not the least bit shy.

Passing a curve and down a slight incline toward a small brook I heard a sharp call which might be interpreted as "pretty! pretty! pretty!" Nearing the spot, I saw a beautiful pair of cardinal grosbeaks. The male had a wonderfully bright and new coat while the female was less brightly dressed but yet beautiful in her solemn feathers. To one from Northern New

England this was a grand sight. Later in the day, I saw other cardinals at better advantage. They truly added much to the color of the surroundings. 

Here and there a flicker would fly from tree to tree and sound his familiar call; "wick! wick! wick!"

These active flyers were evidently on their way North and had stopped here for rest and food. I expect to find them at home upon my return as they are good flyers. 

Song-sparrows, robins and crows were plentiful and sent my thoughts back to my own garden where the two former species were enjoying themselves when 

I left. The song-sparrow had a most welcome tone in his Easter carol.

I ran across one catbird rendering a most melodious song from high up in a large tree overhanging a marshy spot. To see this bird perched so high and giving such a tuneful concert was a new sight to me, as at home I find the catbird near the ground in low

growing shrubs. I paused when I first heard him and after locating him I remained to listen for some time. 

Automobiles were passing in every direction yet he was far above reach and seemed determined to make his truly musical outburst heard above all other noise. 

Another observation which pleased me much was the presence of the mocking-bird. In several different localities, I found this beautiful bird and very much in evidence with his song. At one spot where the shrubbery was rather thick, I located a pair of these birds. Here I paused and seated myself on a grass spot nearby. How these birds did sing and once they came out on the grass very near me. This offered an exceptional opportunity for close inspection. The artful tunes of these birds and the solos and duets they gave me will long be remembered. I only wish we could hear them at home. 

Every now and then I would hear the distant note of the Peabody bird. This seemed so good and a real note of welcome. Once I saw the white-throat perched in a tree just above my head and later he came down on the ground to feed very near where I was. This great ventriloquist always attracts my attention and I look for him each year as he is passing through. No bird to me is more welcome and has a clearer well toned note.

While passing beneath a growth of maple and oak 

I heard another strange note. Pausing and peering up into the trees, I presently sighted a pair of redheaded woodpeckers enjoying themselves in the high tree tops. Their coats were fresh and bright and the red of their heads was very pronounced. For fully fifteen minutes, I remained there to watch them as they fed upon the insects in the bark and listen to their

rather discordant cries. Their activity and companionship, one with the other, was very amusing.

Flying about over the swan pond, I saw a beautifully marked belted kingfisher. He was evidently stopping for a morsel of fish and would then be on his way further North. His presence added much to the setting as the swans glided about on the surface of the pond. 

Having spent four or five hours in this beautiful playground of Baltimore, I felt well repaid for my stroll. Especially gratified at the close observation I had been accorded by the friendliness of the cardinals, mocking-birds and ruby-crowned kinglets. The mixed chorus and melodious notes merely made the 

Easter more impressive. 

Of the thirteen species, I saw during this walk, I cannot hope to meet them all again later at home. 

However, I know the majority of them will be there as they are each year, and who knows but some of the very same little flyers will greet my eye up North.

They will not recognize me, or I them as individuals, yet some of the same family will help us to pass a more pleasant summer as they did me to pass a more songful Easter. This is my anticipation upon returning home.

CHAPTER XVI FOOD AND ITS PREPARATION 

Few people realize that if the song-birds are given the proper amount of food they can withstand almost any temperature at any season here in New England. 

With proper food they will survive even the coldest winters and deepest snows.

In our own garden, one very cold and snowy winter, we entertained a pair of rusty blackbirds and they seemed to enjoy it all very much. Just a little distance from our garden lot this winter, a robin has remained and has frequently been seen feeding upon the berries of the shrubs and vines. Robins are peculiar in their selection of food and seem to prefer berries rather than grain. However they will, at times, partake of doughnuts and bread softened in milk. 

Within three weeks this writer, while out snowshoeing, I observed a lone robin in an orchard. He was feeding upon the withered, frozen apples hanging from the limbs of the leafless trees. This robin, a male, seemed in very good flesh and was very much at home in his northern winter quarters. Give the birds food and they will withstand any weather. 

It must not be forgotten that the digestive process of a bird is much more quickly accomplished than in a human being or animal. It has been said that the 110

seed of blueberry, eaten by a bluebird, was defecated in fifteen minutes. Defecation of birds is accomplished very often during the day and also during the night while asleep. For this reason the alimentary tract of a bird becomes empty in a comparatively short time and the food nutrient is quickly assimilated. 

Again the body temperature of birds is several degrees higher than that of human beings and animals. Because of this they the more quickly burn up the nourishment. Birds breathe very rapidly and their circulation is much more rapid to complete the ratio. It can be easily seen that physiological assimilation of food products and oxidation or waste of these food products is much more rapid than in human beings or animals.

Because of this very rapid digestion, assimilation, and rapid waste, combined with the continuous activity of song-birds, and they never seem to rest, they need per body weight, much more food. The fact is they appear to be eating the entire day and this is the more pronounced in cold weather when radiation is so much more rapid.

During the warm days of summer, birds need less artificial feeding. Nature provides them with abundant insects, moths, worms, and the like and in consequence they can very handily take care of themselves. 

Nevertheless the purple finches, song-sparrows, chipping sparrows and some others come to the feeding stations and partake of the grains, bread crumbs, and sunflower seeds but they never seem to neglect the duty which they are given by Nature to devour hundreds upon hundreds of insects and worms.

During the cold winter days when the ground is deeply blanketed in snow covering up Nature's feed-

ing grounds, these little feathered friends must resort to the tree trunks and bare limbs and search out the little harmful hibernating insects and moths to be found in these places. It is really this time of year when these birds do the most good by destroying these insects when they are asleep thus depleting the supply so that hundreds of thousands of them never come to light in the summer season. Thus our shrubs, flower and vegetable gardens are protected and the farmers gain thousands, even millions of dollars, in crops. This might properly be called a prophylactic measure of inestimable value to the city dweller and farmer alike. 

During the winter months, attract these birds to your home by keeping one or two feeding stations well supplied with millet seed, canary seed, and sunflower seed. All winter birds enjoy millet seed and because of its minuteness most all the birds can eat it. The blue jays especially enjoy the sunflower seeds very much. If seed buying is too expensive for your pocketbook keep a ten pound lard pail handy and deposit bread crusts, meat scraps, pieces of doughnuts and other food in it and thrice or twice a week put these through the meat grinder and you will have at hand food which with the fat scraps from the table, is the best to be had. The fat serves as warmth-producing food and will keep the temperature of birds up to the resistant point. Suet is very inexpensive and a few pieces of this material properly lashed to tree limbs and trunks, or placed in a suet holder will last all winter. The birds, more especially the chickadees, nuthatches and woodpeckers will pick away at this suet and thus supply the necessary body warmth. 

Doughnuts hung by strings from a wire extending from the building to a tree will serve as very good

food for the birds, which they will enjoy. These doughnuts will become frozen and last a long time. 

Take a little time, and it really takes but a few moments, prepare this food two or three times a week and place it at the disposal of the birds and you will be well repaid for any time you have spent. The birds will be attracted to your garden and you will notice the scarcity of insects, worms and moths the next warm season. Your flower and vegetable garden, shrubbery, flowering and fruit trees will show this diligent winter care by producing exuberant foliage and an abundance of flowers, berries and fruit. During warm days put out sliced bananas and unsalted nut meats. The oil in these foods helps the birds. Give the birds the freedom of that food you would otherwise discard. Just learn to give these little feathered friends, who do not have steam heated homes and running water, but live in the open day and night, a little thought and feed them if for nothing more than for the love of Nature and the wide, free open spaces.

CHAPTER XVII CONSTITUTION OF THE MANCHESTER BIRD CLUB ARTICLE I Name 

This club shall be known as the Manchester Bird Club.

ARTICLE II Purpose 

The purpose of this club shall be to encourage protection of and interest in bird life in our community. 

ARTICLE III 

Membership 

Section 1. The membership in this club shall con sist of active membership, associate membership, life membership, patrons, and benefactors.

Section 2. Persons shall become members of this club after their names have been proposed by a mem- 114

ber, their applications approved by the Executive Committee, and their dues paid. 

Section 3. Any resident of Manchester, N. H. 

may become an active member on election by the Executive Committee and payment of annual dues of 

50 cents.

Section 4. Any non-resident in sympathy with the purpose of this club may become an Associate Member on election by the Executive Committee and payment of annual dues of $1.00. 

Section 5. Any person may become a Life Member on election by the Executive Committee and payment of a fee of $25.00.

Section 6. Any person may become a Patron on election by the Executive Committee and payment of a fee of $100.00.

Section 7. Any person may become a Benefactor on election by the Executive Committee and payment of a fee of $1,000.00.

Section 8. The voting power shall be limited to members, resident of Manchester, New Hampshire.

ARTICLE IV 

Meetings Meetings shall be held at the discretion of the Executive Committee. The first meeting after May 1st shall be the business meeting at which the election of officers and three directors for the ensuing year shall be held.

Government The officers of the club shall consist of a President, two Vice-presidents, Secretary and Treasurer. The officers of the club together with the directors shall constitute the Executive Committee, which Committee shall pass on all business that is to be brought before the club for action.

CHAPTER XVIII RECEPTION OF THE WINTER BIRDS The Winter of 1924 

No doubt the oldest inhabitant would say that this was one of the hardest winters Manchester has seen The amount of snow fall exceeded any winter for a long time. With such an amount of snow covering the berry and seed bearing shrubs, small trees and weeds were entirely submerged. While this condition was found all over our part of the state, further North even worse conditions existed. No doubt the average citizen found it difficult to get out about his home and do the little things necessary for cleanliness and upkeep as well as protection. Great business interests were hampered by the deep snow as was illustrated by the large number of freight cars stalled in our railroad yard.

We, human beings, have many extra means by which we can fairly well acclimate ourselves to most any outside circumstance. We can engage help, do much heavy work ourselves and in a short space of time open up the traffic and continue business. While we become accustomed to the conditions, the winter birds who depend upon the weed seeds, berries from the low growing shrubs and buds of small trees find 117

themselves flying about a deep layer of snow and unable to find the food they need. 

No doubt the heavy snowfall of this season caused the death of hundreds of our winter birds either because they could not get food or they were covered in by the heavy snowfall and smothered. The grouse is a bird that will often burrow out a hole in the snow and make this his bed for the night. A heavy snowfall during the night will cover him in and should it rain a little, and form a hardened crust, this noble game-bird awakens in the morning and finds himself entombed. He becomes excited and after repeated efforts to free himself becomes exhausted and will perish within his winter cell. Hundreds of grouse perished this winter just in this manner. 

The beautiful English ring-neck pheasant who has come to us during the past few years and every season has multiplied until now he is rather numerous is another bird who suffers greatly when his food supply is shut off. The pheasant is a seed eater and under average, normal conditions will live through the winter quite well. Of course there is a minimum death rate of these birds every winter but that season caused many of these beautiful birds to perish either from starvation or from being buried up in the great drifts. 

Scores of reports came in telling of the pheasants flocking into the dooryards in search of food. 

This was not only true in the outskirts of the city but also within the more closely built sections. In the 

Northern, Eastern, and Southern sections of Manchester where there are vacant land spaces grown up with alders, birches or other underbrush, these beautiful birds were to be found in flocks of from five or six to thirty or forty. In the warmer hours of the day these

birds scattered to the yards of nearby residences and about hen-coops and sheds, becoming rather tame and indifferent to the inhabitants of these homes. 

On Ray Street, within the centre of a rather thickly built area, a pheasant flew against the dining room window of one of the residences during an evening when one of the storms was in progress. People living about Stark Park and the section known as Wilson Hill had daily visits from these birds. Many of these thoughtful people realized the situations that the pheasant faces and fed them with scraps from the table. These kind acts helped to save the lives of hundreds of these birds within the city limits. 

The Government realizing the situation volunteered to distribute grain in the outskirts by permitting the rural mail carriers to establish feeding grounds along their routes. Perhaps one of the most practical means of tiding the grouse and pheasant over this critical period was accomplished through the cooperation of the Manchester Bird Club and the Boy Scouts of this city. Four large feeding stands were developed where these birds could come and daily enjoy proper food and shelter. These feeding stands were established at Stark Park, Stevens Pond, Goffs 

Falls, and at the Amoskeag Recreation Grounds.

Separate Scout troops under the supervision of their leaders kept these feeding stands open and supplied. 

All of the stands were well patronized by the birds who became very tame when they found out that no harm befell them.

One morning at one of these feeding stands thirtysix pheasants were seen at one time. This was a wonderful sight and would appeal to the heart of any citizen who might witness such an array of beautifully

colored feathered friends. Without doubt this move to keep the grouse and pheasants supplied with food resulted in saving many scores of these birds that winter. All credit is due the Boy Scouts and their leaders for the work done, but every one of this noble organization who has taken part in the work feels that they are fully repaid by the results obtained. 

These feeding grounds were maintained until such a time as these birds could again care for themselves. 

One more illustration of the kind-heartedness of some of our citizens. In the Eastern section of the city there reside two elderly and very modestly situated ladies. They noticed a few pheasants coming to their backyard each morning evidently in search of food. These two kind women were very liberal with table scraps and bread crumbs. They daily fed a large flock of these beautiful birds and the Manchester Bird Club donated an additional supply of scratch feed. It is hoped that many of our well housed citizens will follow such an example and do their little bit to foster these birds through an unusual season. The food one wastes daily will make several of these birds a good meal.

Possibly it is not known that almost any bird can withstand the cold and blustering winds if they but have food. It seems a well established fact that birds of nearly all varieties can live if they have a supply of food no matter how cold the weather is. No doubt many will recall where a bluebird, robin, purple finch, chipping sparrow or others of these songsters have remained about a home here in Manchester all winter. 

They withstood the elements because some warmhearted observer saw to it that they were regularly supplied with food. Of course this kind of bird will

not ordinarily remain so far North in winter, yet when he does, and is fed, he will pull through in surprisingly good condition until the Spring season arrives.

Every citizen of Manchester ought to do a little each day for the winter birds. That season we had many of the smaller birds with us in large numbers. 

The writer was walking home along a closely inhabited street. On the front space of one of the residential lots his attention was called to a group of cheery little birds gathering seeds and dead worms and bugs from a group of white birches. These little fellows were having a gay time frolicking in the sunshine, covering the trees from top to bottom and as these redpolls put forth their winter notes between mouths full of seeds and dead bugs they seemed to sing a carol of good cheer.

A neighbor reported a large flock of redpolls in her garden plot. They were feeding on the barberries. and birch seeds and just enjoying themselves to the utmost. One can never tell what new bird he may see even at this time of the year. The little winter songsters who will ordinarily remain a little further North or in the outskirts will, with the influence of the deep snow, come into the city.

The writer saw a flock of some thirty pine-siskins feeding about a group of white birches and two or three pine trees. These athletic and acrobatic winter birds were getting their fill before retiring to some sheltered tree for the night. Uneasy, active and nervous, seemingly not contented with their location, they would feed high and low in the trees, drop to the snow bank and pick up seeds that had fallen, turn somersaults on the slender limbs of the birches and suddenly all together as if on signal, off

they would fly to another tree nearby. The presence of these active little birds should be an incentive for all to do a bit toward feeding any winter bird that comes near a home.

In one of the smaller, private, residential bird sanctuaries of the city a single, evidently lonesome tree-sparrow was observed feeding from one of the feeding stations. Tree-sparrows are weed-seed eaters and serve the farmer well as an economic factor in his work. Ordinarily they are shy but the natural source of food supply being cut off, this little fellow forgot all except his appetite. Here in this feeding station he filled himself to the limit and now daily visited this same place and found an ample supply of food. Great numbers of tree-sparrows were reported in larger and smaller flocks all over the city. They are handsome birds and from an economical point of view they earn their daily wage by the great numbers of weed-seeds they devour each winter.

Many people have an idea that birds of splendor are with us only in summer days. This is true only to the point of numbers. Of course in summer we have many more beautifully colored birds but in winter also birds with most fascinating colors are to be found, if we will but take the time to observe carefully. In fact during the winter months many birds that come in flocks and remain about the city are not to be seen in the warmer months as they return North with the advent of Spring.

As an illustration of this fact one year ago this winter a numerous flock of evening grosbeaks remained in the heart of the city for several weeks. These birds made their home among the trees in the vacant land bounded by Sagamore and Salmon Streets just east of

Chestnut Street. Such a beautiful coloring in contrast to the snow covered ground! The yellow, black and white of these beautiful birds is a picture in itself as these birds are seen gathered together in flocks. Then again they have most harmonious notes resembling the jingle of sleigh bells. This is most appropriate for the season in which these birds appear. Several bird lovers of the city saw this flock of evening grosbeaks and their pleasure in hunting them out was marked. 

A few years ago a pair of rusty blackbirds wintered in the garden of a residence of the city. Of course this is most unusual as these birds go further South as a rule. However that may be, this special winter was a hard one with so much snow. This pair of birds found nightly shelter in a small group of white pines situated at one corner of a dwelling. During the day they would regularly visit the feeding station and suet boxes. They seemed especially fond of the latter food. Many times during rather severe blizzards that winter, this pair of rusty blackbirds could have been seen through the wind-cast fall of snow feeding in the protection of the stations. The children of the household took special pride in supplying them with plenty of food and for this the birds seemed most grateful. 

These birds remained the winter through. In the spring they disappeared and have not been seen in the same area since.

This clearly illustrates that birds accustomed to going South during the winter can thrive and get along nicely if given a goodly supply of food. 

At this same feeding station for several winters past, four blue jays daily make their visit. As soon as the sun is up in the morning the jays can be heard and all during the coldest days they come and go. Of

course the blue jay is a permanent resident yet he is a sagacious bird and takes advantage of an opportunity afforded by a goodly supply of food. He is a beautifully colored bird, especially so during the winter. In most any locality of the city during a winter like that one, when the snow is very deep in the woods, this bird. will be found coming into the front yards and orchards of the residential sections in search of food. These birds like sunflower seeds and it is very interesting to watch them take several of these seeds in their mouths and flying away to a nearby tree, one by one place these seeds between their toes and the limb and crack the shell with their bills. The bill of a jay is strong and they accomplish this work with dexterity. Any one taking the pains to feed our winter birds will be almost sure to have some blue jays come to their feeding station.

For several years past, herring-gulls have frequented the Merrimack valley in winter. If one will watch from the car window on the trip from Boston to Manchester, numbers of gulls can be seen about sections of the river where there is open water. At first only one or two of these gulls were seen about the open water of the river as it runs by the busy mills of our city. As years pass however the number increases.

Just recently the writer was walking across the Bridge street bridge and counted fourteen of these beautifully winged birds resting on the rocks bordering an open space in the water.

Gulls are a fishing bird to a certain extent but also they are pronounced scavengers and pick up any food found floating upon the surface of the water. If one would but pause a moment and see these gulls as in their flight they gracefully volplane to the surface be-

low and pick up a morsel of food then seek a resting spot to enjoy it, one would be well repaid for the moment spent. These birds are artful flyers, their lengthy wings permitting them to make great progress with a minimum of effort. They resemble miniature airplanes as they tack, rise, volplane and manoeuvre through space, always head in motion and eye alert for food in the water far below. No doubt as years go by the number of herring-gulls wintering within the city limits will increase as they can come here and be sheltered from the severe coast gales. The sight of them as they fly high above the busy mills is certainly pleasing to the eye.

Only a few years ago the English starling was unknown here. Gradually a few were seen until now they have become very numerous. They are permanent residents and multiply very rapidly. That winter within the city limits and in many outskirt areas large numbers of these birds were found. Almost any good bright day one could see these birds in small flocks perched high in elms or other trees, busily chatting to one another. Now and then in the very early Spring someone reports the early arrival of the grackle. No doubt these are English starlings that have been with us all winter. The grackle being a ground-feeder is not apt to make a mistake and arrive too early in the 

Spring. The starling will take advantage of your winter feeding station and the iridescence of his plumage in the early morning winter sunshine will add a soft tone to the atmosphere. 

One little feathered friend that can be found about the city fully enjoying himself on the coldest days is the chickadee. This little acrobat remains in all sections of the city the winter through. The chickadee is

a very domestic fellow and will come about the window sills and even feed from the hand at this time of the year. He also has a very good memory and once you have let it be known to him that your feeding station is for him to enjoy he will return each winter and bring others with him. The chickadee is a seed-feeder and will also do the fruit bearing trees a great deal of good by devouring the hundreds of moth nests containing the eggs he so well enjoys. Just try encouraging this little active fellow to visit you a few times and you will admire his little black cap more and more. 

One of the most talented women of our city has for several years spent much time in encouraging songbirds to come and live about her spacious grounds. 

This noble woman has had built a fine and well sheltered window feeding station at the window of her study. Here she keeps a supply of grain, sunflower seeds and the like. Just now this feeding station is daily visited by goldfinches and white-breasted nuthatches. They come to this feeding station and secure a good meal with as much apparent unconcern as does a human being going to the breakfast table at home. 

The white-breasted nut-hatches seem to take great pride in showing off as they alight on the limb of a nearby tree and perform in the morning sun of a cold winter day. These birds can be found within the city limits all winter and they are certainly an interesting bird to watch. 

The goldfinch, or wild canary as he is sometimes called, remains here in fairly good numbers the year round. On a cold winter morning he can be heard with his peculiar chip as he dips in his flight from one tree to another. When he is in winter plumage he is more difficult to recognize. However that may be, if

one observes the goldfinch, closely, he will be seen very often during the winter right here in the city. If you keep sunflower seeds in your winter feeding station and watch carefully, you will be pretty sure to see a goldfinch ere the winter is over. 

One morning last winter the writer was walking down Blodgett street near Elm street when a quite small bird quickly flew out from the shelter of the base of a large elm and went to the base of the next tree of the same kind. Alert and quick of motion was this bird always alighting on the opposite side of the tree trunk endeavoring to keep out of view of the passerby. He was evidently in search of his breakfast and crept about the tree trunk pecking here and there for a dead worm or moth egg. This little brown creeper was right on the job and no doubt if some good bird friend in that locality had a piece of suet tied to the limb of a tree, before the morning was gone he would have found it and satisfied his keen appetite. One of our school teachers, who is an ardent bird lover keeps a window feeding station well supplied. This teacher lives in the heart of the city and the electric cars pass her door regularly. The feeding station is located at one of the lower windows bordering the walk to the back door. Here people pass in and out at irregular intervals, yet all last winter a pair of downy woodpeckers came regularly each day and supplied themselves with the food necessary to carry them through the cold season. These downy little fellows also enjoyed the suet put out in the apple tree and they became so tame that while at the window feeding station those in the room could approach and watch them without, in the least, troubling the birds. In fact the

birds were photographed through the glass. They remained about this home all winter. 

All will readily recall the severe blizzard that covered the ground with a heavy snow about the middle of last March. Song-sparrows, fox-sparrows, bluebirds and a few robins had arrived in the city. In fact the days, for a week or so past, had been very mild and the ground uncovered. This blizzard came on 

Saturday night and Sunday morning and when we awoke the ground was covered with snow. However the blizzard had passed and the day was bright and comparatively warm. This day, in so far as the temperature is concerned, was comfortable but the natural feeding stands of the early arrivals had been covered up. In one residential section of the city where the children of the household have established a bird sanctuary scores of song-sparrows and fox-sparrows flocked for the food supply of the feeding stations therein. A few bluebirds and robins also came in.

As previously stated the morning was bright, the air not cold and the members of the household were entertained by these birds seeking food. The fruit trees, hedges, vines, and other growths as well as the ground was literally alive with the scores of birds that flocked in. As the sun warmed things up, the fox- and song-sparrows, together with the bluebirds and robins rendered the most enjoyable opera in song that one would care to listen to. The notes were in perfect harmony and all day long these greatest of songsters, the fox-sparrows, rendered a tuneful melody as a thanks for the food which was theirs for the taking. 

Those who know the song of the fox-sparrow, to say nothing of the beautiful Spring notes of the other birds present, will appreciate this natural setting for

this bird melodrama. Thus it is easily shown how much pleasure one can derive from the very little effort or time necessary it takes to keep our feathered friends supplied with food and a home. Of course, in due time the fox-sparrows went on but the other birds remained and sang us into happiness the summer through. 

Much is now being done by many bird lovers of our city to keep the winter birds supplied. This the birds fully appreciate. Those who have not taken the little time required to accomplish this bird feeding can simply try it out and find out for themselves the real pleasure gained from the adventure. The winter birds do our trees good, they assist in the next year's garden work by lessening the number of weed seeds that would otherwise by this young growth, produce torment and toil, to say nothing of the good derived from the efforts of these winter birds which results favorably toward that lawn we are so proud of. 

When men and women of serious mind and of big business enterprises take pleasure in this work why should not we of less fully occupied days give just a little of our time in making it possible to attract more and more of the winter birds to our city as the years roll by? Let us try and do our part in this worthy move and make our community, a center for song birds, in the winter as well as in the summer. Just watch the results as shown in the improvements of the flower garden, vegetable garden, fruit trees, berrybearing trees, and the finer looking shrubbery and lawn of the following summer.

Of course this is most pronounced during the Spring and early Summer song season. Once these birds complete their nest building and begin the incubation period the concert dies away and the birds themselves become less evident.

Those who are fortunate enough to have a lawn covered with shrubbery, a few fruit trees and possibly a berry vine of some type, will always find these songbirds very close neighbors. In the early Spring while planting the flower garden and later in the care of this garden these birds are always in evidence and add cheer to the environment.

The average person and more especially the home owner takes far too little interest in these feathered songsters. Every home should have on the living room table a colored, illustrated book telling of the habits and describing the characteristics and appearance of our song-birds. This should certainly be a part of the modern life in a well regulated daily routine. How can one spend a daily recreative hour more beneficially than in the study of our most familiar song-birds? 130

Then again, every home owner should place about his garden and lawn a few bird homes that birds may have a well protected place in which to meet and raise their young. 

It will be surprising how many songbirds will be attracted to them and the city plot becomes a private bird sanctuary.

A little pan of water placed upon a mound or stool soon attracts the song-birds for the morning bath and a frequent refreshing drink. If one so desires, he may place a few highly colored shells and small stones in this water which will aid in attracting the birds. 

The real pleasure in these song-birds aside from the cheer of their musical notes is in learning how very companionable they will become if one is patient. In our own little garden we have established just such a small bird sanctuary and recently have added a feeding station, and a nesting supply station. Here these songbirds can come to feed and gather supplies with which to build their nests.

One holiday afternoon not long ago I spent an hour in this little bird sanctuary merely to list the birds seen and to try to attract these birds to feed from my hands. It was a beautiful clear, warm day and the song of these little feathered friends was coming from every corner of the garden.

The daisies, nasturtiums, pansies, and other flowers were most colorful. In fact, the entire setting was conducive to silent thinking and study. Hardly had I seated myself in a rather secluded spot among the drooping tendrils of the bitter-sweet vine, than my little friends the humming-birds came forth. This same pair of humming-birds have nested in my garden for five consecutive years. What a wonderful thing to think of, when we realize that these little fliers spend

their winters in South America and make the trip twice a year coming and returning! Quite a journey for a bird no larger than one's thumb. As they balanced back and forth among the nasturtiums they demonstrated that special art in flying, the technique which is known to no other bird, the ability to fly backward just as dexterously as they do forward. Back and forth they would glide in and out of the blossoms. It was their mating time and presently both of these little humming-birds flew to the clothes line and there sat side by side very close together, rocking in the gentle breeze. They would make love by preening their bills together and the little twitter of love notes further showed their love making.

While I was watching these humming-birds, a treeswallow was skillfully flying in circles high above the garden. Presently another tree-swallow joined this one and they chased each other in the air and finally lighted on an electric wire in the further corner of the garden. Here on the wire they sat side by side twittering and enjoying the bright, warm sunshine. This pair of tree-swallows has been with us for five consecutive summers. The first year they came they built in a pole house and raised a brood. The following year they occupied the same home and were as successful. 

The past three years they have occupied a home on the corner of the garage and each year have successfully raised a fine brood. When we think that these birds winter in South America yet can make the round trip returning each Spring and go not only to the same garden plot but to the very same bird home, it seems a very marvelous bit of instinct. The tree-swallow is one of our best long distant flyers, during migration covering five hundred to a thousand miles each day.

It is a very novel sight, when the young come from the nest, to see the entire family resting side by side on the wire with the adult birds taking turns in the food gathering flights, returning to feed the young. Such parental care is a worthy example for humans to study.

A little solemn bluebird flew to her home in a house at the side of the dining room window. It was the sombre colored mother who, as I watched her, made regular trips to and from this bird home of hers which she has occupied for three consecutive summers and each year raised a good brood. I could hear the fledgings twittering within their nest as she entered the home. I timed her for ten minutes during which interval she made five trips each time bringing a moth or other insect in her mandibles and fed the young. The male bluebird with his more colorful plumage was content to rest high on the martin house pole rather as a watchman than an industrious worker. With his mournful notes he would seem to say, "All is well"; "All is well!"

On the south side of the house just at the edge of the garden, is a wren home which has been occupied two successive years by a pair of house-wrens. Just at this moment she appeared on the perch at the entrance to her home and begun to prattle away in her run of linking notes. Such a chattering and scolding and mixture of trilling notes, as with her tail raised high, and her head thrown back she seemed to give one the impression that she was the principal one of the feathered group of the garden. I have not seen the young either year that she has been with us, but I presume that her family just bursts forth and flies away unseen at the proper time. Her mate being a polygamist, was busy carrying in sticks and straws in another wren house on

the bitter-sweet trellis in another corner of the garden. He would take time off from labor and just sit and sing until his throat must have been dry. I presume he was endeavoring to induce another mate of his family to come and join him while his first wife was busy with the incubation of those eggs. He is a poor example of morals and for this reason has always seemed as a social outcast with the other birds. 

Just at this time I heard a most tuneful line of song notes coming from the bitter-sweet trellis and I realized that our true and tried friend the catbird was there. Each year we have a half a dozen catbirds with us in the garden and they become most friendly. 

Aside from the characteristic "meow!" "meow!" "meow" for which they are named they have a most cultured and refined musical chant which they render at times when they are at ease. The note for which they are named is merely their alarm note. Together with the mocking-birds and brown thrasher they can do the finest piece of mockery in a most tuneful and melodious manner.

Just a few evenings since, I chanced to be out in the garden at dusk, and six of these catbirds were having a most enjoyable time taking a dip in the rock-a-way bath near the bitter-sweet trellis. To me this was a wonderful sight as they played about in the graded baths, at times all submerged at once. The catbird is one of the most astute and at the same time one of the most playful and frolicking of our summer songsters. We would be very much at a loss without our annual visitation from the catbirds. 

One bright, warm, moonlight night some ten days ago, I sat in my study writing, and through the open window came this same multiple stanza of melodious

notes. I paused and enjoyed the vesper service for at least a quarter of an hour. The hour was near midnight yet this catbird was so filled with energy that sleep was out of the question and he just made those streams of the silvery moonlight most resonant with music of the brightest quality. 

Another day, I heard the little chimes in the dining room sound the note that my evening meal awaited me and I started to go in the house when Mr. Yellow

Warbler began his high pitched, tuneful notes from the white birch tree. The temptation was too great and I quietly walked around the house to the front door, and there in the white birch tree he sat, and I studied the composer of this song. How beautiful he looked in his golden coat with scarlet pin stripes on the side of his breast. His notes lingered long with me that evening. As I was about to enter the front door I heard from the nearby mulberry, the buzzing notes of that sleekest of all our feathered friends the cedar-waxwing. Here they had come in numbers to have their evening meal of the rubus mulberry while I was enjoying my crackers and milk alone as my family were at camp. Such was an hour in my garden the ending of a truly perfect day.

CHAPTER XX BIRD SANCTUARIES 

The word sanctuary when applied to birds is an indefinite term. A sanctuary may be very small, it may be very extensive or it may be of medium size. The 

United States Government has established bird sanctuaries covering thousands of acres where birds are permitted to gather and raise their young unmolested. 

Especially along our wide coasts and on islands do we find these great bird sanctuaries; the conservation work being supervised by the government. 

Caretakers look out for the welfare of the birds and see that they have proper protection from the prevailing elements, and the wanton human beings. 

At times of stress, such sanctuaries are well supplied with proper food, drink and bathing facilities. All in all, our great United States has found that the money spent in this direction is well placed and yields good returns.

If a government like our own has found this to be a paying, economic venture, we, as private citizens should get pro rata returns from any personal effort put into establishing smaller private bird sanctuaries in our city gardens and orchards, at our summer homes and in the rural communities on farms and wooded land. 136

A small private sanctuary requires but a few feet of space and it is surprising how much real good can be accomplished. Just stop for a moment and think of the hundreds of thousands of back yard plots, gardens, vacant house lots overgrown with the shrubs which birds enjoy, now existing in this great country and how much could be done for our song-birds, if the owners were to become sufficiently interested in our feathered friends.

The large or small bird sanctuary is a centre for the gathering of our song-birds. To this they daily come and enjoy the food, bath and natural shrubbery therein. Right here in Manchester during the past few years many small bird sanctuaries have been started with great success.

As I glance from my study window I can see, across the street, a plot of land some two hundred feet in depth and half as wide. The front of this space is occupied by the residence of one of our younger citizens.

A very common type of home neat and tidy. The rear of this plot is well-wooded with young maple, birch and alder and joins the lawn of a neighbor. 

Here last Spring my neighbor began by clearing the ground of waste material and pruning the trees. He then secured several bird homes and placed them in favorable places among the trees. Here a natural spring and a small chicken yard supplies the food needed for the song-birds in times of stress and strain. On several occasions my neighbor has telephoned me to come over and see the birds that gather within this small sanctuary in the residential section of a prosperous city. He has obtained results from his efforts and the time he has put into this work has paid good interest. 

More of these smaller, as well as the extensive bird

sanctuaries should be found in every city and town all over the United States. It is such an easy thing to construct a bird sanctuary no matter how small the plot of land. In fact it often seems as if the very small city lots give as good results as the larger acreage. 

If we take a city lot fifty by one hundred feet, for example, we shall find about the house an open lawn and the small garden for flowers. Here can be placed an inexpensive bird bath near a tree. Another open space can be selected for a feeding station which can. be built from a small box placed on the top of a post four or five feet from the ground. It should be remembered that feeding stations need a roof to keep the food dry and that the entrance, if there be but one, should face the south. It is a good plan to place a light of glass in each end and thus help to brighten the interior and magnify the sun's rays for warmth during the winter. 

A half dozen bird homes can be placed nine or ten feet from the ground on the side of the dwelling, garage or the trunk of a tree. These homes should be placed where they will not be covered by foliage and growing vines, as birds will seek and occupy the homes that are placed in the open and pass by the secluded ones. The bird bath, feeding station and a half dozen bird homes make a sanctuary suited to an ordinary city lot.

All Bird Clubs should endeavor to further this movement and get the members to establish little sanctuaries about their homes. This in itself will do much to attract the song-birds into the cities and towns and produce very profitable results.

A word might be written concerning the more ex-

tensive sanctuaries. Well organized Bird Clubs have a pronounced effect upon all members as well as an appreciable influence in the community. 

Cemeteries, public parks, country clubs and the like, are very suitable places for the establishment of larger sanctuaries where song-birds can find protection and proper nesting places. The foliage, trees and lawns of these sites will be better if inhabited by song-birds. 

More song-birds mean far better grass, trees free from noxious worms, beetles and moths, foliage free from lice and grass a deep green the season through. 

Winter birds attracted to these areas mean that the hibernating bugs and other insects will be exterminated during their winter slumber. This, in itself, assures early budding and leafing and much better appearing grounds and surroundings when the Spring comes into its own.

Every Bird Club should have a special Sanctuary Committee well organized and efficient. By means of many small contributions this committee can raise a fund by which bird homes may be secured. They should obtain the proper official consent to place these homes on the tree trunks in the cemeteries, parks and at country clubs. Usually this consent is very readily given if the proper approach is made.

Once the consent is obtained, houses should be purchased, the voluntary services of members of Boys'

Clubs or Boy Scout troops should be secured and the homes placed. Thus the work is accomplished without any material outlay by the cemetery authorities, club officials or park commissioners.

Many times individuals are very willing to give a bird bath in memory of some member of the family or prominent citizen.

Last but not least these sanctuaries are amphi-theatres of song and those who visit the parks, country clubs and cemeteries realize this.

CHAPTER XXI SONG-BIRD SANCTUARIES IN PARKS, CEmeteries, COUNTRY CLUBS, ETC. 

Every vacant space where there is shrubbery and trees should be so laid out as to be attractive to our song-birds. No city or town should be without some special area of moderate size which has been set aside and equipped and is known as a Song-Bird Sanctuary. 

This is not meant to include any of the private residential lots previously mentioned. Just at this time, a wave of sentiment is going across the entire country in favor of this public acknowledgment of the economic value of and conservation effort for our songbirds.

Public parks, cemeteries and country clubs make excellent sites for just this type of work. Usually covering such areas there are shade trees in abundance and much bordering and decorative shrubbery. Here birds are attracted and will remain if they are given a feeling of protection.

In any public park, even if in the heart of a good sized city and surrounded by much commercial activity, there will be found many of our song-birds gaily flitting about and enjoying themselves.

The writer recalls visiting a small city in the middle-west where, right at the very busiest corner where bank buildings and office buildings were located, was 141

seen in an adjacent park, many of our songsters enjoying the hustle and bustle of business quite as much as if they were human. Upon closer examination there were found attached to the tree trunks and placed on standards many bird homes suited to the various types of birds. Further inquiry revealed the fact that the local bird club had placed these homes there and that they had been used the first year. Right in this very central, busy area there was to be found a colony of purple martins well established in a home of their own. 

The sight was a revelation in itself and fully illustrates, in a very practical manner, just what can be done with a small city park in the way of establishing a bird sanctuary. This example should be followed in every city and town the country over. It remains for the local Bird Clubs to get busy and collect a small fund, procure the legal consent from the authorities and then purchase and set up the bird homes.

The authoritative consent is very easily obtained through the efforts of any well organized Bird Club. 

To collect the funds is rather a simple matter as there are many who are only too willing to donate a few dollars to this worthy cause. As a rule every locality has a Boy Scout Troop and they are always willing to give a half a day off in placing the bird homes. A guiding hand from some official of the Bird Club in directing their work completes the effort. After this watch for the results.

In placing the bird homes, select a southern exposure of the tree trunk and place the home ten to fifteen feet from the ground. Many times it is a good idea to place the home so that it tilts a little forward and downward. The birds seem to like this and it also affords some protection from their natural enemies. If

the home is placed on a metal standard, face the opening to the south.

The public have come to know that more song-birds mean better grass lands, lawns, shrubbery free from lice and other vermin, and trees free from moths and destructive insects. This is a vital factor in the upkeep of these beautifying spots so decorative to every community.

In cemeteries where there is very often found fine shrubbery and shade trees the same thing can be accomplished. Here in Manchester the Board of Mayor and Alderman were very glad to give their official approval to this work and open two of our largest cemeteries. Here bird homes were given to the cemetery officials and they took it upon themselves to place them about. Such co-operation can be found everywhere, and it means much to the welfare and conservation of song-bird life and also to the vegetation. 

Three years have passed since these two larger cemeteries were converted into bird sanctuaries and the attendants report a very marked decrease in plant and tree destroying insects and moths. If this is true in this locality, and such a practical example is at hand, the same will be true everywhere. 

Manchester prides itself on its fine, large, eighteenhole golf course, a part of the physical activity of its country club. Here is a wide expanse of some hundred or more acres right at the southern gateway to the city. Just recently the Board of Directors have officially accepted the proposal of the Bird Club to place two hundred bird homes on this wide expanse and in addition have established the Manchester Country Club as an official song-bird sanctuary. This is certainly a very splendid example of co-operation on the

part of the Board of Directors and illustrates, in a very vivid manner, just what can be done in any locality where a country club and golf course exists. 

Bird Clubs all over the country should be very willing and anxious to carry the message to these groups of business men and get authoritative consent to do the same. The busy business man enjoys golf and a great many of them are song-bird lovers. How much greater is the pleasure and recreation gained from a good game of golf with added music and aerial action of our song-birds! The combination of play, music, recreation and bird flight merely increases the real spirit and pleasure to be gleaned from visiting such places. Then again both the men and women, who frequent country clubs for restful leisure hours, enjoy such recreation when surrounded by the music and flights of our song-birds. This is only one fact and does not include the more important scientific side of the story, that which affords protection, proper nesting and breeding grounds to the birds, or the economic return that the birds give by their presence in great numbers.

The trees, shrubbery, flat and rolling fairways and the grassland and lawns very early show greater life and a more pronounced decorative value as a result of the gardening accomplished by the birds. Let all 

Bird Clubs join hands and further this movement to establish parks, cemeteries and country clubs the coun try over as SONG-BIRD SANCTUARIES. 

Give the birds good nesting sites and properly built homes and they will come in great numbers, not only in the warmer season, but during the cold days of winter many more birds will be found about such localities. It is a well established fact that our winter birds

do their large part of protecting the natural growths by making a winter house-cleaning of the hibernating and nesting insects so that when Spring comes, these minute insect eggs and nests are cleaned up and never mature. 

Every such area should have at least one purple martin home. Place this on a pole twenty or thirty feet high. Endeavor to place one good bird bath of reasonable size in each one of these established areas. 

In many instances a small pond or running stream already exists but where this is not true, select a good large boulder, hollow it out well and place it properly for a bird bath. Many times this can be accomplished through the personal interest of some individual who will be only too glad to donate the amount necessary that such a bird bath may be named in memory of a loved one who has passed on. Such a memorial marker on a bird bath is becoming very common and serves its double purpose for time immemorial. 

Whenever such things are done, either in the form of establishing a site as a sanctuary, erecting a memorial bird bath or placing bird homes, special appropriate exercises should mark the event and establish it as an official act. 

Bird Clubs the country over should get strongly behind this idea and further the wide spread move to establish parks, cemeteries and country clubs as Song- Bird Sanctuaries. This is a great part of the regular and accepted function of a well-organized Bird Club. 

All it needs is planning backed by committee action and official endorsement. Let us, as song-bird lovers, go forward hand in hand and make this a country-wide move to the end of establishing thousands of such protected areas for safe renting to our song-birds.

Some two years ago, by vote of the Executive Committee of the Manchester Bird Club, a special committee was appointed to meet the Directors of the Manchester Country Club and place before them a detailed plan for developing their beautiful grounds into a 

Song-Bird Sanctuary. 

The eighteen hole golf course of the Manchester Country Club, declared to be one of the best in the country, is well adapted for the nesting ground of many of the song-birds who visit here each year. The beautiful fairways edged by a very plentiful growth of pine, oak, maple and birch trees make a natural, reasonable nesting area for the song-birds. Through one section of these delightful grounds, runs a brook of clear water giving a most natural watering and bathing place. This is so well protected by the watchful eyes of the members and caddies that it seemed to the 

Executive Committee of the Manchester Bird Club, an excellent place in which to add one more song-bird sanctuary to the many others already established here in New England.

A survey was made of the grounds and a census of the birds was taken by the special committee of 146

the Manchester Bird Club. As a result it was found that between forty and fifty different species of songbirds inhabited this area during each of two different summers. CHAPTER XXIII SPRINGTIME BIRDS 

This is the time of the year when the sun runs higher and the days begin to get warmer. Gradually the snow patches on the lawns about the cities begin to melt away and the grass to be uncovered. A wonderful time of year when everyone can look forward to something new each day! Among these new things is the arrival of our song-birds from their winter grounds in the South. What a cheer and gladness comes with each new arrival! 

One morning at daybreak I heard a new note for this Spring, that of the grackle, so familiar to all bird students, and has been likened to the squeaking of the wheel of a wheelbarrow. The usual notes of the winter birds, the starling, bluejay, nut-hatch, woodpecker and the tree-sparrow had been heard about my home each morning all winter but the addition of this grackle note this morning at sunrise carried with it a thrill and renewed vigor. On going out I found that a flock of grackles had arrived during the night as do most of the migrants. This adds to the anticipation of each new arrival which we may find in the morning. These grackles were having a very joyous time among the branches of the tall pine trees behind the house. 150

For many years we have kept account of the arrival of the songsters and the variation in time is most interesting. 

I recall the note of the song-sparrow, a real musical strain which helps to lighten the work of the day, which this special year was the first arrival of the Spring and so far as could be observed he was alone. 

A few years ago, Easter Sunday came on April first, when a snow-storm greeted the church goers and the wind blew the light snow around the corners of the house and porch roof. In the middle of the afternoon I was surprised at the arrival of a large flock of bluebirds, who gathered in a bitter-sweet vine trailing up the northwest corner of the house. Evidently these bluebirds were very hungry and, attracted by the withered berries on the vine, they had stopped to feed. For fully an hour this large flock of bluebirds remained and indulged their appetites to the limit. 

Many strange and unusual things happen in bird. life. Any close observer will record the many surprises. A few years ago, early in April, there was a cloudy but rather warm day although the night before a fair amount of light, fluffy snow had covered the ground. On all sides, even in the middle of the street, on the snow covered lawns, and about the base work of many homes, to my great surprise could be seen large numbers of hermit-thrushes. At first it seemed to be almost a mirage but a close inspection revealed it to be true. Evidently the snow-storm had interrupted the migration of a large flock of these hermitthrushes and they had sought ground close to city houses in search of food. It was a very unusual sight to see these rather deep woods, wary birds come in large numbers to the residential section of a city. They

could be observed very closely and all wariness and fear seemed to have disappeared. A beautiful sight were these hermit-thrushes in their very smooth and sleek new Spring coats. That night it was quite warm and the following morning the sky was clear but no hermit-thrushes were found. They evidently had continued their migration and gone to wooded areas. 

This instance recalls to mind another Spring day in April some ten years ago. It was a Sunday and during the night before, a rather heavy snow-storm had fallen, but the morning came with a clear sky and higher temperature. The garden area was literally filled with melody of notes rather unusual for this region. Upon investigation it proved to be the fox-sparrow, a flock in large numbers. Anyone who is familiar with the song of this bird will recall it as one of the finest in all bird lore. All the day this entertainment of the highest type of symphony music continued. 

Such are the early Spring arrivals. These birds meet many vicissitudes and hazards and the toll of life must be severe under these unnatural conditions. At times one really marvels that there are any song-birds left.

Just now we can anticipate much in Nature's contribution to music. At most any day now the return of the vanguard of bird migration can be expected. The note of the robin, the first call of the chipping sparrow and of that ventriloquist of all birds, the whitethroated sparrow, will be heard. A few years ago early in Spring while in Baltimore I went, at day break, out to Druid Hill Park for the purpose of observing the birds. On one of the curving paths circling the 

Zoo section of the park I heard this high pitched note of the white-throat but I could not find him. Cau-

tiously his note came, seemingly from a distance when there, not twenty feet in front, was this ventriloquist sounding his call to Mr. Peabody. Any bird lover will fully appreciate the thrill of this adventure.

A little later will come the return of the greatest of all flyers in so far as the smaller birds are concerned, the members of the swallow family. One of these, the clever barn-swallow, can cover a thousand miles on the wing in twenty-four hours. These birds are such a wonderful help to the farmer in keeping down the increase of those tormenting mosquitoes. 

Then there is the bank-swallow, eave swallow and the tree-swallow of lustrous plumage. Each year I visit a colony of eaves-swallows some dozen miles from my home. Here on the south side of a large barn, a colony of these birds have built each year for many years. This colony comprises some hundred birds and they are most interesting to study with their cleverness in constructing their cone shaped, mud homes right up under the eaves. In the Spring when 

I go fishing up in the White Mountains I always search out another colony of these eaves-swallows in the centre of a little mountain hamlet. Sunrise finds me out in the fields watching this colony of birds busily engaged in seeking food as they soar and volplane high and low and then return to add a little to the edges of the newly started nests.

The smallest of the swallow family, the bank-swallow, is a very clever flyer. In and about this locality there are numerous colonies of these birds. They are most interesting to watch and particularly in the evening they are very active, just before the sun goes down.

Although the chickadee is a bird who is with us all

winter, yet he is a good forecaster of Spring. Scarcely a year goes by but someone reports that he has heard a phoebe along in late February or early March. 

Of course the snow is yet on the ground and the phobe has not yet arrived and will not for some time to come. 

However, everyone declares that he has heard the phoebe. In part the observation is correct, as no doubt that most musical note interpreted: phee-be, phee-be, phee-be was heard. Yes! the notes are moulded into this word far more clearly than the phoebe himself can do. It is the chickadee giving his Spring love note.

It is a most musical and well sounded call as he repeats, over and over again, to his mate; "phee-be! phee-be! phee-be!" The note of the real phoebe is more harsh and more run together. Some good frosty morning late in February or early in March when this call is heard, just find the little chickadee singing in mirthful glee.

The real phobe is a very interesting little bird. 

Just below my home a brook crosses the highway and flows into the nearby Merrimac River. Near the shore of the brook is located a barn just behind one of the residences. The cellar to this barn has an open space where would ordinarily be located the sliding door. Here, on one of the rafters of this barn cellar, a phoebe has built her nest for the past eight or ten summers. Each Spring I look for her return and have watched her build her nest and go through the incubation and brooding stages. It is always a joy when I can record the date of her return and continue our friendship. I surely believe that she has come to know me as my presence never seems to cause her any alarm.

In this same locality there is quite a growth of mod-

erate sized pine, maple and birch trees. This is a very fine area in which to observe the new arrivals. 

The rose-breasted grosbeaks come here in great numbers and their musical call is always a sign that Spring is really here for good. This great bird is one of the most interesting of the larger songsters. Possibly no bird is a better friend to the farmer, since the grosbeaks seem to have a special appetite for the potato bug, and is a tried and trusted destroyer of this enemy to the potato crop of our rural sections. 

One Spring, two or three years ago, a yellowthroated vireo built a nest in the crotch of a maple tree bordering this brook in the wooded area. I watched the building of this nest from start to finish and carefully observed this bird until she had raised her brood. This special bird builds a very firm nest and even now, after passing through three winters, that nest still hangs from the crotch in that maple tree.

Most people look forward to the advent of the robin before declaring Spring is with us. Several of the early birds will often come in advance of the robin. 

As a matter of fact in many localities throughout all parts of New England the robin can be found as a winter resident. They enjoy old orchards bordering a heavy pine growth, as these old orchards furnish decayed apples from which they can get the seeds they enjoy so much.

A little later the warblers will begin to come. Thus will the influx continue well into May when the nest building is in full swing and by the middle of June most birds are incubating and the best part of the song season of the year is past. Another year will come around before the best of the four seasons with its song, sun and showers will be here to enjoy again.

CHAPTER XXIV A SPRING MORNING WITH OUR SONG-BIRDS 

One must arise with the sun to get the full benefit of a bright Spring morning. All of our smaller songbirds are regulated by the sun, for when we consider the lives of song-birds, daylight means activity and darkness rest and sleep. 

One pleasant morning a few days ago I was awakened promptly at daybreak by the enthusiastic whistling of an oriole perched in the maple tree just outside my window. This orange colored, active little member of the feathered tribe was simply letting loose because his wife was building a finely woven nest in the very next tree. Possibly some of my neighbors would not appreciate his song as I did. In a few moments I was dressed and out in the bracing morning air to spend an hour in the open with the songsters.

Several times since I have offered silent thanks to this oriole for his waking me because it proved to be an exceptional morning for new comers.

The purple finches, who come in great numbers to one of our feeding stations, were just singing in unison in a chorus that was very inspiring. Perched on a wire that crosses my garden was a tree-swallow twittering away to his heart's content, no doubt competing with the oriole in his joy that his wife had rented a 156

bird home attached to the corner of my garage. While he was gleefully musical in his admiration she was busy gathering twigs and bits of grass and carrying them to the house she had rented, as a nest was in the process of construction.

As I stepped down onto the lawn and rounded the corner of the house I found a robin busily engaged in taking her morning plunge in the bird bath. How she did enjoy letting herself down into the fresh water, spreading her wings and then lifting herself high on her toes, giving herself a good shaking! This process of the toilet was repeated several times. Finally she hopped to the edge of the bath, again shook herself all over once and concluded the action by carefully preening her feathers, leaving a few small ones floating in the water. A fine looking specimen she was when she had completed her toilet. She then flew to a nearby tree and uttered a few call notes intended no doubt for her mate or other friends.

I was about to go back into the garden when a proud and haughty song-sparrow, perched on a small blue spruce, began his song of the morning's entertainment because of the really classical musical notes he uttered. 

I was also deeply interested, because I felt sure his wife had a well concealed nest somewhere in that tree. 

Investigation proved this to be the fact. The clue was one strand of twine hanging down from the inner section of the tree. The other end of the twine led directly to a very well selected place so concealed, that, had the mother bird not overlooked the using of one end of that twine it would have been rather difficult to find. My approach caused her exit and by gently spreading the closely leaved limbs I peered in and saw four handsome eggs. You may rest assured

that I retired after carefully placing each limb back in the perfect form for concealment as I had found them. 

My next objective was the rockaway bird bath in the further corner of the garden. Here I was quite sure I would find several catbird friends just merely playing and drinking in the bath. If one has ever taken the trouble to closely observe these birds at the bath he has noticed that the catbird always plays while bathing. He is a rather carefree bird at times and yet at the next moment may signify his wariness by a few of the meows which have given him his name. 

This morning I was not disappointed for I found three catbirds playing and bathing.

A strong pole ascends from the centre of this bath and at the top, a distance of twenty-six feet, is a martin home. Here were two martins just flying about in the air apparently trying to decide to remain when a bluebird appeared and perching himself on the very top of the martin house took command and the martins disappeared. 

A pair of bluebirds are nesting in a home located in another corner of the garden and this male was evidently the master of the situation. We have noticed in years gone by that bluebirds are seemingly of a jealous disposition and prevent the residence of other birds.

While thinking of this fact I looked across the garden toward the clothes lines and there I saw a pair of humming-birds perched on one of the lines. These little fellows were just admiring each other and at the moment were quite talkative in their very thread-like musical chant. As I approached them, the male stood out very markedly in his greenish color with his ruby

throat. Presently they flew away to a point unknown.

These little fellows have been with us for five successive years.

Two years ago they built their nest directly over the sidewalk where in passing, my hat would come within a few inches of the nest. In this diminutive, well moulded nest they hatched a pair of young but some mischievous boy later destroyed it all. 

Such a tragedy! 

The English starlings, grackles and the chipping sparrows were very numerous on this particular morning and seemed to be in the majority. No doubt the starlings find nesting places in holes in the tree trunks and in crevices about the eaves and cornice work of nearby residences. I have never known them to place a home in our garden. The grackles live in a large group of nearby pines while each Fall we find numerous nests of the chipping sparrow in the hedge bordering the garden. These little, welcome, cheering friends seem to enjoy the ibota and barberry hedges very much.

It was very near breakfast time and as I was about to enter the front door a yellow warbler, sounding his high pitched notes, greeted me from the white birch.

In the maple near at hand could be heard, but not seen, a red-eyed vireo and just across the street in another maple was a warbling vireo.

As I stepped up onto the veranda and glanced back for a final survey my gaze met a pair of cedar-waxwings quietly resting on a low branch of the white birch. Off in the distance could be heard the clattering notes of a house-wren, no doubt singing to his mate well housed in a nearby home. 

This was a pleasant half hour like scores of similar early morning visits to my garden. When one consid-

ers the location in the centre of a large residential section of a big city it merely goes to show what can be accomplished in the way of attracting birds to a small garden area by merely placing bird baths, feeding stations and bird homes about a residential lot. 

I entered the house, and going upstairs to prepare for the morning meal I took a final look at the nursery window feeding station and there I found two males and one female rose-breasted grosbeaks having a breakfast of the sunflower seeds. This made a very fitting, closing scene for the morning observations.

CHAPTER XXV A BIRD SANCTUARY IN A SMALL RESIDENTIAL GARDEN 

The writer has long felt that a well-established home with a small adjacent garden has something wanting about its environment if it does not have the things necessary to attract birds. 

Song-birds certainly play an important part in the happiness of a home. Also, if children who have reached the proper age are continually playing about the garden, they will develop traits of observation if given something in nature to observe. The writer has children of this age and they are continually making inquiry about birds and plants, trees, and flowers. Such inquiries must assist the parents to be better observers. and naturally to acquire the desire to help the children. 

Back of our home we have a space some fifty-five feet square. We have bounded this area by a hedge of ibota and barberry. Within the space we have set out apple, plum, pear, and cherry trees for our own use, with the possible exception of the cherries which we contribute freely to the robins and cedar-waxwings.

For small shrubbery we have set out rose bushes, elderberry, red cedar, mountain-ash, fir balsam, blue spruce, plain ash, bitter-sweet, Virginia creeper, Dutchman's pipe, white pine, red pine, and numerous other varie- 161

ties of low-growing shrubs. These were planted with the idea of creating an environment proper for birds in the fall and winter that they might find enjoyment within the branches and food when they lacked other resources for nourishment. All of these have flourished and are doing well and seem to fulfil the desires of various varieties of birds.

About two years ago, after things had begun to grow well, we set about to plan out a systematic home for the birds with the idea of having them brood their young within this small area.

Our children were becoming intensely interested, and our own enthusiasm was augmented by theirs. As a result, plans were drawn and ready-made houses, baths, feeding stations, nesting supply station, and the like were secured.

We secured the services of a mason and carpenter and things were begun to make birds happy. This idea was not without its drawbacks, for the numerous house-cats, the English sparrows, the small space at hand were all hindrances. However, we made progress and, wherever possible, we placed some sort of an enticement for birds. To eliminate the house-cat my boy used his air-rifle. The same method proved effective for driving away that pest, the English sparrow. 

Houses for wrens, bluebirds, chickadees, flickers, and other birds were placed under the advice of an ornithologist of some note, in positions where they would be attractive.

The attractive little circular wren house was placed on the south side of the residence, near the rose-arbor and Virginia creeper, and close at hand, on the lawn,

was placed a bird bath, which has proven to be a great relief to all kinds of birds.

On the south side of the house, just outside of the dining room window, was placed a wall feeding station which the birds frequent and enjoy very much. Just a little to the left of this feeding station, but at a higher elevation, was placed a bluebird house. This house has been occupied this season and a fine brood of youngsters raised. This rearing of a bluebird family, just outside of our dining room window, has been a great source of pleasure and instruction to my children.

In the rear of the house, facing the east, is situated the garden and some fruit trees. Here, near a cherry tree, we placed a large protected feeding station. This has been frequented by various kinds of birds both summer and winter. Because of our neighbor's housecats it has been necessary to surround this feeding station with a wire fence. At each corner of the feeding station we have placed a red cedar tree. 

Among the fruit trees has been put another bluebird house of the circular type, placed on an eight foot galvanized iron pole. This house was visited by bluebirds last year and again this season. At the base of the pole we have planted dwarf red pine and some bitter-sweet vine.

Just at the south border of the garden has been placed a nesting-supply station, with very happy results for many birds. This also can be used in winter for a suet supply station as well as a grain feeding station.

The rear of the garden is bounded in part by a trellis which is covered with bitter-sweet vine. In among this vine are placed two wall suet cone feeding sta-

tions, two robin shelter nest shelves and one wren. house. In this wren house a pair of wrens built this 

Spring but after completing the work they deserted the nest and built in a wren house in a birch tree across the street. Here they successfully reared a brood. 

These sprightly little songsters have been a source of great pleasure to us all and as I write I can hear their song of "five notes all in one," as Burroughs puts it.

In the far northeast corner of the garden we erected a purple martin house this Spring. This house has fifteen apartments and is placed on a pole thirty feet long. At the base of this pole was built a rockaway bird bath four feet high and four feet acrcoss the top.

This bath contains three basins of cement varying in depth from two inches to a half inch. This has been a rendezvous for many birds in which to make their toilet. The song-sparrows seem to enjoy it most, and this just at dusk each evening. How happy they seem in their bath, well protected from cats and shaded by the fruit trees, vines, and the sumac tree which grows very near!

Just at the south of this bath we placed a swing feeding station which serves its purpose very well. All in all, this corner of the garden, bounded by the ibota hedge, garnished with the evergreen trees and Styrax japonica, seems a little bird city all by itself.

The garage limits a portion of the eastern border, and here, covered as it is by bitter-sweet and trumpet vines, the birds gather to refresh themselves. On the south wall of the garage was placed a bluebird house and a chickadee house. The bluebird house has been visited and these houses soften the appearance of the garage wall on this side.

A general view of the garden, hardly fifty feet

square, is rather pleasing to one interested in birds. 

The cherry, plum, pear, and apple trees are there, not entirely for our own use, but that the birds may enjoy them as well. The vines, hedges, and flowers of the garden attract the humming-birds and wrens. At the close of day the song-sparrow and the chipping sparrow enjoy the rockaway bath. This space is for the birds and will remain so while the writer owns it. It furnishes a pleasant, worthwhile recreation. 

Regarding the martin house, it pleases one interested in birds to know that in this, the first season it has been out, four martins have quite regularly made visits. They come early in the morning and remain during the forenoon. The bluebirds persist in fighting them away but in time, possibly next year, we hope that the martins will return, conquer, and survive. 

How pleasing it would be to establish a martin colony in a city from which they have been absent for several years! 

Here is one little interesting incident in closing.

Early this Spring, in May, a pair of the bluebirds were seeking to nest in one of the houses. A male English sparrow took command of the house and the quarrel began. Such a noise and confusion! The bluebirds called in co-operative forces and while the argument was at its height, seated on a wire running from the residence across the garden to the garage were seen the following birds: a pair of bluebirds, a pair of wrens, a humming-bird, a pair of purple finches, a pair of yellow warblers, and a pair of chipping and of songsparrows. They were all arrayed in a straight line and with the seeming intent of driving the English sparrow away. It goes without stating that the English sparrow lost out and the bluebirds built and reared

their brood. Many are the birds seen in this little garden daily. The catbird, blue jay, grackle, flicker, waxwing, as well as those enumerated as gathering on the wire, are seen at almost any time.

This account is given merely to encourage others to establish these little sanctuaries. We cannot do too much for our bird friends, and the recreation gained by this hobby is a beneficial one. May others take this work up and thus properly mold a fitting environment so that our feathered friends may enjoy coming to the city.

CHAPTER XXVI BITS OF BIRD Lore Crested Flycatcher 

Not long ago, I was called to a small town among the hills west of Lake Sunapee. The house which I visited was situated upon a lonely cross-road where the farms are moderately large and the dwellings far apart. Upon my return trip, while motoring along at a moderate rate of speed a loud whistling note came to my ear from somewhere in a bordering orchard. 

Curious to know just the location of this note, I stopped my car by the roadside and proceeded to investigate. The day was sunny and warm and it was mid-afternoon. Jumping the stone wall I proceeded up through the old orchard with its dead and decaying trees. Once a place full of delicious apples, but now the mark of a deserted farm. No one was about and all was still except for this loud and pronounced intermittent whistle. Soon I found the tree from which the whistle came and presently located a moderately large bird perhaps the size of a robin or a little smaller. 

This fellow was perched on an edge limb and appeared to pay no attention to me. I paused and focused my glasses on him.

The olive-gray back, throat, and breast a little 167

darker and the mild yellow of the underparts were plainly seen. Most pronounced was the crest of comparatively large size and somewhat darker that the back. This crest stood out as a pronounced identification. The white wing markings and the dark reddish brown of the tail and stronger wing feathers completed the hurried identification of this bird. 

As I approached nearer the bird flew to a large hole in the upper part of the tree trunk. There he clung to the rough edges for a moment and then glided back to another limb. This fellow of solitude uttered several louder alarm notes, noisy and harsh. He had evidently spied me trespassing on his domicile. 

How beautiful he was, smooth as a pigeon and selfnamed master of this old orchard! I watched him for several moments. Presently he flew some distance to the very top of a large spruce bordering the roadside. 

There I watched him dart for flies and could very plainly hear his mandible as it sharply closed upon the prey. Deeply impressed by these acrobatic feats of this wonderful balancer of the air I selected a grass plot and sat down. Here I observed this crested flycatcher catch many flies and now and then when he misjudged his victim he would actually turn a somersault in mid-air and make another more successful dash for the mark. 

Never did he make a third attempt and seldom did he misjudge his initial spring. Precision, accuracy and self-confidence were his to the point of conceit. For a quarter of an hour I watched him earn his meal.

A few recreative moments well spent with the gain of an object lesson in accuracy and speed, were not soon to be forgotten. No doubt if I return this way some later date I will find a nest of moss and grass

possibly bordered by a cast off snake skin partly hanging from the hole of that old dead apple tree trunk. 

A signal that young crested flycatchers will come forth in due time. If I chance this way again I will surely stop and look.

CHAPTER XXVIII BITS OF BIRD LORE A Tree of Melody

CHAPTER XXIX RECREATION 

One day recently I felt myself longing for the snowcarpeted hills and the gentle song of the winter pines. 

I could not resist the temptation, and so with my oldest son as a companion, we hurried out to Goffstown with snow-shoes under our arms. Here we were joined by that staunch friend of mine, who knows birds so well, Maurice Blaisdell.

Across fields, over stone walls, through meadows and across brooks. Presently, we reached the foot of the mountain and began the ascent in and out among the juniper, alder, birch and young pine. Now and then we would halt for a moment and listen for the call of a bird or we would ourselves lisp or twitter in an attempt to call a bird to us. Through the thicker and larger pine, ash, chestnut, and cedar growth we wended our way, stooping under limbs, brushing aside small trees, slipping back on the ice, stumbling when the snow was soft 176

and deeper. Exercise, exhilaration, animation and recreation which bring to one that feeling of wellbeing were all ours.

Maurice is a keen naturalist and he knows this country better than any other. Those who have not had the fortune to know companionship with him, linked with his own style of friendship, have missed something very worthwhile.

Of course the main object of our tramp was birds. 

We were not successful in seeing many, however, as a few grouse, chickadees and one downy woodpecker were the only ones seen in the three hours thus spent. 

The flutter of the wings of the grouse as she rose and glided to another spot, the homelike spirit of the chickadee as he came down when called, and his artful moves and flights in and out among the branches of a cedar or pine, the gymnastic turn of the downy woodpecker and his soft, clean, feathery, winter coat all served to satisfy our desires. Crochet mountains met our gaze, snow-capped and deep in the slumber of desertion and heavy snows.

There were many farms whose fields were so well marked, all carpeted with snow, and the chimneys with the smoke from birch and pine burning in the kitchen fireplace, a real Thanksgiving sight when one but recalls the harvest of those fields but a few months past.

All was still, not a sound was heard, and we realized the restfulness that is ours away from the noise and bustle of the city. No rest is so filled with that which renews life and vigor as this. We paused for some time and thoroughly enjoyed the views. 

To the chickadees about us, we bade good-bye and made the descent, where now and then a stumble by one of us would bring forth a laugh. Past the sawdust mounds which the woodsmen had left, over the brooks, and picking up our coats, which we had hung in a pine low in the valley, we hurried back to Maurice's home.

I always learn something new and worthwhile about birds from Maurice for he knows them all so well. To satisfy us both, all we have to do is to spend a few hours together with those of the feathered flock which we can find at any season.

Just now the ebb of bird flight is low, but those we do find give more time for closer observation. A loss in numbers means a gain by closer study of the few. 

The sun was setting between Kearsarge and Crochet and the day had been well spent. Maurice and I parted with the friendly spirit we always show only to renew our walks and talks at another day in the near future.

CHAPTER XXX A REAL IMITATOR 

Recently, during an early morning walk, I came across Mr. Catbird, perched high in a small maple tree and fully enjoying himself in the bright sunshine. All about were scattered many other birds among them a number of warblers. In turn or in small grouped unison, these birds were giving their respective calls. 

It was a glorious morning for them all and to the listener, it was a melody of chimes coming from perfect throats.

Mr. Catbird was also at his best. Few people know this bird except by the cat-like meow meow meow! 

This, in reality, is only an alarm note and usually is entirely omitted from the vocabulary unless a stranger is seen or he is alarmed by some unusual noise. 

At this special moment, he thought himself safe and he was declaiming at his very best. For a quarter of an hour or more I stood still and remained very quiet lest I miss one of those fine, carefully moulded notes. 

Off in the distance a Maryland yellow-throat called, and immediately Mr. Catbird took up the note and repeated the call perfectly. This was soon followed by the call of the chestnut-sided warbler and Mr. Catbird tried his best to repeat this. This same act was tried after the towhee, chebec, redstart and even the 179

song-sparrow. It was amazing to realize how well the catbird could conquer these various calls. In none was he nearly perfect yet he was trying his best and doing well.

Between times, when no distant or extraneous notes or calls stimulated him on he would trill and sing his own strains of many varieties but never once giving the meow meow meow! How I longed for a witness because his imitations were so good, that one must surely credit this active bird with more than ordinary ability.

Possibly ten minutes passed, when suddenly he altered his entertainment, and originated many new tunes and then became very talkative. This proved to be a real talk feast. In various keys, at times modifying the quality, intensity, pitch and duration he kept on and I allowed my imagination to verbally record. "Pretty! pretty!-one! two! three!-here me now! here me now!"-this was followed by a long laughing group and it was a real good laugh. He then continued "Come here! Come here!"-several trills, "sit! sit! sit!-ha! ha! ha!"-This was followed by a strain very similar to the Baltimore oriole. A short pause and he continued "Hurry! hurry!-cheery! cherry-here-e! here-e! come here! come here! come here-watch it! watch it!"

This last exclamation was followed by a long trill. 

Then he dropped from his perch to the underbrush where no doubt the real cause of all of this hilarity, Mrs. Catbird, was quietly incubating a nest full of eggs. Later I investigated and found this to be true. 

For the less ardent, or even the ardent bird lover this description of mirth and entertaining mimicry and chat may be overdrawn. Think as you wish but

just take an early morning walk, locate a catbird in vocal action and listen attentively for a few moments. 

You surely will not interpret all of these notes exactly as given above, yet your admiration for Mr. Catbird as a real imitator and linguist will be augmented.

CHAPTER XXXII THE TREE-SWALLOW 

On a hot, sultry evening when the temperature had been hovering around the century mark since early morning, there was not even a slight breeze stirring. 

At the Country Club on the lawn separating the broad veranda of the club house from the fairways was gathered a group of people. In the clear air one or two hundred feet above, the tree-swallows were having a joyous time catching insects on the wing, playing in the open air and gaining their evening meal at the same time. No doubt this was their usual program each day at this time, but it seemed more interesting to the watching group of people because of the early season of the year and the recent arrival of the sea birds from South America. 

As I gazed skyward and saw this most picturesque sight, with its background of color from the radiating streams of light of the setting sun my thoughts turned to the wonderful flight of these beautiful birds of the air. Now and then one of these clever flyers would volplane from a height in its course and as it turned to rise again its snow-white breast and under part of the body could be seen. As it climbed into the air the irridescence of its back, augmented by the sun rays, appeared in all its beauty. 187

How these marvellous flyers made time and distance as they flew from near the earth to soaring over the tops of the distant pines! Apparently never tiring, these tree-swallows seemed to gain strength and speed as they flew. Now and then one or two would soar over the distant pines and seem to stand at attention in mid air, then with a flutter of their wings would gracefully sink into the branches of the tree beneath, where no doubt they had a nest in the hollow of the trunk, to feed their young or add to the weaving of the new nest. Occasionally a pair would fly side by side very swiftly as if in a race in mid air, turning and twisting, at the same instant, as if by command, in the same manner and direction. How very swiftly they would move and never miss a single turn together as if held attached to a string! Sometimes others would soar, volplane, turn and twist in mid air at times almost doing a tail spin. Occasionally one would apparently see an insect some feet above him, immediately change its course, turn straight upward, and with a rapid movement of the wings actually climb the air, get its prey and then again take a direct flight. This was many times repeated. 

All the while the musical prattle of these graceful flyers voiced the wonderful tones which bring the gladness of the open air season to everyone on the beautiful golf course with the fairways in all directions bounded by a splendid growth of pine, ash, hemlock, spruce, birch and maple as well as sturdy oaks.

Mile upon mile was covered by these great flyers during the half hour of observation and all over an area of a half mile in all directions. From this it was clearly seen how these very graceful and powerful flyers could make five hundred or even a thousand

miles in a day in their migratory flight, this week in South America and the next in New England. Any person æsthetically inclined must envy these powerful flyers their grace of motion, joyous prattle of musical chatter and the wonderful ease of it all. 

The hundreds of troublesome mosquitoes, gnats, black flys and other small bugs consumed daily by the tree-swallows places them among the most useful of our summer birds in New England.

CHAPTER XXXIII THE EARLY SPRING SONGSTER

CHAPTER XXXIV SEEN IN MY GARDEN

Few birds of any size have greater wing power than the flicker. Although not unusually large, this bird, like the hundred yard dash athlete, is built for speed.

Shaped like a torpedo, at least in a way, more or less pointed at both head and tail, this bird can literally dash through the air at great speed.

CHAPTER XXXVI WINTER FEEDING OUR SONG-BIRDS 

Just at the season of the year when the ground is covered with snow, lovers of song-birds naturally turn their thoughts toward their little feathered friends who are seen about the residential sections of our cities and along the highways.

A great many of these birds are ground feeders and with a deep coating of well crusted snow upon the ground, it is a serious task for some of these birds to find their daily rations. For this reason, many of these birds are found coming more closely to the dwellings hoping to find some form of food. 

This morning at sunrise as I looked out the window across my garden and on the street behind I spied a fine cock-pheasant strutting down the sidewalk. Although it was in a rather, thickly settled residential section of a busy city, this pheasant, a ground feeder, was out at dawn seeking food for his breakfast. sympathized with his anxiety as I watched him until he disappeared behind a dwelling. I hope he found that which would satisfy his keen appetite after a night spent, no doubt, in some sheltered corner of a nearby home.

Such incidents as this will cause even the busiest of individuals to pause and think. As a result we find in 198

the recent years that human beings are saving the table scraps and crumbs and placing them in a convenient place in the garden or on the lawn where these feathered friends find the food and enjoy the hospitality. 

Hundreds of winter birds come into the cities and towns when the snow deprives them of their natural food. It is a very interesting fact to note how really tame and careless many of these birds become when driven into the towns for food. The chickadee, treesparrow, nut-hatches, kinglets, redpolls, pine-siskins, blue jays and many others will find food put out for them and will very easily learn that the donor is a friend. Table scraps, ground up meat scraps, bread and cake crumbs, doughnuts and millet seed, when placed on a dish or on a feeding shelf soon attract these birds and they become daily visitors. A piece or two of suet tied to the limb of a tree will most surely bring the chickadee or woodpecker and once he has found the tempting morsel he will return daily and partake thereof.

As I glance out upon the garden area of my own residence I can, at most any time of the day, find the feeding stations, grain shelves, suet lumps or doughnuts attracting some one or more of these winter birds.

It is a pleasure to know that, what might otherwise be thrown into the garbage-can or incinerator, now does service to these little, ever-helpful birds.

Then again there is another side to this befriending of the birds. The more of these birds that can be attracted to one's garden during these cold, bleak days, the more sure one is that in warmer days to come in 

Spring and Summer fewer will be the bugs, moths and caterpillars to destroy the fruit trees, shrubbery and flowers. When food is hard to find and these birds

come to us seeking a small daily supply they are ever busy and you may rest assured that the smallest crevice in every nook and corner among the leafless limbs of trees and shrubs will be looked over and the hibernating insects and moths destroyed.

Thus these winter birds serve as a very important economic factor and many times pay for the trouble one goes to in properly placing a little feeding shelf outside the window or some suet on the limb of a tree. 

The good winter birds do, by this process of insect elimination, yearly save millions of dollars to the city dweller and the farmer.

A little piece of stout wire stretched from one tree to another or from the corner of the house to a nearby tree will serve very well as a means of suspending suet and doughnuts. Anyone will be surprised to see how fond winter birds are of doughnuts. Hanging from a string attached to this wire a doughnut soon freezes hard and these little feathered birds come and enjoy picking away at it and getting a good meal. In between times one will always find these little fellows ever active among the trees and shrubs seeking their dessert, so to speak.

Not many years ago, New England passed through a very hard winter for wild feathered life. The snowfalls came very frequently and piled high. Even the weeds of moderate height, which will ordinarily remain above the snow surface, in this particular year were buried far out of sight. Thousands of grouse and pheasants perished from starvation. Here in

Manchester the Bird Club established four feeding stations and through Boy Scout Troop organization, these stations were kept supplied with grain furnished by the Manchester Bird Club.

All the winter through, scores of pheasants and grouse came to these stations and were saved. Almost any morning, from ten to forty of these birds could be seen feeding at any one of these stations. Many individuals took up the work and put table scraps out. No doubt, in this way the lives of hundreds of birds were saved that winter here in this part of New England. 

Few are the citizens who do not wish that bird life should be conserved and do not desire it to increase from year to year. The vicissitudes which birds meet even in warmer days are so numerous that at times some of us wonder how any of the birds survive. With the cold, stormy days of winter these worthy friends of man should be fed. It costs nothing except a few moments of one's time. The food needed would otherwise go into the garbage-can and with the little feather creatures just waiting outside for the morsel, should we not be willing to give and thus save this economic principle for future activity?

The writer has three such feeding stations in the garden and they are kept supplied the year through. 

A few years ago a pair of rusty blackbirds took shelter in a group of high pines not far from the garden. 

Daily during the hard cold winter experienced here in New England that year, this pair of blackbirds were seen at the feeding stations. 

A neighbor tells me that for several years he has fed a pair of robins all through the winter months. 

During the day they feed from the berry-laden vine clinging leafless to his veranda. At night they seek shelter beneath his porch cellar coping and seem to thrive well, notwithstanding their inability to pull the worms they relish in warmer days.

It is a well established fact that many of our sum-

mer season birds will thrive well in winter here in New England if they but get food, for the cold does not seem to upset them so long as they get enough to eat. 

Here about our city, we yearly have numbers of songsparrows who remain the winter through. All they need is some place to which they may go each day and get food. Where is the citizen who does not love the little song-sparrow at any time of the year?

All last winter a feeding station placed outside of the nursery window drew to our home a large flock of tree-sparrows. What an invigorating sight it was any winter morning to watch these little, active, soldierly birds enjoy themselves, partaking of the grain and crumbs put out for them.

A pair of chickadees has for several years come to our feeding stations and made this area their winter home. Each Fall we look forward to their return and enjoy making ourselves their hosts. Any of the children of the household can go out and with a handful of nut meat crumbs, or doughnut crumbs, attract these little fellows to them. They become very tame and domestic as morning after morning these little fellows will come to the dining-room window and wait for their food.

New England is the haunt of the finest game and song-bird life. No area in all the country is better adapted naturally to attract wild bird life. Every citizen should take up the slogan, "Feed Our Winter 

Birds," and thus get the thrill that comes from that form of recreation. 

Establish a crumb tray for the birds to-day. Put out a piece of suet and just watch the results. Give them a fifty-fifty chance and they will bring to you a thousandfold return through the good they will do in

destroying the insects, worms and moths so harmful to your fruit trees and shrubbery as well as the vegetable and flower garden. "Feed Our Winter Birds."

CHAPTER XXXVII BIRD OBSERVATION WALKS 

We probably learn more about our song-birds in one hour spent with them in the open fields and woods than we should learn from several hours of book study. In no way should the worth of bird books be undervalued. 

As a matter of fact what would we do without them? 

They serve best to prepare us for these open field observation walks and should always be found in the library of a student of bird life.

Never be without your books when at home with time to study. This is most essential yet the real, life long standing impressions of song-bird life are gleaned in the open fields and woods. There again bird observation walks not only give to the observer knowledge, but health. How stimulating a good, early morning stroll through the open grass-land and among the pines, cedars, hemlock and graceful birches! To arise early on a clear brisk spring morning and with field glass in hand, saunter off to an environment of song and uplift! The heart is gladdened, the respirations become deeper, the voice clear, eye bright and when an hour later, one returns to the breakfast table with a keen appetite and a feeling of well being, the day opens up with added prospects and ambitions. 

When the work is done after a hard day at the 204

bench, in the office or a long business drive in the auto and one feels a little fatigued, what wonders will be created by an hour spent among the song-birds in the open! The rest from such recreative study brings on renewed vigor and a readiness for the evening meal to be followed later by a good night of refreshing sleep. 

Where is there a pastime to be enjoyed so fully even by a single individual as that spent in the bird adorned fields and woods? Of course, companionship adds to the pleasure, but it is not always at hand and one can go along alone and be fully repaid.

However, there are marked advantages to companionship in bird study. Each individual sees a bird in a little different light. These differences help to check up on the observations. The very new student can go with the old observer and learn. Those old in the art can impart new and renewed knowledge to the other. 

Groups of individuals of both sexes can go out and enjoy the study as a unit, choosing the most talented and experienced as the leader. Father and son or daughter or again the sons and daughters, even the youngest, can go out and learn much in the way of 

Nature study.

Athletic and less vigorous recreative and competitive games serve their purpose always and are ever to be encouraged as a vigorous road to manhood. However, most contests and recreative games have set rules while open field, bird observation walks have no such rules and regulations. Every new walk each day as the hours pass makes its own rules and regulations as one proceeds onward. There are, of course, certain general considerations to be thought of, but no matter how many open field walks one may take, each is a maker of rules within itself.

Keenness of sight is developed, acute hearing brought into play, alertness at every turn, promptness and tactfulness are needed with every move. The highest type of thought soon becomes a part of one's daily acquirement.

From these walks the older in age, those of middle and adolescent life and even the little tots gain their share of benefit mentally and physically. The benchworker, desk-toiler, office man, school teacher in fact those of all walks of life can meet here and impart the highest knowledge, one to the other. Take the teacher who has been through a day of tedious, exasperating, patience trying experiences. Let her take the same class out for a bird observation walk and what a new light it will bring to the life of the teacher and pupils! 

Kindness and gentleness are developed during these walks. Love of Nature extending to the love of home and fireside come from such walks. Business associates and competitors can meet each other and enjoy these walks not only being better men and women because they learn much about the lovable song-bird, but because they also acquire stronger love for each other and for the inherent good qualities always to be found in all.

It is the strong individual traits that, passed on to others, do the big, round world so much good. Just acquire the open field and woodland high and low, as your recreative arena where one can find song-birds in abundance. Uplifting carols, alert actions and tidy appearance will greet one on every hand. Become one of Nature's students in the open fields and woods. The benefit mentally, physically, morally and spiritually is beyond measure. Just try it out and become a convinced enthusiast.

CHAPTER XXXVIII ON THE WAY HOME 

This evening I walked home from my office enjoying one of the beautiful sunsets of this time of the year. 

Yesterday Nature had covered the ground with some three or four inches of damp, pure white snow which had fallen after some two weeks of most agreeable warm, early Spring weather.

As I sauntered along in the select residential section of our city I noticed on the lawn of one of our finest estates a bird unusual for this special area. This little fellow flew across my path and as he passed I noticed his rather large wing spread, moderately slow flight and caught the copper red of his back.

At first I thought it was a new arrival in the person of a fox-sparrow, but was not sure of my diagnosis. 

Passing on after this bird had disappeared in the distance, I noticed others of the same kind and as some of them were so very tame I approached them very closely. I soon recognized that all about me were scores of hermit-thrushes with now and then a veery. 

Of course this discovery surprised me greatly and as I proceeded on my way passing lawn shrubbery and hedges these wonderful members of the feathered tribe became more and more numerous. They had no song at this time and many of them appeared cold. 207

Their feathers were ruffled and their wings partly spread as they sauntered about on the lawns and among the shrubbery and hedges in search of food. 

When I reached home that evening I found my own garden and orchard, which has become a small sanctuary, literally filled with these hermit-thrushes and a few veeries.

How often I have sat on the edge of a wooded growth in May and June and heard both of these birds repeat their beautiful, and to me, inimitable notes!

How shy they have been and only occasionally and then usually in the very early morning, have I been able to see them! Now right in my very dooryard, in the centre of a thickly populated residential district I was just surrounded with hundreds of these beautiful birds.

My interpretation of this unusual circumstance was that the rather deep snow had covered the grounds in the woods and thus deprived these birds in migration of their natural food. For this reason they had sought open ground and had come into the city where the bright sun during the day had uncovered many lawns and other grounds. Here they could satisfy their appetites on the winter berries of the bittersweet and barberry so plentiful about the residences.

In fact my interpretation was verified by seeing the hermit and veery feeding upon these berries. 

Dusk prevented continued observation that day, but the next morning I was up with the sun and found about my home and all through the neighborhood scores, yes hundreds of hermit-thrushes and the veery feeding upon the berries and the uncovered grass land. 

How tame they were, permitting one to come very close to them! This same circumstance no doubt has

been noted by others but personally I have never seen these birds in such large numbers in the city. In fact 

I have never even heard their notes near my home at any time of year but always have been obliged to go out into the wooded areas to hear them. 

During this morning bird walk I noticed besides the hermit and veery many juncos, song-sparrows, whitethroated sparrows and a great many robins and a few chickadees. I also saw a few pine and yellow palmwarblers. 

To me it was a great bird morning. Of course all but the hermit-thrushes and veery were not unusual for this section of the city, except perhaps that they were a little in advance of the usual time, but one might expect that in view of the advanced Spring of this year. 

In fact the song-sparrow has been with us for some time and the phobe for a week or two while the chickadee and junco have remained with us all winter. 

Such an inspiring sight upon such a lovely, warm, sunny morning! I shall never forget it and I hope my endeavors to supply food for them all was rewarded. 

I feel sure it was, as scores of these birds have remained about our feeding stations during the past three or four days since I first observed the influx.

CHAPTER XXXIX COMBINED FORCES 

Just outside the East window of my dining room is a bluebird house. For several years a pair of bluebirds have nested here and raised a brood evidently with satisfaction and success. With the coming of each Spring we look forward to the advent of these tenants and welcome their melodious tones. This year as in years past they returned and one mid-day early in June evidently had decided to start house keeping in the old home.

My wife was in the pantry, the east window of which looks out on to the garden. Suddenly her attention was attracted to the boisterous outburst of clattering notes of many birds. This was so very unusual that she stepped out into the garden to ascertain the cause of this noisy clatter of the feathered tribe. Here she witnessed what was evidently a quarrel between a bully male English sparrow and his mate and the pair of bluebirds. The trouble seemed to be as to who should gain possession of that bird house on the east corner of the house. At the time the bully male English sparrow had possession and peering out from the entrance he was, in no soft tones, declaring his intention of being master of that house. His mate was perched on the top of the house and now and then would lend her 210

voice to the argument. Perched on a telephone wire `running from the corner of the house were the two bluebirds, the male was nearest the bird home and from his rather solemn and firm attitude seemed to be trying to gain entrance. The two bluebirds sat there evidently working out the problem of attack and all the while tuning in with their rather melancholy, scolding notes.

Now and then the male English sparrow would dart forth from the entrance and fly fiercely at this male bluebird while the wives of both were taking an active part in the talk. This drama was repeated several times, the male English sparrow always returning to the home following his attack and with his head peering out of the entrance would again repeat his noisy challenge. Finally both bluebirds uttered strange notes and, as if called into action, presently a Baltimore oriole flew to the top of the nearby cherry tree. 

Soon his mate appeared on another branch of the same tree.

On the ground to the right a little distance off and near the ibota hedge were two chipping sparrows who became very excited and chirped in vigorously. The call for forces continued and a male song-sparrow came fluttering to the nearby ash tree and gave his alarm note.

Strange as it may seem within five minutes the electric light wire running to the garage held perched in battle line a pair of purple finches, a pair of hummingbirds and a yellow warbler! All had evidently come to the support of the pair of bluebirds and held here a council of war.

Just at this point the vital move of the whole affair took place. Suddenly out dashed bully male English

sparrow probably realizing that his defeat was assured and as if by agreement he and his mate took flight and did not reappear.

The notes of all those remaining birds took on a new pitch and quality. The male bluebird flew to the front of the bird house and after a short pause entered while his mate edged nearer on the wire. The oriole in the top of the cherry tree whistled as if applauding. Birddom seemed called to that spot to celebrate a victory and just from around the corner on the south side came the joyous, incoherent, prattle of the house-wren, taking quite a share in the victory. 

This array of bird talent remained about for some half hour or so continuing to talk it all over except the humming-birds who seemed contented to watch silently.

The possession of the bluebirds was permanent and they went about their work of building their nest with system and regularity. In due time they raised the annual breed and in the Fall departed, well satisfied as we think, and this Spring have again returned. 

This special drama the writer had never before observed. Purple finches, orioles, yellow warbler, humming-birds, chipping sparrows, song-sparrows, all coming evidently at the call of the bluebirds, by their presence and advice, without any vicious clash, had routed the English sparrows and through the final word of the house-wren gave vent to their joy at overcoming a troublesome foe.

CHAPTER XL BIRDS IN MY GARDEN 

In the midst of a thickly populated residential section of the moderate sized city in which is my home the song-birds are attracted to our gardens by the proper amount of foliage, well supplied feeding stations and cleanly kept bird baths.

My own garden is small; possibly fifty-five feet wide and forty feet deep. Here I have arranged the shrubbery, trees, flowers and hedges so as to afford the greatest amount of protection to the birds. The feeding stations are kept well supplied and the baths, in the warm season, clean and filled.

The varieties of birds seen in even such a small city garden is surprising. Of course, in summer the numbers are much greater yet in the midst of the short, cold winter days one is impressed by the numbers and the varieties of visitors. At this very moment while I am writing the ground is heavily covered with snow and the mercury down around zero, yet the little feathered friends are with us in good numbers. 

From sunrise to sunset, any day, if one will but keep watch the number observed will be most gratifying.

One Sunday a few days ago I kept account of the birds visiting this little sheltered patch and the following birds were seen: chickadees, white-breasted nut-hatch, 213

red-breasted nut-hatch, tree-sparrows, downy woodpecker, goldfinches, four bluejays, redpolls, pine-siskin and golden-crown kinglets.

The chickadees come in numbers and delight the children of the household with their athletic abilities. 

Tree-sparrows come in a large flock. We have banded a goodly number of these and it is surprising how very neighborly they have become. We have a great many of this type of bird return to the trap. The little fellows seem to enjoy being caught and handled and become very fond of our attentions.

The white and red-breasted nut-hatches and the downy woodpeckers do not come in groups, but singly, yet they are constantly with us and enjoy the suet, doughnuts and grain placed at their disposal. The goldfinches, redpolls and pine-siskins come in flocks of from half a dozen to a dozen or more and enjoy the garden every day.

One real severe and stormy winter several years 'ago, a pair of rusty blackbirds remained with us through the entire winter. At night they roosted in a nearby pine tree and all during the cold days remained in the garden feeding and partaking of the shelter afforded.

About three winters ago a white-winged crossbill fraternized with a group of sparrows and remained with us all winter. He was a beautiful little fellow and very likely had strayed away from some migrating flock late in the same Fall. When the warm days of 

Spring came he disappeared and no doubt that majestic blue spruce which had adopted him was as sorry to see him go as we were.

The bluejays come daily, arriving early in the morning and feed upon the sunflower seeds. They are

curious fellows, rather greedy and boisterous, yet, we enjoy them. Seizing a sunflower seed in their bills and flying to a nearby tree they will place this seed beneath their toes and proceed to crack the shell with their bills and devour the contents. All day long they carry on this carpenter work and at night seem to fly away to distant woods always returning in the morning. 

As the warmer days of Spring approach, these winter birds scatter and disappear going north or to more mountainous regions for nesting. They always come back to us in the Fall and remain for the Winter season. The variation from this rule is the chickadee. 

He remains and nests with us and for the past few years has been a permanent resident, no doubt because we supply him with what he wants. 

Early in March on some morning especially warm, I am awakened by the first note of the song-sparrow.

This little fellow comes early and remains with us until late in the Fall. Numbers of them nest in our little garden and are very successful in raising broods. 

The same can be said of the bluebirds. Hardly does the snow begin to go when the early Spring songs of these birds are heard. The bluebirds always occupy their quota of the bird homes and successfully raise, generally two broods, often both in the same house. 

They are very neat housekeepers and when the homes are cleaned out in the Fall no waste is ever found in their nests which are usually made of well woven pine needles. 

Robin redbreasts come in great numbers. Just as soon as the lawn is softened by the passing of the frost they will be found in great numbers guarding the grassed land by keeping the worms cleaned up. The robins nest in the maples, vines and ash trees and when

the young begin to come, it is always necessary to protect them from the house cats. 

The little chipping sparrow comes to us in great numbers and nests in the hedge and shrubbery. Without him we would seem lost in our endeavor to attract the summer birds. Their sprightly manners and semiwariness combined with their choice of a snug nesting site which they keep secret are the peculiarities we enjoy. The Baltimore orioles are among the summer residents of our garden. They enjoy the cherry and mulberry trees and their whistle just adds zest to the enjoyment of our garden. The rose-breasted grosbeaks come in fairly good numbers and they also enjoy the berry bearing trees. 

Yellow warblers and cedar-waxwings are fond of our white birch tree. They seem to take great pride in keeping this one tree free from insects and worms. 

Some of our very regular tenants are the catbirds. 

For several years past these birds have nested in the shrubbery about the porch and strange as it may seem, they seem to lose their shyness and become very friendly, bathing directly beneath our dining room window with as much unconcern as the neighbor's children frolicking on the lawn.

For several years a pair of humming-birds have nested in our garden enjoying the larkspur and nasturtium blossoms. Strange little bits of feathers! 

How proud they are when perched on the clothes line swinging to and fro with the gentle summer breezes!

Just last summer this pair of humming-birds built in the crotch of one of the maples so near the sidewalk that my hat would have touched the nest if I had not been aware of its location. We have no proof that these are the same pair of birds, except that they prove

it by the regularity of their coming. Although we are unable to talk with them we are satisfied that they know us and appreciate our protection.

Purplefinches visit us each summer in great numbers. A special window feeding station is theirs, and here they congregate and enjoy the sunflower seeds. 

They nest all about us and when the young can take the wing our feeding stations are surely over-crowded. 

Now and then in the Spring we see a Canadian warbler, a blackburnian warbler or a myrtle warbler but they never have remained so far as we are able to observe. One hot sultry day a black-billed cuckoo came to the garden, and, picking a quarrel with another smaller bird evidently must have been defeated because later in the day we found him dead. He had committed suicide by hanging from a crotched, steel support of the nesting supply station. This incident was a sad one and we deplored its occurrence yet this little fellow has ever since adorned a special shelf in the study and has become a permanent resident as he silently watches over the children of the household. 

Grackles come in great numbers and nest in the large pines. They may have their faults but we enjoy them and let them alone. 

The tree-swallow and the chimney-swift are always with us. How we do enjoy watching them in their airy flight! Clever are these birds and we appreciate their presence when on a summer evening we sit out on the veranda and enjoy the air without the tormenting mosquitoes. One bird has come to us on several occasions but never to remain. We have erected a special house for this bird but he does not remain for long, although he has returned at intervals for a day or so. 

This is the purple martin. How we all wish they would

remain! It has been noticed that whenever they come the bluebirds will always gather and fight them off. 

This may account for their infrequent visits to the martin house. We hope some day they will establish a home and learn to come year after year. 

One little bird that has been with us the past two summers is the indigo-bunting. A pair of these have nested here and they seem to enjoy the place very much. They are wonderful little birds and the colors so emphasized in the male, stand out in contrast to all else in the garden.

For several years we had looked for a little, but cheery fellow, the house-wren, but he did not appear until three years ago when on Memorial Day I heard his note. We have been accorded the pleasure of his presence each summer since and hope he will keep his lease. As is the usual custom of this little fellow, while his mate is incubating in one house he is constructing a new nest in another home. We enjoy this pair and look forward to their coming and remaining for they have discovered a good location.

During the early migration one Spring some few years ago, a deep snow fell, covering all of the natural feeding grounds. It was on a Saturday and, when I awoke the following morning, which chanced to be

Easter Sunday, my garden lot was filled with a concert of music of the best sort. A large gathering of foxsparrows had come to the stations for food. What a joyous day it was! They remained with us for several days. Few birds can equal their notes. 

Each Spring and Fall we have flocks of junicos as they pass North and South. They are always welcome, and we hope that sometime a few at least will remain with us for the winter. These graceful ground

feeders seem to add so much to the activity and peacefulness of the surroundings. 

Last Spring, a late heavy snow fell and drifted about a good deal. The following days were rather warm but, of course, the natural feeding grounds were blanketed with snow. One evening, just at sunset, I reached home to find my garden and in fact the entire lot, literally filled with hermit-thrushes. These beautiful songsters were apparently caught in the air during a long flight and driven down by the heavy snow. 

They were very neighborly and not in the least shy, coming very close to the buildings evidently seeking open turf ground near the base construction. These agile feathered friends remained about several days, but with the gradual disappearance of the snow they also left. No song notes were heard from their throats during this unusual visitation. During each winter now and then a larger or smaller flock of snow-buntings will come and remain a few hours, never for long and not often on consecutive days. A few pine-grosbeaks and evening-grosbeaks are seen each winter. 

They never can be said to live in this little sanctuary yet they are around about the vicinity each winter. 

The whistle of the pine grosbeak and the cheery, semimusical, rather sharp notes of the evening grosbeaks are always welcome sounds. 

Once on a cold blustering winter day, a Northern shrike appeared in the leafless vineway. He evidently was looking for prey in the form of some smaller bird. I took the pains to put him to flight and he promptly flew to the topmost branch of a large maple trée and there perching for a time, eventually flew away.

A sparrow-hawk came into the garden lot one sum-

mer afternoon staying only a short time and not returning. 

A pair of king-birds have nested with us two seasons. We are always very glad to see them, as they are rather handsome and most neighborly although at intervals they acquire a scolding mood. However, we always welcome them and trust they will not overlook the latch key which is ever out for them. 

This list of the birds seen about our garden merely serves as an encouragement for others to establish a little bird sanctuary in a city, residential lot no matter how small. Just fit it up and the returns will fully warrant the effort. The birds are about us in their season and it remains only for us to attract them with food, and shelter, combined with bathing and nesting facilities and protection. Once we have done this our joy will be great both summer and winter by the frequent visitations of our little feathered friends. May the time come when all city residential lots will be so inhabited!

CHAPTER XLI AN EXPERT DIVER 

Early in the Spring I chanced to spend a day or two at Sebago Lake. As the bird migration was just beginning, this was excellent opportunity for one to get out into the woods and enjoy the musical notes of these birds.

Among those less musical was a little colony of belted kingfishers. There were six of these birds in this colony on the sandy banks of North-Eastern outlet, a small stream running out from the lake and passing on through the town of East Sebago. 

I located the birds high up in the tops of some old, dead chestnut trees. Hidden by the undergrowth I found a soft seat of sand and remained quiet. Before me was a little lake formed by the head waters of this small North-Eastern stream. Every now and then one or two of these kingfishers would take wing and cross to the other bank. After a time they would return or fly about over the smooth water for a few moments, always clattering their notes especially at the beginning and end of this flight. Evidently they were in search of food, as the smelt were having their Spring run and were plentiful in this part of the lake. 

Presently one fine specimen of a kingfisher launched forth, flew in circles over the water and when fifty 221

feet or so above the surface suddenly closed in his wings and dived straight down, cutting the water perfectly and disappearing beneath the surface. In five seconds he reappeared with one of the fish in his bill. 

Again taking wing he flew back to his comrades. 

Hardly had this happened when another kingfisher appeared this time taking an angular dive and bringing up a fish nearly as long as himself. Without apparent effort he rose to the wing and carried his prey to a distant limb and proceeded to eat it. 

I have never seen better diving or the completing of these stunts in such perfect form. These birds would actually fold in their wings tightly to their sides and at times dive straight downward from a height of at least a hundred feet. One remained under water for ten seconds and upon coming to the surface took to the wing as perfectly as if from the limb of a tree, without any evidence of fatigue. I watched for one hour during which time a dozen fish were caught.

CHAPTER XLII THE VANGUARD 

It is always interesting at this time of year to keep a record of the birds as they return from their winter in the South. If we look back over such records for several years we find that the disappearance of the snow agrees with the coming of the birds. Thus it is seen that most birds like open ground to work North on and will generally follow the snow line as it is melting away.

Some ornithologists aver that migration in the Spring starts practically at the same time each year yet the records would hardly support this theory. 

One bright, sunny, Sunday morning the writer awakened his boys and we started out to learn what birds had already arrived as a vanguard to the warbler flood which would probably arrive within ten days. 

Tramping along the edge of a picturesque little stream bordered by leafless alders, birches, maples and other growths of the same nature the first sound heard was the distant yep! yep! yep! of the flicker. Following up the note we soon found the caller with a number of his brothers and sisters gathered in an old maple tree near the edge of a pine growth. 

While observing this interesting group of one of the most valuable of our birds, off in the distance we heard 223

the high pitched note of that marvelous ventriloquist the white-throated sparrow. Hardly had this note died away when to our right was heard that cheery call of the field-sparrow. We followed this and found our little feathered friend perched on the top of a low scrub oak. Again he gave his call as if about his neck was hung a cluster of bells which he first chimed separately and then shook all together. 

While watching this musical friend a large flock of purple grackles flew across over head, no doubt, seeking a pool of water for their morning bath. In an old orchard bordering a field just West of the pine growth several robins were busily engaged gathering their breakfast pausing now and then to send out their gleeful notes.

Presently we sauntered over to the edge of a small pond and on the way we came across several towhees. 

They were swishing about among the dead leaves on the ground very like a flock of hens, calling chewink! chewink! chewink! with its ending trill each time. They are always so active, seeking food in the thick under brush.

As we walked on and nearer the pond we heard that fine, thread like, high pitched, even note of the blackand-white warbler, the real advance member of his family. In years gone by we have found this little. fellow a week or ten days before the other warblers.

The hour had passed and the children were ready for breakfast, so we started homeward. On the way we passed through, on one of our main streets, the door yard of a neighbor and here we found a pair of meadow-larks perched on the ridge pole of the house sounding their plaintive, high pitched call and now and

then rising on the wing and seemingly standing still in mid air.

As we entered our own garden and orchard we found great numbers of purple finches at the feeding stations enjoying the sunflower seeds. Perched on the top of the garage was a song-sparrow always intermittently uttering her musical phrase. A morning walk for the health of the children, an educational hour of recreative pleasure, a good appetite for the breakfast awaiting us, made a very good beginning for such a beautiful Sabbath.

CHAPTER XLIII FEEDING THE WINTER BIRDS 

As time goes by we find more people taking an active interest in our song-birds as well as in the larger birds known as game birds. This fact is not only true regarding the summer season when our songsters are rather more numerous but also in the winter. Of course the number of birds found in the Northern states in the winter is very much less, yet there are those birds that spend their summers further north and breed there that do come down to us when the snow gets deep and their natural feeding grounds are covered.

Then again, the pleasure derived from furnishing our little feathered friends with food during the blustering days of a long winter is wonderful. Having a feeding station placed in plain view of one of the windows, the ground outside covered with snow and a goodly number of smaller winter birds enjoying the food you put out is a pastime which grows with interest the more you indulge in it.

Most song-birds of the summer season have plenty of food furnished them by nature. In fact their wants and the supply necessary for the fledgings are to be found almost anywhere in the city or country during 226

the summer.

In the winter it is much more difficult for the birds that remain with us to find food. Most of the winter birds are seed eaters and unless the snows become very deep they can usually find a good supply of weed seeds about the open fields. However with such winters as we sometimes have, when the snow is piled high everywhere, all the natural feeding grounds are covered over.

When such winters as this appear we find the snow-buntings, tree-sparrows, redpolls, pine-siskins, chickadees, golden-crowned kinglets and the like leaving the wooded areas and open fields and coming into the rather thickly populated towns and cities. They do this for the reason that here they are able to get a better food supply around the freight houses and grain shops where grain leaks out onto the ground.

Not only this but to-day so many people are making a practice of keeping up a good, weather proof, feeding station for the winter birds that they very soon learn of these stations and come into the towns and cities for this reason. 

Another very creditable habit that many bird lovers have developed is that of tieing pieces of suet to the trunks and limbs of the fruit trees. Here the downy woodpecker, brown creeper, chickadee and other birds come and enjoy that heat producing food a thing they need most of all. When deep snows are prevalent these little feathered friends leave their otherwise natural winter abode among the pines, cedars and spruces of our rural sections. The most natural thing for them to do when in eager search for food is to seek the location of a few barns and homes hoping to find crumbs, grain and suet placed there for them. 

The blue jay is a sagacious bird and fully realizes

that during these hard winters he must get closer to the homes in order to find some weathered sunflowers with their high stalks supporting the drooping heads well above the snow. Such a feast and they enjoy it no less than do the goldfinches that are sure to come near us at this time of the year. 

Then again a robin, bluebird, song-sparrow, purple finch or even the rusty blackbird may now and then be found to have remained for the winter and he must be fed if he is to survive the cold and blizzards. The window feeding sill now quite popular in the villages and cities serves this purpose very well. 

The writer knows of one noble woman who has caused to be built just outside her study window quite an elaborate window feeding shelf. This is so constructed as to be well sheltered from the storms. Here the blue jays and white-breasted nut-hatches come and feed regularly. She informs me that both she and her husband take great pleasure in watching these birds. 

The chickadees also visit this shelf and they go on with their feeding while the folks within, not over three or four feet away observe them without upsetting the little birds in the least.

What a pleasure it is to watch the white-breasted nut-hatch and the chickadees as they come and feed later flying to the slender limb of a nearby tree and there go through their acrobatic stunts performing for the spectators, thus showing gratitude for the food they have received! Such practical results as this just cited has brought the winter feeding station into popularity.

Any household with children will soon develop a high pitch of enthusiasm for the winter feeding station. Possibly a bird that in summer is rather wary

is found in winter to become very tame. For example the little black cap chickadees will become so accustomed to people that he will very readily feed from the hand and even enter the open window and alight on the dining-room table for a morsel. 

The English ring-necked pheasant, now becoming very plentiful throughout New England, will appear at the back door soon after sunrise. Here in large flocks they seek the table crumbs and other food, one might throw out for them. Not long ago a neighbor of mine told me that during a heavy snow-storm in the early evening one of these pheasants flew against his dining room window. He tells me that he feeds these birds regularly and they come all about his house which is located in the heart of a residential section. 

The winter feeding of the birds that come down from further North and remain with us throughout the winter season should be encouraged in every town and city. Such writers as Ernest Harold Baynes in, 

"Wild Bird Guests and How to Entertain Them," has clearly shown the value of feeding our winter birds. 

Mr. Baynes has so vividly shown the economic value of these birds to the farmer and city gardener and orchard keeper.

Every one who takes the pains to erect a good, well sheltered feeding station for the winter birds will soon discover the large interest paid on the investment by the economic good done his garden, field, orchard and lawn. The weeds and destructive moths and caterpillars will be fewer the following open season.

Get the children of the household to help you in building a feeding station. Observe the interest they take in watching the birds and keeping a supply of food in the station for their little feathered friends.

Soon the children learn much about the winter birds they could never know in any other way. It is an uplift movement for our children as it keeps their little minds well occupied and is an incentive for them to remain out in the open. Thus they grow into better and stronger men and women. All bird lovers should encourage this feeding of our winter birds. No better or more gratifying recreative pastime is to be found when once we have engaged in it.

CERT CHAPTER XLIV BENEFICIAL RECREATION 

During the active migration of birds this season the writer has had the pleasurable companionship of Mr. 

Lewis Dexter of Manchester and Mr. Maurice Blaisdell of Goffstown in early morning and evening observation study.

All State Audubon members know the ornithological ability of Mr. Dexter, but if you are not familiar with his personal fellowship, you have missed a valuable asset.

He is a keen observer with an acute ear. 

If he cannot see the bird, all he needs is the song. How often he has exclaimed: "Here, doctor, peep through that bush," and guided by his finger, my glasses met a sight worthwhile. Again, "Doctor, did you get the song of that Peabody bird or that Tennessee warbler ?" 

It may be safely stated that no observer knows the birds of this section and their haunts better than Maurice Blaisdell. He has recently written the ornithological chapter for the history of Goffstown now about to be released from the press. If any one interested in birds fails to read this part of Goffstown's history, he will miss a treat. 

The writer also feels that any bird lovers passing through Goffstown by auto will be well repaid if they 231

stop and make the acquaintance of Mr. Blaisdell, a true naturalist and keen observer of birds since boyhood. He never tells doubtful stories, but you are sure to see or hear something new and interesting. 

One evening, not long ago, Mr. Dexter, Mr. Blaisdell, my oldest son and I went out in the lower environs of the Uncanoonucs. It was about dusk and we had climbed the road between the north and south mountain. Hearing a veery in the distance we stopped the car and the concert began. Not one but several veerys were in their glory. The lunch box was opened and our digestion improved by the song and our appetites both animal and ornithological fully satisfied. 

A little later in the distance, a scarlet tanager began his song. We left the car and followed his trail and finally located him perched on the top of an old dead pine. How he chanted and filled the air with his tunes! Glory was his but appreciation of his song was ours.

Returning to the car we slowly coasted back down between the mountains and had not gone far when the distant song of the wood-thrush was heard by Mr.

Blaisdell. Again we halted at the edge of a thick tall growth and for ten minutes listened to the varied songs of four of these masters of vocal sound in tunes unrivaled. The lisp, the chat and the semi-circular variation of notes was everywhere about us. Three or four of these fellows had gathered presumably to show off or try and outdo one another. The writer has never heard a musical program more scientifically executed. 

Presently the hermit-thrush put in his flute tunes and thus added to our enjoyment. What a song he did bring forth! John Burroughs' description was fully

endorsed and with regrets we left, while the concert yet continued.

Previously to visiting the mountain district we had motored to the farm of Mr. Blaisdell's boyhood days, occupied and carried on by his venerable father, a man nearly eighty years of age. Here we saw the cliff-swallow in a well established colony underneath the barn. 

Sliding doors on each side closed in this ell. We opened the doors and in passing through found two nests of cliff-swallows built on some old sunflowers hung from the beams, a most unique form of architecture.

Passing through the barn yard to the barn cellar there we were greeted by numbers of cliff-swallows peeping out from the small entrances to their nests well within reach of our hands. They seemed not disturbed and as we moved about now and then one would fly out and pass within a foot or two of our heads.

We counted ten nests here making twelve in all with those in the ell. The nests in the ell were higher up, but those in the barn cellar could be easily reached. 

As master of nest architecture these birds were quite at home.

It is stated on good authority that this type of swallow has visited this vicinity for many years. At one farm near at hand two hundred nests have been counted in one colony. Mr. Blaisdell has himself counted seventy-eight nests at the same house. Here they built outside under the eaves. For the past three years, this colony has not been visited. Why the birds did not return after so many years of rearing is not known. 

The colony we saw is rather small but most interesting. 

Another interesting sight was near the northeast corner of the barn, just on the edge of the eaves,

where some time previously a rat had gnawed a hole. 

Just why he selected this place to do this cannot be stated. However that may be, a tree swallow had taken advantage of a good opportunity and built her nest there. She was seen perched on a projecting piece of wood just outside the entrance. We all hoped she would be successful in raising her brood and return another year. Barn-swallows also were plentiful and had a well established colony in the barn. 

We then motored to a point about two miles distant and on the direct road to the mountain haunt found a well organized but rather small colony of bank-swallows. The sand bank was within twenty-five feet of the highway and not larger than a moderate sized shed.

Here the sand bank architect was enjoying the environment and a promising, well protected season was theirs. 

So the swallow family was carefully observed and all within a very small radius, and these are but a very few of the interesting things Maurice Blaisdell knows about. Our day of business rush was perfectly closed with this recreative hour of observation with Maurice and there is not better relaxation after the rush of business.

We motored back to Goffstown and left Mr. Blaisdell at his home. On our way into town, Mr. Dexter and myself declared we were better men for the visit. 

Business would be more easily dealt with the following day for this was real recreation.

CHAPTER XLV SWALLOWS AND WARBLERS 

It was a beautiful day at high noon and I chanced to be quite a distance out in the country at an old New England homestead. This fine old set of buildings was situated in the lowlands surrounded by small hills crested with pine growth.

Not far in the rear of the barn was a clear water stream the banks of which were lined with alders reaching back some distance. I sauntered back into this growth and sat down on an old log. This special spot I had been attracted to because from mid-air came the prattle and chatter of scores of swallows flying hither and thither, diving, seemingly at times perching momentarily in mid air.

Literally a game of tag was in progress with the participants ever vigorously on the move. The marked forked tail and semi-irridescent, rufus breasts revealed the barn-swallow. Others had the pure white breast and less forked, almost square tail, that beautiful irridescent plumage so strongly revealed in the bright sunlight, our valuable little friend the tree-swallow. 

What a good time they were having right over my head and fully unaware of my presence as I was hidden in among the budding growth of alders. For the next quarter hour I was entertained to the greatest game of 235

tag and display of circus tricks including dexterous diving and mid air somersaulting that I have ever witnessed. The air was filled with these birds scolding each other, individually, now and then suddenly ceasing the play and angulating to grasp a moth or insect I could not see but their sharp eyes had noted and no less trap-like mandible would grasp. This was a sight to renew and reinvigorate any person who might feel that life was not worth living.

While enjoying this real outdoor life to the fullest degree a faint lisp came to my ear from the left and turning my head there in a small bush not six feet away was perched that little fellow with the black throat, white breast and golden area wings, the golden-winged warbler. 

How welcome he was, the first of his tribe I had seen this season. Presently several of his mates came and flitted about.

Hardly was this surprise over when a half dozen other active little fellows came along and settled down among the alders. These were of a different kind however as I noticed the yellow caps, yellow rump and white striped tail feathers with the drab, black and white markings of the body and wings. The myrtle warbler had returned and in five minutes great numbers came accompanied by that similarly marked cousin the magnolia warbler.

It certainly was a welcome sight and I hardly breathed for the next fifteen or twenty minutes lest I scatter this little warbler congregation. They would rest on a limb and presently, as if imitating the flycatchers, one and then another would fly off in space, grasp a moth and alight again to devour this dainty morsel.

The sun was warm, very little breeze astir and my momentary reaction was interrupted only by professional duty demanding my return to the old farmhouse. 

Two hours later, before leaving for home, I returned to this spot and although the sun was yet high and its rays warm and invigorating the real animated art, present when I was called away, was now gone. Not a bird in sight, not a note in the air. Such is bird lore.

A migrating flock of swallows and warblers pausing only for lunch time and then again taking up the call had moved further off seeking their summer camping ground unknown to me but surely their objective possibly only a few miles away and yet most likely a night ride on the rails.
