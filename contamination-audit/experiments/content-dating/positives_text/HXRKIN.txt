We were seated in Lindy's Broadway Restaurant, shooting the breeze and talking shop one midnight. Included in our little group were Willie Hoppe, perennial billiard champion; Sam Levy, my former booking manager; a couple of friends, and yours truly, Andrew Ponzi.

Hoppe and I were relating incidents that had befallen us when we toured America as a playing team. 

I had just recited the story of how some speculatively inclined Chicago gentlemen had gone home in a barrel after backing Allen Hall, Chicago three-cushions wizard, against me in a series of games. 

They probably reasoned: "Oh, this guy Ponzi is just a pool player. What chance has he got against Hall in a three-cushions billard match?”

Little did they know or reckon that under Hoppe's tutelage I had become an expert at the angle game and had often given Willie some hot battles during our exhibition tour. They were soon to learn this fact, to their sorrow and to the eventual depletion of their respective bankrolls.

When I had finished my tale, one of the group laughingly observed: "Gee, Ponzi, you ought to write a book.”

This chance remark was the awakening of an idea that had lain dormant in my mind for a long time. I turned to Levy and said: "Don't you think it would be a good idea to tell the fans of the many experiences I've encountered during my twenty-five years as a professional billiard player? If you will collaborate with me, I'll give all the technical details while you furnish the incidental music."

Levy studied a moment while he blew a cloud of smoke into the air. He is a tall, gaunt figure who spent many years on the vaudeville stage before embarking upon a career as booking manager for many famous bowling and billiard celebrities.

During his years on the stage, a newspaper writer once described him as "a long drink of water" which may be an adequate description of his lean, angular frame. "Andy," he said, "the knowledge and experience you have gained in your career should prove of inestimable aid to any fan seeking to improve his or her game. The fans also would like to know the romance, the thrills, the agony of failure, that go into the making of a champion. There are thousands of new and old devotees of the game who would be interested in learning just how you got started in the fascinating game of billiards, and

what difficulties you had to overcome before you reached the top." 

I nodded in approval. It sounded like a very good suggestion, and I was about to tell Sam that I agreed; but on second thought I held my tongue and decided to hear him through. 

You see, Levy is a good talker and I am a good listener.

This is probably the secret of our ideal companionship. "Here's another thing," said Levy, "I've read a few books on the subject, and I've noticed that most all billiard stories start the player's career while he is at the zenith of his fame. They give one the impression that, like Topsy, they 'jest grew up.' In all too many cases they neglect to tell how and where the player learned to handle a cue. I am sure the public would be interested in learning all these facts, and if you combine them with your technical knowledge of the game, such a story would serve a useful purpose and make for interesting reading." 

This suggestion sounded plausible, because these very same questions had been put to me in the various billiard halls, clubs, veteran hospitals and military camps where

I have given exhibitions and instructions for the past six years under the sponsorship of the Billiard Association of 

America, governing body of billiards.

I chalked my first cue in a little neighborhood billiard parlor located in South Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. 

There is nothing startling or unusual in this fact; Hoppe, Schaefer, Caras and Mosconi all started in the same way. 

Where else was I to learn the game? My family were not endowed with the worldly goods that would have permitted me a table in a game room. How many youngsters can afford to join a club, where they may play the game in an atmosphere of refinement and luxury? 

So I went for my recreation and pastime to the little corner billiard parlor, which also served as a headquarters for the other lads who lived in my vicinity. 

This was our clubhouse - our meeting place where we could spend an hour or two in a friendly game, or sing the praises of our individual baseball heroes.

To our way of thinking, it was just as dignified and harmless as hanging out in the corner drug store. We were just a bunch of average boys who grew up into citizens. If one wishes to compare the conduct of presentday teen-agers with that of the boys who frequented billiard halls twenty-five years ago, please remember

A few generous friends and relatives financed mother in a little greens and grocery business of her own. With indomitable courage she set about to mend the family fortunes and to provide a home for herself and her children.

Although surrounded by kind Sisters at St. John's, my childhood was marked by a great loneliness. I missed my dear mother terribly, and I longed for the playful companionship of brothers Nick and Felix and sister Theresa.

On the days that mother found time to leave her little business and visit me I was the happiest boy in the world, and her promise that we would soon be together in our own little home filled me with a great gladness. 

One day she came and, taking me in her arms, said: "Andrew, the good Sisters tell me that you have something wrong with your eyes. The doctors are going to cure you so that you will see everything clear and bright.

I was a little too young to gather the full import of her words, but it seems that Sister Frances Joseph had noticed cataracts forming, and physicians had ordered an operation lest I go blind.

It was early fall in 1925. With the passing years I had won Mr. Romeo's house tournament, besides many other local tourneys produced at the large downtown rooms. 

One of the largest recreation centers was the Hudson, on Broad Street. It boasted 90 bowling alleys and 80 pocket billiard tables. 

I played there frequently. If some aspiring pocket player disputed my right to be called the best player in the establishment, I would accept his challenge to play for money, marbles or cue chalk.

Big sums of money hinged on the outcome of these games, as my friends and backers felt that I was invincible and were prepared to back me to the limit. 

Many times it was a case of win or go hungry, and I can assure you I missed many meals; but as a general rule I more than held my own with the best of them. 

I was improving all the time and was making frequent runs of 90 and 100 on a 5 x 10 table.

Some of the sharpshooters, with visions of a financial clean-up in mind, brought professional players from other cities to play against me. 16

MR. PONZI... 

On one occasion they brought an opponent, then unknown to Philadelphians but who, it was later learned, had played in a national tournament. He was to meet me in a series of matches. Those "in the know" wagered big sums against me; but I was in rare form, and won game after game and purse after purse.

If you recall, it was about this time that Charles Ponzi, Boston financial wizard, was accused of mulcting the public out of thousands of dollars when his 100 per cent scheme collapsed. So, when one spectator turned to another and asked: "Who is this kid that is winning all the money?" the other answered, "I don't know his name; but from the way he keeps beating these guys out of their money, I think he's another Ponzi."

Some of the reporters got hold of the story and it ran in the local newspapers. From that time on everybody started to call me "Ponzi," so I adopted it as my "nom de billiards."

Soon afterwards, I annexed the Pennsylvania state championship and was in much demand by local billiard room operators to put on exhibitions. These usually netted me from ten to twenty-five dollars, depending upon the size and prosperity of the establishment.

Like a siren's voice, the road beckoned me for the next few years, interrupted only by the tournaments and challenge matches which are held every few months during the winter season.

Chicago was the home of the 1928 tournament, in which I finished fifth to Ralph Greenleaf in a closely bunched field. My chances to finish in the first three went a-glimmering one night when the cue ball unfortunately caromed off the bunched triangle and scratched into a side pocket. It was a perfect break shot, but my luck ran out at a most inopportune moment.

When the tourney was over, I was signed as Ralph 

Greenleaf's playing partner for a three months' tour. I realized that Ralph was one of the greatest players of all time, and there was much that I could learn from his style and execution. For this very same reason I accepted an offer to become the playing partner of "Willie" Hoppe and make a road tour to the west coast.

In my opinion, the fault lies in the fact that the player started off on the wrong foot without learning the fundamentals of the game.

Here are a few simple suggestions which, if followed, are bound to improve your skill:

We shall take it for granted that you know how to form a firm bridge so that the cue has no chance to wobble. 

The tip of your cue should not be more than four or five inches away from the cue ball.

Your position at the table must be easy and relaxed, with weight comfortably placed on both feet. Any time you find yourself shooting from an awkward position, it is ten to one you are doing the wrong thing.

Grip the butt of the cue as near the balance as possible. 

I wish to stress this point; it is very important. It may sound fantastic but I honestly believe that fifty per cent of all players hold the cue incorrectly. I grip my cue about twelve inches from the butt-end. My contact with the thumb and forefinger is light, but firm. This assures a live ball with plenty of action. 

Don't put too much English on the ball. A trifle to left or right of center will suffice.

Hold the cue level and follow through on every stroke. 

Do not pocket a ball in a haphazard manner. Always figure ahead where your cue ball will stop, so that you will be in position to make another ball. 85

McGirr was emphatic in declaring that the player of today is far superior to the champions of yesterday. The last week of February, 1948, found me back in

Although I had played nothing but the pocket billiard game during my exhibition tour, I felt that my chances were good to annex either phase of the tournament. 

I am afraid this feeling was born of over-confidencebecause I soon realized that my three-cushion stroke was not up to par; that it would be best for me to concentrate all my efforts on the national pocket billiards title. In fact, it was a matter of debate whether I could ever defeat such a great player as Ezequiel Navarra, the Argentine champion, who was the eventual winner of the national three-cushions title, with a record of eleven wins and no defeats.

INSTRUCTIONS FOR THE PONZI DIAMOND SYSTEM 

To get accurate results you must use high running 

English on all shots played with the SYSTEM.

The numbers shown on charts are starting point numbers for the cue ball only. Memorize these numbers and the starting points they cover. For the purpose of calculating object ball locations the diamonds retain their regular numbers from 1 to 7, counting away from the first short rail which the cue ball will strike.

This is important to remember as the SYSTEM is based on the principle that the total of the contact points on the two long rails will equal the number of the starting point. For example turn to Diamond (5) System. 

From the starting point (5) you will note by following each angle that the contact point of the lines on the two long rails will total five diamonds. 

Fix this principle in your mind and the method of calculating all angles will be easily mastered.
