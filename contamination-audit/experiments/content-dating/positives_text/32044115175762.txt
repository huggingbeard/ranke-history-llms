THE Introduction HE NATIONAL ACADEMY FOR ADULT JEWISH STUDIES, under the auspices of the Jewish Theological Seminary of America, has been organized for the purpose of spreading Jewish learning among Jewish adults, thereby hoping to establish in this country a popular Adult Jewish Education Movement.

One of the vital tasks involved in carrying out such a large enterprise is the creation of a much-needed text book literature especially designed to meet the specific requirements and interests of adult Jewish students. An important factor to be reckoned with in the preparation of such text materials is the limited amount of time that most adults are able to devote to the pursuit of Jewish learning. 

The experience of those who have dealt with this problem would indicate that, at the present stage of the development of Adult Jewish Education, the most practical and hence the most successful Courses of Study are those which are described as Unit Courses and require the student to attend no more than ten sessions during any given semester. 

The text book must therefore not be too long, it must be concise, clear and unified in theme so that the adult student, in the short time at his disposal, will be able to acquire at least one significant unit of the great treasury of Jewish culture.

The National Academy for Adult Jewish Studies has already published a fairly large number of texts in the following fields of Jewish knowledge: The Jewish Religion, the Prayer Book, Biblical and Post-Biblical Literature, Modern Jewish Literature, Jewish History, Palestine and

Zionism, and Contemporary Jewish Life. It is planned to enlarge the number of text books in each of these areas of our rich cultural possessions. 

The Academy takes great pleasure in publishing this book, "The Ideals of the Jewish Prayer Book," by 

Dr. Simon Greenberg. We Jews may indeed be grateful for the heritage of our Prayer Book. It has been our solace in times of sorrow and our song in days of gladness. 

Jewish Prayer is spoken of by our Rabbis as the "service of the heart." Surely, during these trying days the heart of the Jew needs to be strengthened for the trials it has to endure and for the service of the Jewish faith. Dr. Greenberg succeeds in achieving this goal. He has selected one of the main parts of the Prayer Book, the Shema and its Benedictions, as the basis for his study. He writes as one who loves the Prayers of Israel, who sees in them the noblest expressions of the religious ideals of the Jewish people and of the highest strivings of the individual Jew, and as one who has found inspiration in them. This work is, therefore, not historical in character, although sufficient historical material is included to illumine the content of the prayers. The major emphasis in the book is devotional and inspirational. The author aims to win friends for the

Jewish way of life and to influence Jewish thought and Jewish behavior patterns. He seeks to make the ancient 

Shema relevant and functional in the lives of modern Jews. It is hoped that this book will help to inform the 

Jewish mind, to strengthen the Jewish heart and to discipline the Jewish will for the challenging tasks of today and tomorrow.

ISRAEL M. GOLDMAN, Director, National Academy For Adult Jeewish Studies.

HE LAST DECADE HAS WITNESSED A MARKED CHANGE 

in the attitude of many laymen and educators towards the traditional Jewish Prayer Book. It is becoming more generally recognized that in our personal lives and in our educational activities we have not made the best use of our Prayer Book. We permitted it to become a book in which children and many adults merely practised the mechanics of Hebrew reading, or whereby they participated in a meaningless ritual. The wealth of ethical and historic material imbedded in its pages remained largely unexploited. 

This commentary on a portion of the Siddur is an attempt to direct our attention to the national ideals, the ethical doctrines, the religious principles, and the universal aspirations which form the heart and substance of our prayers. It is our hope sometime within the reasonably near future to study the rest of the Prayer Book in the same manner. 

I am grateful to Dr. Mordecai M. Kaplan for having read and carefully commented on the first half of this volume. His suggestions and criticisms were very helpful in revising many a passage. I feel particularly indebted to Dr. Max Kadushin for his interest in this work. Many of his written and oral comments have been incorporated into this text. The style of the book was determined largely by the

CHAPTER I Introduction THE BOOK THE JEWS USE MOST AND KNOW BEST WHEN WE SPEAK OF THE JEWS AS "THE PEOPLE of the Book," "The Book" we refer to, is the Bible (Tanach). 

The saying reflects the attachment of the Jews to the Tanach and the enormous influence which the teachings and the laws of the Tanach have had upon the Jewish people. The extent of that influence cannot be exaggerated. Nor do the Jewish people possess any other book or collection of books equal to the Tanach in significance. 

Nevertheless they have produced a book which they use more frequently and know better. That book is the Siddur, in whose pages the teachings and the ideals of the Tanach are incorporated.

The Siddur is "The Book" of the Synagogue, and The Synagogue is "The Institution" of the Jewish people. 

Three times daily the pious Jew uses his Siddur to recite his prayers. In its pages he finds the teachings of his religion, and the hopes and aspirations of his people. 

Through it his faith in God is strengthened, deepened, and purified. To it he turns in time of joy to give expression to his gratitude, and in time of sorrow to find comfort and consolation.

A Jew, therefore, must know the Siddur if he wants to be intelligent as a Jew, to feel at home in the Synagogue, to know his religion, to appreciate the spirit of his forefathers, and to feel at one with his fellow Jews throughout the world.

THE HISTORY OF THE SIDDUR 

The Siddur as we have it today has a long history behind it. It was not written by one man or by a group of men at any one time. It grew gradually. Its contents were not and are not absolutely fixed and unchangeable. 

The contents of the Siddur used in one Jewish community were not always exactly like the contents of the Siddur used at the same time in some other Jewish community. 

And even when the contents were exactly the same, their arrangements often differed. But all these differences were rarely of a very serious nature. They might be compared to the differences in the manner in which English is spoken in the South, the East, in England, or in other parts of the globe. Though no group will give up its own way of using the English language, all can understand one another, and take pride in the use of a common language. Thus all 

Jews were at home in the Siddur of any Synagogue, though they might prefer their own particular Siddur.

There were and are Jews who composed Siddurim which differed very greatly both in contents and in arrangement, from the other Siddurim. These Jews formed separate sects, or parties. Their influence was either very short lived, or very limited in scope. 

Interesting studies have been made of the history of each passage in the Siddur, who its author was and when it became part of the Synagogue Services. Detailed accounts have been written about the differences for example between the Siddur used by the Jews of Spain, and that used by the Jews of Eastern Europe. All these matters are of importance. But our study will touch upon them only indirectly. We will be much more concerned with the contents of any given passage in the Siddur than with its history. Our interest will be centered, not upon

THE SIDDUR IN ORDER TO BE KNOWN, MUST BE USED 

The Siddur cannot be known if it is approached like a book of history or science, which is read or studied once and then put aside. The Siddur can be known truly and profoundly only by those who use it regularly over a long period of time. It is not meant for any one period of life, for youth, or middle age, or old age. It is to be one's companion throughout the whole of life, and to be used daily if one really wants to know it and derive the most benefit from its companionship.

One who can not read, or who has never learned to distinguish between better and poorer literature, or who has no ideas of his own, does not derive the greatest benefit out of any book. The same is true of our Siddur. To get the greatest good out of using it, one must have the following minimum preparation : 

1. The ability to read Hebrew with sufficient ease and accuracy to follow a Synagogue service. 

2. An understanding of what prayer is, and what it is intended to bring into our lives. 

3. A knowledge of the contents of the Siddur. 

4. A knowledge of the structure of the Siddur, the order of the Service, and the basic facts regarding the history of the prayers.

The foregoing represents a minimum preparation if we want to derive the most good from the Siddur. There were, it is true, millions of Jews who knew only how to read Hebrew, and knew the order of the service, but had little or no knowledge of the contents of the prayers, and had little or no understanding of the words they were saying. Yet they too derived a great deal of good from reciting their prayers, though they did not get the maximum of good out of it. The queston how the reading of the prayers without a thorough knowledge of the contents would be of benefit to them, will be taken up towards the end of this study, together with a number of other general questions regarding prayer,—such as (a) Why should we pray in Hebrew? (b) What good comes from prayer? (c) Why do we have fixed prayers?

THE BARCHU-Page 37 

The Barchu is the call to the congregation to prayer. 

This call to prayer has come down to us from the days when the Temple in Jerusalem was the center of our religious life. As soon as the rays of the rising sun would appear above the horizon, a priest would call out- "Barchu!" "Bless ye the Lord who is to be blessed,” and those gathered in the courtyard would respond, "Blessed is the Lord who is to be blessed for ever and ever." After the Temple was destroyed we continued to use the same manner of calling upon the congregation to pray. 

At the present time the Shema section is the third section of the Shacharis. In olden times it was the beginning of the Shacharis service. The Barchu is not recited unless a minyan is present for the simple reason that otherwise there is no congregation upon whom to call for prayer. 

The position of the Barchu before the Shema continues to emphasize the supreme importance of this part of the Shacharis.

A. The first prayer of the Shema section is devoted to meditation on God's love of all of mankind as evidenced by His creation of the light of the sun. We have just referred to the fact that in Temple days services started with the rising of the sun. The Talmud records that even after

the destruction of the Temple the more pious Jews would await the sunrise and recite the Bracha for the light of the sun, as soon as it appeared above the horizon. This beautiful custom had a double significance. In the first place the religious individual wants to begin the day with some act of piety, some deed by which he could show that he is conscious of God's loving kindness, and that in return, he loves God. In the second place the religious individual does not want to enjoy anything in this world without expressing his thankfulness for it. The light and heat of the sun are, of course, among men's greatest blessings. Without them no life on this globe would be possible. How therefore can we start our day more appropriately than by expressing our gratitude for the divinely created powers of the sun which make our physical existence possible. 

WHY THANK GOD FOR OUR BLESSINGS? 

One of the most beautiful and most important characteristics of the religious person is that he does not take for granted the good things, the blessings, which come into his life. There are some people who do not appreciate good health until they get sick. They realize how good it is to be able to see, or hear or walk, or talk only after they have lost the power to do any one of those things. The same is true about the attitude of some people towards their parents, families or friends. They begin to appreciate them only after they are gone. The religious person does not have to be deprived of a blessing in order to learn to appreciate it. He tries to be as happy with a blessing while he has it, as he would be sad if he were deprived of it. 

Hence when he gets up in the morning and sees the light of day, he does not take it as a matter of course. He does not feel that it must be, and that it always will be thus. 

He is aware that being alive and having the ability to see

the light of the sun, are in themselves great blessings. He pauses therefore to give proper expression to his gratitude. 

By not taking his blessings for granted, the religious person also makes his own life fuller and better. Let us see why this is so.

We live as human beings only when we are conscious. 

A human being can remain alive even after he loses consciousness, but no human being would consider life worth living if he were to lose his consciousness, without hope of ever regaining it. But even though our physical existence does not completely depend upon our consciousness, our joy and happiness as human beings are completely dependent upon it. Moreover, a thing can benefit our bodies whether we are conscious of it or not. If for example, while a child is asleep a storm arises and the father closes the window in the room, the child benefits by this act, whether he ever knows about it or not. But this act also revealed the father's love for the child. Now every child likes to be loved by his parent, and unless he knows and appreciates what the parent does for him, the child misses the joy which comes from feeling that his parent loves him. And to the extent that we are not conscious of the blessings we possess, to that extent our life is poorer and emptier and less happy. 

Unfortunately, many of us go through life benefiting by innumerable blessings but never enjoying them because we take them for granted. All children benefit by the love and kindnesses of their parents, yet how many children find real joy in these blessings? We all benefit by our powers to hear and to see-but how often do we take the time to become conscious of these great blessings so that we may derive joy not merely from what we hear or see, but simply from the fact that we can hear or see. as we do in a great democracy we benefit by the inestimably precious liberties which our constitution bestows upon us. Yet how many American citizens take the time daily to think of these precious liberties and thus to experience the joy which comes with consciousness of their possession?

Innumerable other examples of the same truth can easily be mustered. But these few should be sufficient to illustrate the truth that every time we benefit by a blessing without being conscious of it, we are missing an opportunity to experience real and profound human happiness. We thus reduce to a minimum the value the blessing may have for us.

In addition, there are many glorious things in the world by which we do not benefit at all unless we become conscious of them. A beautiful sunset, inspiring music, great poetry, sweet and pleasant odors, all contribute nothing to our welfare or happiness unless we become conscious of them. How poor that human life is, which goes through this world without becoming conscious of these blessings. 

Our religion therefore tries among other things to guard us against that kind of spiritual poverty. It seeks to train us to be conscious of the multitude of good and beautiful and beneficial things constantly surrounding us. 

That is why a religious person makes a bracha before eating his food, or when he sees something beautiful, or even when he smells a pleasant fragrance.

That also explains why a religious person most often is, and always should be a happier and a more optimistic person than others. The religious person, instead of concentrating his thoughts on what he misses in life, turns his mind to the many good things that he has in life. The blessings of life are always so numerous that he can always

find something to be grateful for, no matter how many difficulties may at the moment be facing him.

There are some people who think that this kind of an attitude discourages progress. They argue that if a person always keeps in mind the good things he has and never realizes the importance of the things he is missing, he never wants to do anything to improve his condition. That is not true. Our religion has never encouraged an attitude of self-satisfaction. In the prayers of request contained in our Siddur our minds are turned to those aspects of our lives which require improvement. In these prayers we ask for those blessings which we do not now enjoy. But it is very natural for a person to want what he does not have, and what he sees that others enjoy. All that our religion does is to try to help each one of us balance his point of view so that he should not become embittered and disappointed, and that his heart should not be eaten up by hate and by jealousy, when he see some one who is in any way more blessed than he. Religion speaks to every one of us every moment of the day and says, "Remember, you too, have much for which to be thankful." Thanking

God is, therefore, merely one of the things the religious man does every morning so as to make himself conscious of the blessings he enjoys and to teach himself to be humble before God and to be grateful to Him for these blessings.

The word Torah is used in various ways. Sometimes it refers only to the Chumash, the Five Books of Moses.

THE LORD IS ONE 

"Hear O Israel the Lord our God, the Lord is One."

Notice this very extraordinary fact. This passage, the most important one in the Siddur, does not begin with words like "Hear O Lord," but "Hear O Israel." It speaks

not to God but to us. How clearly this demonstrates the truth that when our fathers prayed, they were not always trying to ask something from God, or even to tell Him anything. Prayer meant also telling themselves something, bringing into the center of their consciousness ideas or truths which they wanted always to remember. We cannot recall too often that Tefilah is not always synonomous with "Request" or "Supplication. وو The first paragraph of the Shema commands us to remember that there is but one God in the world. Jews take a justified pride in the fact that they were the people through whom the Christians and the Mohammedans acquired their monotheistic faiths. But very few Jews themselves understand the real significance of believing in one God. Why is it so very important?

In order to understand that, we must know something about the ideas which preceded the modern monotheistic religions. Before Judaism came into the world even the most civilized of the ancient nations such as the Egyptians, the Babylonians, the Greeks, and the Romans had some very strange ideas about God and His character. All kinds of animals were worshipped as gods, such as the bull, and the serpent, and even mice. People believed that each locality had its own deity. Each city had its own god who was supposed to watch over it. Then there was a different god for every different thing in nature. One god ruled the wind, another the rain, a third the growth of the crops, another the ocean, and so on, without end. Among some peoples there was the belief that the royal dynasty governing them was descended from God. Such a belief is still prevalent among the Japanese who look upon their emperor as a descendant of the sun god. Naturally the god who is the ancestor of the emperor is especially interested in protecting the emperor and his empire, and

therefore fights against all the emperor's enemies. The ancients thought that each nation had a different god for the same reason that they believed each phenomenon in nature had its own god. Just as it was hard to imagine, for example, that such two opposing forces as fire and water, light and darkness, good and evil, should have the same source, so was it difficult to imagine that a nation and its enemy had the same god. The prophets and rabbis found it necessary, therefore, to teach constantly the truth that- They may know from the rising of the sun, and from the west, That there is none beside Me. 

I am the Lord, and there is none else. 

I form the light, and create the darkness 

I make peace and create evil; 

I am the Lord that doeth all these things. (Is. 45; 6-7)

It is true that there were some outstanding non-Jewish philosophers and religious leaders who started to question the belief in many gods, and some of those thinkers even rejected that belief completely. There was a tendency to choose one chief god and gradually to make him the only god. Thus we know that one of the early Egyptian kings, Ikhnaton, tried to make RA the sungod, who was the chief deity in the Egyptian pantheon, the only god to be worshipped by the Egyptians. But his efforts failed. It is true also that such great Greek philosophers as Socrates, Plato and Aristotle rejected much of Greek paganism, and developed some ideas of monotheism. Moreover, non- 

Jewish philosophers and thinkers developed most fully the logical or philosophic arguments why there cannot possibly be more than one God in the world. Nevertheless it still remains true that the Jews were the first people to apply the belief in one God to the affairs of men and to show how belief in one God should influence the actions of a

human being towards his own life and towards his fellow man, and the action of one nation towards another. And unless a religious belief makes a difference in our actions and in our thoughts it is not very important from a religious or any other point of view.

Let us then inquire why it is important to believe in one God and how Judaism applied this belief to the affairs of men. 

Why not believe in many gods? The first obvious answer is, of course, that such a belief is not true. Philosophers from Aristotle to our day have developed quite a number of arguments to prove that logically there can not possibly be more than one God. We cannot list all of their arguments here. But it might be of interest to state at least one of them.

One of the qualities we associate with God is omnipotence, that is, that He is all powerful. Now let us assume that there are two gods in the world. Obviously the government of the world would have to be divided between the two of them. That would mean that god "A" would have no power or authority in that part of the world over which god "B" rules, and vice versa. Hence neither god "A," nor god "B" would be omnipotent, or all powerful.

One god would of necessity limit the power of the other god, and hence neither of them would be god in our understanding of the term. Hence since there cannot be two or more all-powerful rulers of the universe, there cannot logically be more than one god.

We might then ask wherein it is objectionable to believe something which is not true? Are there not many people who believe in false things such as ghosts and luck numbers, who are otherwise apparently very good people? 

To be sure that is so. Believing in a false idea does not necessarily make people wicked, but it can and often does

lead to unpleasant or to definitely harmful results. People who believe in ghosts, in lucky and unlucky numbers, and in similar superstitions, suffer from unnecessary fears. They often decide very important matters by the utterly foolish practice of depending upon a number turned up by the roll of dice, or some similar completely illogical manner. 

Thus also, the people who believed that the world was square, and flat, might have been very kind and generous, but they would never have undertaken to sail west in order to reach India, as Columbus did. Columbus ventured forth into the unknown because he believed that the world was round.

But as we mentioned above, having the correct idea is not always enough. Not all people draw the same conclusions from it. There were other people who believed that the world was round, but it did not occur to them to sail west in order to reach India. There were many individuals who knew that sound was caused by wave vibrations but it never occurred to them as it did to Edison to try to make a phonograph. Let us now briefly examine only a few of the evils that resulted from the belief in the false doctrine that there are many gods.

We can see at once that people who believe that fire, water, wind and every other phenomenon of nature each has a separate god controlling it, can never develop any real understanding of the world and therefore can never have any real science or philosophy. Why? For many reasons, only one of which we shall discuss as clearly and as briefly as we possibly can. 

Why did the primitive peoples attribute a different god to every different phenomenon? Why was Neptune the god of the ocean, and Zeus the god of thunder? They believed that because they could see no possible connection between the ocean and thunder. Each one of these two

things seemed absolutely independent one from the other. 

The power who controlled the thunder, could not to their way of thinking, control the ocean, nor could the power who governed the ocean control the thunder. Therefore each one had to have a different god who had created it or was now controlling it. As soon as the ancients discovered a relationship between two separate and different things they no longer required two separate gods for them. The number of gods, or divine spirits in which the ancients would believe, therefore depended upon their ability to discover the extent to which all the various and often apparently conflicting phenomena in the world really were interrelated and inter-dependent. 

Now science and philosophy try always to prove that things which to the ignorant person appear completely different, are in reality the same, or very similar. The child can see no relationship between water, ice, and steam. 

An ignorant adult cannot see any relationship between water, oxygen and hydrogen. Anybody who has studied any science knows that the scientist tries to find one Law to cover as many separate and different phenomena as possible. The same is true in philosophy. Plato and 

Aristotle were great scientists and philosophers. They therefore found it necessary to reject the belief in many gods, and reduce the number of gods to a minimum. They felt and recognized that all diverse phenomena in the world have very much in common.

To be sure there were differences of opinion regarding the nature of the common factors. Some of the early philosophers reduced the number of basic elements in the world to four,-fire, water, earth and air. They argued that all things, whether trees or beast, man or rock, were made up only of these four things. Aristotle reduced them to two. He said all things were made up only out of

what he called "Form" and "Substance." Moreover, he argued that since everything in the world moved, and since no motion starts by itself, there must be in the world what he called "An Unmoved Mover," the "First Cause" of all motion.

We cannot here go into a complete discussion of the whole interesting field of Greek philosophy. We refer to it, however, even if briefly and inadequately, to indicate that Greek philosophers and scientists also started to break down man's faith in many gods. They had no way of proving that there were only four elements or two elements in the world. But it was only when men started with the assumption that the million different organic and inorganic objects in the world really were made up of the same substance and had very much in common, that they were able to develop real science and philosophy. We might, therefore, say that the Greeks developed philosophic and scientific monotheism. But that is as far as they went. 

The Jews also believed that all things in the world were inter-related and had only one creator. But they did not develop the idea into elaborate philosophic and scientific systems. They developed it into the world's greatest and most influential ethical system.

Remember that we said that the ancients found it hard to believe that the same god who created fire, also created water because fire and water were always destroying one another, and one god would not create one thing, and then create a second thing which would destroy the first. In the same way, the ancients could not believe that the same god who created them and watched over them, also created the enemies who were trying to destroy them. Every nation, therefore, had to have its own god who was interested only in his own nation and desired to make it supreme over all other nations. Each nation brought

sacrifices to its own god in order to please him. He was the nation's partner who was supposed to help them in carrying out all their plans regardless of whether they were good or evil. When a nation lost a battle or suffered drought they thought that their god was angry at them for not bringing him sufficient sacrifices. Hence they would often sacrifice even their own children in order to appease their gods. But if they continued to lose battle after battle until they would finally be completely conquered by the enemy, they would give up their god altogether. They would argue very logically, "Why bring gifts to a god who does not have the power to defend you? Why not worship a strong god?" They would thus start by abandoning their own religion and end by disappearing as a separate people. That is one of the reasons why all those nations which we read about in the Bible like the Egyptians, the Ammorites, and Moabites, no longer exist. 

Just as the defeated nation felt that its god was beaten and no longer worthy of worship, so the victorious nation felt that its god gave to it special privileges in the world, which other nations and peoples should not enjoy. The victorious people felt that their god gave them the right to kill, plunder, or enslave the defeated people, because their god was interested only in them, in their welfare and their glory, and in no way interested in the welfare of their defeated enemy.

We see at once therefore how the wrong idea that each nation has its own god justified in the eyes of the strong nations all the exil they committed against a weaker people. 

The Jewish people were the first to whom there was revealed the truth that there is only one god in the world, that He is the God of all nations and all peoples, and that

He is interested in the welfare and happiness of all of them. 

Israelites and Moabites may be enemies of one another just as fire and water appear to be. But one God created both of them, and one does not have more privileges in the world than the other. What is it that Israelites and 

Moabites have in common? It is this-they are both children of the same God and He is their father. If that is so, why do Moabites and Israelites fight one another? Why are they enemies? It is because both of them are sinful, both of them disobey the law and the teachings of their 

Heavenly Father.

There were, of course, many Jews in early days, as there are many even in our day, who did not and do not understand what believing in one God really means. There were many Jews who also believed that God was the God of Israel only, and that He too wanted only gifts and sacrifices in return for defending Israel against its enemies. But it is against this wrong and unholy idea about God that our prophets, down through the centuries, warned the Jewish people. The Bible nowhere ever speaks against another nation merely because that other nation was different from the Jews either in race or color. The prophets speak only against those people who acted unjustly, cruelly and inhumanly towards their fellow men. But they spoke against Jews who were unjust or inhuman with the same anger with which they spoke against the cruelties of the 

Babylonians, and Assyrians. On the other hand, they pleaded for the righteous and the kind non-Jew with the same warmth with which they pleaded for the righteous Jew.

Thus Abraham pleaded with God to save the wicked inhabitants of the two cities of Sodom and Gemorrah, for the sake of the righteous in those cities although these people were in no way related to him. (Read the story in

Genesis, Chapter 18, verses 16-33). Moses never tires of reminding the Jewish people to treat everyone justly. "And a stranger shalt thou not wrong, neither shalt thou oppress him." (Exodus 22, vs. 20) "The stranger that sojourneth with you shall be unto you as the home-born among you, and thou salt love him as thyself; for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt. I am the Lord your 

God." (Lev. 19, vs. 34)

The prophet Amos tells the Jews very clearly that in the eyes of God they are no better than any one else. The same God who brought the Jews out of Egypt also brought the Philistines from Caphtor and Aram from Kir. God is interested only in the righteous and is set against the wicked no matter who or where they may be. These are 

Amos' words: "Are ye not as the children of the Ethiopians unto Me, Oh children of Israel? said the Lord.

Have not I brought up Israel out of the land of Egypt, and the Philistines from Caphtor, And Aram from Kir? 

Behold the eyes of the Lord God 

Are upon the sinful kingdom, And I will destroy it from off the face of the earth.” (Amos 9, vs. 7-8) 

Hundreds of other quotations of the same kind could easily be added from the Bible and later Rabbinic literature. But the full meaning of believing in one God was summarized most clearly by the prophet Malachi when he said- "Have we not all one father? 

Hath not one God created us? 

Why do we deal treacherously every man against his brother?" (Mal. II, vs. 10)

That is the real revolutionizing idea which Judaism brought into the world. To the Jews, believing in one 

God did not mean only that He created and governs the oceans, and the thunders, fire and water. It meant that 

He also created all man. Therefore our prayers speak of 

God as "Our Father" and "Our King." The most important bond which unites the children of one family is that they all have one father. The bond that should unite all men regardless of race or color, is our recognition that God is the one Father of all of us. The Brotherhood of 

Man depends upon our faith in the Fatherhood of God. 

Believing in the Fatherhood of God is not at all important unless we understand that it implies the Brotherhood of Man, and act accordingly. This teaching is the foundation upon which all our ethics rests. One who does not believe that all men are brothers, children of one God, has no basis upon which to demand that men should act justly and mercifully one towards the other.

When we look around at the world and see how much injustice there is, how cruel men are to one another, how they slaughter one another in wars, we tend to doubt that men are really brothers. They seem to act rather like beasts of the jungle toward one another. To be sure, we can also point to many things that men do in which they really act like brothers. We have federations of charity, hospitals, orphan asylums, and we now pay taxes to our government to help care for the aged, the sick, the unemployed. We help one another in any number of ways daily. 

We visit the sick, help a friend in need, assist a blind or old person to cross the street, and in many other ways act as brothers towards one another. If there would be more cruelty than kindness in the world, more injustice than justice, civilization would be altogether impossible. But believing that men are brothers is not a matter of logical

conviction. It is primarily a matter of faith-a faith which can be amply supported by logic. 

When the Jewish martyr who died for his religion, died I with the cry of the Shema on his lips, it was as if he were saying, "Even though I am being treated by my fellow

I men as if they were beasts rather than my brothers, even thought they are cruel and unjust to me, I nevertheless I believe that there is only one God and that all men are brothers."

Thus also the dying Jew recites the Shema as his last confession of faith. Death appears to all of us as a cruel 

I thing. No normal person is happy to die, and very few 

I people feel that they should die when they do. When we are face to face with death it seems hard to believe that the power which is taking away our life from us is the same as the kind God who first gave it to us. Death seems to be the work of some evil spirit not of a loving divine 

Father. But the pious Jew, even in the face of death,

I declares that even though God takes his life, he nevertheless continues to believe that the same God who gave him his life, now takes it way, and that He is the one 

God, the Father of all men.

There are many people in the world, hundreds of millions of them, Jews, Christians, and Mohammedans who class themselves or are referred to by others as believers in one God. But there is a great difference between being classed as a believer in one God, and acting in accordance with that belief. Judged by actions, there are very few people who really believe that there is one God. Only those who treat their fellow men like brothers, no matter of what color, race or religion they may be, only those are the true believers in one God.

That will help us understand more clearly why it is that the Nazis who consider themselves better than all other

peoples, hate not only the Jews and Judaism, but also Christianity and the whole of the Bible. That is why they are trying to revive the old pagan religions of the Germans. 

It is only paganism which can justify their barbarities towards all other weaker nations. The Bible and the religions based upon it condemn their cruelties as the most despicable deeds which human beings are capable of performing.

But it is not an easy thing to believe in one God and act accordingly. Therefore Judaism bids us to recite the 

Shema at least twice daily, morning and evening. At the beginning of every day and at the end of it every true Jew should say, "I believe that there is but one God in the world. He is the Father of all. He has no favorites. He wants only righteousness and mercy. I accept this faith and I am going to try to live today and always in such a way as to prove by my acts that I believe God is the 

Father of all, and all men are my brothers.”

All of these ideas are not specifically stated in the one line of the Shema. That is why the Rabbis insisted that this line should be recited with Kavana-with the greatest possible conscious concentration on all of its manifold and significant meanings. It is the custom of the pious Jew, in accordance with Rabbinic teaching, to recite this line slowly word by word, and especially to dwell at length on the last word "O-N-E." The Rabbis ask us that in pronouncing this last word we should meditate on it long enough consciously to think of God as ruler over "North and South, East and West, Above and Below." We should not rush through those words or any of the words of the Shema, without concentrating on the many important meanings which they have.

Obviously if people would believe in one God and act in accordance with that belief, war, the greatest crime

of all, would no longer destroy millions of people in every generations, and such things as slavery, hatred and persecution of Jews, or Negroes or other small or weak people would necessarily cease.

We noted above that the recitation of the Shema is called by our Rabbis the act of "accepting the yoke of the Kingdom of Heaven." The same as the soldier salutes the flag of his country every morning and thereby proclaims his intention to live up to its laws, so we, by saying the Shema, salute, as it were, God, as our King, and express our desire and intention to live up to His teachings, as expressed in the Torah.

Because faith in one God is the foundation upon which all the teachings of our Torah rest, the first paragraph of the Shema is the first prayer that young Jewish children are taught to recite almost as soon as they can speak. 

The Shema was the last words spoken by the thousands of Jews who gave their lives for their religion, and the final confession of faith upon the lips of every dying Jew. From the beginning of our lives until the end, these words should be in our hearts and guide our lives, and make us staunch in our determination to fulfill our duties as "citizens of the 

Kingdom of God."

OUR DUTIES TOWARDS GOD 

1. "And thou shalt love the Lord your God."

This passage seems to command us to love God. We know however that no person can be forced to love or to cease to love anything or anyone. Love is something which rises in our hearts of itself and we can rarely control it. 

Love can not be made a duty. It is a very rare and sacred privilege or blessing. This passage is therefore not to be taken as a commandment in the same sense that "Thov

shalt not steal" is a commandment. It is rather an indication of what our attitude towards God ought to be. 

In the Tefilos preceding the Shema we meditated on God's love for us and for all mankind, as evidenced in the light of the sun, and the glory of the Torah. Meditation on God's love for us and on the innumerable kindnesses with which He surrounds our lives should naturally call forth our love for God. We took occasion in a previous discussion, to point out that a truly religious person is aways conscious of God's love for him, and of the many blessings God constantly brings into his life. This ever present consciousness of God's love for him rouses in him a desire always to reveal his love for God. 

But how does the pious Jew do that? How is one to love God? The Midrash answers that question as follows: "To love God and to cleave to Him means to try to be like Him. Even as He is just, be thou just, even as He is merciful, be thou merciful, even as He is truth, be thou truthful. Hence let no man say I will study Torah in order that I may be honored, that men may call me Rabbi, and that the Lord may reward me. Let him do it rather because of love of God and of God's ways."

The truly religious Jew therefore performs the will of God as revealed in the mitzvos of the Torah, not primarily out of fear of punishment or hope for reward, but because they offer him the best means to prove by action how deep and sincere is his love for God, and his appreciation of all the kindnesses which God's love for him daily brings into his life. We might compare it to a situation somewhat as follows. A son becoming conscious of all the love which his father bears towards him, wants to do something which in turn would reveal all of his love for his father. But he does not know what to do. He spends much thought and time on this problem but makes no headway. Then some-

one comes and tells the son that his father would be made very happy if he, the son, would learn to speak softly and gently. The son gladly undertakes to train himself in that manner of speech. He is grateful to the man who told him what to do, and he loves gentle speech chiefly because it enables him to show his love for his father. Later he may, of course, discover that gentle speech brings him other pleasant rewards such as the respect of his friends, or calmness of spirit. But all of these will forever remain as mere incidental reasons for his continuing to speak softly. 

His primary reason will always be his desire to prove the sincerity of his love for his father. Therefore, if on occasions gentle speech may make him appear weak in the eyes of his enemies, and bring him reverses or disappointments, he will nevertheless continue to speak gently, because he did not start to speak that way for purposes of personal gain. He started it to show his love for his father and as long as he knows that gentle speech continues to please his father, the son will continue to speak gently no matter what other pleasant or unpleasant consequences result from it.

It is in that spirit that a religious Jew observes the mitzvos. Whatever immediately pleasant or unpleasant consequences result from his observance of these mitzvos, are all considered as of secondary importance, or of no importance at all. As long as he feels certain that the observance of these mitzvos continues to prove most impressively his love for God, he will continue to observe them. 

THE LOVE OF GOD AND THE FEAR OF GOD 

This love of God which rises naturally in the heart of any human being as soon as he becomes deeply conscious of God's manifold mercies towards him, is the real seed fom which all great religions, and particularly Judaism, have

grown. Nothing is farther from the truth than the opinion frequently expressed that religion is rooted in fear. Nevertheless, those who feel God's mercies most deeply, are also most conscious of God's justice. If God were only kind and merciful, evil would never be punished. But we know that wickedness does end with calamity and grief for those who practice it. The evil regimes which ruled over 

France before 1789, and over Russia before 1917, were drowned in blood when the wronged nations rose in mighty revolt against their oppressors. The sin of slavery brought the Civil War upon the United States. Our prayers remind us that "On account of our sins we were exiled from the Holyland." Evil doing can not go on indefinitely either in the case of the individual or of the nation without bringing dire distress in its wake. Later on we shall discuss at some length many of the questions that arise in our minds in connection with the manner in which God's justice reveals itself to us. It involves the mighty question of the suffering of the righteous and the prosperity of the wicked. But for the present let it suffice to stress the fact that the religious person believes that God is not only merciful, but also just. In so far as He is just, He punishes evil, and there is nothing wrong or immoral about fearing punishment at the hands of a just God. 

Nevertheless we all recognize that a human being whose actions are based on fear of punishment has not as yet attained as high a level of ethical development as one whose actions are based on love of God. The book of 

Proverbs says that the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom. (Pr. I, 7; IX. 10.) Moses, addressing the people of Israel, says to them, "And now, Israel, what doth the Lord thy God require of thee, but to fear the 

Lord thy God, to walk in His ways, and to love Him." (Dt. 10, 12). These two passages, especially the second

one, indicate very clearly the relationship between the fear and the love of God. Fear is usually the first step in our education. Why? Because fear is associated primarily with refraining from doing a thing which would cause a result painful or distasteful to us. It is, for example, much easier for a child to learn to be afraid of fire than to desire to eat vegetables which are good for its health. A sensible adult does not drink a mixture of milk and vinegar even though it may taste pleasant to him, because he is afraid of what it may do to his stomach. Nor is he ashamed to admit that he is afraid to drink such a mixture. We all recognize that there is nothing cowardly or wrong about being afraid to do something which may bring us momentary glory or physical satisfaction, but will ultimately cause us physical pain or spiritual grief. A person who has no such fear is not brave. He is merely stupid. Nevertheless it is unfortunately true that there are many among us who lack the strength of will to keep away even from the things which they know will prove harmful to them. If therefore a person does not have sense enough even to be "afraid" of going to prison, or injuring his health, or grieving his beloved ones, he can never become a really ethical individual. He has not learned how to take the first step toward human decency.

But a person who merely refrains from doing something positively foolish or evil, is obviously not the highest type of human being. To be able to say of a person that he never murdered or stole, or lied, is, of course, very good. But most of us would feel insulted if we were praised that way. It is not enough to refrain from evil; we must also do good. That, in part, is the difference between fear of God and love of God. God desires that we live good lives. He commands us not to murder, not to steal, not to commit adultery, and not to do similarly evil things

He also commands us to love our neighbors, to be charitable, and merciful. Those who only fear God try to obey only the negative commandments. Those who love Him try to fulfill the positive commandments also. 

But that is not the only difference between love and fear of God. Perhaps an analogy with the relationship between parents and children will help to make our idea clearer. Children who obey their parents may be divided into the following groups: 

1. Children who are afraid of the punishment which the parents will administer for disobedience. 

2. Children who want the reward which the parents will give them for obedience. 

3. Children who love their parents and want to please them. 

4. Children who believe that the parents have greater wisdom and that therefore the things which the parents want them to do are good things and worthy of their effort.

The children in the fourth group finally discover that the things their parents ask them to do are in and by themselves good things, and should be done whether the parents will reward them or not.

Obviously the child who obeys its parents because of the third and fourth reasons is better, a finer child than he who obeys merely for the first two reasons. Although it is better that a child should do the right thing even for the first two reasons, than that he should not do the right thing at all. The analogy with fear and love of God is too clear to require further elucidation. 

The three steps that most of us take in our character development probably correspond to the three different commands in the passage from Deuteronomy. First we fear God; then we "walk in His ways," i.e., we do what

He tells us to do. Finally, as a result of our following "His ways" we discover what we should have known from the very beginning, namely that these ways are good in and by themselves regardless of rewards. 

The injunction to love God, the Midrash says, makes it our duty not only to love Him ourselves, but to act in such manner as to cause others to love Him. The Hebrew word “ve-ahavta” is interpreted by the Midrash in two ways. 

1. "And thou shalt love." 2. "And thou shalt cause others to love." The Midrash then continues as follows: "The pious person must act in such a way as to cause God to be loved by all as a result of his actions. He who studies 

Torah should speak gently with people, deal pleasantly and honestly with everyone so that people who see him should say, 'Happy is this man's father because he taught him Torah, and happy is his teacher. How unfortunate is the one who does not study Torah.' But if one studies 

Torah and then speaks rudely and deals dishonestly, people say, 'Woe to the father and the teacher who taught him Torah. Unhappy is the man who studies Torah, for see how he acts.' ." Thus the first type of man causes God to be loved by his acts; the second type causes men to lose faith in God. 

2. Love God With All Your Heart. 

The Rabbis ask, "Why does the Torah command us to love God with the whole of our hearts? Is it possible to love with a part of the heart?" And they answer, "The heart is the seat of many conflicting desires, some good and some bad." There is, in the heart, "the Yetzer Tov," the good inclination, the tendency towards mercy and justice; and there is in the heart the "Yetzer Rah," the evil inclination, the tendency towards anger, pride, cruelty. 

To serve God with the whole heart means to serve Him

with all the various desires in our hearts. It is easy enough to see how we serve God with our "Yetzer Tov," our good inclinations. How can we serve Him with our "Yetzer Rah,"-our evil inclination?

We serve God with our evil inclination by keeping it in check. A person who has a tendency to get angry but controls his anger, is serving God in a manner in which a person who does not even have a tendency to get angry cannot serve Him. But we cannot always control ourselves and our emotions. Nor is it always ethical and moral for us to suppress them. Anger is surely an evil but there is such a thing as "righteous indignation," and one who can not experience it is not a better person thereby. A very prominent American when asked why he never condemned publicly the Nazi persecution of the Jews answered that on that question he thought it advisable to remain neutral. An individual who can see a bully beating a youngster and remain neutral in his feelings is not to be praised. 

Pride is certainly not a very praiseworthy feeling. But pride can be a highly moral quality. President Wilson once said about the United States that "We were too proud to fight." A person ought to have pride enough in him to keep him from being a coward, or a cheat, or from taking unfair advantage of another individual.

The Rabbis use the sex instinct to illustrate what they mean by serving God even with the evil inclination. Our sex instinct can surely lead us astray into many immoral actions. Judaism does not require us to suppress that instinct completely. That instinct can be directed into very noble paths. He who marries, raises a family, and establishes a home in which peace and companionship reign, he is serving God also through the apparently evil sex instinct.

Thus the Shema reminds us daily of our duty to subdue all the emotions, desires, and impulses of our heart to the service of our Heavenly Father. 

3. Love The Lord With All Your Soul. 

How can we love God with all "our soul"? The word "nefesh" means not only "soul" but "life." This sentence therefore asks us to love God with our very life. But what does that mean? Our great teacher, patriot and hero,

Rabbi Akiba gave us the most courageous and inspiring example in his own action, of what it means to love God with all our soul. When he was being slowly tortured to death by the Romans, his pupils standing around him asked him, "O our teacher do you still believe in God even now when He brings you to so cruel a death after you have lived so religious and patriotic a life?" And 

Rabbi Akiba answered them, "All my life I wondered what the Bible meant when it commanded us to love God with all our soul. Now I know the answer. It means to love Him even though He takes our souls. And now that 

I have the opportunity to fulfill this commandment, shall 

I waver?"

In that way, too, the patriots love their country, parents love their children, artists and scientists love their work, with all their souls. Thus also, religious people love their 

God and His laws of truth, goodness and justice. In a world as full of cruel tyrants, of intolerant, so-called "religious" leaders, of greedy, selfish men who oppress others and take advantage of the physically weak and defenseless, religious people who should above all be lovers of liberty and human decency, must train themselves to be ready to sacrifice everything, to give their very “souls" if necessary, in defense of their faith and in behalf of the poor, the weak and the oppressed.

4. Love The Lord With All Your Might. 

How do we love God with all our might? "Might," the

Rabbis say, refers here to our worldly possessions, our money, and goods. There are many people who are honest, or kind, or just, as long as these require only a minimum of effort or sacrifice on their part. But these high moral qualities often require great effort and sacrifice from us. 

It is not easy to overcome the desire to take advantage of our fellow man in a business deal. There is a great temptation to turn a deaf ear to the appeals of charitable organizations, and of friends in need, or to answer the appeals with as small a gift as possible, so that we do not in any way miss what we give away. There are people, the 

Rabbis correctly remark, who love their wealth more than their lives. They take no rest, subject themselves to all kinds of dangers and hardships, undermining their health in order to amass more wealth. Such individuals cannot love God. 

If we truly love God, we must be prepared to prove it, by readily sacrificing our worldly possessions in order to remain loyal to His teachings of honesty, goodness and mercy. 

5. "And these words which I command you this day shall be upon thy heart."

Those powers of memory and thought which we now know are associated with the human brain, the ancients associated with the heart. Hence the command that the words of the Torah should be upon "our hearts," really means that they should be in our minds. We are to know the Torah so thoroughly that we shall never forget it. This sentence, therefore, commands us to study and to know the Torah. This command is considered by some of our

great Rabbis as the most important of all, for how can one act properly if he did not first learn what proper action is! The empty headed individual cannot fear sin because he does not know what is sinful and what is not. Thus also the ignorant person can not be truly pious for he does not understand the true character of piety. (See Ethics of 

Fathers, Ch. II, Para. 6, p. 187). That is why we often find that those who have not studied the Torah understandingly confuse religion with superstition or magic. 

Because the Rabbis believed that "study leads to action," they taught "These are the things, the fruits of which a man enjoys in this world, while the stock remains for him for the world to come: viz., honoring father and mother, the practice of charity, timely attendance at the house of study morning and evening, hospitality to wayfarers, visiting the sick, dowering the bride, attending the dead to the grave, devotion in prayer, and making peace between man and his fellow, but the study of the Torah is equal to them all."

The Greek philosopher, Socrates, also taught that "knowledge is virtue," that is, if you have proper and full and true knowledge, you are bound to act virtuously. Unfortunately this is not always true. We all have seen individuals who know what is right but do not act in accordance with their knowledge. But it is surely true that proper knowledge encourages proper action. If you have an idea in your mind about mercy or justice, holiness or truth, that idea is always somehow speaking to you and saying, “Act in accordance with me. Do as I ask you to do." And if we do not listen to the "voice" of that idea and instead act unjustly, or cruelly, the idea continues to disturb us, to rebuke us constantly as if to say, "You might have been just, but you were unjust. You might have been kind, but you were cruel!" But if we do not even have

the proper ideas, we sin and do not know we sinned, so that there is no hope of ever improving our ways. 

Upon whom does the duty to study the Torah rest?

Maimonides answers that question in the following manner. "The study of Torah is incumbent upon every man in Israel, whether he be poor or rich, healthy or sick, young or very old. Even one who begs alms from door to door, or one heavily burdened with family responsibilities must set aside two periods a day, one during the day, and one at night, for the study of Torah, for the Bible commands, 'And thou shalt meditate therein (i.e. in the Torah) day and night.' (Joshua Ch. 1, vs. 8). Among the greatest of the sages of Israel were men some of whom were hewers of wood, others water carriers, and some were blind, yet they busied themselves with the study of Torah day and night, and were part of the chain of tradition transmitting the Torah from generation to generation, starting with Moses." "Until what period of life does a Jew have to continue to study the Torah? Until the day of his death, for it is written in the Torah 'Only take heed to thyself and keep thy soul diligently lest thou forget the things which thine eyes saw (at Sinai) and lest they (the words of the Torah) depart from thy heart all the days of thy life.' (Dt. 4, 9).

And when one does not study, he not only adds nothing new to his knowledge but even forgets what he previously knew." (Maimonides-Laws for the Study of Torah in the first book of his great code of Jewish law called the "Mishne Torah.")

Our fathers fulfilled this commandment much more carefully than we do. In every synagogue in former days, there were classes not only for children but for men of all ages, and of all walks of life. They would gather in the synagogue every afternoon or at least on Saturday, and

study Torah. They organized themselves into various "chevrot,” that is, "groups" or "clubs." Some knew only how to read the Psalms. These called themselves the "Chevra 

Tehilim." The "Chevra Mishnayis" studied Mishna. The "Chevra Shas" studied the Talmud. But every man considered it his sacred duty to study Torah daily all of his life. 

We too, in our Synagogues, have study groups for adults, but we have a long distance to go until we enroll as great a number of Jewish adults in these groups as were in the groups to which our fathers belonged. The continued and systematic study of the Torah by adults is a goal still to be achieved by American Jews. It is one of the noblest goals towards which we might strive. 

6. "And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children."

Our fathers instead of saying that they went to school to "get an education," said that they went to "study Torah." "Studying Torah" meant more to them than "getting an education" means to us. "Studying Torah" in the days before there were public schools which Jewish children were permitted or obliged to attend, included learning how to read and write, and as much science and history, law and morals, as the Torah contained. Above all "studying Torah" was the noblest way in which a Jew could serve God. Therefore the Jews felt that every Jewish child should be given an opportunity to study Torah. Hence teaching a child Torah is just as great and sacred a duty as studying it yourself. Jewish law further rules that he who is commanded to study the Torah is commanded to teach it, or to enable others to spend their time in teaching 

Torah to children.

Democratic countries also gradually came to the conclusion that every child should "get an education." Today

in the United States the law compels every child to go to school up to a certain age, and every community must provide each child with the opportunity to go to school. 

This is, of course, a comparatively recent achievement of 

American life. Before 1880 only a very small percentage of the children, even in America, attended school. Poverty, the indifference of the parents or the absence of schools, deprived many a child of any schooling. Recall how 

Lincoln had to struggle for an education. 

There are many reactionaries even today who do not think that education from the Public School through the University should be free to all. It is still not very easy for a really poor boy to go through college in cities or states which do not provide higher education for a very small tuition fee.

We can, therefore, take a just pride in the fact that the Jews were among the first to stress the duty of one generation to teach the next generation, not only the duty of parents to teach their own children, but the duty of the community as a whole to see to it that all children receive instruction in the Torah. Thus the Jews were the first people at least to try to create a system of education which would provide instruction for all children regardless of whether they were rich or poor. The attempt never succeeded completely. But that has been our goal for over two thousand years, and whenever we enjoyed at least a semblance of peace for an extended period of time, and had the power to organize our community life as we saw fit, we came very close to achieving that goal. 

The Talmud gives us a very brief but a most interesting account of the development of general education among the Jews. In the tractate of the Talmud called Baba 

Bathra (p. 26) the following incident is recorded. "The

High Priest Joshua ben Gemala (held office in the year

67 

63 C.E.) is to be remembered for good. Had it not been for him the Torah might have been forgotten in Israel. For in the beginning he who had a father, the father would teach him Torah. But he who had no father would not study Torah, (for there was no one who would teach him.) Then it was decreed that public teachers should be provided in Jerusalem for all the children. The result was that a father (who could not teach his son himself) would bring his son to Jerusalem to be taught. 

But a child that had no father would still remain without instruction for there was no one to bring him to Jerusalem. 

Then they decreed that there should be publicly paid teachers in every city, and boys and girls of 16 and 17 were to be taught by them. But these boys were too old and would not subject themselves to discipline. Then 

Joshua ben Gamala decreed that there should be publicly supported teachers in every city and every town, and that children should start their schooling at the age of six or seven.' 

From the above account we get a fairly clear picture of what happened. First fathers taught their own children.

Then those fathers who were either too ignorant or too busy to teach their children, hired teachers for their own children, but the children of the poor, ignorant or busy fathers went untaught. Then the law was passed requiring each community to support a teacher who was to teach all the children. 

Throughout the last eighteen hundred years practically all Jewish communities made heroic efforts to educate their children, especially the boys. Parents made all kinds of sacrifices to pay the necessary tuition, and in every community some provision was made for the education of the children of the poor.

No one compels Jews to maintain Synagogues and

Hebrew schools. All the money required is given as voluntary contributions. Every school admits some children who cannot pay at all or cannot pay fully. The Federation of Jewish Charities also maintains Hebrew Schools open to all. But in this, too, we are far behind our forefathers. Today in the United States it is estimated that not more than about 30 percent of all the Jewish children between the ages of 6-16 are in Hebrew Schools at any one time. Even most of these do not attend for more than about one or two years and get a very poor education. What wonder then that so many of them grow up having no love for Judaism, nor a desire to remain Jews. 

Whose fault is it? The blame falls first upon the parents who do not do enough to encourage their children to go to Hebrew School. Sometimes they fail in their duty as 

Jewish parents because they do not want to spend the money that a Jewish education would cost. Hebrew School tuition comes last in their budgets, not first, as in the case of our fathers. In most instances this is due to the fact that the parents are under the wrong impression that the public school education is enough for their children. They forget that the public school teaches no Jewish history, no Hebrew, no religion, and that these are absolutely necessary for a Jew who wants really to live a selfrespecting, happy life as a Jew.

We are living at a time when the name of the Jew is being attacked on all sides. All kinds of insults are being thrust in the press, from the platform, and even over the radio at the religion, the history and the present activities of the Jewish people. A Jewish child who grows up ignorant of his people's faith and history has no way to answer these false charges satisfactorily. Many of them even begin to believe them. This plus the difficulties they encounter in the outside world on account of their being

born Jews often turn them against themselves and their people. They begin to hate their own ancestors and even themselves. These are not mere fantasies. Books have been written about Jewish self-hatred. In the world in which we live nothing but a Jewish education can prevent this most unfortunate result.

But Jewish education is needed not only to prevent self-hatred. It is needed in order to enable Jewish children to develop and to satisfy their innate religious needs and strivings. The public school can not do that for them. Only the Hebrew School can give them this religious training. 

Children, and some parents, may imagine that they do not require it. Unfortunately too many of them find out too late how sorely they need it.

We can not here go into a full discussion of all the value which a Jewish education has for a Jewish child in this country. We have, we hope, however indicated that a Jewish child can not, without it, develop full self-respect as a Jew, nor a healthy and complete spiritual and religious life as a human being. Parents therefore who fail to provide their children with the opportunities for as full a Jewish education as possible are committing a wrong against them.

Secondly, blame must be placed upon the shoulders of Jewish community leaders who do not set aside for Jewish education a large enough portion of the money raised by the Federation of Jewish Charities for its various activities. 

Finally, a good share of blame must be apportioned to the children themselves, especially those above the age of thirteen who should by that time have sense enough to recognize their responsibilities to grow up as intelligent Jews. So many of them, however, refuse to make the slightest sacrifice in order to acquire Jewish knowledge, despite the urgings and the pleas of their parents. Jewish

children might well remember that no Jewish men or women ever regretted the fact that in their youth they attended a Hebrew School and acquired Jewish knowledge, while Jewish men and women by the thousands who did not go to Hebrew School or who did not acquire any Jewish knowledge, have deeply regretted it. 

It is therefore high time for the Jewish parents and the Jewish children of America to realize that if they want to be a great Jewish community, they must begin to obey more fully the commandment, "And thou shalt teach them deligently unto thy children." 

7. And thou shalt speak of them when thou sittest in thine house, when thou walkest by the way, when thou liest down, and when thou risest up.

There are those who consider themselves as very religious people but restrict the sphere of influence of their religion only to certain times, or places, or occasions. They do not all agree on the time and place that are to be assigned to religion. But they do agree on the general proposition that religion has only a certain limited time, and a certain limited place. Some among them restrict their religion to three days a year, Rosh Hashona and Yom Kippur. Others may add Saturday, and the other festivals. Most of them. agree that "religion" belongs at weddings and funerals, but not at social parties or business gatherings. The Synagogue is generally the "place" assigned for "religion." But they are quite certain that "religion" has no place in the gymnasium, the theatre or the store. These are only a few of the limitations that many people place upon the sphere within which their religion should influence their lives. 

It is against this attitude towards religion that the Shema warns us. We must learn to think of religion as embracing the whole of our lives, individual as well as social. Our

Torah and its teachings are not to be restricted to any one area of our lives. It must be made the guide of all our actions, and at all times. The ten commandments must be remembered and obeyed not only in the Synagogue, or on Yom Kippur, but every day of our lives, and no matter where we are. The command "Be ye holy" is to be obeyed not only in one's home, or in a house of worship. We are to make every effort at all times to live up to it. Honesty in speech, decency in thought, righteousness in action, holiness in all things, these are the very heart of our religion. 

These must ever be with us "when we sit in the house, when we walk by the way, when we lie down and when we rise up." One of the psalmists tells us how he attempted to carry out this principle in his own life. He said, "I have set the Lord always before me." (Ps. 16, vs. 8).

That is, "I tried always to act in the way I would act if I were in the presence of the Lord." All of us are always in the presence of the Lord, whether we are in the Synagogue, in the open field, in the crowded street, or behind the locked doors of the innermost chambers of our homes. 

Unfortunately we are not often conscious of God's presence.

The Talmud relates that when Rabban Jochanan ben Zaccai was about to die, he called his most beloved pupils to his bedside. Among the last words he spoke to them were these "May you fear the Lord at least as much as you fear man." When they left his bedside, the students expressed surprise that their master should have compared the fear of the Lord with the fear of men. Whereupon one of them related the following incident. "One day our master and I were walking along the roadside. It was very hot and we grew tired and thirsty. Before long we passed a vineyard and were attracted by the large luscious grapes. Both of us quenched our thirst from the grapes

that had fallen to the ground. Then overcome by a desire for the refreshing fruit, I started to fill my garment with clusters of freshly ripened grapes which I plucked from the vine. Suddenly our master turned to me and said quietly, "He is looking! He is looking!" In a flash I was out of the vineyard with half my booty gone. When I regained my composure I looked around but saw no man. 

Somewhat angered I asked, 'Who is looking, I see no man.' Our master pointed toward Heaven and repeated again, 'He is looking.' Then I understood. Had the owner of the vineyard been looking I would have been afraid to take that which was not mine. But I was not afraid though the Lord of all vineyards was looking. That is what our master had in mind when he expressed the hope that our fear of Heaven might be at least as great as our fear of man."

That, then, is what the Shema asks us to do in the command to talk of the Torah, to study it, to remember it, and obey it all the time. Our religion is not for any one time, or any one place. It is for every occasion, and for every moment of our lives.

The words "I have set the Lord always before me," are frequently inscribed over the Ark or pulpit in the Synagogue. It is very fitting to inscribe them there. But it would be even more fitting if each one of us inscribed them upon the "tablets of our hearts" or upon our minds. 

8. And thou shalt bind them for a sign upon thine hand and they shall be for frontlets between thine eyes. 

After reminding us of our duty to know, to teach and to obey the Torah, we are advised to surround ourselves with objects that serve at least two purposes. They remind us of our responsibilities, and they stimulate us to carry out those responsibilities. These objects are often referred to as

the symbols or ceremonials of our religion. But our Rabbis never called them by any such term. We have no word in the Hebrew language for "symbol" or "ceremony" as these words are often used in the English language. In our religion we know only of Mitzvos. What is a Mitzvah? 

It is rather difficult to answer that question. There is no one English word which is its equivalent. Trying to define it would lead us into the same difficulties which we encountered when trying to define the term "God the creator"—or to translate Tefilah by Prayer. (See above pages 4 and 14). The word Mitzvah comes from a Hebrew word which means "to command." Mitzvah, therefore, means "commandment." Hence every commandment of the Torah is a Mitzvah. But that is only a part of the meaning which the word Mitzvah has in our religion. 

There are many acts which a Jew can perform and which are called Mitzvos. But they are not specifically commanded in the Torah. It is a Mitzvah, for example, to visit the sick, to attend a funeral, to make peace between two who may be quarreling, to rock a weeping infant to sleep when none of its attendants is present, and to do an infinite number of other kind, or just deeds which each one of us has an opportunity to do almost every hour of our lives. Any good deed which has to do directly or indirectly with fulfilling the specific commandments of the Torah, or its broad ethical ideals of justice, mercy, and holiness, is called a Mitzvah.

In the sentence of the Shema which we are at present discussing, we have the law concerning the Mitzvah of Tefillin, to which we now turn our attention. 

THE MITZVAH OF TEFILLIN 

Daily prayer is probably the most universal and the most necessary form in which the truly religious individual

expresses his feelings. Judaism asks of its followers to pray at least three times daily-in the morning, the afternoon, and the evening. The morning service is the most elaborate, not only in its contents, but in the preparations that are to be made before the worshipper starts to recite it. Every Jewish male who has passed his thirteenth birthday must put on Tefillin, one on his left arm, and one on his forehead, before he starts to pray, every morning except on any holiday or festival.

The Tefillin are very simple in their structure. They are made of leather from the skin of those animals which Jews are permitted to use for food. Each one of the 

Tefillin is a cube which may vary in size. Inside of the 

Tefillin for the arm there is placed a rolled parchment on which four paragraphs of the Bible are written by hand. 

The Tefillin for the forehead is divided into four sections. 

Each section contains one of these same four paragraphs written by hand on parchment. The Tefillin for the arm is fastened on the left arm above the elbow, with a leather band, which is then wound seven times around the arm from the elbow to the wrist and then in a prescribed manner around the fingers of the hand. The Tefillin on the head also has a leather band to hold it in place, and leather bands that hang down on either side of the neck.

The paragraph found on page 15 of the Siddur is usually recited before the Tefillin are put on, because that paragraph gives a very inspiring explanation of this Mitzvah. 

It reads in part as follows: "I am about to put on my 

Tefillin in order to fulfill the command of my Creator, who commanded us to put on Tefillin, as it is written in the Torah 'and thou shalt bind them for a sign upon thine hand and they shall be for frontlets between thine eyes.' 

." The four paragraphs of the Torah (Dt. VI, vs. 

4-9; XI, vs. 13-21; Ex. XIII, vs. 1-10; vs. 11-16) which

are enclosed in the Tefillin are to remind us that God is one, that He wrought great miracles for us when He redeemed our ancestors from Egypt, that He is all powerful, and has dominion over everything above and below, doing with all in accordance, with His will. We place the Tefillin upon the arm to remind us of God's ever powerful, and ever helpful "arm" (as the great protector and benefactor). We place the Tefillin upon the left arm over against the heart to remind us to put all the powers and desires of our hearts at God's service. The other 

Tefillin we place upon the head over against the brain, indicating that we seek to subject all the thoughts of our mind as well as the faculties of our body which are controlled by the mind, at the service of the Lord.”

The paragraph then ends with the prayer that the performance of this Mitzvah may help us to lead holy lives, to think holy thoughts, to avoid sinful actions, and to serve 

God joyfully and whole-heartedly.

There is really but little more that we could add to this beautiful interpretation of the Mitzvah of Tefillin. We do, however, want to recall again the important truth that no one can give all the possible meanings that a Mitzvah can have for every individual who performs it, or even for the same individual performing it at different times. To give but a few simple illustrations. To a boy of thirteen putting on his Tefillin in his father's home in the morning, the Tefillin have a significance quite different from what they will have to the same boy later in life if he uses them on some battle field in the morning before going up in his warplane to meet the enemy. No Mitzvah has one and the same meaning and significance at all times and in all places. Giving the full and complete meaning of a 

Mitzvah, is therefore something like trying to define 

Democracy, Justice or similar words. And we had occasion

earlier in this study to refer to the problems involved in our efforts to define these words. It is thus important for us to remember that the meaning of a Mitzvah, the significance it has for us, depends to a large extent upon our age, our knowledge, our wisdom, our experience, and upon the circumstances under which the Mitzvah is being performed. This thought we hope will be more fully developed in another section of this commentary. 

Secondly, it should be mentioned that there are very grave spiritual dangers against which one has to guard when performing a Mitzvah like Tefillin. Since this danger is common to a number of other Mitzvot, such as the Mezuzah, and the Talis, we will discuss the whole subject on page 140. At this point we will limit ourselves merely to a discussion of the general subject of preparing ourselves for prayer.

PREPARING OURSELVES FOR PRAYER 

Every individual who expects to participate in an activity which is important to him, usually prepares himself so that he may be properly equipped physically and spiritually for what he is to do. A man going to his daily labor will dress in a manner appropriate to his calling. A college professor and an automobile mechanic do not do their work in the same type of clothes. A doctor will see to it that his bag contains all that he may need for the day. 

The business executive will probably examine his trade journal or the daily newspaper before he starts. If one is preparing to play golf, or tennis, or attend an evening dinner, he will again dress himself to suit the occasion. 

The clothing is not incidental to the occasion. It is really part of it; it helps to create the mood that is desired, both before and during the occasion.

If proper spiritual and physical preparation is necessary

for even the commonplace activities of man, it is doubly necessary for Prayer which is one of man's loftiest spiritual achievements. A man who has been engrossed all day in his business affairs, or his sports, cannot, without at least a moment's preparation, turn to the recital of his prayers and expect to be able to call forth within himself that exalted spiritual frame of mind which prayer requires. 

Hence Judaism sets some minimum regulations which are to be observed before we start to pray. Washing of the hands is always a pre-requisite. Before Mincha or Maariv many Jews would spend some time either in studying Torah or reciting psalms, to help them get into the mood for prayer. There were some Jews who used to wear their 

Tefillin and Talis all day. As we shall learn later, the 

Talis is worn by pious Jews all day even now. But since the daily morning service is the longest and most important, the preparation for it is the most elaborate and impressive. Hence in the morning we put on the Tefillin and 

Talis. These are among the Jew's "garments of prayer.” 

Just as the Torah commanded the Priests in the Temple to wear special garments when performing their priestly duties, so also every Jew is commanded to put on his special religious garments, helping him to call forth the mood most fitting for prayer.

The Tefillin are not worn on holidays or festivals because the whole day is to be devoted to distinctly religious activities. The fact that on holidays and festivals we are to refrain from performing the regular daily labors, is in itself sufficient to call forth that spiritual frame of mind which the Tefillin help to call forth during the ordinary week-day.

Many traditions record the variety of ways in which various Jewish saints and sages used to prepare themselves for prayer. We had occasion to refer to the fact that

some of them would get up before the sunrise, and wait expectantly for the first rays of the morning sun and recite the Yotzer Or as soon as the sun appeared. Others would take a ritual bath every morning, not merely to clean their bodies for sanitary purposes, but because physical cleanliness encourages spiritual cleanliness. They wanted to be as clean as possible, physically and spiritually, before they prayed to the Lord. Of others we are told that they would engage in a period of study or of reciting psalms before prayer. Still others would merely sit in silence, or walk out into the open to ponder over the marvels of God's creations and thus call forth within themselves the mood for prayer.

Religious people prepare themselves not only for prayer, but for all great religious experiences. Think of the many things pious Jews do in preparation for the Sabbath. All meals for the Sabbath are cooked on Friday. All business activities are suspended Friday afternoon. Special care is taken to clean the house and set the table, and the family dresses in its best clothing. In many pious homes, Friday afternoon is also the time to take a bath, not merely to clean the body, but primarily in honor of the Sabbath. 

Concerning that custom, the Talmud relates that our great teacher, Hillel, used to dismiss his students early Friday afternoon in order to take his pre-Sabbath bath. When they asked him for his reason, he answered, "If an important guest were to visit your home would you not take special care to have it spotlessly clean? So it is with me.

On the Sabbath I receive as a special guest into my body the 'Sabbath soul,' the holiness and sacredness of the Sabbath, and I want my body as clean as possible so it may be an appropriate host for so holy a guest." 

What is true of our pre-Sabbath preparations is also true for all other holidays and festivals. And because our

fathers prepared themselves properly, they were able to celebrate joyfully and meaningfully.

We have indeed wandered far afield in our discussion of the Tefillin. But we hope this wandering was not aimless.

We hope it helped to impress upon us the need for proper preparation for participation in the higher forms of religious experience. We hope it will help us appreciate the great value of the Tefillin as one of the "garments of ¡ prayer” of the pious Israelite. 

9. "And thou shalt write them upon the doorposts of thy house and upon thy gates."

This sentence refers to the Mitzvah of the Mezuzah. 

The word "Mezuzah” means “doorpost," and this Mitzvah is called the "Mitzvah of the doorpost" because it refers to a ceremony associated with the doorpost. On the right hand panel of the entrance to a pious Jew's home, or to any room in the home, there is placed a container having in it the first three paragraphs of the Shema. This is called the Mezuzah. The paragraphs of the Shema are written on parchment, by hand, and the Mezuzah is placed about a third of the way down from the top of the entrance.

The main purpose of this Mitzvah is quite clear. It is to designate the home as a Jewish home, not in any spirit of haughtiness or clannishness, but rather in the spirit of holiness and piety. The Mezuzah is to indicate that the home is occupied by people who believe in, and observe, the teachings of the Torah. Every occupant of that house is to be reminded by the Mezuzah on the doorpost as he leaves the house that the teachings of the Torah should accompany him whithersoever he may go during the day. And as he enters the home the Mezuzah is again to recall to him his duty to conduct himself within the

walls of his own home, and in the presence of his own family, in accordance with the laws of the Torah. 

The statement that the Mezuzah is to be placed upon the "gates" refers to the time when cities had walls around them. The entrance to the Jewish city was to be marked in the same manner and for the same purpose, as the entrance to the Jewish home. 

Regarding the widespread misuse of the Mezuzah, see page 141 under the general heading of "Misusing the 

Tefillin, Mezuzah, and similar Mitzvot."

REWARD AND PUNISHMENT 

The third paragraph of the Shema. 

When discussing the relationship between fear and love of God (see above p. 55), we had occasion to point out that Judaism stresses with equal emphasis the teaching that the Lord is a God of Justice, as well as a God of Love. In the Tefilos we studied heretofore, the emphasis was upon the manifestations of God's love for us, as evidenced especially in the light of the sun and in the Torah. 

The central theme of this, the third paragraph of the Shema is God's justice, which punishes wrong doing. This teaching of Judaism is clearly and forcibly expressed in the following words of the paragraph: “And it shall come to pass, if ye shall hearken diligently unto My commandments which I command you this day . . . I will give the rain of your land in its season . . . and I will give grass in thy field for thy cattle, and thou shalt eat and be satisfied...." But 

But if ye turn aside and serve other gods, "The anger of the Lord will be kindled against you. . . and ye shall perish quickly from off the land which the Lord giveth you." Maimonides the greatest Jewish theologian and philosopher (died in Egypt 1204) made this teaching the eleventh of the thirteen principles of the Jewish religion

as he formulated them. "I believe with a perfect faith that the Creator, blessed be His name, rewards those that keep His commandments and punishes those that transgress them." (Pages 89-90). There is an agada in the Midrash which relates that when God first planned to create the universe He intended to govern it in accordance with the laws of His justice only. But the ministering Angels on hearing this plan pleaded with the Lord that the universe could not possibly exist if divine Justice alone were to rule it. The Angels argued, "How could the frail and weak willed creatures who are to inhabit the earth, ever hope to win the right to exist if their acts are to be judged by the standards of absolute divine Justice only?" In response to this plea the agada says. The Lord decided to govern the universe not only by the laws of His justice but also by His love.

But Love and Justice appear to our minds to be contradictory principles. The average child cannot discover a parent's love in any punishment that the parent metes out to him, no matter how earnestly the parent may argue that the punishment was administered only because of his love for his child. The first law of love is forgiveness, the first law of Justice appears to be punishment for transgression. How can the two ever be reconciled? The truth of the matter is that with our imperfect minds we have never been able to reconcile them even where simple every-day matters are concerned. Two good judges in a court will rarely agree upon the extent to which justice or love, should be shown in any given case. Two equally good parents will have the same disagreement in their attitude towards the actions of a child whom both love with equal fervor. A child who has committed some offense frequently finds that one of his parents is inclined to forgive him with the hope that the child will not repeat the

offense again, while the other parent of the child will argue that for the child's sake he should be punished, and thus "taught a lesson." What wonder therefore that man can not always understand why God seems to display His 

Love in one instance and His Justice in another.

Questions regarding the manner in which God exercises His Justice or His Love, are not something new or original with us. We find those doubts expressed very boldly in the Bible itself. The prophet Habakkuk cries out- "Thou that art of eyes too pure to behold evil, And that canst not look on mischief, Wherefore lookest Thou, when they deal treacherously, And holdest Thy peace, when the wicked swalloweth up 

The man that is more righteous than he." (Ch. I-v. 13). 

And when the people ask the prophet why God should show His love to the repenting sinner and forgive him instead of punishing him, the prophet answers in the name of God- "For My thoughts are not your thoughts, Nor are My ways your ways, saith the Lord

For as the Heavens are higher than the earth 

So are My ways higher than your ways 

And My thoughts than your thoughts." (Is. 55-v. 8-9). 

Indeed the whole book of Job is devoted to the problem of God's justice and its manifestations in the world. 

Nevertheless though our religious teachers were fully conscious of all of the difficulties involved in their belief in God's Justice, they persisted in including it as one of the fundamental teachings of Judaism. Calamities and suffering beyond measure have come upon the Jewish people at the hands of barbarous peoples who were certainly not more righteous than the Jews. Nevertheless,

the Rabbis were unwavering in their faith that God governs the universe and all that is in it, not only by His love but also by His justice, that He punishes those who transgress His commandments and rewards those who obey them.

Recognizing fully, therefore, the many difficulties this question presents even to the wisest, maturest and saintliest minds, we shall nevertheless venture upon a discussion of it, with the hope of understanding it to the best of our limited abilities. We shall start with the following question :

A. WHY IS IT IMPORTANT TO BELIEVE IN THE PRINCIPLE OF REWARD AND PUNISHMENT? 

What do we mean when we say that we believe in reward and punishment? Basically we mean this: that there is a relationship between what we do, and what happens to us afterwards. It means that we believe that our happiness or our suffering depend in one way or another upon our actions. No one questions this statement when it refers to some kind of physical action. Let us examine the following simple illustration. A very clear sign warns that the ice on the lake is thin, and that skating on it is dangerous. Mr. X reads the sign but disregards it. He skates on the thin ice, breaks through and drowns. 

Everyone who hears of the incident agrees that Mr. X brought the tragedy upon himself. No one blames God or anyone else but Mr. X, for it. We assume that Mr. X, having read the sign, should have been more careful and should have obeyed.

But why should we not argue that Mr. X was punished too severely for his mistake? What great crime did he commit for which he had to pay with his life? He did not harm anyone by going on that ice! Is it just or fair

to kill a man merely because he did not see, or did not have the sense to believe, what is written on a sign? Most of us, however, do not argue that way. Why? Because we recognize that there are certain definite laws in the world which we cannot break without suffering the consequences. Imagine if that were not so. Imagine what a chaotic world it would be if thin ice sometimes would break, and sometimes would not break; if a man slipping from a ladder would sometimes fall down and sometimes fly up; if fire would sometimes make water boil, and sometimes turn it into ice. In such a world we, as we are today, could not live at all. It would be a world without any order or logic. All of our science would be impossible because all of science is based on the proposition that things behave in accordance with certain laws which we can discover and follow. Primitive men did not know as many of the laws of the world as we have discovered. But they could not help noticing that when a flame touched straw, or a stone was thrown into water, or when rain failed to fall on a field, certain definite results always followed. These simple observations are the beginnings of science. They make us believe without questioning that in the physical world there is always a necessary and inescapable relationship between action and result.

But when we leave the physical realm and enter into the realm of morals and ethics, the relationship between actions and consequences is not so obvious. On the contrary, very often the results seem to be the very opposite of what we thought we had a right to expect. Consider this simple illustration. The Torah commands—“Thou shalt not murder," "thou shalt not steal," "thou shalt not covet," and so on. And then the Torah says— -"If you obey these commandments you will prosper. If you disobey, you will suffer." But all around us we seem to see evidence

to the contrary. There have been men who murdered and stole and plundered and yet lived to ripe old ages apparently very happy and contented. There are, of course, many murderers and thieves who meet very unhappy ends. 

Hence we have this peculiar situation. When a man skates on thin ice, it appears that he will always break through. 

But when he murders or steals it seems that the result cannot always be predicted. Because the immediate consequences of evil actions are not always the same, many people come to the conclusion that it really makes no difference whether a man is honest or a liar, cruel or merciful. 

His actions whether ethical or unethical apparently in no way determine his happiness or suffering. 

Now if that is to be accepted as true, if we are to assume that a man's happiness or suffering are in no way connected with his moral actions, if we are to assume that it makes no difference whether a man gives charity or steals, whether he is just or cruel, kind or heartless, saintly or crude, then why should a man prefer to be honest, charitable and kind, instead of crooked, cruel and mean?

If the innocent and the guilty all meet the same end, why then should one prefer to be innocent rather than guilty?

But this is not the whole of the matter. The human mind, when it is healthy and normal, prefers logic and order to chaos and accident. We noted that in the physical world there is a certain order and logic, and certain predictable consequences always follow certain definite action. 

A stone always sinks when thrown into water and fire always consumes dry straw. We say, therefore, that at the heart of the world, there is some principle or some power which endowed our minds with their inclination towards logic. This power of our minds enables us also to discover the unchanging logic and order in the relationship between water and stone, fire and straw, and the other physical

objects in the universe. But what about truth, and justice and mercy? Is the principle or power which runs the universe interested only in what happens when a stone is thrown into water, but completely uninterested when an innocent person is murdered or a lie is passed on as a truth? If we assume, therefore, that there is no relationship between a person's moral actions and the consequences that follow, we are really saying that the power running the universe is not concerned at all with justice and mercy, truth and beauty. We are really then saying that God is not concerned with whether we are honest or not, but very much concerned with whether we skate on thin ice or not, for in the one case He has seen to it that something very definite should happen, while in the other case He apparently made no provision for any kind of logical or predictable consequence. That is the real meaning and import of atheism. An atheist is not necessarily one who denies the existence of a power which runs the universe, or even brought it into being. An atheist is one who denies that that power is concerned with truth, and justice and mercy. 

On the other hand, he who claims that he believes in the reality of truth and justice and mercy, that he believes that it does make a difference whether a man acts kindly or cruelly, but nevertheless does not believe in God, most often simply does not know what he is talking about. It is as if one were to say, "I believe the earth rotates on its axis, but I do not believe that that has anything to do with the coming of night and day.”

There are, moreover, many people who believe there must be something or some one controlling the universe, keeping the planets and the stars in their courses, but they do not believe that that power can be concerned with such physically unimportant creatures as human beings. It is against all such ideas that Judaism has fought. To believe

merely that there is a God who governs the stars, is not important for us, and could make no significant difference in our lives or our actions. Such a God is not the God in whose name our prophets spoke. What concern is it of ours whether there is, or there is not a God Who controls the stars? But it is of tremendous importance to us whether we believe or not that this God Who directs the stars also is concerned with our actions-whether we steal, murder, and lie, or we are honest, just and kind. The God in Whom our Prophets and Rabbis taught us to believe is He Who created the ethical laws as well as the physical laws of the universe. He decreed not only the law of gravitation, but also the law that "the soul that sinneth it shall die." (Ezekiel 18:3). 

The God Who "maketh the Pleiades and Orion 

And bringeth on the shadow of death in the morning, And darkeneth the day into night;

That calleth for the waters of the sea 

And poureth them out upon the face of the earth." 

He is the same God Who warns that because "Ye turn justice to wormwood 

And cast righteousness to the ground: 

Because ye trample upon the poor,

Afflict the just and turn aside the needy in the gate." Therefore- "Thus saith the Lord 

The God of hosts, the Lord;

Lamentations shall be in all the broad places, And they shall say in all the streets, Alas! Alas!" (Amos, Ch. V.)

SUMMARY 

Belief in the principle of reward and punishment is important, therefore, because- 1. The religious person cannot accept a world in which it would make no difference whether a man mur-

ders, steals, and lies, or is honest, kind and just. Such a world makes no sense and has no meaning for him. 

2. The religious person cannot accept a God Who is interested in preserving the law of gravitation, but not the law of justice.

The religious person, therefore, says "Despite the apparent evidence to the contrary, I believe with a perfect faith that the Creator, blessed be His name, rewards those that keep His commandments, and punishes these that transgress them."

B. WHAT MAKES IT DIFFICULT TO BELIEVE IN GOD'S JUSTICE? 

We have presented a rather lengthy discussion seeking to show why it is necessary to believe in the principle of reward and punishment. But we said at the very beginning, and we shall repeat again, that this principle is the most difficult one for us to understand and to apply in our daily lives. We emphasize this difficulty because our experiences in discussing religious problems with various individuals shows that sooner or later the discussion centers on the problem of God's justice. At that point most nonreligious people ask a long array of questions about the fortunes that befall some mutual acquaintance or some group or nation. They then expect an immediate simple answer showing to their satisfaction how in each particular instance God's justice revealed itself. We shall show later why such an answer cannot possibly be given by a human being. But because we cannot explain to the full satisfaction of another human being, God's treatment of any individual or group at any one given time, non-religious people will assume a victory for themselves in the discussion, and will point to it as a justification for their nonbelief. It is as if a man were to challenge an engineer to

create a perpetual motion machine, and then declare the engineer's failure to perform this impossible task as evidence that engineering is essentially a false science despite all of its other triumphs.

Nevertheless, though religious thinkers have not been able to answer fully all of the questions raised by the doctrine of reward and punishment, they have made very great progress in their efforts to grasp and understand it. 

They have, for example, analyzed the causes which make us doubt this principle. And just as in medicine the discovery of the cause of an illness is the first and most important step towards finding a cure, so also in this instance, an understanding of the causes of our doubts represents the first and most important step in overcoming them. Let us then list some of the chief causes for our doubts, and discuss each one of them briefly. 

1. A wrong conception of what constitutes a reward and what constitutes a punishment.

The most important cause of our doubts concerning the principle of reward and punishment is the frequently childish and immature ideas which we have concerning what is good and what is bad for us. Most of us think only in terms of physical health, and financial prosperity. 

Both of these are very important, and Judaism does not underestimate their value. Indeed, these are the rewards which are mentioned in this very paragraph of the Shema. 

Our fathers were promised that if "they observe the commandments, they will gather their corn, and wine, and oil, and eat and be satisfied." Moreover, "their days and the days of their children will be lengthened in the land which the Lord promised them." The great advances made by man in producing the things he needs, make it possible for us to see more clearly than ever before the literal truth

in this promise. Men are not hungry today because we cannot produce enough food for all. They are not poorly housed or clothed because we have not the knowledge or the means to house and clothe them. Our generation sees want in the midst of plenty, and we recognize more and more that the failure is not inherent in the world which God created, but the failure rather is in man who has not learned to follow God's laws directing him how to make use of what God has provided for him.

If all men lived honorably, if they helped one another as brothers should, there would be more wealth for all of us, and each of us would have a better opportunity to live a longer and healthier life. Consider for a moment how much we have been able to do in America to make life healthier and happier for everybody, because we have made at least the first few steps towards human cooperation. Think of the health services available to us because we have medical schools, and hospitals and government boards of health supervising the purity of our water and food supply, and caring for us in our illness. Think of how much richer each one of us is, because the government does so much to conserve the natural resources of the country and because we have learned to work together in building the great industries, which supply us with the necessities of life at a lower rate and in greater quantity than any other people has as yet enjoyed. Through working together as brothers, we have even learned how to overcome some of our most dangerous natural enemies. We built dams to control floods which killed thousands and impoverished hundreds of thousands of people every year. We know now how to build structures that can withstand most earthquakes. The organization of our great brotherly relief agencies like the Red Cross enables us to come at once to the help of those stricken by any kind of natural catas-

trophe and reduce to a minimum the harm it brings to human beings.

Recall also what we are now doing in the United States to provide pensions for aged people, insurance for unemployed, and all kinds of assistance, both private and governmental, to the orphan, the widow, the sick and the weak. None of these things could be done by any one man alone. They are all the result of our gradual recognition of the fact that all of us are brothers, and that we have to live together in a spirit of kindliness and brotherliness. 

How much more we could do and how much healthier and richer all of us would be if that spirit of cooperation and brotherliness would be more universal, and if we would all follow it in all our actions in life! The rewards promised in this paragraph could and would indeed be ours. We would "eat and be satisfied," and "our days would be lengthened in the land."

But unfortunately we do not all act honorably and decently. We do not all cooperate willingly and wholeheartedly. There are altogether too many among us who continue to be selfish and greedy and cruel. That is why we still have many people who are good and pious, honest and kind, and yet frequently suffer poverty, sickness, and loneliness. And when we see a person who is sick, poor, and lonely, but who is also decent and kind, we ask: "Why does he not have enough to eat and be satisfied? Why are his days not lengthened? What rewards has he received for his goodness and piety?" It is then we must remember that there are other rewards in life in addition to health and wealth.

There is above all the satisfaction of having a clear conscience, and a heart not ashamed of itself. When a human being's last moment arrives, and he is preparing to meet his creator, he can find no consolation in the money he

made, or the power he exercised. Consolation can come only from the thought that he lived the kind of life worthy of a human being, a life which set him above the animal. 

And let us remember always, that the only thing which really makes us human and sets us above the animal, is our ability to distinguish between right and wrong, good and evil, kindness and cruelty. An animal, as far as we know, can never be consciously kind, or good, or right. A hungry lion will never show mercy to a lamb. A hungry human being can control his hunger, and share what he has with another. The greatest reward which a human being can have, therefore, is the feeling that he is human. No normal person wants to feel that he is sinking to the level of the beast, and only he who practices justice, kindness and piety can feel deeply and profoundly that he is human and not beastly.

That is what is really meant by such a statement as "I would rather be right than president," that is, “I prefer the satisfaction of feeling that I am a human being in that I set a higher value on the distinctly human quality of loving truth, than on possessing power or glory which any fool has sense enough to want." That is what the rabbis meant when they taught that "the reward for the performance of a mitzvah is the mitzvah itself-the satisfaction of feeling that you have the necessary human qualities to do a mitzvah-and the punishment for a sin, is the sin itself (P. 195, ch. IV, para. 2)—the feeling that you have permitted yourself to sink to a level lower than that of a human being, lower than the one which you yourself recognize as being better and more beautiful. Hence the simple but the profoundly true Biblical statement: "Better the poor that walketh in his integrity 

Than he that is perverse in his ways, though he be rich." (Proverbs Ch. 28, vs. 6).

This summarizes the experiences of the noblest and best among man. Let the so-called "worldly-wise" and practical men scoff at this sentiment if they are so inclined, but let us at least know that in scoffing at it, they are scoffing at nobility of spirit, at honesty of heart, at purity in thought, and at all of the greatest saints, heroes and patriots whose lives made human life generally better and more beautiful.

Let us consider now the individual who is sick and poor even though pious and decent. Assume that instead of having been pious and decent he would have been a drunkard and a thief. Would that have made him healthy and rich? Surely not. Simple as that truth is, it is good to bear it in mind because people frequently talk as if all they needed to do in order to amass a fortune was to be selfish and dishonest. Merely because some thieves and misers and murderers acquire wealth and power does not mean that all who would follow their example would attain the same goals. It is important to remember that our prisons, our poor houses, our dens of human derelicts are not all populated by men and women who, on the whole, have been good and pious and decent. 

But let us look at the man who having been a thief and scoundrel is also poor and sick. What possible comfort or consolation can he have? What a complete and utter failure his life has been! In the case of the poor decent man, there is at least the satisfaction of knowing that he tried and succeeded in living like a human being. The suffering scoundrel has nothing at all in his life from which to derive even a modicum of spiritual satisfaction. 

The reward of a clean conscience, of a peaceful, selfrespecting soul, is thus to be considered as equal to the reward of wealth and health. The greatest human beings, those who have built civilization and made for the advance-

ment of mankind have been those who set the joy of a clean conscience and a self-respecting soul, above wealth and mere physical well being. They made sacrifices, when necessary, of health and wealth, in order to enjoy the reward of a pure and self-respecting heart. They are the religious men who died for their faith. They gave up home and wealth and life rather than lead a hypocritical existence, believing one way and acting in another. They refused to save their skins by sacrificing their souls. They are also the patriots who gave up their possessions and their lives for their country, the scientists who preferred to pursue truth rather than make money, the pioneers who with their life blood built new lands,—American pioneers three and four hundred years ago, and the chalutzim of the last fifty years who laid the foundations of the new Jewish community in Eretz Yisroel. All of these testified by their lives and actions that there is a reward which men can win for themselves in this world, greater and more desirable than wealth and riches. This supreme reward can be won only by living honest, decent and religious lives. 

2. Our immature views of what constitutes righteousness, piety, and mercy.

Not only do we have wrong, childish and immature ideas about the true nature of life's highest rewards and punishments, we also have rather immature ideas about righteousness, piety and mercy. We look upon an individual and say, "He is a pious and righteous man." But the truly pious or righteous man, no matter how great and sincere an effort he may make to live up to his ideal, never considers himself fully pious and righteous. He knows that "there is not a righteous man upon the earth who doeth good and sinneth not." (Ec. 7, vs. 20). Therefore,

the truly pious and understanding man never feels as if God owes him anything. He is always conscious of the vast distance between what he is and what he would like or what he ought to be. Hence, instead of feeling that 

God has not been kind enough to him, he feels that God has been most merciful in bestowing upon him even the humblest of blessings. For on the basis of his own goodness he, in his own opinion, would merit little indeed. 

Thus one of our greatest Tefilos exclaims, "Not because of our righteousness do we offer our supplications before 

Thee, but because of Thy great mercies. What are we, what is our life, what is our righteousness, what is our strength? What can we say before Thee, O Lord our God, and God of our Fathers? Are not the powerful as naught before Thee, and men of great fame as if they were not, and wise men as if they had no understanding?"

This disposition of the truly pious, to justify God even when they themselves are the sufferers is amply illustrated in our history and literature. When Rabbi Akiba was being tortured to death, he uttered no word of complaint against God. Nor was Akiba the only one who met a martyr's death during the Roman persecutions following the revolt of Bar Kochba (c 135 C.E.). Rabbi Simon and

Rabbi Ishmael were also condemned to die. The Midrash records a conversation between these two Rabbis. Whether historic or imaginary, this conversation clearly reflects the attitude of the Rabbis toward their own suffering. We are told that Rabbi Simon-"wept in despair over the inscrutability of God's justice which condemned them to the fate of those who transgress and those who desecrate the 

Sabbath." Rabbi Ishmael replied, "There were times when we sat as judges over cases and did not decide correctly; and (there were times) when false witnesses came and testified before us and they (the defendants) were

found guilty by us (on this false testimony); and (there were times) when we entered the bath and took time to eat and drink, and orphans and widows came to plead for their sustenance and the sexton told them, "They are busy just now.' See how great is the punishment for this!"

Question and answer both reveal that the two Rabbis regarded their tragic fate as a just punishment by God for their sins, Rabbi Ishmael having quieted the mind of his comrade only by finding some specific grounds for the severe punishment they were about to suffer. To most of us it would appear that the punishment was too severe for the sin. But the gravity of a sin depends very much upon the character of the individual who commits it, just as the seriousness of a stain depends upon the nature of the garment on which it is found. An ink stain on a pair of overalls is overlooked. But even a small dark speck of ink on the white front of a dress shirt is sufficient to cause us to discard the shirt until the stain is removed. Thus even a minor unethical act becomes grave when a distinguished person commits it, while many a gross sin is forgiven if committed by hardened sinners.

The readiness to defend God's justice even at the price of one's own life is illustrated also in the Midrash which relates the slaying of the seven sons of Miriam, the daughter of Tanhum. When the Emperor asked the youngest son-"Why, if God has power, did He not save your brothers?" The boy replied, "Fool that you are! You are not worthy that miracles be performed through you.

We had incurred the penalty of death. Would you not kill us, the Omnipresent has many (other) slayers,—many bears, many leopards, many snakes, many scorpions, many lions, that would have struck us down." What an inspiring affirmation of faith in the infallibility of God's justice!

But we do not have to be saints in order to recognize that all of us are sinners, that all of us of necessity share in the sins of society as a whole. As the poet so well expressed it : "Yet none of us can be spared the taint 

Who live among unspeakable events."

It is by now quite evident that the greatest part of men's suffering is not due to any cause beyond human control, like earthquakes and hurricanes, or uncontrolled diseases.

Most of our troubles are due to the wrong doings of men, either as individuals, or as groups. "Man's inhumanity to man makes countless thousands mourn," said the great 

Scotch poet Robert Burns. Because there is graft and corruption in government and business, because there is dishonesty and exploitation in the dealing of one man with another, because there is lust for power and prestige, there is suffering among men. In all of this corruption, all of us have a part, directly or indirectly. When there is a plague in the community and a man walks about doing business with the sick people, benefitting by their services to him, profiting by their illness, even though he himself may be healthy, even though he himself may try to conduct himself as honorably as possible, he has no right to complain if he too is attacked by the disease and suffers from it. 

Let us take one or two well-known historical situations in which decent people generally considered innocent of all crime, nevertheless suffered greatly. 

a. THE AMERICAN CIVIL WAR 

The American Civil War was one of the greatest tragedies in the world. Millions of human beings who were apparently otherwise very good and upright, were ruined in its wake. Why? It would be stupid and haughty for anyone to assume that he knows the facts which would fully explain every individual case. We will return to a

fuller discussion of that later. Who could say why Mrs. 

Bixby should have given her five sons as her "costly sacrifice upon the altar of freedom"? But we do know some very important things. We know, for example, that the underlying cause of the Civil War was Negro slavery. We know that everyone living in the United States benefitted directly or indirectly by Negro slavery. Everyone accepted these benefits. Every one in America was thus in one way or another implicated in the dreadful crime of slavery, just as even today every one of us in this country who sits by and accepts the cruel discrimination against the Negroes, is in one way or another a partner to this crime. 

Hence when the Civil War came, all of America suffered. 

It was Lincoln himself who towards the end of the war said in his immortal inaugural address, "The Lord has

His own purposes. 'Woe unto the world because of offenses! for it must needs be that offenses come; but woe to that man by whom the offense cometh.' If we shall suppose that American slavery is one of those offenses, which, in the providence of God, must needs come, but which, having continued through His appointed time, He now wills to remove, and that He gives to both North and South this terrible war, as the woe due to those by whom the offense came, shall we discern therein any departure from those divine attributes which the believers in a living 

God always ascribe to Him? Fondly do we hope-fervently do we pray-that this mighty scourge of war may speedily pass away. Yet if God wills that it continue until all the wealth piled by the bondman's two hundred and fifty years of unrequited toil shall be sunk, and until every drop of blood drawn with the lash shall be paid with another drawn with the sword, as was said three thousand years ago, so still it must be said, "The judgments of the 

Lord are true and righteous altogether."

b. THE JEWS OF GERMANY AND POLAND 

The most overwhelming tragedy in Jewish history of the last 450 years has been the indescribably cruel, inhuman persecutions visited upon the Jews of Germany and Poland by the Nazis. Jews in great numbers have naturally been asking "If God rewards the righteous why should the pious Jews of Poland suffer so?" "Were the Jews of

Germany so terribly bad that they deserved such a horrible punishment?" Again we say, we cannot possibly know the full answer to these questions. "Who can fathom the spirit of the Lord, and to whom among men has God made known all of His plans." (Is. 40). But there are some things we do know. The Jews were part of the whole of German and Polish society. Both of these countries were full of social injustice and crimes, and many 

Jews among them lived fairly prosperously.

The Nazi disease spread through Germany for the same reason that a disease germ spreads through a human body, when the body, because of natural weakness or abuse and exposure, is too weak to resist the germ. German society was spiritually weakened by the great suffering of the German people. Hence the germ of anti-Semitism which has always been present in the minds and souls of the Germans, grew and developed. The Jews were not the only ones who suffered because of the spread of Nazism. 

They were among the first because they could resist the effects of the germ. A great Jewish philosopher-poet, Yehuda Halevi (d. 1145) said that "Israel is the heart among the nations." Just as the heart suffers when any part of the body is affected with a disease, so Israel suffers when any part of mankind is affected by a disease. Wherever injustice raises its head, the Jews will be among the first to feel its evil effects. But they did not long remain the only sufferers. Austria, Czeckoslovakia, Poland, and

now all of Europe are paying the penalty for permitting the germ of Nazism to take root. In the end, Germany herself will undoubtedly be the worst sufferer. 

All of mankind had a share in the injustices which made the appearance of the Nazis and their coming into power possible. And the Jews of Germany had their share in it. 

They did not give large sums of money to fight the Nazis before they came into power. They did not make great sacrifices to build up Eretz Yisroel. 

Does all this mean that the Nazis were justified in doing what they did? No! A thousand times No! No more than a pneumonia germ is "justified" in attacking and overcoming the body of a poor child weakened by the lack of proper available care. But one of the laws of the universe is that a pneumonia germ can overcome a weakened body. Thus also is it one of the laws of human life that evil forces can overcome a society in which injustice and inequality continue to exist for any length of time. The pneumonia germ destroys a weak human body. The Nazi germ has destroyed weakened political and economic bodies.

Because we are not all always as innocent of participation in the great crimes of humanity as we sometimes imagine, Jews on Yom Kippur confess their sins as a community. Almost all possible sins are included in the long list to which we confess. (See Siddur p. 259-262). The confession is written in the first person plural. We say, "Our Father, Our King, we have sinned before you,' Some of the sins listed we may perhaps not have committed personally, but they were committed in the society in which we live. Most often we did very little to prevent the commission of those sins. By an inexorable law of nature we suffer the evil consequences that must result. 

Nor should we complain too bitterly at sharing in evils

which in our opinion, we had no direct share in bringing about. That is the inescapable responsibility we carry as members of human society. But let us never forget that the fact that we are members of human society, brings us also untold blessings which we, by our own action did not make possible or even deserve. Let us always remember that we all share in the blessings which come to society as a whole through the goodness of the few. We all partake of the blessings which men like Washington, Emerson, Jefferson, Edison, and an infinite host of others have brought to America. None of us ever rises to say "I will not enjoy this blessing because it is not of my making." 

Thus all of us must be ready to share in the misfortunes which come upon our society because of the evil deeds of a few. As human beings we are all one society, all one family partaking in small or large measure of the sorrows resulting from the evil, and the blessings resulting from the good, in our midst. 

13. Our Desire To See Immediate Results.

Another factor which causes us to doubt the principle of reward and punishment is the fact that in ethical and spiritual matters, results do not always immediately follow the actions. "Because sentence against an evil work is not executed speedily, therefore the heart of the sons of men is fully set in them to do evil." (Ecc. 8, vs. 11). Very often a long time intervenes between the act and its consequence. "The wheels of the gods grind slowly, but very fine."

When a man falls from a tenth story window, the result is apparent at once. When a man murders, or commits adultery, or steals, the results may not be visible for years, sometimes for generations. Every one's experiences offer ample illustrations of this truth.

During the period of prohibition in the United States, when the manufacture and sale of alcoholic drinks was illegal, many men organized themselves into groups who not only violated the prohibition law, but corrupted government officials and murdered men while conducting their illegal business. But many of these violators of the law became very wealthy. They controlled city governments, were accepted in the so-called "best" society, and were apparently honored and respected, although every one knew that they were little more than thieves and murderers. Many went about asking, "Why obey the law? 

Why try to make an honest living when thieves and cutthroats are honored?" The great number among them who were murdered were forgotten. The few "successful" ones were envied. But the time came when the American people recognized what was happening, and one by one these men met their proper ends. While they were successful many pointed to them as proof that honesty and decency "do not pay," and are not necessary. But when these "successful" gangsters entered upon their proper "reward" those same people turned to some other temporarily successful thief or liar to prove their point. They conveniently overlooked what happened to their previous examples.

The same thing is true on a larger scale in world history. 

Over and over again people will argue that “might,' "the sword," is the only thing that counts in human affairs. And they will point to the nearest contemporary example to prove their point. In the days of Napoleon, he was the irrefutable proof of the statement that "God helps the largest or best equipped army." Later it was 

Bismark. Now it is Hitler or the Japanese. Or they will point to Alexander the Great, or Caesar, or Rome. But they forget that the work of every one of the great con-

querors of the past lasted only for a comparatively short time. Rome existed longer than all, but even her great imperial power lived for only some four hundred years and then crumbled. And those four hundred years were crowded with civil strife, with all kinds of injustices and social brutalities, so that the greatest Roman historian records the natural death at an advanced age of one of the public men of Rome, as an extraordinary event. 

The power of the Czars of Russia was crushed; the Hapsburgs who once governed Europe are no more. No evil force has been able to erect a permanent structure. Evil must in the end destroy itself, even though it often takes what to us may appear an unduly long time.

The workings of the law of justice and mercy may in some ways be compared to the working of the most inescapable law of nature, the law of gravitation. An architect may build a house and disregard the law of gravitation. 

He may build it with a flaw. Perhaps the rafters supporting the roof may not be as strong as they should have been. The house will stand, and the roof will remain up for a while, a week, a month, a year, or more. But gravity will be exerting its pull on that roof all the time, and in the end it will fall.

The same is true in the life of individuals, even in the physical sphere. A man may be warned by a doctor to avoid foods harmful to him. He can disregard that doctor's warning for a week, or month, or year, but if the doctor was right, he will pay the penalty in the end. Thus it is with the moral life. Religion warns us that dishonesty, adultery, impiety, are harmful to us. They will undermine our spiritual and in the end our physical well-being. 

We disregard the warning and for a time we think that we are "getting away with it." The arrival of the day of reckoning is, however, unavoidable. Thus those who

have lived and observed life for any length of time know how much unhappiness there is in this world, not because men lack food, shelter or clothing, but because they lack faith in the inviolability of the laws of morality, honesty and decency. 

Just as the punishment is often postponed, the reward is often also delayed. Everyone of us can think of people whom we know, who labored and sacrificed for many years before the results of their labors appeared. Every parent who had to deny himself rest, recreation, and comfort in order that his child may acquire a higher education, is a living witness to that truth. It was illustrated on a national scale in the lives of that heroic and adventuresome generation of Jews who migrated from Eastern 

Europe to the United States fifty and sixty years ago. 

What sufferings they encountered in their wanderings!

What heartaches were involved in the separation of parents from children, and husbands from wives. The people who suffered those difficulties were not especially wicked. They were on the whole good and innocent people, and many a time they must have asked why they should have thus been subjected to grief. In most instances it took years before they recognized that the courage which made it possible for them to undertake the migration was in itself their greatest blessing. But the full reward for their travail only we today can see in the very existence of the great American Jewish community, and in the liberties which it enjoys. Who among the Jews who left Eastern Europe half a century or more ago would not willingly again undergo all of the hardships if he knew what we today know about the comparative conditions of life in Europe and life in America.

Let us therefore not consider a man as blessed or punished because of what happens to him at any one period

of his life. The final judgment must be made only after seeing the whole of the picture. 

4. Our knowledge can never be complete. 

We are now ready to discuss why it is that we cannot expect ever to be able to explain fully how the principle of reward and punishment applies in any one given instance. 

The simple truth is that our knowledge of any given situation can never be sufficiently adequate for us to be able to pass correct judgment. We, for example, observe that 

Mr. A., who to us appears to be a very fine gentleman, is suffering. How can we proceed to explain his situation? 

Do we know his secret inner thoughts and purposes? Do we know how Mr. A. is associated with evils not particularly of his own making, but which he nevertheless may not have made a sufficiently heroic effort to avoid or counteract? Can we possibly forsee how Mr. A.'s present sufferings may prove for him and for his dear ones to be in reality blessings in disguise? How can we properly balance a man's merits against his faults, or measure correctly the sum of blessings due him?

Moreover, all that we can possibly know is what we see, and we see mighty little at best. We know the calamities that come upon an individual, but can we know the ills which he has been spared? Rabbi Eliezer elaborated this thought in a comment which he made on the Biblical verse, "God alone performs miracles." He explained it as follows: A man may be reclining on a couch in his garden and a poisonous snake may be crouching underneath. The man knows nothing of the snake's presence. 

He gets off the bed, and the snake frightened by the suddent motion withdraws instead of attacking the man. Now the snake might just as easily have turned on the man and poisoned him. The man then might have blamed the

Lord for his misfortune. But the man having been spared, without his even knowing of the danger that was lurking, goes on his way without any particular sense of gratitude to God."

Thus many of us who complain when an evil comes upon us should take into consideration how many evils we may have been spared, without our even being conscious of the fact that these evils had threatened us. 

Lincoln's assassination was a mighty tragedy. But who can tell what his life would have been like had he continued to live in the corrupt, passion laden atmosphere of the American political scene following the Civil War.

Think of how tragic Wilson's life was after he returned from Europe and was repudiated by the Senate of the 

United States.

Thus also we know the blessings bestowed upon a man, but can we know what blessings have been denied him? 

Who has been blessed the more, he who starts at the lowest rung of the ladder and with mighty effort and sacrifice works his way up, experiencing at the same time the thrill of growth and achievement, or he who can live in ease and comfort, vegetating upon the fruits of the labors of others?

We can proceed almost indefinitely with this series of questions and doubts. But the above, we believe, is sufficient to indicate how hopelessly impossible it is for us to know enough about any given situation in order to explain fully how God's justice is revealed in it. Only God can possibly have the necessary knowledge of a man's past actions and associations, of his deepest motives and purposes, and of the future results of present joy or grief, to decide whether he is being treated justly at any moment. 

Hence for one of us to challenge God's actions by declar-

ing this one worthy of the punishment he is now suffering and the other unworthy of the blessings he is now enjoying, is the grossest form of presumption. Therein was the unforgivable sin committed by Job's friends. They saw him in great distress, suffering the loss of wealth and family and health. In the very understandable bitterness of his soul he expressed his resentment at God's ways with him. The friends apparently anxious to defend God try to justify God by declaring Job a sinner. But when we see a fellow human being suffer, it is not our first duty to defend God. We cannot do it if we tried. We cannot know whether this is a punishment for a sin directly or indirectly committed, or whether this present suffering is only a preparation for a greater reward. Our first duty, therefore, is to help the sufferer, to sympathize with him, and to stengthen him by word and action in his faith that God's mercy will again be bestowed upon him. Human beings are indeed one of the agents whereby God's justice as well as God's love are revealed in the world. But the 

Rabbis have taught that God's "choice of His instruments is not haphazard, for good is brought about by means of those who are themselves worthy, and evil by means of those who are themselves guilty." A Torquamada, an 

Attila, or a Hitler may be the "rod of God's anger,' (Is. 10, vs. 5), wherewith God's justice inflicts its punishments upon the sins of men, while a Hillel, a Baal Shemtov, a Herzl, a Baron Rothschild, a Baron Hirsch, a Jacob

Schiff, a Pasteur, every father and mother, and a host of others, may be the agents of God's mercy. But since we have the choice to be the agents either of God's mercy or of His justice in this world, the pious and the best among men will try to be the messengers of His mercy. Our chances of erring are ever so much less. To be merciful to a fellow man does not require the vast amount of د,

knowledge which sitting in judgment over our fellow men necessitates.

Hence the truly religious man has what might be called a "double standard" in his attitude towards blessings and punishments. When he himself suffers, he will tend to attribute his suffering to his own sins and shortcomings. 

But when he sees the suffering of his fellow man he will not justify it by referring to God's justice. He will view it rather as the occasion for him to become the messenger of 

God's mercy to that man. And if in the practical affairs of men, we of necessity must at times sit in judgment, let us do it with hnmility, and with a complete absence of the spirit of revenge. We can presume to be the agents of 

God's justice only to the extent to which we very clearly and obviously prevent the weak from being wronged by the strong, or restore to the unquestionably wronged and oppressed the opportunities to exercise their inalienable rights as the children of God. 

5. Why do men have the will and the power to do evil?

The question of God's justice frequently involves the question of why God created man with the will and the power to do evil. It seems so reasonable to argue that had 

God created men without the ability to do evil, He would not have to punish them.

The question seems simple indeed, but few people who ask it realize that what they are asking is really "Why did 

God create man altogether?" Because the only quality which really makes us human beings is this power to choose to do either good or evil. None other of God's creations, to the best of our knowledge, has that power. Certainly the inanimate objects of the world, the stones, the air, and the water, do not have that power. Nor do any of the living creatures from the one celled amoeba to the chim-

panzee, display any such faculties. It is interesting to note, also, that in Jewish thought even the angels which are considered the spiritual superiors of men, were not endowed with this power of choice. Hence to deprive man of his power of choice is in reality to deprive him of the quality which above all others makes him "man"—and to transform him into something other than what he is. 

For that same reason we cannot ask why punishment for moral wrong-doing is not immediate and automatic. If it were, there would again be no reality to man's power to choose between one type of action and another. The fact that a man will not willingly place his hand into fire displays no particular human virtue. The effect of such action is too automatic, and the extent to which man is reduced to an automaton, to that extent his stature as man is reduced. Obviously it would be foolhardy even to discuss the question "Why did God create human beings?"

We might as well ask why He created the world? But we should not imagine that these questions reflect profundity of thought. To try to seek a purpose for something which in itself should and does represent an ultimate goal or purpose reveals merely immaturity of thought. Is there any merit to such questions as "Why should human beings enjoy music? Why can't a stone move around? Why can human beings talk?" All of these matters belong to the given ultimate data of the world beyond which we cannot possibly penetrate.

CONCLUSIONS 

Though this discussion of the reasons which make us doubt the principle of reward and punishment has been lengthy, it has been far from exhaustive. Let no one who reads the preceding pages imagine therefore, that on the basis of them alone he can acquire a full grasp of the sub-

ject, or a complete acquaintance with all of the rich and fruitful literature of our religion bearing on this question. 

A mere section in a commentary on the Prayer Book cannot possibly deal completely with a subject which in itself requires a whole volume. All that we did was to touch upon some of the subject's many aspects and indicate some of the lines of thought developed by our Rabbis on this theme.

C. SOME OTHER QUESTIONS REGARDING THE PRINCIPLE OF REWARD AND PUNISHMENT WHICH ARE RAISED BY THIS PARAGRAPH OF THE SHEMA 

Before closing our discussion of the principle of Reward and Punishment, we should indicate at least two more questions raised by this paragraph of the Shema in connection with this principle. 

1. Are the rain and the wind and other natural phenomena influenced by human action, whether good or bad?

This paragraph states clearly they are so influenced. 

It reads, "And it shall come to pass if ye hearken diligently unto My commandments . . . that I will give the rains of your land in their due season." If, however, the commandments are not obeyed, the Lord "will restrain the

Heavens and there will be no rain." Important as this question is, its discussion will be delayed to a later point in our study. It belongs more appropriately to the paragraph in the Amidah in which we pray that God may cause the fields to produce abundantly and give sufficient rain to assure a plentiful harvest. When our study will progress to that prayer we will take up the whole question of the relationship between man's actions and what we call the immutable laws of nature.

2. 

Is our reward or punishment equally dependent upon the observance of all of the laws of the Torah? 

The Rabbis divide the mitzvos into two large groups. 

The one group is called "the mitzvos which refer to the action of man towards his fellow man," such as-Thou shalt not steal, kill, bear false witness. Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself. The second group is called: "mitzvos which refer to actions of man in his relationship to God," such as prayer, observance of the Sabbath, the dietary regulations, the Festivals and their various observances, and many similar mitzvos.

Those who observe the first group of mitzvos only, we I usually describe as "good" men. Those who observe the second group of mitzvos also, we usually describe as "good and pious" men. 

Most people readily see the relationship between the observance of the first group of mitzvos and the welfare For the suffering of human society. It is quite obvious that a human group in which the last five of the ten commandments are flagrantly and widely violated, is bound to meet with disaster. But there are very many today who question whether there is any relationship between the second ✰ group of mitzvos and the welfare of a society, or of an individual. What difference does it or should it make to 

God, or to anyone else, if a man says his prayers in the morning or does not say them; if he observes the dietary laws or does not observe them; if he works or does not work on the Sabbath.

The answer to this question obviously involves a discussion of the whole subject of piety and its contribution to human welfare. It necessitates an examination of all of 

It the mitzvos guiding man's relationship to God. To do that at this point would take us altogether too far afield

from our main study. We will, therefore, again have to limit ourselves only to some brief remarks indicating a few main lines of thought on this question. 

1. The average individual who has given little thought to the problem of ethical action imagines that the commandments not to kill or steal, or to "love thy neighbor as thyself," are matters merely of "common sense" or "logic." Their observance, therefore, does not require any particular spiritual quality in the individual. A person is honest because "honesty is the best policy," in the sense that it will make him a more successful business man, politician, or professional man. "It stands to reason," we are frequently told, "that a man should not steal because if all steal, all will suffer." A host of other apparently irrefutable "arguments" are mustered to prove that all an individual has to do is use "common sense" and he will arrive at the whole ethical code of action implied in such concepts as justice, honesty and mercy.

Nevertheless, while there is no doubt that common sense and logic support the ethical demands of justice, honesty and mercy, it still remains a fact that many otherwise logical and intellectually able individuals do not follow these principles. The Greeks and Romans were very able logicians and philosophers. Yet the commandments to love your neighbor as yourself, to love the stranger, to protect the weak, the orphan and the widow, or to seek peace and pursue it, never became integral parts of their philosophies, their politics, or their religions. In the world today we also have whole nations advocating in theory, and applying in actual life, principles which are the very opposite of justice and honesty and mercy. These nations have declared might to be superior to right. They insist that honesty is not a virtue if it interferes with their domination over others, and that mercy towards anyone who

disagrees with their policy, is a vice, a weakness rather than a virtue. They defend their point of view with an array of "logical" arguments which are not easily refuted. Obviously, if among the intellectually more advanced peoples of the past and the present there were and are such abysmal differences of opinion regarding justice and mercy, these cannot be simply a matter of "logic and common sense.' وو Moreover, the very arguments we hear about the prosperity of the wicked and the suffering of the righteous, are arguments which in reality deny that to be just and honest is a matter either of common sense, or of practical wisdom. For what "common sense" can one discover in the suffering of the righteous, and what "logic" is there in the prosperity of the wicked? It is only with the help of religious faith that we can find any "logic" at all in such situations. There are too many occasions in life when temptations successfully shake men's confidence in the wisdom of justice, mercy, and honesty. Too many logically minded individuals succumb rather easily to dishonesty, selfishness, and injustice, for us to believe that noble character is a matter merely of common sense and logic. 

These daily occurrences are ample proof that something much more than "common sense" is needed if our lives are to be fortified against the lure of greed, pride, and the desire for power. True piety is the chief, if not the only fountain from which the human soul draws the strength it needs if it is to fulfill unflinchingly the obligations placed upon it by the mitzvos regulating the relation of man to his fellow. We know that there are many "pious frauds." But that is no more an argument against piety than the fact that there are traitors is an argument against patriotism. 

We know also that there are some very splendid human beings who are not pious. But there are many illiterates who built and build great industrial enterprises. We do

not consider that an argument against learning and education. Let it be sufficient for the present to state the historic fact that the highest conceptions of what the proper conduct of man towards his fellow man should be, came not from philosophers, logicians, or scientists, but from religious leaders, from saints and prophets in whose life piety of one form or another played à dominating role. 

Moreover the noblest devotion to those ideals is most fully illustrated by those individuals in whose lives piety still is a living influence either because of their own actions or because of their memories. Many Jews there are today who themselves are not pious, but at least try to practice charity, to support institutions of Jewish learning and be otherwise communally useful citizens. In most instances they grew up in pious homes. But because the homes they themselves built were no longer sanctified by the atmosphere of piety, they most frequently failed to transmit to their children even their own lukewarm loyalties to good causes. Many of the "non-pious" good people today are thus living on the memories and ideals implanted in them by their pious parents. But their children having no such vivid pictures of sacrifice and pious service to God and man before them, show clear indications of retrogression even in their practice of mercy and charity. 

This result is inevitable, for the relationship between piety and goodness may be compared to the relationship between a doctor's continued study of medicine and his increasing proficiency in his profession. A doctor may graduate with the very highest honors from medical school, but if upon graduation he ceases to study, he may continue for many years to be accepted as a passably good doctor. 

He may even achieve a great degree of success. But in reality he is deteriorating. On the one hand he is forgetting much of what he learned at school. On the other

hand, by not increasing his knowledge he ceases to develop his maturing abilities. In the same way, a young lawyer trained in the office of a great lawyer may adopt some of the great man's ways or manners, and thus even meet with considerable recognition. But unless he continually applies himself to study and thought, his imitation of the great man will become routinized and mechanical and ultimately futile.

Now piety is, among other things, the climate in which an individual's inclination towards "goodness" can best grow and mature. In prayer, in Festival and Sabbath observance, in our Jewish dietary laws, we are constantly refining our characters. Rab, the great Babylonian Jewish teacher and scholar (about 160-247) expressed this view when he said that "the mitzvos were given to man in order to refine him." Through piety we are constantly training ourselves to be conscious of our actions, to meditate, to think about our deeds and ambitions, and to criticize ourselves. Through piety we attempt always to be conscious of God and of our duties as His children. True piety never permits us to become complacent, self-satisfied and smug in our judgment of ourselves. Hence we are stimulated always to improve ourselves in our actions towards others. 

But one in whose life piety of any kind never plays a part, one who never prays, nor observes a religious festival, nor sets aside regular periods for study and contemplation, his spiritual life will deteriorate as inevitably as will the proficiency of a doctor who ceases to study. In the end that deterioration is bound to show itself in his actions towards his fellow men.

Our Jewish generation is from a Jewish point of view, a generation which was given a good start by the memories of the past. But our spiritual qualities are unquestionably weakening with the decrease of piety in our midst.

Many will challenge that statement especially since it must here be left without further elaboration and proof. 

But its essential truth can be substantiated. 

Piety then is inseparably bound up with justice and mercy, and he who would separate the two is trying to separate one side of a coin from the other, one of the two tablets of the ten commandments from the other, or a parent's sacrifices for his children from the love in his heart for those children. All of these things can conceivably be done. But the separation can not continue for any length of time, without doing irreparable harm. 

2. Piety, within the framework of the Jewish religion, serves group welfare in yet another very direct way. The observance of Sabbath, of Festival, and dietary regulations, and regularity in recitation of prayers, whether alone or in the Synagogue, keeps alive not only the sense of proper action towards all men, but the special relationship between the individual Jew and the Jewish people. A 

Jew whose life is devoid of all acts of Jewish piety, is a Jew who has cut himself away from his people. He may continue for a while to make his contribution to Jewish charitable endeavors. But he will not display energy and initiative in creating Jewish cultural and spiritual values. 

He will not support Jewish education. He will not help build synagogues. He will not help maintain Jewish periodicals. The inevitable result of such an attitude is a deterioration in the quality of Jewish community life. The punishment for the Jewish community, and that individual as a Jew, is then usually not long delayed, nor can it be mistaken. The Jewish community disintegrates. It is not prepared either spiritually or organizationally to meet attacks from its enemies. The result is either an empty or meaningless "life," or assimilation and death. Greater punishments than these cannot be visited upon any group.

There may be some who will point to non-pious, nonreligious, or even anti-religious Zionists as a refutation of the above statements. To them we would say but this. 

Anti-religious Zionists are very few in number. They, and the non-religious group may be able at the present time to help drain swamps and build industries. They yet have to prove that in real adversity they will have the stamina to carry on Jewish life as our fathers did, even after the destruction of the Jewish state and the dispersion throughout the world. But more important even than that is the fact that most of the so-called non-religious Zionists are merely trying to develop a new kind of piety to replace the older type. Instead of reciting the Shema daily, they urge the reading of two chapters from the Bible. Instead of reading the old Hagada on Pesach, they try to write a new one. Instead of devoting the Sabbath to the study of Torah, they try to devote it to cultural activities of another type. 

Some of their innovations are undoubtedly very worthwhile. The Jewish people will be indebted to them for their resourcefulness and courage. But on the whole, they still have far to go to create a new piety which will equal the old in spiritual richness and effectiveness.

Anyone acquainted with the long history of our people needs no further proof of the above statements. Our history and experience have proved more conclusively than any logic could, that a Jewish community devoid of traditional Jewish piety degenerates spiritually and ultimately disappears. The warning sounded by this paragraph of the 

Shema that "If ye turn away and worship other gods, ye will be destroyed from off the face of the earth,” has unfortunately been proved too many times in our history. 

And he who loves Israel will not again want to test the validity of that warning.

D. COMMENTS ON SOME ADDITIONAL IDEAS CONTAINED IN, OR SUGGESTED BY, THE THIRD PRARAGRAPH OF THE SHEMA 

1. Kindness to animals.

Among the rewards promised in this paragraph for the observance of the Torah there is the following: "I will give grass in thy fields for thy cattle, and thou shalt eat and be satisfied." The Midrash comments on the fact that in this sentence, the cattle precede the human beings. 

First there is the promise of grass in the field for the cattle, then, "thou" shalt eat and be satisfied. This verse is quoted by Rab (d. about 245 C.E. in Babylonia), one of the great teachers of Judaism, as Biblical support for the law that "a man is not allowed to taste a morsel of his own meal before he serves his cattle their food." This concern for the welfare of the animals is not merely a whim with one or two of the Rabbis. It is an essential part of our religion. It is another one of the great ethical principles which Judaism first placed before mankind. 

None of the pagan civilizations with which Jews were acquainted had anything to say about kindness to animals. 

Since the Greeks would expose even weak or unwanted human infants to death, we could not expect any tenderness on their part to dumb creatures. The same indifference to animal welfare characterized the Roman attitude. 

Like all of the great teachings of Judaism, the principle of kindness to animals is rooted in Biblical law and narrative, and was later amplified by the Rabbis. 

Thus the Bible commands: a. "Thou shalt not plow with an ox and an ass together" (Deut. 22, 10) because their strength is not equal and one or the other will suffer by it. b. "Thou shalt not muzzle the ox when he treadeth

C. out the corn" (Deut. 25, 4). It is cruel to have an animal in the midst of its food and not permit it to partake thereof. "Thou shalt not see thy brother's ass or his ox fallen down by the way, and hide thyself from them; thou shalt surely help him to lift them up again" (Deut. 22, 4 and Ex. 2, 5). These laws are concerned more with the plight of the animal lying under the burden than with the principle of helping a fellowman. d. A newly-born calf is not to be brought even as a sacrifice to the altar until it has been with its mother for at least seven days (Ex. 22, 29). e. "Thou shalt not seethe a kid in its mother's milk." (Ex. 23, 19). That action would show a cruel indifference to the tender relations existing between an offspring and its mother, even in the animal world. f. The Ten Commandments enjoin rest on the Sabbath not only for human beings, but also for the ox, the ass, and any of the cattle. (Deut. 5, 14). g. The story of Jonah tells of God's mercy not only on the human inhabitants of Nineveh, but also on its "many cattle." (Jonah 4, 11). h. The Jewish laws of slaughtering an animal are designed to prevent all unnecessary pain to the animal. The shameless hypocrisy of anti-Semites of all ages is therefore clearly evidenced by their attacks against the Jewish laws of Shechitah, of slaughtering animals. They who are themselves ruthlessly cruel to human beings, accuse the Jews of cruelty to animals. Every unbiased student of

the subject has testified that the Jewish method of slaughter was far more considerate of the animals' welfare than any other method used by any people. 

As a matter of fact, it was only within the last generation or two that other nations started as much as even to give thought to the need of changing their exceptionally cruel methods of animal slaughter. When the advocates of more humane methods for slaughtering animals first made their demands for legislation on the subject, they pointed to what the Jews had done centuries earlier. i. Hunting or fishing, as a sport, without thought of using that which is caught for food, is forbidden by Jewish law. j. The principle of "compassion for the suffering of every living creature" is one of the great principles of our religion. It was the forerunner of our modern "Societies for the prevention of cruelty to animals."

Judaism's attitude towards animals is characteristic of its attitude on other ethical questions. It is characterized by an extraordinary combination of idealism and realism, of recognition of man's human weaknesses and an unwearying effort to raise man to as high a level of ethical action as he is capable of achieving. No extreme ethical doctrine is advocated merely because it may sound good, even though an honest estimate of the human being as we now know him points to the impossibility of his achieving that ethical goal. Thus Judaism largely avoided the extreme doctrines advocated by other religions or philosophies.

We noted for example, that the religions of the Greeks and Romans knew nothing at all about kindness to animals or about consideration for them. Cruelty was practiced and tolerated. That was one extreme. The other extreme is represented by some oriental religions, especially Hinduism, which practically deifies animals. The cow, in India, is considered a sacred animal and therefore man dare not make use of it. We readily acknowledge the lofty idealistic position of Hinduism. It not only enjoins vegetarianism, but also in many respects considers the life of an animal as inviolate and as sacred as the life of a human being. 

Nevertheless, we cannot close our eyes to the harm this attitude has done to the followers of Hinduism. It is a great contributing factor making for poverty and disease among them. In many respects it turns out to be cruel to the animals themselves. The "sacred cows" of India being more numerous than the country can support, are one of the most pitiable sights to behold.

Judaism avoids both these extremes. It forbids cruelty and enjoins all possible kindness. But it does not “deify" or "sanctify" any animal. Nor does it set the same value upon an animal's life as upon that of the human being. 

If the welfare of human life necessitates the sacrifice of animal life, whether for food, or for the protection of health, or safety, that sacrifice is permitted.

Without any lengthy discussion, we shall refer only to two other illustrations of this Jewish inclination to avoid extremes. 

1. Divorce. In some societies, divorce is as simple as marriage. In Soviet Russia until but a short time ago, all that a man and woman had to do to be divorced was to register their intention with some designated state authority. On the other hand, Catholicism does not permit

divorce under any circumstances except by a special dispensation from the Pope. Judaism permits divorce, but surrounds it with safeguards which prevent it from becoming too simple and insignificant a matter. 

2. War. Nazis, Fascists, and many others look upon war as one of the noble activities of a nation. They see in it the highest expression of a people's spirit, and therefore enter upon it with enthusiasm and fervor. Quakers view war as an absolute evil, participation in which is never, under any circumstances justified. Judaism, unquestionably hates war, and decries it as the greatest evil which can befall the human race. It views peace as the highest and noblest goal. Yet Judaism does consider participation in war under certain conditions as justifiable and even obligatory.

In none of the above instances is it a matter of merely taking the "middle of the road" between two extremes. 

Judaism did not decide upon its course after studying the two extremes. Judaism's path was laid by the original and unique genius of our gerat religious teachers who were endowed to the highest degree with a rare combination of precious spiritual and intellectual qualities. On the one hand, they were idealists striving to set human life on the highest possible level. On the other hand, they were honest men who looked at the realities of life with clear minds and open eyes. They thus had an extraordinary appreciation of the limits to which man's spiritual and intellectual equipment would enable him to attain. Like the ladder in 

Jacob's dream (Gen. 28, vs. 10-12) their heads were in heaven but their feet were firmly planted on the earth. 

Moreover, the Rabbis never went to an absurd extreme in their allegiance to any one ethical principle. They

recognized and were loyal to many ethical ideals. They were interested not only in peace, but in justice, and Israel, and Torah, and in a great many other ideals. They knew that no one of these could be declared superior to all others, under all conditions and circumstances. Thus for example, they at times set their devotion to justice above their devotion to Israel. Hence though they loved their people with an unbounded love, nevertheless they did not say, “Israel, right or wrong." When Israel sinned, they recognized that justice required that Israel be punished. They loved peace with a passionate love. But when in the days of the 

Maccabbees, devotion to peace meant endangering the existence of Israel, the Rabbis were ready to join the Maccabbees in their heroic struggle for Jewish existence. 

Thus also the Rabbis certainly believed in the sacredness of the home, and did not want to see husband and wife separated. But they also had a great respect for the dignity of a human being, and they could not therefore insist that a woman must be forever bound to a man who may be physically and spiritually repugnant to her. Nor a man to a woman. A man who has only a narrow spiritual life, one who appreciates only a small number of ethical ideals can become fanatically extreme in his devotion to one of them, to his own hurt, and to the hurt of society. He can be compared to a man who loves music, but knows how to play only a few pieces. One of those pieces tends to become his chief and unchallenged favorite. He plays it almost incessantly. But the greater the musician a man is, the more musical numbers he can play, the more varied will be his musical expression. He will never say that any one piece of music is greater than all others in all respects, and for all purposes. Thus the Rabbis were acquainted more intimately than any other men, with a larger variety of ethical principles.

2. The importance of rain. 

The chief reward promised in this third paragraph of the Shema is that "the rain will come in its season, the former rain, and the latter rain." In the original Hebrew of this passage, there are three different words for rain: 

1. Matar-heavy rain. 2. Yoreh-former or early season rain. 3. Malkosh-latter rains. Palestine has only two seasons: a rainy season and a dry season. The rain is especially appreciated in Palestine because it is one of the few sections in that part of the world in which rain falls. 

The Bible points to that as one of the great virtues of 

Palestine. "For the land whither thou goest in to possess it, is not as the land of Egypt, from whence ye came out, where thou didst sow thy seed, and didst water it with thy feet, as a garden of herbs; but the land whither ye go over to possess it, is a land of hills and valleys, and drinketh water as the rain of heaven cometh down." (Deut. 11, vs. 10-11). Egypt depends upon the overflow of the Nile

River, and most of the fields had to be watered through irrigation. Very often that irrigation consisted of nothing more than carrying the water in some container, from the river to the fields. That is what the Bible refers to when it says that Egyptian fields were watered "with the foot."

To the south and east of Palestine there are vast deserts. 

Rain-fall in Palestine begins about the middle of September and ends about the middle of April. The "former or early season rain" prepares the ground for plowing and planting. The "heavy rain" falls during the middle of the rainy season while the crops are growing. The "latter rain" is usually very gentle rain, merely helping to complete the full maturing of the crop. 

The Midrash speaks of the rain as being a blessing which includes within it all the other blessings since it is indispensable for the growth of our food supply. In comment-

ing on this blessing of rain, the Rabbis quote the Biblical verse which says that even "a King is servant to the field" (Ecc. 5, vs. 8) because even though a king may have all available gold and silver, he can subsist only on that which "cometh out of the field."

Modern city-bred people who ordinarily associate their food supply with the grocery store should frequently be reminded of the truth that in the last analysis their welfare depends upon what the fields produce. That, in turn, depends largely upon the rain, and the rain is as yet not under man's control. 

3. Rain in its season.

The Midrash adds an interesting comment on the fact that the rain is promised in its season, because the amount of rainfall a country gets is not the only thing which is important. The distribution of the rain is of equal significance. It may come too early or too late in the season or may fall upon the mountains and forest rather than on the planted fields. Hence, the promise is "rain in its season." 

THE FOURTH PARAGRAPH OF THE SHEMA 

The fourth paragraph of the Shema starting with 

Vayomer (p. 41) and ending with Emes (p. 42) is found in numbers Ch. 16, vs. 37-41. In the Chumash it comes before the other paragraphs of the Shema, but in the Siddur it is the last paragraph. The Rabbis naturally discuss the reason why the order of the paragraphs in the Siddur differs from the order in the Chumash. Among the answers they suggest is the following one. The main new element introduced by the fourth paragraph of the Shema is the law of the Tzitzis. This Tzitzis law, however, applies only to garments worn during the day when the Tzitzis could be seen, and not to garments worn at night.

Hence this paragraph does not have the same constant applicability that the preceding paragraphs have. It is, therefore, placed at the end of the Shema. There is good reason to believe that in early times this paragraph was not recited during the reading of the Shema at the Maariv service. Today we recite it at the Maariv as well as at the 

Shacharis services.

While the mitzvah of Tzitzis is the main new theme which this paragraph adds to the general content of the Shema, it is exceptionally rich in other very many significant ideas. The Midrash is replete with Rabbinic interpretations of these few sentences. Before proceeding with the discussion of the mitzvah of Tzitzis itself, it is well, therefore, that we note some of these Midrashic comments. 

1. FIVE IMPORTANT IDEAS CONTAINED IN THIS PARAGRAPH 

The Midrash lists at least five important ideas or teachings stressed in this short concluding paragraph of the Shema. a. The mitzvah of Tzitzis-as is specifically stated: "Speak unto the children of Israel, bid them that they make them throughout their generations fringes in the corners of their garments." We will discuss that at fuller length later. b. Recalling the emancipation from Egypt-"I am the 

Lord your God Who brought you out of the land of

Egypt." This will be more fully dsicussed later in this section.

C. Our duty to accept the yoke of the commandments --that is, to obey the laws of the Torah, as is said “And it shall be unto you for a fringe, that ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of the Lord, and do them."

The first paragraph of the Shema was the declaration of readiness "to accept the yoke of the Malkut Shamayim, the Kingdom of God." But our acceptance of the yoke of the Kingdom of God without performing the Mitzvos, is a meaningless gesture. d. A warning especially against the sins committed because of lack of mastery over our sexual impulses.-That is found in the admonition "that ye go not about after your own eyes." It is by means of our eyes that we are at first attracted to a member of the opposite sex. That is how Samson was first drawn to the Philistine girl whom he desired to marry. Samson's parents were unhappy about his actions and they said to him: "Is there never a woman among the daughters of thy brethren, or among all thy people, that thou goest to take a wife of the Philistines?" 

And Samson said unto his father, "Get her for me; for she is pleasant in my eyes." (Judges 14, vs. 3). Samson chose the Philistine woman not because of any particular virtues of character she had, but merely because he followed "his eyes.” He was not the first person nor the last one to make serious mistakes in his sex life because he permitted his "eyes" alone to determine his actions. e. A warning against irreligion or idolatry,—is contained in the words that "ye go not after your own hearts ... after which ye go astray." To our ancestors, the heart was the seat of thought, and the Psalmist writes "The fool hath said in his heart: "There is no God.'" (Ps. 14, vs. 1).

It is through the questioning and the doubts and false arguments of the "heart" that men are frequently betrayed into atheism, or into following some other religion. Against these dangerous pitfalls of thought, this paragraph warns us to be on guard.

2. ON DOING ALL THE COMMANDMENTS 

The paragraph states: "And it (the Tzitzis) shall be unto you for a fringe, that ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of the Lord, and do them."

This statement, involves a matter which is especially significant in our time and place. It apparently commands every Jew to obey all of the commandments. How often have we heard Jews arguing that they are going to be either complete Jews or nothing at all. As they put it, they do not want to be "hypocrites," by observing some of the laws and neglecting others. "Is it not hypocritical," they will ask, "to obey the laws of kashruth when I have to work on the Sabbath in order to support myself?" Or "Why should I refrain from eating forbidden food since

I must use non-kosher dishes anyhow, when I eat in a public restaurant?" Such and similar questions are frequenly heard in any Jewish gathering in which Jewish observances are a subject of discussion. 

While the Rabbis could not have had in mind the specific situations we face in America today, they too were confronted with the problem of the tremendous difficulty involved in trying to observe all of the commandments of the Torah. They freely admit that no individual, no matter how pious he may be, could fulfill all the requirements of the laws of piety and morality included in the Torah. 

Apparently there were many in their day also who argued "If you cannot obey all the laws, there is no point in obeying any. It is either all or nothing." It is upon this attitude of mind which the Midrash comments in the following passage. "Thou shalt not say I cannot obey all the laws of the Torah, for 'the measure thereof is greater than the whole earth' (Job II, vs. 9). It may be compared to a king who had a bottomless pit to fill. He ordered an

officer to hire laborers to fill the pit. The foolish laborer would look into the pit and say, 'How can I ever fill it?' (and depart). But the wise laborer would say, 'What difference does that make to me? I am a day laborer. I am happy that I have found work.' Thus also does the Lord say to every man-'What difference does it make to you whether you ever fill the pit completely (or observe all the laws of the Torah completely). You are a day laborer and receive your reward for whatever work you accomplish during the day,'' i.e., during your life which in God's eyes is but a day.

One of the great Tanaim expressed the same thought somewhat more simply when he urged his pupils to remember that "It is not incumbent upon you to finish the work. 

But the fact that you cannot finish it does not mean that you have a right, much less an obligation, to give it up altogether." (p. 190 in Siddur).

It is incumbent upon every Jew to observe as many of the mitzvos as he can possibly observe. If circumstances beyond his control compel him to violate some of the mitzvos, he should not consider himself a hypocrite if he nevertheless continues to observe those which it is possible for him to observe. As one of the Rabbis remarks rather pointedly: "Ecclesiastes says 'Be not wicked overmuch' (Ecc. 7; 17). Does the Bible mean to imply that being wicked overmuch is to be condemned, but being moderately wicked is acceptable? Not at all. The Bible means merely to tell us that a man should not say since I have already eaten garlic and hence have an unpleasant breath, I might as well continue to eat more of it."

As a matter of fact many of the laws of the Torah can not be observed today, because their observance depends upon our living in Palestine, and upon the existence of the Temple. After the destruction of the Temple and the exile

from Palestine, there were many Jews, who taking the position of "all or nothing," abandoned Judaism. But those who observed as many of the laws of the Torah as they possibly could, preserved our people and our religion to this day. The same is true for us of this generation as well. 

We cannot all be martyrs. Not all of us are even capable of great sacrifices. But we can all make as honest an effort as our spiritual and intellectual powers enable us to make, to observe the teachings of our religion. What we observe is all to the good, both for ourselves and for our people. It is obviously unwise and unreasonable to eliminate even that amount of good because we cannot add more to it. 

3. THE TRUE MEANING OF FREEDOM 

This paragraph, after commanding us "to remember the laws and to do them" reminds us that "I am the Lord your God who brought you forth out of the land of Egypt to be your God." The Midrash asks-Why should the two ideas be next to one another? How are they related?

The answer is a significant one, because it involves the idea of freedom. The Midrash states that "When God redeemed the children of Israel from Egypt, He did not redeem them merely to be 'free.' He redeemed them so that they may be His 'slaves.' If, when He commands them to do something, they refuse, He may remind them that it was He Who redeemed them for Egyptian slavery, and they are His."

At this point in our studies, we surely no longer need to explain that the Rabbis did not view God as a whimsical tyrant, who like a human tyrant, enjoyed having slaves. 

We have here rather a Rabbinic interpretation of what freedom from Egyptian slavery really was to mean for the 

Jew. Freedom does not mean merely doing what you

please and when you please to do it. That is the immature child's opinion of freedom. The young child considers the parent an unreasonable taskmaster if the parent insists that the child take precautions to preserve his health, or that he develop decent manners, or acquire learning. To the young child the ideal parent would be one who would let him do what he pleases and when he pleases. But all human experience teaches that such a parent is the child's worst enemy. Mature and thoughtful human beings know that true freedom is possible only when we subject ourselves to laws. The only reason that twenty-two million automobiles can be driven on the highways of America is because the vast majority of drivers obey traffic regulations. It is those who want to be "free" and follow their own whims who bring on catastrophe. Science is making us free men, enabling us to fly, to have radios, and numberless other comforts, because it teaches us the laws of the world in which we live. Knowing those laws, we know what to obey, and therefore to live more happily. Freedom does not imply anarchy, it implies orderliness, and orderliness means obedience to laws. The finer, the better, the truer the laws are, to which a man makes himself "a slave," the freer that man is.

In Egypt the Israelites were slaves to other human beings. They had to observe the commandments of their 

Egyptian taskmasters. These Egyptian commands were inspired by hate, greed, pride, selfishness. Any man obeying commands inspired by such emotions is indeed a slave, whether it is his own pride and selfishness which he obeys or that of some other human being. But as soon as the 

Jews left Egypt, they were taken to Mt. Sinai and made 

God's "slaves." He gave them His commandments of justice, piety, mercy and holiness. Only he who is "a slave" to Him and His laws is truly free.

In the Ethics of the Fathers the same truth is taught in the following words "Only he is free who busies himself with (studying and observing) the Torah." (p. 205 in Siddur). 

4. THE MITZVAH OF TZITZIS 

"And the Lord spoke unto Moses saying-Speak unto the children of Israel and bid them that they make them throughout their generations fringes in the corner of their garments, and that they put with the fringe of each corner a thread of blue."

The Midrash opens its discussion on the Mitzvah of Tzitzis by remarking: "God gave Israel an opportunity to fulfill His will in practically every act which they had to perform. Each act has some Mitzvah associated with it. 

When an Israelite plowed his field he had to remember not to harness to his plow an ox and an ass together. When he harvested his field he was commanded to leave the 'corners of the field,' and other parts of the crop for the poor. When he threshed his corn he had to obey the 

Mitzvah not to muzzle the ox which did the treading, etc., etc. And when he put on his garment he had to see to it that the garment in some way also was dedicated to God's service, by putting the Tzitzis at its four corners."

The Tzitzis also serve as reminders of the need for repentance. That is one of the reasons the Rabbis give for the wearing of the Tzitzis on Sabbath and Holidays, though Tefillin are not worn on these days. According to the narrative in the Bible (Num. 15, vs. 32-37) the law of Tzitzis was given after a man was found violating the 

Sabbath. "On a weekday with Tefillin on his head and arm, he would have seen and repented of his deeds; now without Tefillin this man profaned the Sabbath." God thereupon instructed Moses to explain to Israel regarding

Tzitzis, "which they are to wear on holy days and Sabbaths also."

ARE THERE IMPORTANT AND UNIMPORTANT MITZVOS? 

The mitzvah of Tzitzis is one which is easy to perform. 

Apparently it is ethically far less significant than the Mitzvah of accepting "The yoke of the Kingdom of 

Heaven." "Why then," the Midrash asks, “were these two mitzvos, one so grave and significant, and the other so 'light,' placed together in the Shema?" The answer given by the Midrash contains another very important teaching of our Rabbis. "This is done," the Midrash says, “so that you may learn that the light mitzvah is like the grave one, and the grave one like the light one." 

What do the Rabbis mean by a "light" mitzvah. They themselves never explain or define the term. They merely speak of one mitzvah as being "light" and another as being "grave." And there is good reason for their silence about the exact meaning of the words "light" and "grave.”

In the first place, a mitzvah which might appear "light" to one Rabbi, often appeared "grave" to another. Moreover, the same mitzvah may be viewed by the same person as being "light" under certain circumstances, but very "grave" under other conditions. Stated in general terms a mitzvah could be described as "light" if- 

1. Its performance required only very little expenditure of energy, time, or money. 

2. The punishment prescribed for its transgression was not very serious. 

3. The effect that its observance had on other fellowmen or on ourselves was not very marked, or important.

Strange as it may seem, we have one Midrashic passage in

which even the mitzvah of "Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself," is spoken of as a "light" mitzvah. The reason is quite obvious. The Midrash is there contrasting the commandment of "Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself" (Lev. 19, 18) with the commandment "Thou shalt not hate thy brother in thy heart" (Lev. 19, 17) and "Thou shalt not take vengeance nor bear a grudge.” (Lev. 

19, 18). He who violates these last two commandments, the Rabbis say, will tend also to violate the commandment "That your brother shall live with you" (Lev. 25, 36) so that the end will be that "he will hate his neighbor and lie in wait for him, and rise up against him, and smite him mortally that he die." (Deut. 19,11). Thus by violating the "light" mitzvah to "love thy neighbor," one may finally be led to violate the very "grave" mitzvah "to let thy brother live with you.' د, Surely the mitzvah to "love thy neighbor" would not ordinarily be considered a "light" mitzvah. The great teacher Hillel spoke of it as being "the whole of the 

Torah." But in comparison with the very grave sin of murder, the sin of not loving your neighbor may be considered light.

Why is this subject of importance to us? For two reasons. It is quite obvious that the Rabbis did not consider all the commandments of the Torah of equal ethical significance under all conditions and circumstances. While they were as careful in observing the mitzvah to wash their hands before sitting down to a meal, as the mitzvah "Thou shalt not steal," they undoubtedly recognized that one of those mitzvahs was "graver," more significant for the larger welfare of society than the other. They would not condemn a man who did not wash his hands and make the appropriate blessing before eating, with the same vehemence that they would condemn a thief. On the

other hand, the Rabbis, having an unequalled genius for ethical and moral matters, were careful not to classify certain mitzvos as being always "light" and others as being always "grave." They knew that in moral and ethical matters what may be a light mitzvah on one occasion, may be a very grave one under other circumstances. Moreover the consequences of observing a "light" mitzvah may at times be as significant as those resulting from observing a "grave" mitzvah. A few illustrations will perhaps explain our thought.

Let us take the very simple matter of washing the hands before eating. The religious person washes his hands before sitting down to a meal not merely to be physically clean. 

His meal is not intended to be merely for the sake of filling his stomach. He thinks his table as "the table which is before the Lord." (Ezek. 41, 22, see also p. 191, para. 4 in Siddur). He washes his hands as a symbol of spiritual cleanliness. He wants to be clean spiritually as well as physically, when partaking of a meal at a "table before the Lord." This act of washing the hands is thus united with a consciousness of our being in the presence of God even when we eat, for our food is from His hands, and we eat in order that we may live well in His eyes. The washing of the hands, moreover, goes back to other very important historic and religious circumstances. The priests who served in the Temple in Jerusalem were not permitted to enter the Temple area or to eat of any of the offerings or the tithes given them unless they were "clean" or "Levitically pure." Among other things a priest had to do to become "clean" was to wash his whole body in water. 

Today every Jew considers himself to be equal to the priest of old. The pious Jew likes to feel that every meal he eats is like the meal that was eaten by the priest in the Temple, and he wants to be as pure as the priest was for

his meal. The washing of the hands is a symbol of the washing of the whole body before partaking of the “meal before the Lord."

When one becomes conscious of these and other religious ideas associated with the washing of the hands, this mitzvah no longer appears very "light" to him. Nevertheless, one who disregards this mitzvah because of ignorance, or because of preoccupation with other matters, or for some similar reason, can not be condemned as a grave sinner. But he who disregards this mitzvah because he does not believe in God, and has no feeling of gratitude in his heart to God for his meal, nor a desire to be spiritually as well as physically clean before the Lord, is committing a grave sin by his refusal to wash his hands before eating. Obviously the quality of an act often depends very largely upon the spirit with which it is done. The spirit often makes the act "light," or "grave."

Thus also the mitzvah of Tzitzis is spoken of by the 

Rabbis as a "light" mitzvah. But the Midrash goes on to tell how a man who was about to commit a very grave sin suddenly saw his Tzitzis and was reminded by them, at a very critical moment in his moral life, of his obligation as a Jew, and as a believer in the Torah. That sharp reminder at that critical moment brought him to his senses and he restrained himself from sinning. Young people away from home are thus frequently helped to refrain from doing foolish or wicked things by the picture of their parents staring at them and admonishing them, so to speak, not to do anything to disgrace themselves and their dear ones. How many times have tragedies been averted because of the simple act of having a parent's picture always "looking" at a child wherever he may be. 

There are moreover, ever so many "small," apparently inconsequential, acts of kindness and decency that lead to

very significant results later. Speaking a kind word to a stranger, giving a seat to a lady in the car, returning a lost object may establish a friendship of inestimable value. 

Disobeying traffic regulations is considered by most people but a "light" transgression. The punishment which the law provides for it is also very light. But how many deaths occur annually because some "wise" man thinks it is foolish not to pass on a curve, or to stay within the speed limit. Hence while each one of us cannot help but make distinction between important and unimportant mitzvos, let us not lightly dismiss those which we may at any one time consider unimportant. Just as the real artist does not consider any line in his drawing, even the slightest, as unimportant, and just as the poet gives grave consideration to every word he includes in his poem, so the religious person, striving always to live as moral and spiritual a life as possible, gives due consideration to every act he performs.

Thus also, a Jew who wants to do all in his power to live a full Jewish life, and to preserve all the memories and all the treasures of his people will give serious thought to every law and custom and tradition of his people. He will dismiss none of them lightly or frivolously. He will always remember the admonition of our great leader of the second century, Rabbi Jehuda the Prince. He used to say to his pupils, "Be heedful of the light precept as of the grave one, for thou knowest not the grant of reward for each precept." (p. 186, ch. II, para. 1). Children do not know what a great conflagration the small flame of a match may start. Hence they handle it carelessly. Only he who is ethically a child will carelessly disregard the small, moral flame inherent in the so-called "light" mitzvos, of the Jewish Sabbath, Festival, diet, and prayer, and will thus extinguish it before it has an/opportunity to kindle

a larger spiritual light in his life, as a human being and as a Jew.

HOW TZITZIS ARE MADE 

The Tzitzis were originally made of wool and dyed a kind of blue called by the Torah Tcheles. Since we no longer know the exact shade of that color nor how that particular dye was prepared, our Tzitzis are made either of plain white wool, or silk.

Four threads are used at each corner of the garment, and when doubled, these four become eight strands. One thread which is longer than the others, is wound around the rest. The knots and windings are so arranged that together with the numerical value of the word Tzitzis, they symbolize the 613 commandments of Judaism, all of which are to be recalled when looking at the Tzitzis. The Hebrew term for the 613 commandments is one which every well informed Jew should know. It is Taryag Mitzvos. Each 

Hebrew letter has a numerical value and the values of the Hebrew letters making up the word Taryag add up to 613.

CHANGES IN THE OBSERVANCE OF THE MITZVAH OF TZITZIS 

Originally the Tzitzis were worn on the outer garment, so that they were seen at all times by the wearer and by others. Their purpose is clearly stated in the Bible: "That ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of the Lord, and do them." But as the conditions under which the Jews lived changed, it became increasingly difficult for Jews to observe this commandment literally.

It identified them too obviously to their enemies, and often made them the objects of mockery and attack. While there always were, and there are today Jews staunch enough to brave this mockery and danger, the majority

could not be expected to do so. Hence the Rabbis interpreted the law in such a manner as to make it possible for Jews to observe it without exposing themselves to its possible discomforts.

The Rabbis noted that the Torah specifically states that the garment is to have "four corners." Hence garments not having the four corners are not subject to this law. 

Since our outer garments now usually do not have four corners, Jews are not obligated to place Tzitzis on them. 

However, in order to fulfill this commandment, every 

Jew should have at least two "four cornered" garments in which these Tzitzis are placed. One of these garments is called the Arba Kanfos—meaning the "four corners." It is sometimes also called the small Talis. This simple, small, four cornered garment is worn underneath the outer shirt but not next to the body. It is put on every morning together with the rest of one's clothes, and is obligatory for men only. The second such garment is the Talis which all men above the Bar Mitzvah age wear every day of the year when reciting their morning prayers. The Talis is not used at the evening services because the Bible says of the Tzitzis that "ye are to look upon them." Since the evening service is to start after sunset, there is no natural illumination and the Tzitzis cannot therefore ordinarily be seen. The only exception to this practice is the Kol 

Nidre Service with which the observance of Yom Kippur starts. But Kol Nidre is to be recited before sunset. Hence the Talis is used. In addition, the use of the Talis adds to the sanctity and solemnity of the day.

That, then, is the origin of the Talis. It was the outer garment of our forefathers.

To a Jew today who knows the history of the Talis, putting it on before prayer is more than an act of preparation for the recital of the prayers themselves. The Talis

transports him, in memory, to his earliest ancestors. It is another one of the living bonds uniting him with the hundred generations who preceded him. Paraphrasing one of the great passages in Lincoln's first inaugural address we can in truth say that the Talis, like the numerous other Mitzvos of Jewish life, rouses in every sensitive Jew one of those "mystic chords of memory stretching from every Synagogue and every Jew who ever stood before his God in prayer, to every Jewish heart all over this wide world, and swells the chorus of the great universal Jewish brotherhood."

All great people treasure these sacred memories of their past and pride themselves in the antiquity of their national ways of life. To discard the Talis is to bespeak a woeful lack of historic memory and spiritual sensitivity. 

THE VARIOUS ATTITUDES TOWARDS CHANGE IN JEWISH MITZVOS 

The history of the Talis may well be used to illustrate three prevalent attitudes towards Jewish mitzvos. The two extreme positions are easily stated and understood. The one extreme represented today by the unflinching orthodox Jew, is that no Mitzvah is to be changed or modified in any way. The final form was given to all mitzvos by the 

Torah and the Rabbinic interpretation of it as recorded in the Shulchan Aruch. Any variation from these interpretations is wrong. Hence some of these Jews to this day will wear Tzitzis which are easily visible, even though they may not be in the actual outer garment. The other extreme is that taken up by the radical reform Jews who argue that any custom which distinguishes us from our neighbors should be discarded. Hence they have discarded the Talis altogether. In consistently reform Jewish 

Congregations, no one wears a Talis at any time.

The most prevalent Jewish attitude has been at variance with both of these extremes. Generally stated it might be described somewhat as follows. No mitzvah should ever be completely discarded unless it cannot possibly be observed under any conditions or circumstances, or unless its observance is definitely at variance with clear-cut present-day moral and ethical standards. This approach would, for example, not want to see the re-introduction of animal sacrifice in any presently conceivable form as a method of divine worship, even though to our ancestors, animal sacrifice was an essential element of worship. However, this attitude opposes vigorously the elimination from Jewish life of the Mitzvah of Tzitzis, or dietary laws, or the hat, or the Succah, or any of the mitzvos as long as we can continue to find some way in which we can meaningfully observe them. There is difference of opinion regarding the extent to which any one of the mitzvos can be observed in our day. But any Jew who honestly attempts to observe the mitzvos to the maximum which the conditions of his life will allow, belongs to this group of Jews. In our own day and country this middle group is often called the conservative Jewish group. Its highest spiritual and cultural representative in the world today is the Jewish Theological Seminary of America. This is not the only principle which guides this group or determines its character. But this brief discussion may help us somewhat to arrive at an understanding and an appreciation of what this group is seeking to do in Jewish life. 

THE DANGER TO BE AVOIDED IN THE OBSERVANCE OF SUCH MITZVOS AS TEFILLIN, MEZUZAH AND TZITZIS 

We noted above that the Talis, with which the mitzvah of Tzitzis is today most completely identified, in itself has significance as a garment of prayer and a living bond of

union between us and our forefathers. But in accordance with the very specific statement in the Bible, the Tzitzis were originally intended to serve as a reminder of truths and teachings greater than themselves. The Tzitzis were to turn the attention of the Jew to the Torah and its commandments. To a large extent the Tefillin and the 

Mezuzah were also to point to principles beyond themselves. These three Mitzvos resemble therefore things which in other aspects of life are designated as symbols. 

Any one particular American flag is in and by itself nothing more than a piece of cloth, having some orderly arranged colors on it. It derives its significance from the historic memories and the living realities for which it stands. Such symbols are the indispensable precious possessions of every people and every individual. The symbol is a sort of short hand for memory, since at times the slightest memento may be the spark rousing an infinite number of sacred and treasured associations. Nations have flags, old cannons, parts of ships, pieces of ground, which they treasure. All religions are rich with such symbols. Individuals have coins, books, letters, pictures, and what not, to serve the same purpose.

But the spiritual danger involved in every symbol is that we frequently dissociate it from the memories, realities, or ideas which it is to bring to our minds and ascribe power or effectiveness to the symbol itself. In personal life it will often take on some such form as the following. A boy will be presented with a watch by his father. He will at first treasure the watch in and by itself as a worthwhile thing. 

As years pass and his path leads him away from his father and home, the watch will be precious to him because it recalls all the pleasant associations with his father, as well as the principles which his father tried to teach him. 

But if that watch will have been with him on occasions

when happy or fortunate events took place, he may begin to associate that watch with "good luck." Before long, that watch becomes a "charm," not an object rousing memories but possessing some secret supernatural power to bring good luck. All religious objects have, with some people, at one time or another, deteriorated into charms. 

Strange as it may seem, it is not the truly pious and learned religious individual who turns the religious object into a charm. Very often it is the otherwise non-pious and nonreligious individual. Jews who wouldn't think of putting a Mezuzah on the door of their homes will carry a Mezuzah in their pockets or on a chain around their necks, as a "charm." Simple pious souls will think of the Mezuzah on the door as a protection against evil spirits. We cannot guard ourselves too carefully against all such perversions of the uses and the purposes of these Mitzvos. 

Nevertheless, from the fact that Mitzvos may be misused it does not follow that they should be discarded. As we have said, no people, no religion, and no individual can get along without mitzvos of this kind. The more we love a thing, the more objects we want around us to remind us of it. Despite the danger involved, the existence of these mitzvos has been helpful and beneficent. 

THE THREE-FOLD CHORD 

Rabbi Eleazar ben Jacob spoke the truth when he taught: "He who places the Mezuzah on his door, the

Tefillin upon his head and arm, and the Tzitzis on his garments, the chances are very strongly in his favor, that he will not sin." He will not forget his obligations toward

God, toward man, and toward Israel—"for did not King Solomon write that 'the three-fold chord is not easily broken'?" (Ecc. 4, vs. 12).

THE CHAPTER IV God-The Redeemer of Israel HE FOURTH PART OF THE SHEMA SECTION OF THE 

morning services starts with the words "True and Trustworthy" on p. 42, and ends with the words "the Redeemer of Israel" on p. 44. This section is called "The Redemption," because the largest portion of it is devoted to meditations on God as Redeemer.

A passage in the Jerusalem Talmud gives us a very clear picture of how this fourth part of the Shema Section gradually developed. The following discussions and decisions are there recorded. "He who recites the Shema in the morning must recall the emancipation from Egypt in the 'Emes Veyatziv' prayer. Rabbi Judah the Prince (d. about 

190 C.E.) was of the opinion that this prayer should also make mention of the slaying of the Egyptian first born, and the crossing of the Red Sea. Rabbi Joshua taught, that in addition to all of these matters the prayer should include the words "The rock of Israel and his Redeemer." 

The part was originally probably all one paragraph, but as we now have it in our Siddur it has four paragraphs. 

Its contents are in accordance with Rabbi Joshua's opinion. 

It is quite obvious especially from the frequent use of the word Emes-"It is true"-that this concluding section of the Shema was intended to be, and is, a commentary on the Shema itself. The Rabbis wanted to make sure that every Jew who recited the Shema should know exactly what that recitation implied. They wanted to be certain that the Jew who said "Hear O Israel," etc., should know that by saying that he accepted the "Yoke of the Kingdom of Heaven," and that when he recited "And it shall come 144

to pass" that he knew he was thus accepting the "yoke of the Mitzvos." Originally, this part apparently consisted of the present first two paragraphs only. Then the meditations on God as Redeemer of Israel were elaborated. 

These meditations centering around reminiscences of the emancipation from Egyptian bondage became the main new contribution which this part makes to our Tefilos. 

Following are the four paragraphs of this section: 

I. ("True and Trustworthy,"-until "Israel Thy servants" p. 42).

This short paragraph opens with sixteen adjectives affirming our unalterable and unshakable acceptance of the Kingdom of Heaven. We say, "It is true that the Lord of the Universe is our King . . . and His throne is established, and His Kingdom and Faithfulness are everlasting." This reaffirmation corresponds to the main idea of the first two paragraphs of the Shema. The series of sixteen adjectives with which this paragraph opens makes rather strange reading for us. We are not accustomed to such a multiplicity of emphasis. This apparently over abundant use of adjectives is an expression both of the oriental predilection for exaggeration, and the lover's interminable praise of the beloved object. Both of these characteristics are reflected in our ancestor's attitude towards God and the Torah. Nor can one say which of the two characteristics was the dominant one. Our fathers never tired of adding adjective upon adjective when speaking of God and His Torah, not only because they were orientals and therefore influenced by the literary style of their environment, but primarily because their hearts were literally brimful and overflowing with a fervid and glowing love for God and 

Torah. Oriental inclination towards exaggeration can by itself never explain such a song of praise for the Torah as

that found in the 119th chapter of Psalms. And just as it is true that only he who himself experiences love can appreciate a lover's praise of his beloved, thus also can only those who themselves love God and the Torah fully appreciate our ancestors' attitude towards them as expressed in the Siddur and the Bible.

This paragraph also reaffirms our faith in the eternity of His words, of His laws and commandments, and it declares them to be obligatory "upon us," "upon our children ... and upon all generations of Israel." This therefore is equivalent to our "acceptance of the yoke of the mitzvos," as we are required to do when we read the third paragraph of the Shema. 

II. "For our ancestors" until, "There is no God beside Thee." (p. 42, p. 43).

The fourth paragraph of the Shema referred to the fact that God redeemed Israel from Egyptian bondage. This paragraph carries the thought further. God is the redeemer not only of our fathers, He is also "our redeemer, our creator, the rock of our salvation." 

III. "The help of our fathers" until, “The Lord will reign unto all eternity." (p. 43, p. 44).

This paragraph is a very excellent illustration of a prayer of historic reminiscence. Its central theme is meditation on the unprecedented event which occurred when Israel was emancipated from bondage in Egypt. In the course of time this event took on ever increasing significance. 

A. THE HISTORIC SIGNIFICANCE OF THE EMANCIPATION FROM EGYPT 

1. For the Jews.

For the Jews, the emancipation from Egypt represents the real beginning of their history as a nation. The posi-

tion which that event occupies in Jewish history is the same as the position occupied by the Revolutionary War in 

American History. Without the Revolutionary War the colonies would probably never have become the American Nation. Without the emancipation from Egypt the Israelites would never have become the Jewish people. We would have long disappeared. The Passover Hagadah states that fact very emphatically. It says "And had not the Holy one, blessed be He, brought us forth out of the land of Egypt, we would still be slaves to Pharaoh in Egypt." Hence the destruction of the Egyptian people would have included with it our destruction also. One who regrets the existence of the American Nation can certainly have no kind feelings towards the Revolutionary War. And one who regrets the existence of Israel will not gratefully recall the emancipation from Egypt. But one who loves his people and desires its continuous existence into the future will always recall with deep gratitude and dignified pride the events and the forces which made it possible for his people to come into being. 

2. The significance of our emancipation from Egypt for the world at large.

In the history of mankind, the emancipation of the Jews from Egypt is the first recorded instance of a slave people being freed from the yoke of its oppressors. In the memory of the human race, such a thing had never happened before. Hence the enslaved, the persecuted, the oppressed people of the world, who accepted the Bible as the chief source of their religious and ethical ideals saw in the emancipation of the Jews a hope and an example for themselves. Heretofore, all religions taught submission to the yoke of the master. The Bible was the first religious doctrine known to the Western world which had at its very

center, a story of revolt against a human tyrant; an inspiring account of how God punished the tyrant for his cruelty to the slaves, and then freed the slaves from his domination. 

In the history of the English people there were two great revolutions against tyranny and despotism. The first was led by the great poet Milton and the Puritan military genius, Oliver Cromwell. They revolted against the usurpation of power by King Charles I, in the seventeenth century. The second was our own American Revolution under the leadership of Washington, Jefferson, Franklin and others, against the tyranny of George III. It is a well-known historic fact that in the case of both of these revolutions, the emancipation of the Jews from Egypt served as one of the greatest sources of inspiration and encouragement to the revolutionaries. The Bible was the book best known, and most widely read by the Englishmen of those two generations. And in the mind of the average Englishman, as well as in the sermons, writings and speeches of those days the tyrannical king was the Pharoah of his day, and the leaders of the revolution were 'Moses and Aaron.' Like them, the leaders of the English revolution felt that they were not merely defying constituted authority, but were carrying out God's will in overthrowing a human tyrant.

This attitude became a permanent part of the American tradition when the Seal of the United States was drawn up by Benjamin Franklin and Thomas Jefferson. Pictured on the Seal are Pharoah, Moses, the Israelites and the divided waters of the Red Sea. The motto on the Seal reads: "Rebellion to Tyrants is Obedience to God."

To the Negro slaves of America, the story of the emancipation of the Jews from Egyptian bondage has also always been a source of infinite encouragement. No Negro

spiritual is better known than "Let My People Go." It speaks of Moses and Pharaoh and Israel, but to the heart of every Negro it brought and brings the message of his own emancipation. Hence the emancipation of the Israelites from Egypt is the first great act in the heroic drama of man's struggle against tyranny and for human freedom. 

B. THE RELIGIOUS SIGNIFICANCE OF THE EMANCIPA- TION FROM EGYPT 

1. Does Judaism teach dependence upon miracles?

The story of the emancipation of the Israelites from Egypt as it is recorded in the Bible and as it is referred to in our Siddur, has nothing to tell about anything which the Israelites themselves did in order to gain their freedom. 

God only was active. He slew the Egyptians, and He redeemed the slaves. The Israelites themselves were merely the recipients of His goodness. Yet this complete inactivity on the part of the Israelites seems inconceivable to us. It may be true that they fought no pitched battles. They did not win their freedom by defeating the Egyptian army. But they must have done something. We know that they did have Moses and Aaron as their leaders to represent them before Pharaoh. We know that they had to take steps to prepare themselves to leave the country. The 

Bible records that the Israelites were commanded to offer a sacrifice, in defiance of the Egyptians, as a symbolic act of their freedom, even before they left Egypt. 

We know, moreover, that it is not in the spirit of our religious teaching to ask human beings to sit with hands folded and expect some miracle by itself to achieve for them what they ought themselves to try to achieve. 

This attitude of Judaism is beautifully illustrated in an imaginary conversation between God and Moses, which is related in the Midrash in connection with the exodus from

Egypt. We are told that when the Israelites had left Egypt and were caught between the Red Sea in front of them, and the Egyptian army in the rear of them, Moses started to pray to the Lord for His help. Whereupon the Lord turned to Moses and said, "Why criest thou to Me? The ocean is in front of them, the Egyptians in the rear of them, and you stand there and pray? Speak unto the children of Israel and let them move forward.” (Ex. 14, vs. 15).

Any number of Rabbinic teachings can be quoted to prove the statement that Judaism does not teach inactivity and dependence upon miraculous help from on high for the attainment of our ideals or goals. It is specifically forbidden "to depend upon a miracle," and to say that if only we pray or study, but do not plow, and sow, harvest and thresh, God will nevertheless supply us with food as He supplies food for the fowl of heaven and the fish of the seas.

The saying "God helps those who help themselves,” is often used by non-religious people when mocking the attitude of the pious and the faithful who pray to God for help or guidance. But in reality, even the most pious may also truthfully say that God helps those who help themselves if he understands what the statement really means. 

God has equipped human beings with five senses, with limbs and muscles, and with a brain, the most remarkable blessing which He bestowed upon any of His creatures. 

He did not give us these blessings of eyes, ears, feet, hands, and the power of thought, for us to discard them, and to depend merely upon the power of prayer. Every one would recognize at once that a pious person who merely engages in prayer while he is drowning, but refuses to use his hands and feet in an effort to save himself by swimming, is one whom our Rabbis describe as "a pious fool." Man's

physical and mental equipment, his eyes and hands, feet and brain are means given him by God to use, and not to disregard. This equipment is one of the means whereby 

God helps man to help himself. 

In this regard also, we can note how Judaism steadfastly avoids the extremes advocated by other groups. On the one hand, Judaism rejects the attitude of the cynical or materialistic non-believer who thinks that because he uses his hands, and eyes, and brain to help himself, he is completely independent of God. On the other hand, Judaism rejects with equal vigor the attitude of those extreme doctrines which would teach that man can do nothing and dare do nothing to help himself whether in illness or in other types of distress, except to pray and to have faith. 

Our Rabbis frequently express the thought that the righteous participate with God in guiding and governing the affairs of the world. The honest judge who uses his best honest judgment in settling a dispute, the charitable individual who responds to the request of the needy, these are "partners of God," His agents in doing His will. In the same way, whenever we use our God-given physical and mental equipment to attain a desirable and worthwhile goal, we are acting, not alone and independent of God, but rather in partnership with God. He alone will not take us to our goal. We alone cannot achieve it. Only when we cooperate with Him, can we have hope for success. In that sense it is indeed correct to say that 

God helps those who help themselves. 

2. Why does the account of the emancipation stress the role of God as the Redeemer, rather than the acts of the Israelites?

If what we said in the preceding pages is true, and anyone acquainted with Rabbinic teaching will readily

recognize its truth, why does not the Bible or the Siddur tell us more about the part the Israelites played in their own emancipation? We cannot possibly know the final answer to that question. There may have been no pitched battles connected with that historic event. Or many details of it may have been forgotten. One thing, however, we can say with certainty, and that is that the Bible and the Siddur are in this instance both interested in emphasizing that God is concerned with the welfare of the oppressed, the enslaved, the disinherited and the wronged among men. 

Notice in how many different ways the prayer repeats that thought, not only concerning God's relations to Israel, but to all men. "Thy habitation is in the heights of the universe, but Thy Judgments and Thy righteousness reach out to the ends of the earth. . . . Let the beloved praise and exalt God, Who is great and to be feared Who humbles the proud and exalts the lowly, Who releases the bound, redeems the humble, helps the poor, and answers 

His people when they cry unto Him." Note that the prayer does not say that God releases the Jewish bound, redeems the Jewish humble, or helps the Jewish poor. Hence because of God's concern for all the humble, the poor, and the oppressed, it is but natural that Jewish religious literature should never refer to God as the patron of the mighty, the rich, or the powerful, though they bring Him more offerings and sacrifices.

That fact in itself was and is of tremendous importance in the development of our religious lives. The pagans and idolaters had hundreds, even thousands of fiods. There were gods for the winds, the fields, and every other aspect of nature. There were gods who defended the kings, but the slaves had no great god who was concerned primarily with their welfare. Yet the slaves were the most numerous group in ancient society. Pagans considered it

below the dignity of a god to be worried about slaves, just as the great poets, painters, and sculptors of antiquity, with the exception of the poets of the Bible, considered it below them to use their talents in describing the life, the doings, and the ideals of a humble man. Hence in stressing 

God's part in the emancipation of the Israelites from Egypt, we meditate not only on His power and might, but above all on His justice and His love; His justice in punishing the oppressor and His love for those suffering oppression. 

C. THE PSYCHOLOGICAL SIGNIFICANCE OF THE REDEMP TION FROM EGYPT 

It was through the prophets, the great religious leaders of Israel from Abraham to Malachi, that God first revealed Himself to man as the God of justice, Who stands at the side of all those to whom justice is due. The emancipation from Egypt was the first striking evidence given to mankind that the affairs of the world are so arranged that slaves can be freed not merely as individuals freed by the will of a kind master, but as a group, and as a matter of inherent right. Just as before the days of Columbus people believed that the world is so constructed that the waters to the west of Europe cannot possibly be crossed, so before the emancipation of the Israelites from Egypt men believed that the world is so created that slaves must always remain slaves, and masters must always remain masters. Columbus proved that the world was so created that the waters of the great dark sea can be crossed. The emancipation from Egypt proved that the world is so created that slaves can be freed. 

But that was not all. Since it was God Himself Who acted in behalf of Israel as the oppressed people in Egypt, and since we believe Him to be the God of all mankind, we believe that He did, does, and will do, for all oppressed,

what He did for enslaved Israel. Hence we believe not only that the enslaved can be redeemed, but that ultimately they should and will be redeemed. That is the basis of our faith that the wrongs which disfigure human life today must eventually be righted, and the oppressed and enslaved must eventually find freedom and equality. 

Hence, meditation on the redemption from Egyptian slavery gave our fathers courage to look with hope towards their own future. Jewish fortunes were never so low as they were in the days of the Egyptian bondage. The outlook for the future was never more disheartening. 

Nevertheless Israel came through the ordeal triumphantly, and under the leadership of Moses entered upon one of the greatest eras in all of human history. If it could happen once, it could happen again. And it can and will happen still again.

The tyranny of the Pharaohs of Egypt mentioned in this prayer serves only as a symbol for all the other tyrannies 

Israel and mankind had to face. The Pharaoh of Egypt was later replaced by a Nebuchadnezzar of Babylonia, an 

Antiochus of Syria, a Titus of Rome, an Attila, a Genghis

Khan, the Inquisition, the Czars, and others. But all of them met the same fate that Pharaoh met, and we are still here. Hence though all of these tyrants caused us and mankind untold suffering, God's justice finally caught up with them. They were overthrown and their peoples disappeared, while we lived on.

It is this undying hope in the final victory of God's justice in this world that daily meditation on the emancipation from Egypt, should keep alive in our hearts as it did in the hearts of our forefathers. (The relationship between this discussion of "God the 

Redeemer" and our discussion on the whole problem of reward and punishment is, we believe, quite obvious. Many

questions which might naturally arise when studying this passage have been touched upon and discussed in our discussion on reward and punishment. It might therefore be well to reread that discussion.)

D. HOW DOES GOD REDEEM? 

It is but natural that many should ask, How does God redeem the oppressed? How can we better understand

His working in the affairs of men? An attempt to answer these questions belongs more appropriately to a discussion of the second bracha of the Amidah, where we read that "God sustains the living, quickens the dead, supports the falling, heals the sick," etc.

E. COMMENTS ON SOME OTHER PASSAGES IN THIS THIRD PARAGRAPH OF THE FOURTH PART OF THE SHEMA SECTION 

1. "In the heights of the universe is Thy habitation and 

Thy judgments and Thy righteousness reach to the farthest ends of the earth." (p. 43). 

Compare this with Isaiah ch. 66, vs. 1, 2. See also 

Job 22, vs. 12-14. Psalms 10, vs. 11, ch. 29, vs. 11, ch. 94, vs. 3-7, Ezek. ch. 8, vs. 12).

This brief statement in the Tefilos touches upon one of the very important principles of religion. There is an attitude of mind which says, “Even if we assume that there is a God Who created and governs the world, we do not believe that He is concerned with the actions of human beings. 

God is too great, too exalted, too far above the petty concerns of mankind, to be in any way associated with them, or bothered about them." At first, this attitude seems to imply a very praiseworthy humility. It seems to say, "I am too insignificant a creature for God to take notice of me." But a moment's reflection will show that

in so far as it applies to daily human activities this attitude is no different from that of the atheist. (See p. 86 above). 

There have been very great religious teachers who took the position that God is concerned not with the welfare of any individual creature but rather with the welfare of the species as a whole. "God," they say, "is concerned with the welfare of mankind, not with the welfare of any one man; with the course of the stars as a whole, but not with the fortunes of any one star; with the fate of the fish species, but not with the fate of any one fish," and so on.

This attitude tends to weaken the sense of personal responsibility for all of one's actions, which the truly pious man feels and must feel. The dominant teaching of Judaism is that God is concerned with every action of any individual, no matter how insignificant that action may appear, or how humble the individual may be. God governs the whole vast universe, but His ordinances also touch upon every little detail of life. This religious teaching is of great importance. Let us therefore try to understand it. 

A child cheating in an examination at school may well ask, "What difference does that make to God? Has He not more important things to attend to?" The same question can be asked by a murderer who is slaying his enemy. "What difference does it make to God if there will be one person less in His Universe?" To be sure it makes no difference to God in the sense that cheating makes a difference in the pupil's mark, or the teacher's attitude, or that murder makes a difference to the murdered man, or to his relatives. But when we say that God is concerned with all of these action, and that "His judgments reach to the furthest ends of the earth," we mean that none of these things go unnoticed by Him and by the laws whereby He governs the universe.

The law of gravitation, for example, is not concerned merely with holding the stars in their courses. That same law affects the destiny of every grain of sand in the universe. No boy throwing a pebble into the air can say, "What difference does it make to the law of gravitation whether this pebble falls down or stays up? Does not the law of gravitation have more important matters with which to concern itself?" Perhaps it does have more important matters to which to attend. But it is powerful and universal enough to attend to the largest, as well as to the minutest object. And when a pebble is thrown into the air God's law of gravitation immediately affects its course. 

That is true of every other great law which governs the physical universe. Neither the smallest nor the largest objects can escape its effects.

The same is true in the moral and spiritual realms. 

These, too, have their laws. A child who cheats, a man who steals, a nation that plunders, the universal law of justice, of truth, and of honesty affects each of them in proportion to the gravity of each situation. God is not concerned if the boy cheats, but His law of honesty registers an effect upon the cheating child, just as certaintly as gravitation registers its effect upon a pebble thrown towards heaven.

God's serenity is not disturbed by a plundering nation but His law of justice will not overlook the evil deed. It is in that sense that we say that though "God's habitation is on high," His laws governing both the ethical as well as the physical aspects of life "reach out to the furthest ends. of the earth.” 

2. "All their first born Thou didst slay... and didst drown the proud." (p. 43).

This passage unfortunately gives the impression that in

our prayers we are rejoicing at the catastrophe which befell our oppressors. Though that would be humanly understandable, and though undoubtedly many of our sorely persecuted brethren derived some satisfaction in contemplating the dire punishment visited upon our tormentors of the past, we nevertheless would not expect that such a fundamentally unethical sentiment should become a fixed part of our daily prayer. As a matter of fact this passage is not intended to stress the element of revenge and of joy at the enemy's discomfiture. The Midrash makes sure to point out that the destruction of the Egyptians was in no way cause for rejoicing. The Lord Himself rebuked the angels who wanted to sing while the Egyptians were drowning. He said to them, "My handiwork is drowning in the sea and ye would sing!"

The purpose of this passage is to emphasize through contrast, the great mercy the Lord showed to Israel on that occasion. Note that every time the punishment visited upon the Egyptians is mentioned, it is followed by the corresponding mercy shown to Israel. Thus the prayer reads, "All their first born Thou didst slay, but Thy first born Thou didst redeem; Thou didst divide the Red Sea, and drown the proud, but Thou madest the beloved to pass through." Hence we have here an illustration of how 

God's justice punished the haughty and cruel Egyptians, while God's love saved and emancipated the enslaved Israelites. 

3. "Thou didst divide the Red Sea."

This passage involves a rather lengthy discourse on the whole question of miracles, their place in Judaism, and the part the faith in them should have in our present day religious outlook. Since in the section of "Songs and 

Praise" (p. 16-36) the song of Moses celebrating the

crossing of the Red Sea (p. 35-36) is included; we will discuss that whole subject more fully when we comment on that song.

We are told that the first act of our redeemed forefathers was to break forth into exultant song recognizing only God as their

IV. O Rock of Israel. (p. 44). 

This short paragraph concludes the fourth part of the Shema section. Its contents and purpose are very clearly stated. It is a simple heartfelt plea for the redemption of 

Israel from its present sad plight. The Siddurim of our fathers noted in connection with this Tefilah especially that it is to be recited "with a most contrite and humble heart, and with all possible spiritual concentration.” "Judah and Israel" are mentioned in the prayer separately because of the historic fact that the Kingdom of Israel was exiled in 722 B.C.E. and the Kingdom of Judah in 586 B.C..E. The Kingdom of Israel consisted of ten out of the twelve tribes of Israel. The exact fate of these ten tribes has been a subject for much controversy among historians and even greater speculation among weavers of legends. During the centuries various explorers announced that they had discovered the "lost ten tribes" in the heart of Africa, in the wilds of the Caucasian mountains, and among the Indian tribes of America. There is in existence a society of Englishmen who believe that the English are

the descendants of these lost ten tribes. All of these speculations have no foundation in historic fact. The most likely explanation of the fate of the ten tribes is that many of them joined their brethren in the kingdom of Judah, while probably a greater number were assimilated with the people among whom they were exiled. But the prayer mentions both of them together as an expression of hope that these lost ten tribes, as all those many Jewish men and women who were lost to us during the centuries of exile and persecution, will return to us, and that all of us will form one united people. The division of the Kingdom of David and Solomon into two kingdoms was one of the great tragedies of Jewish history. The division of the 

Jewish people today into many different and often conflicting groups is also a source of great weakness to us. In praying for the redemption of Judah and Israel we stress our interest in all groups and parties in Israel, and our hope that in a redeemed Jewish people all of these different groups will again be united as one in the service of God. 

It is customary to recite this paragraph while standing.
