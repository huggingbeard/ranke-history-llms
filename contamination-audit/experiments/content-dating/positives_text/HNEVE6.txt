Economics - basic characteristics 

HE purpose of this discussion is to show that notwithstanding a popular contention to the contrary, the economic system which serves the purposes of man is founded upon principles, forces and processes which have basic scientific characteristics. There will be no attempt to formulate a new system of economics to serve as a panacea for all of the inequalities and misfits in the economic and social relations among the people, but rather to develop, if possible, a better understanding of the system which we already have, and to bring out some of the reasons why we have it.

In the dictionaries today there appears to be a confusion with respect to the word 'economics', and a tendency to associate it with the word 'economy'. As a matter of fact, the two words have very little in common.

Economy has a meaning analogous to thrift and frugality. 

Economics is the system or process of production, service, possession, accumulation and exchange through which the people are enabled to procure the means, resources and facilities for physical subsistence, comfort, growth and well-being. Economic activities may function with thrift and frugality, or they may be extravagant and wasteful. No transaction will be an example of 'economy' unless it involves frugality or thrift, and it will be an example of 'economics' if it involves the process of production, service or exchange. Thus it will be seen that the two words are not synonymous to each other.

Apparently much confusion arises also from a failure to recognize the difference between economics and sociology. It will be observed that sociology is grounded essentially in the emotions, opinion and sentiment with respect to human association, which may range from the sublime to the ridiculous, while economics, on the other hand, is based more particularly upon the reaction of natural laws and principles to whatever is done in the matter of production, serv ice, exchange, etc. The moti vating influence or impulse may be sociological or emotional, but the resulting process will be in terms of economics. there is a substantial difference between the operation of a science and the emotional activities of the people. 

There is also an element of science in sociology, if anybody should want to consider that phase of the subject-there are certain natural laws affecting human relations, just as certain other things are affected by natural laws. But a distinction should be maintained between the scientific characteristics of economics and those of sociology -care should be taken to avoid confusing human vagaries with the facts concerning 'any' of the sciences. 

It will be observed that science is the system by which various principles, forces and processes function with respect to things and conditions, and with respect to each other. Sciences are not invented nor developed arbitrarily, but they are discovered through the study of various phenomena of nature. The effort will be to examine into the scientific aspects of economics.

It has been claimed that economics is not susceptible of being treated as a science because it is so closely interwoven with human interests. But what would the sciences of mathematics, electricity or chemistry be worth if it were not for the use It which is made of them by the people except as the principles of those sciences may be involved in the functioning of nature more or less throughout the universe?

The fact that a science is used by the people does not change its natural characteristics. often happens that people do some very peculiar things with respect to mathematics and other sciences, and the results will be 'right' or 'wrong' according to the reaction of 'natural laws', rather than to the hopes and expectations of the people.

Sciences can be used constructively, or they can be misused and abused-with corresponding results. 

Just as ores have to be put through a smelting process to separate the metals from the earthy conglomerate in which they are found in nature, before the metals will be available for use in the service of man, so if economics could be lifted out of its emotional conglomerate long enough to disclose something more of the natural characteristics of its basic principles, civilization might move forward more constructively in the performance of its natural functions.

That is to say, one should be able to recognize what he is looking for, whether he is hunting for rabbits or for scientific characteristics. When we look for scientific characteristics in the realm of economics, plenty of them will be found.

They will be interested not so much in self-control as in means for controlling other people. It seems apparent that such progress as has been made in economics has been instinctive rather than studied. 

A sharp distinction should be drawn between the science of economics and the art of making money'. People sell their effort and skill, and make money. 

They may cultivate the land, sell the products, and make money.

Loose Thinking 

During the past few years there appears to have been developing an amazing amount of 'loose thinking', particularly with respect to economic relations among people-which perhaps is natural enough, because economics is related to so many phases of human welfare and is prolific in its influence upon the emotions. It is in the realm of the emotions that loose thinking most naturally finds its inspiration.

We are living in an era of such marvelous scientific and mechanical developments, and there are so many new things and processes, and so many changes in methods, that there seems to be a growing belief that nothing is stable-that anything and everything is liable to change. But fundamental principles, founded upon natural laws, never change. It is only the superficial things which change, as they are buffeted about with the varying activities, passions and moods of man.

The only new things about chemistry and electricity are the new characteristics which are being discovered by man.

The proc- esses involved in photography and the radio could have been used back in the time of Plato

Weather conditions will be a matter of 'fact', but opinions regarding those conditions may vary according to the individual reactions of the people with respect to them. By the same to ken, social conditions and relations, and the status of people in respect to civilized refinement will be a matter of 'fact', though opinion on those subjects may vary over a wide range and more or less of the opinion may or may not be correct.

We should not permit ourselves, through loose-thinking to float off into space, so to speak, imagining our feet to be firmly planted upon the ground.

Loose-thinking on the part of those who have more or less influence in the molding of public thought is likely to bring about natural reactions detrimental to the welfare of our nation. The more loose-thinking we have among the masses, or among those who influence the masses, the more difficult it will be to keep the affairs of our nation running smoothly and orderly, for loose-thinking is but the forerunner of loose action — and sometimes it may not take very long to develop a good deal of

It may be well, therefore, to check back occasionally to fundamental principles, and so guard against being carried too far, through the influence of sentimental or emotional forces, from a safe course founded upon reason and fact. 

One of the greatest human weaknesses will be found in the tendency to permit opinion and the emotions to prevail unduly over fact and reason. A degree of flexibility or easy balance in respect to those things may go far, at times, in promoting the welfare of the people, as long as the flexibility is kept within reasonable bounds. The importance of exactitude in human relations will vary according to circumstances.

Humanics, or the laws and principles governing human relations, should be taught, if at all, upon the basis of established fact rather than upon the basis of sentiment, or somebody's theory as to what they possibly ought to be. Right now the most important duty of everyone interested in the welfare of our country is to exert every effort toward correcting the wave of loose-thinking which has already gone far in disturbing our economic and social relations.

The cultivation of civilizing influences should be done, not by over-stepping primary prin- ciples, or advocating altruistic theories which may be practical enough in certain places and under certain conditions, though not adapted for general standardization among the masses. 

Our endeavor should be to discover fundamental principles involved in human actions and reactions, the manner of their functioning, and their relation to the affairs of mankind, and not wander too far away from those principles in our relations with other people. 

While propagandists may acquire a cleverness in the use of words and in the expression of ideas yet nature writes its own history tells its own story of what has happened, for it is the realities of life which ultimately control our destinies, and they will be whatever they happen to be when the forces and processes of nature and environment come along to fill up the gaps and catch up the loose ends of whatever man may have done or left undone in the performance of his contribution to the trend of the times. Economics and Civilization 

IVILIZATION is com posed essentially of 'people', and exists only in the spirit, character and culture of the people as manifested in their association and reactions with respect to each other. In a sense a civilization may be measured by the degree in which the laws, forces and processes of are utilized but its spiritual or emotional phase will be measured by its respect for and observance of the principle of the golden rule, which marks exactly the point of cleavage between barbarism and civilization, where man is supposed to recognize and respect the normal rights and welfare of his fellowmen to the same extent that he in turn expects his own normal rights and welfare to be recognized and respected by other people just as when we come to the inter-functioning of two or more sciences it becomes very essential that due consideration should be given to the characteristics of each of the sciences, according to its relative importance otherwise the result will be likely to fall short of what otherwise might have been expected. 

At this point a distinction is to be recognized between what I have just said and what I did not say. I did not say that one should take the same degree of interest in the affairs of other people as in his own affairs except as he has taken on a responsibility with respect to them through employment or through some form of voluntary interest or incentive. 

Economics is one of the three primary factors of which civilization is composed-the other two being reason and emotion. 

Obviously economics will come first, because without the means and facilities for supporting human existence nothing else would have any meaning. 

Reason is founded upon natural. laws, and upon fact — it is the primary basis of human understanding, and serves, among other things, as a chart for the guidance of human activities and development, and is a stabilizing influence between emotion and economics. Emotion, on the other hand, may range anywhere from the sublime to the ridiculous.

Emotion or sentiment may be regarded as a sort of effervescence of spirit, and it varies much in quality and character. 

It is popularly supposed to have its seat in the heart, and probably it is as important in commercial and industrial relations as reason, for it provides much of the warmth, spirit and force to 'make things go'. It becomes a source of inspiration, joy and sorrow, and represents the spiritual side of life. It is through the emotions that we get our humor, which plays such an important part in the maintenance of a healthy mind, as well as many of our tragedies, which are so destructive to human happiness. The emotions may be not only the source or foundation of marvelous strength, but also of deathly weakness, as well.

Manifestly it is unsafe, therefore, to follow sentiment or emotion too far from the path of reason. In other words, it is not wise to permit the heart to run away with the head, nor should the head be allowed to exercise too much dominion over the heart. That is to say, emotion should be sufficiently tempered with reason to make it safe -maintaining an easy balance between the two, so far as that may be practicable. 

Economics, reason and emotion may be employed to supplement each other without being converted into each other, and without changing or sacrificing their essential characteristics.

It will be observed that the three factors, economics, reason and emotion, correspond with the essential elements of a treeeconomics being represented by the roots, reason by the trunk, and emotion or sentiment by the boughs and foliage. The roots form braces anchored in the ground to support the tree in an upright position, and they also gather nourishment from the soil. The trunk is the supporting element between the roots in the ground and the boughs overhead. The boughs and foliage not only give grace, beauty and dignity to the tree, but gather elements of life and nourishment from the air, sunlight and rain, thereby supplementing, and coordinating with, the work done by the roots.

The amount of bough that can be safely carried by a tree will depend upon its relation to the trunk, the manner in which the tree is rooted in the ground, and also upon the nature of the soil. 

When the bough becomes too big, the trunk may break, or the tree may become uprooted.

Obviously, it will be similarly true that the extent of emotional activities which can be supported by a nation or by a civilization will depend upon their relation to economics and reason. Usually such activities will expand along with increasing economic prosperity, particularly when the prosperity is wide-spread among the masses, or total population.

It will not be practicable to maintain a balanced relation between economics, reason and emotion at all times and under all conditions, owing, among other things, to the fact that people differ so widely and so widely and fundamentally in their characteristics and in their outlook upon life and human relations, and further to the varying degrees of development and the varying traditions and environment of mankind.

But the emotional spirit overdone causes economics to break down- just as extravagance and improvidence overdone will force people into a lower scale of living conditions. Among certain people too much prosperity tends to break down the moral character. The quality of a nation and of a civilization depends upon the character of the people of whom it is composed. 

To such extent as civilization breaks down, man naturally will drift back toward a lower state of existence.

The spirit of the golden rule finds much of its inspiration in the emotional aspect of human nature, and will be readily recognized as the keynote or mainspring of civilized relations among people. 'Live and let live' is another way of expressing the spirit of the golden rule.

The principle of 'exchange' 

therefore, becomes a logical function of the golden rule wherein, except as ownership is transferred by gift or inheritance, one expects to 'give' something, or 'do' something in exchange for what he receives from other people, as distinguished from a state of barbarism, where certain people, either individually or in groups, proceed to 'take' whatever they want by force and violence, and without regard to principles of right or wrong.

The balancing of rights or wants by the process of voluntary exchange is essentially a characteristic of civilized relations. It would be difficult to imagine a more fair and logical method of bringing about an adjustment between supply and demand.

There is, however, so much intermingling and overlapping between civilization and barbarism that sometimes we may wonder which order of collective relations it is under which we are living.

It is to be expected that barbarism will not hesitate to usurp and use the various methods, arts and equipment of civilization whenever it may seem expedient to do so, whereupon the business may have the outward appearance of being done according to civilized procedure, but the barbaric characteristics will be ready to function whenever its purpose may be served thereby. Much of such appropriation and adaptation will be found in the industries and commerce, finance and government. 'Barbarian' is the term applied to man in his primitive, wild state, wherein his characteristics will be on a parity, more or less, with other creatures in nature which are wild and undeveloped. His pastime and his egotism will find expression in attempted domination over his fellow-man and over other living beings; and according to his mood from time to time that domination will involve various forms and degrees of oppression, intimidation, cruelty and perchance destruction - whence he also acquires the term 'savage'. The moment he gets away from his primary instinct and begins to recognize the rights of other people he commences to take on characteristics of civilization.

A man is barbarian or civilized, not by the kind of clothes he wears, or where and how he lives, but because of what he is from within-though outward appearances are often an indication of the quality of the inner man or character. Barbarism does not necessarily mean ignorance-it may develop a degree of culture without having the fundamental attributes of civilization; it may function as a sort of cultured piracy. Manifestly there are many savage or barbaric influences thriving in the midst of civilized life. 

Normal rights are those, first, which do not interfere with the natural and obvious rights of someone else, and, second, those which are in approximate balance with the rights of other people-as, where any interference or conflict of rights which might occur has been adjusted or compromised. In other words, normal rights are on a basis similar to the laws of nature where man takes his place in the

universe along with everything else in nature, and notwithstanding the fact that he is born into certain group relationships such as family, community, national, social, political, etc., yet when he emerges into manhood and leaves the parental control he is, first of all, essentially a and has an individuality, as distinguished from any group of whatever kind or character -just as though he were the 'first' man in the world, or the 'last' man or the 'only' man in a vast area - where he will be, justly and logically, a law unto himself until he comes into contact with his fellow-man. man 

That is to say, the individual possesses, as a fundamental principle of nature, 'every' right which does not conflict unduly with his association with other people whereupon a reciprocal bending or compromise of rights will tend to preserve a balanced relation without undue invasion, over-riding, or usurpation on either side, although in practice the usurpation of the rights of others may seem vastly more prevalent than compromise. Such compromise as does occur may be either voluntary or compulsory. It will be obvious that man's responsibility to any group depends upon the extent to which he becomes involved in the group, or comes into contact with it. 

Freedom of speech, and freedom of the press, will be like freedom of the highways and of other public property - it will be for 'use' and not for 'abuse'. 

Anything else would be a travesty upon common decency.

Herein a distinction is made between the freedom or liberty which is in accord with the principles and spirit of civilization, on the one side, and the usurpation or confiscation which is common to the spirit of barbarism, on the other. 

It is important to note that there is a vast difference between the status of the individual, or his group, on the one side, and that of the 'rest of the world' on the other side. In some respects there is a mutuality of interest, and in other respects the interests will be just as properly opposed to each other.

These differences are natural, and are logical, and are in harmony with the great scheme of nature. The individual is the point of contact between the various forces and activities of the human race with respect to each other. His status is personal, while that of the mass is impersonal. It is he who experiences the sensations of joy and

satisfaction, or distress, hunger, cold, etc., as he comes into contact with the conditions which cause those sensations. The principle corresponds with the nerve tissues in the fingers which are the basis of the sense of touch.

It will not be reasonable nor justifiable to sacrifice the individual for the benefit of the mass as long as he refrains from encroaching unduly upon the reasonable or normal rights of other people. 

One reason why so many people are sojourning in the jails all over the country is that they just cannot behave themselves when they come into contact with somebody else's rights. 

While various groups in the industries, in commerce, finance, politics, etc., are working and fighting to develop new ways to exploit each other and the masses, the responsibility rests upon the individual to go on living and providing for food, clothing and shelter for himself and for those who may be dependent upon him. Exploitation of the masses means exploitation of the individual. It is the individuals within the groups and masses who have to bear the ultimate burden.

Group or Collective Interests 

Some well-meaning people, having become enthusiastic over the 'group' idea and the 'collective' interests and activities of people, may contend that because we are living here more or less together, the group idea should be supreme, and that the individual should have no rights beyond those which are assigned to him by whatever authority may have acquired control of the group. And there is nothing new about that idea, for all down through the development of the human race there have been people, individuals and groups, who felt that it was their mission in life to control the destinies of their fellow-men, through operations ranging from nothing on up in magnitude. 

Free moral agency is a natural concept not only in the matter of human rights, but every living creature is a free moral agent within the limitations of its relation to the influence of other things in nature-animate or inanimate.

People must naturally co-ordinate in some degree with certain other people in civilized life, to such extent as they come in contact with them, but the

standardization of people tends to submerge individuality. A person primarily will be no less an individual, and should not be expected to lose too much of his individuality, because of his association with other people. 

Along with his co-operation with the group there will always be an impulse or instinct to maintain his primary individuality. 

There is a 'dignity' in individuality which is dear to both man and the nation. The dig nity of individuality is a heritage of nature, born of pride and ambition, vanity and selfishness, love and hate all of them easily abused but they are human attributes, and are manifestations of the laws of nature as much as the sunshine or the rain. The spirit of individuality has been envied, hated and worshipped throughout the ages, and unquestionably it always will be. The one thing more than anything else that dignifies and ennobles our great 

American Constitution is its conception of Man inspired with the dignity of his own individuality, while at the same time he recognizes and respects a similar dignity in his fellowman. 

Co-ordination and association may be selective. It is not essential that persons should co-ordinate in every respect with 'everybody else'. It is something which naturally and properly will vary according to people and conditions, and as a rule will take care of itself. 

Along with the development of intelligence, tolerance and moral character, there naturally results a sort of aristocracy of culture and refinement, in contrast to those people who are undeveloped in those higher and finer characteristics. Obviously there is that distinction between people, regardless of what it may be called.

It would seem logical that the normal rights of that higher order of people should expand in proportion to such development, because of the reasonable likelihood of freedom from abuse as they administer their relations with other people, and for the further reason that it is to that class of people to whom we must look for the development of civilization. It is a fundamental principle that rights should be in proportion to re sponsibility. 

On similar grounds the normal rights of the lowest and most depraved order of mankind should be more restricted in their scope, and elemental in character.

have a high degree of culture along with a beastly depravity. 

Naturally in such a composite there will be a vast medley of right and wrong.

Human characteristics, how- er words, certain people may ever, are so complex, interwoven and overlapping in the same people, that a just and equitable administration in the matter of rights on a universal basis would require a higher order of wisdom and character than would be easily and currently available. The work would be done by people who are as variable in wisdom, character and fallibility as other people would be for they naturally would be drawn from the same class of people who are now engaged in the administration of public affairs. One of the weaknesses of civilization is the variableness in the quality of the human element. 

It will be observed that we are now considering people from the standpoint of their relation to the attributes of civilization, and not from the standpoint of their economic or social position, for both wealth and poverty will be found all up and down the entire range; some of the highest types of humanity will be living in poverty, while some of the most ignorant and depraved, and people representing various types of the barbaric element will be found basking in wealth and reveling in the refinements of civilization. Civilization is a stupendous force working for the benefit of mankind, but it will never function on a higher level than the spirit and character of the people who happen to be influencing the trend of the times. 

Just as electricity will not bring the same response or reaction when applied to all substances, so this force or principle of the golden rule will not bring harmonious reaction except among certain people and under certain conditions. The people on both sides must be adapted to the reaction. All through nature substances as well as forces and processes must be co-ordinated before they can work together. In the realm of chemistry, certain elements will have a great affinity for each other will combine with and absorb each other; others will not mix so readily- some will not mix at all, while certain others if brought together in certain proportions and under certain conditions, will cause an explosion.

with respect to people and other living beings.

Some people simply do not have the texture of body, mind or character, nor the personality, to do and to be like certain other people; differences in texture, culture and refinement may, with great propriety, influence the manner and extent to which people associate with each other.

The operation of the principle of the golden rule is wonderfully exemplified in the modern regulation of traffic at highway intersections where the people are at liberty to travel back and forth as often as they please as long as they alternate that right at stated intervals and places with other people desiring to cross their path. This will be recognized as a fundamental principle in man's relation to man, in the realm of civilization.

Because the laws regulating traffic at the crossing of highways appeal to the average person as being just and reasonable it follows that they usually will be observed by a majority of the people, just as any resonable law or regulation will always be likely to have the support of a majority of the people-while certain other people, true to their barbaric spirit, and those who are undeveloped, the irresponsible and the thoughtless, will dash through in violation of signals, thereby usurping and ignoring the natural rights of other people-taking what they want because they want it. Such people must be regarded as being, in varying degree, a menace to the welfare of the public. 

It is especially at the crossing of the highways of life that we find human nature classifying itself in many ways, according to its predominating characteristics.

The exercise of a certain degree of self-restraint and more or less self-sacrifice will be expected expected under the so-called golden rule, yet it need not go to the extent of self-elimination, for that would be contrary to the principles of self-preservation. The laws of nature are not supposed to contradict each other.

The great system of natural laws, and their inter-functioning, becomes a marvelous example of the high order and breadth of the wisdom of the 

Great Creator. 

Nature must be the starting point, and its influence persists throughout eternity. 'Self-preservation' is not only the first law of nature, in the realm of

the human race, but it is the primary animal instinct. The aim of civilization is to rationalize and refine that instinct to the point of bringing it into accord with the spirit of the golden rule.

While civilization is founded essentially upon ideal relations among people, yet its practical application must be regarded as a compromise between the first law of nature and the golden rule. A reasonable consideration for other people is about all that could be expected under a balanced functioning of the golden rule-as where there is as where there is an adjustment, compromise or exchange of rights. And the process or relation may not extend beyond that point.

The operation of the golden rule will be in two phasesthe first phase naturally will consist in the balancing of rights, which may be ruthless and uncompromising in its exactions. Anything further would have to come under that higher order of civilization which voluntarily transcends the mere balancing of rights, as where somebody does certain things for other people, or refrains from doing things which might injure or oppress other peoplewithout any hope or expectation of recompense, and perhaps just for the sake of being decent; or he may be moved by any one or more of the many emotional impulses which are recognized by certain people upon certain occasions.

So, regardless of the extent to which it may be cultivated, it is clear that a is clear that a universal or standardized functioning of the golden rule is impossible of attainment, and unquestionably too much should not be expected of it. Its misuse will induce natural negative reactions, depending upon the varying temperament of the people, just as surely as the misuse of any force or principle in nature will induce a negative reaction.

Owing to the conflict of interests and forces between the various intermingling and overlapping human elements, civilization may become more of a theory than an existent fact. Its highest type will be an ideal toward which people may strive, with probably relatively few measuring up to it— each individual and each group will rate high or low on the scale, according to the way he lives, and his reaction to his environment.

There will always be a conflict between civilization and

barbarism, and the net result, at any time or place, will be manifested on the side of either one of those two forces according to the attitude of mind, force and character of enough people to influence the general trend. The preponderance of influence on one side or the other will determine the current status of civilization with respect to barbarism.

As in the case of an individual, a nation may attain a high degree of civilization and then revert back again to a state of barbarism, and there are times and places where it seems apparent that the barbaric element is holding its own in a substantial degree against the advance of civilization. 

Like other forms of life, a civilization will be subject to the processes of decline and decay. It never will be able to exist or to develop beyond its ability to resist the various destructive forces and influences which it may encounter; and there may come times when it will be obliged to assert itself for the purpose of self-protection, and to fight for its existence, if it is to survive. At such times moral suasion, arguments and sophistries may have to be temporarily abandoned and replaced by aggressive force. 

Civilization cannot afford to go too far in temporizing with the destructive influences of barbarism, or over-reach itself in the application of the golden rule for that principle is of value only so far as it promotes the welfare of mankind, and should not be carried to the point of weakening the cause of civilization and contributing to its overthrow. Civilization, at times, may have to be as ruthless as barbarism. 

Throughout the development of the human race one civilization or culture after another has come into existence, flourished more or less, for a longer or a shorter period, and passed out -and there probably is no more reason for expecting that this present civilization will last forever than that those which have preceded it should have lasted longer than they did; and who can foretell what will be the agencies of its destruction? 

Certain of those agencies may arise out of the arts of civilization-prosperity over-done will induce relaxation and indulgence, which in turn may cause a weakening of character and of the principle of self-preserva-

tion. Over-indulgence in insipid pastimes tends toward a letting down with respect to those things which develop and sustain character. So with various forms of dissipation which which

Iwould have the effect of weakening the people physically, mentally or morally. It will be easy and natural for weak and insipid minds to become absorbed in trivial and insipid subjects. Indifference with respect to future welfare may have a similar effect. Apparently there are people who will not be capable of more than a certain amount of development. This may be as true of a nation as of an individual.

Sometimes the breaking down process may be due to influences outside of the nation, or it may be developed within the nation. 

The barbaric spirit may stampede the spirit of civilization in various ways and from various directions. A nation may become weakened or destroyed through the development and spread of unsound doctrines which fire the imagination to effect reforms or changes which are not in harmony with natural laws.

After a civilization has broken down and passed out another may develop some time, somewhere but it will be for the benefit of 'other people' who happen to be living at that time and in that place.

New ideals will have to be subjected to the reaction of the laws of nature, as time passes, as a test for their justification.

As it is these laws of nature which ultimately prevail in the world's great court of last resort, it is difficult to see how any substantial or lasting good can come from planning human relations in defiance of those laws. 

As long as there are enough people of the higher order, and as long as they have enough influence over the people of the lower order, the civilization will flourish, but to such extent as the condition is reversed the civilization will weaken and tend to decay. 

The breaking down or loss of financial or economic resources may cause the downfall of a nation, just as would happen in the case of an individual So, to such extent as a nation fritters away or otherwise exhausts its financial resources it will be just that much easier for some other nation to 'walk in', so to speak, and take possession of whatever it wants as has happened, is happening now, and

probably always will continue to happen in various parts of the world. The modern mech anization of warfare has added enormously to its cost and destructiveness, and it is logical to expect that methods of warfare will become still more costly and destructive as time advances.

When a nation breaks down, such civilization and culture as it may have developed will break down also. The end of the world and the end of civilization will be about the same thing, so far as human welfare and development are concerned. 

Achievements of the highest order may be lost to the world through the decline of civilization, and thereupon they will have to wait until they are redeveloped by future generations. 

Future generations may have occasion to say of this period that civilization broke down because scientific and mechanical developments came along with such rapidity that the people were unable to absorb them into their daily lives in orderly conjunction with other current activities; that through the misuse and excessive application of the new facilities, things and conditions which became available, the people lost too much of their sense of proportion; that civilization broke down also because the people went too far in extending the principles and spirit of the golden rule to too many who were were unworthy, and to those who were not adapted for participation in its application; that the people developed more leisure than they were able to use intelligently or with safety to their physical or moral welfare; that they wasted their resources and indulged too extensively in vapid and insipid pastimes, all of which had the effect of weakening their selfcontrol and their moral character. In other words, they tried to drive too fast and recklessly along the highway of life.

The science of economics is manifested in the things and conditions which are characteristic of people in general, rather than to the temperamental peculiarities of certain individuals regardless, more or less, of who or where the people may be. Those characteristics or principles will be just as basic and impersonal as those which pertain to any of the recognized sciences.

Production and Consumption 

LL down through the ages people have been longing for and dreaming about a time and a condition of existence in which man would be able to live without working.

Every now and then, when some new development is being announced, we hear comments from people of more or less prominence to the effect that it marks just another step toward that Utopian condition. But the process of 'living' involves, among other things, the process of 'consuming'; and to support that process it becomes necessary to search out and to gather, manipulate, combine and utilize various elements in nature. Somebody has to do that work-and and it will be done, not through selection and direction, but by various people according to their ability or adaptability and opportunity, and according to how they drift into their current responsibilities through choice or necessity. Production and consumption are the first essentials of human existence.

Many people apparently have a belief that when God made this world he made an awful mess of things, and that if they could only be commissioned to go around and correct some of 

God's mistakes they would create a new order of life in which there would be no misfits or inequalities among the peopleno incompatibilities. It would be a new system of existence, all right; but unless an adaptation of human relations is provided to meet the conditions which are found to be characteristic in this world, it may be presumed that most people would shortly have occasion to pray, more fervently perhaps than now, for Divine assistance God, help us! God, help us! 

Changes are constantly being made in the order of life among people, but fundamental principles remain unchanged - it is the reaction of various people toward those principles which changes. The reactions will be variable in many ways, more or less as weather conditions are variable so it seems likely that inequalities and misfits will go on developing as long as the human race lasts. 

But the older I become and the farther I travel, the more respect I have for the wisdom of the Great Creator. This universe

was not created for me alone, nor for you, nor for the other fellow but the universe 'is here' and we are here', and we find that marvelous provision has been made for the welfare of the human race. 

It probably is not essential that the Creator should have left a blue print, so to speak, and a detailed description of all that has been done, and its purpose. 

But there has been left for us a system of laws, principles and processes, all in in established working order, which the people are free to study and use. it happens that mis-use will not break down the system, though the effect may or may not wreck the destinies of the people who happen to be involved. And

It only remains for man, as he takes his place in the great scheme of life, so far as he may be able and inclined to do so, to make use of the things, forces, and conditions which have been provided by nature, and supplemented by human genius and effort, individually and collectively. It has been said that nature is no respecter of persons, but 

I am inclined to question that statement, for nature apparently does have a very high regard for any man who respects nature's laws. He may go as far as he pleases in manipulating, refashioning and combining those elements to serve his ends; nature will help him to accomplish his purpose to such extent as he adheres to the rules and formulas which have been laid down by nature, and follows the straight and narrow path, so to speak, doing the right thing, in the right way, and at the right time. Naturally the result will vary according to the manner and extent of what he does, how he does it, and what he leaves undone. 

Nature does not attempt to standardize people beyond a very few broad principles. In fact, nature may not care particularly whether somebody obeys certain of its laws, or not. 

If he does obey the chances are that he will succeed in his undertaking, but if he does not the chances are that he will not succeed except as the project may be carried forward due to its relation to other things. One of the greatest restrictions upon development and progress is the tendency to ignore natural laws. 

There is, naturally and logically, an individual responsibility to participate in such economic activities as may be available, where and when they occur. 

That is to say, 'everybody' must do 'something' to make a living,

unless he has sufficient resources to enable him to live without working, or unless somebody else is looking after his economic responsibilities for him-wherein we run into a labyrinth of human relations, interests, incentives and cross-purposes. I I am not now speaking of the wage-earner any more than of the business man, in whatever line of enterprise he happens to be engaged, for the rule or principle is fundamental, and will apply to all people according to their status in relation to the affairs of life.

As long as people are self-supporting there will be no 'economic' reason why they should do 'any' work which they do not want to do the less they do, and the more they buy, and the more they employ the services of other people, the more valuable they will be, in an economic sense, to the community in which they live, because of the increased markets which they will be creating for other people.

The creation of markets is one of the most important functions in economic relations.

The greater a man's intelligence, personality and willpower, the more likely he will be to accumulate resources and develop influence among his fellow-men. The greater his resources, the wider range of choice he will have as to what he will do for himself and what he will be able to procure someone else to do for him whereby he becomes a creator of markets which will be available to other people. It will be man's logical, normal and sovereign right, however, to make that selection for himself, although he is going to be affected more or less by his environment and his relation to various other conditions. As his resources diminish, the more things he will be obliged to do for himself and the less he will be able to have done for him by others until, the farther down in the scale of resources he goes, the more he 'must' do for others in order to live at all if he is going to be self-supporting in a civilized community. Upon reflection it will be found that this is exactly man's relation to the economics of life. 

It is not that the getting of money is the most important thing in life, but man's economic resources have such an important bearing upon his comfort, his development and upon his social and cultural status, and that of his family and others in whom he may be interest-

ed to say nothing of his many other responsibilities and interests, private and public, that the procuring and development of economic resources obviously becomes one of the fundamental functions of civilized life. One must be able to give something of acceptable value in exchange for what he wants to acquire, and in order to give he must 'have'.

Hence it probably is true that the art of making money' is as important as a knowledge of the science of economics. Each will serve as a valuable supplement to, but not as a substitute for, the other. 

In the absence of private enterprise as where all business is subject to the direction and control of a governmental overlord, or bureau, or the directors of some community group, where nobody has anything of his own, and where there are no rights pertaining to private property, then people living under such conditions would have little use for buying-power. But they would have to be content to live upon a relatively small scale, and they would not be able to participate in industry or commerce except as they confine their activities to the process of merely trading one thing for another. Even in barter one must have something to give in exchange for whatever he wishes to acquire each element of the exchange represents buyingpower in the hands of the respective traders. It is probable that the individuals would merely perform the tasks which are imposed upon them, and would have no responsibility beyond doing what they are told to do.

The masses, or approximate total population, would be automatically in the 'servant class'.

Such a condition of life might appeal to those passive, helpless creatures who are without initiative or spirit to forge ahead by themselves people who do not have the ambition nor the ability to travel on their own power, and must be towed along, so to speak, by somebody else. The care and attention which they receive will depend upon the character, the mood and the resources of whoever happens to be in authority over them, wherein they may be comfortable and happy, or existence may become a tragedy, as has happened in the case of both domesticated animals and human beings.

In any contemplation of this subject a marked distinction, of course, should be drawn between those people who have reached the age of adult manhood and

womanhood - people who are supposed to take their place in the great caravan of life, and children on the other hand, who have not been in the world long enough to develop the ability to care for themselves, or people who are dependent because deficient in mind or health, and therefore are without the ordinary ability to care for them- -selves. 

The so-called idle rich are essentially business producers, creating countless demands for the multitude of commodities and services for which they may be abundantly able to pay. All of those things have to be produced by somebody, and as a rule have to be marketed by somebody, in order that they may reach either the rich man or the poor man, who may be the ultimate buyer; and when the transaction is completed his money will have been distributed all along down the line, so that each individual gets such portion as may come to him in payment for the part which he took in the production or marketing.

Whether the demands of the rich are for necessities or for luxuries is of little importance to the producer. One man's 

One man's necessities may be a luxury in the hands of another man. It is something which cannot always be determined in advance, and there may be no proper reason why it should be. The product, whatever it may be, goes out into the world and pursues its course through the various channels of trade; but the matter of greatest importance to the producer is that he has a market for the product of his effort.

The people did not all make their appearance here at the same time. It has been a continuing, overlapping process, extending back through the ages. 

People are continually coming into this life, while others are passing out, and each one while here maintains his own peculiar relations to his fellow-beings, according to his environment, and to the trend of the times. His relation to wealth will be upon a similar basis.

The situation is much the same as when people go into a railway coach, a theatre or other public gathering, where each will be on an equal basis, entitled to a seat if there is one which is not already occupied. Human relations are not in the nature of one great picnic of today, though life 'is' a picnic for some people, and it is full of distress for others. Probably most people will be somewhere between the

two extremes and for as many reasons. 

Time, place and environment are essential factors in the development of so-called propertyrights, and of human relations in general. Rights founded upon economic resources which have been accumulated over a period of time, together with social rights and customs, should be respected and protected against usurpation by newcomers of today. This will be one of the duties pertaining to the normal functioning of civilization. 

Most people have to do 'something' for what they get. Some have to do more than others, because of the nature or limitations of their ability, or other characteristics, or because of their reaction to their opportunities and their environment, or their relation to the trend of the times. New developments may make man's work easier, increase his opportunities, resulting in increased resources and an enlarged scale of living, but people always will have to do whatever may be necessary to acquire such things as they may need or want.

Certain people have vastly greater ability than others mentally, physically and temperamentally, and there is no proper reason why those people should be held back because of the unfitness or limitations of others, as long as they do not take any improper advantage, or impose unwarranted burdens upon other people. 

Economic wealth is 'produced', 'discovered' or 'earned', and as long as it survives it passes from one to another, through the channels of trade or by gift or inheritance, varying from time to time in volume and rate of flow according to place and the ever shifting relations of people to each other. 

Certain wage-earners and certain business men will be in extremely unattractive and unremunerative occupations - but they have to do ‘something', and they may or may not be doing the best that they might be doing, from the standpoint of ability, environment or resources. When they can find a better occupation or a better business activity, they may or may not take advantage of their opportunity to better their condition - people do not always act in accordance with their best interests. Not all people will have the same urge for betterment hence the masses always will be found scattered anywhere between the highest and

lowest levels of living conditions.

It is amazing to find how many people will be attempting to carry on a business enterprise in places where there is no market which would be worth the effort or the investment of resources. Civilization, at times and places, will be moving forward or backward, carrying certain people one way or another according to the force and direction of the tide of events. 

While undoubtedly there is enough for all, it is not to be expected that all people are going to perform the economic functions necessary to procure the things which they want and might have. Some of them could, but won't, and others would but can't due, perchance, to limitations in the matter of ability, initiative or opportunity, or to hindrances placed in their way by other people. 

Human equality is a fallacy which, in some parts of the world, has come down through the ages. It is being disproved every day, yet it persists in the minds of many people. The idea is not founded upon reason, but is a product of the emotions. In some respects it has a wide range of emotional justification, wherein it may serve as a stabilizer upon human relations. But, like other things, it becomes mischievous when overdone. 

The nearer people approach to a state of poverty, the nearer they will come automatically to an equality in the possession of economic resources. The farther they become removed from poverty, the farther they will get away from a condition of equality. 

There always will be certain people who will be obliged to perform the drudgery and clear away the dirt and rubbish which is incident to civilization— not because they are selected for such work, but the various kinds of drudgery will have to be done by 'somebody', and they will constitute opportunities for people who do not have something better to do, or who are unable to do any better class of work. Many people never get beyond small-scale operations in the production or the sale of trifles, or the rendering of service which is of little value, or for which there is but a meagre market again, not always as a matter of choice, but rather depending upon their characteristics and their relation to the trend of the times.

Drudgery is a term which should be defined and interpreted according to the mental status of the people involved. 

Various kinds of work and working conditions which would be attractive to certain people would be regarded as 'drudgery' by others. In any great civilization there will be a vast variety of things to be donesome will be of a high order, and on a large scale, while some other things will be of a low order and on a small scale. Probably most people are engaged in some sort of business, selling their services, or buying and selling products, rights etc., on their own account or for somebody else, on a scale somewhere between the two extremes. Obviously, there are vastly more people who are doing business on a small scale than upon a large scale. And, while speaking of extremes, there always will be the highest and the lowest like the two ends of a stick. 

What chances for monopoly there would be, and control by so-called big business, if the masses were more highly standardized, and supplied abundantly with buying-power from heaven, or by a bountiful government. 

But right here, of course, somebody is going to think of something disagreeable-where will the government procure its necessary financial resources unless the masses, by and for themselves, are carrying on profitable, wealth-producing activities? Wealth is created by the people, not by the government. 

The government merely spends what it gets from the people. 

It happens that so many fireside meditations do not work out in practice according to expectations I have had that experience over a long period in my development work. So many times I have had visions of certain changes, combinations and eliminations which ought to result in a wonderful piece of apparatus. Then I would call in a mechanical draughtsman, and have him reduce that nebulous nothing to scale and proportion under my direction. I would have him detail certain of the parts, to see how they would go together and function on the drawing-board. Right there is where many of those bright ideas choked to death a wonderful idea, if it would only work. In many instances I would get past that point, and would have some preliminary patterns made from which I would procure sample castings, and have them machined and put together, before

finding that it was not what I had thought it would be. Sometimes I would get it on the market, and then discover that nobody wanted to buy it, or not enough to make it worth while.

Sometimes I would find that the business would be of such a nature that I would not want it.

It is of such experiences that the progress of the world is made up - trial and failure trial and success! But the failures were not a total loss, for 

I learned something, and the losses represented what I paid for my education. And, altogether, it made some business for the draughtsmen, the patternmakers, machine shops, and all of those people who took any part in the project. So and so much more business was done by them at a profit that otherwise they would not have had. When people pay for their own adventures and mistakes they contribute to the business of other people. Fortunately the successes overbalanced the failures, or I would not be where I am today - and the experience has made me better able to judge probabilities with respect to new ideas. It is tremendously important to be able to observe the natural relation between certain things. To such extent as we develop a degree of seasoned discretion we will not be so likely to be found now and then stranded by the wayside. 

The great problem of civilization does not lie in the direction of restricting people in the development of economic resources, but rather in promoting and safeguarding the means whereby each person may be privileged to carve out his own career to run his own affairs according to his intelligence, ability and reaction to his opportunities and environment subject only to such restrictions as may prevent or discourage him from overstepping, or unduly abusing, the normal rights of other people. The basic concept of a government by and for the people will be to protect and foster such a status among the people. The great difficulty in that kind of government will be to get proper representation for the people which will enable them to live without undue regimentation, and without the imposition of unwarranted burdens.

The regimentation of people involves a limitation of the principle of 'free moral agency'. It is essentially an invasion of individual rights- particularly the right to manage one's Own

affairs. Some people have to be regimented or restrained to keep them within the bounds of decency. But there is no justifiable reason for imposing unnecessary restraints for the benefit of other people, except to safeguard the normal rights of all of those who would be affected. 

No wealth has ever been created through the failure of business to function, and vast fortunes have been lost because certain business was not done. 

Somebody may want to know what became of the lost wealth - who got it? And the answer will be that nobody got it. The wealth simply never came into existence. It was 'lost' because it naturally would have come into existence if the business had been done as in the case of the millions of fish which never got into the channels of trade just because they were never caught in the nets set by man, or like the crops which were never raised, or the work which was never done, or the wages which were never earned. 

It is the current functioning of business which creates new wealth, and supports that which is already in existence. 

But perhaps we have not done so badly after all, in a broad sense, here in the United States, when we stop to consider that the people who are selected to administer the affairs of government are not selected on the basis of their peculiar fitness for the job, as would be done in the case of a private enterprise, but rather because of their 'popularity'. Furthermore, the selection on that basis is not made by substantial business and professional men around a council table, but it is made at a 'ballyhoo party' for what else can be said of almost any political convention or political election?

It is just one great ballyhoo party, where the attempt is made to stampede the voters in some certain direction, or to espouse some certain, or uncertain issue. And what sort of results can be expected to emerge out of that kind of a situation? 

Our great saving grace, or ultimate protection to the people, comes from the development, now and then, of a large enough under-current of sound thinking on the part of enough people to influence the trend of the mass. People sometimes will stand for a great deal, and sometimes for a long while, but when the change of heart takes hold of enough people, then it may be expected that a change

will be made for the better in the political situation.

Thinking is done by the 'relatively few' - the masses, on the other hand, are stampeded, but the effect will be the same as though they did think. It may be even more effective for good or bad than if they did think for themselves. The safety and welfare of the masses depends upon what sort of thinking is done by those few their intelligence, integrity, etc. And there is nothing new about the vagaries of mass psychology-the subject was recognized centuries before the Christian era. Mass psychology always has been a problem in human relations, and there is every reason to expect that it probably always will be. 

Mass psychology is an important element in human relations. 

It is a subtle force, more susceptible to the emotions than to reason. It is composed of human characteristics which may be powerful for good or for evil one of the most outstanding is its 'impersonality'. It enables concealment of responsibility, and so may become a 'throne' or a 'hiding place'. Its influence may be either offensive or defensive. Usually it will be more or less organized, or it may have certain characteristics similar to organization.

In the final analysis, it is natural that the individual should be closer to his own personal interests than to those of other people otherwise self-preservation would not be the 'first law of nature'. The condition relates back to the basic principle which applies to incentives, and marks the difference between the personal and the impersonal relations between the individual and the rest of the world.

Supply, Demand and Buying-Power 

HE wants of a civilization represent a mighty force reaching out to procure those things, conditions, equipment and processes which are essential or incident to its existence, comfort and happiness, its culture, expansion and safety. The supplying of those wants through production, marketing and countless forms of service is another force which naturally grows out of the demand thus created.

It is through the inter-change of the things and the services required for current use and occupation, consumption, wearing out, destruction and replacement, that the masses are furnished with a daily recurring market where they may exchange their effort and skill, their products and other property or rights for whatever may be essential or desirable to satisfy their own wants. Facilities for such exchange are fundamental to the growth and development of civilization and culture.

Here we have the birth of 'supply' and 'demand'; but those two forces, or factors, may languish alongside of each other, starve to death, so to speak-as they have been doing, figuratively speaking, with rspect to so many people during the period of the recent depression - until that third element, known as buying-power, in the form of money or credit comes along to serve as a circulating medium or flux to enable supply and demand to flow over into each other- just as two or more chemical substances may continue in an inert relation to each other until the introduction of some other element causes them to blend. 

Exchange in the channels of trade is an essential and indispensable function of economics -it becomes the 'spirit of life', so to speak, or vitalizing force which enables the various economic activities to function in the service of man. It is not customary for people to turn their resources over to somebody else without getting something which they want in exchange. It is through the process of production, service and exchange that buying-power, or wealth, is created and acquired by the masses in general.

The relation which exists between supply and demand is as fundamental as any law or condition in nature-like the balancing of any two forces or impulses. They will rise and fall with respect to each other, supply always seeking to balance with demand, or to augment demand so that it may absorb an increased supply. Obviously, it forms the basis of our entire system of producing, serving and exchanging to meet the wants of civilization.

This is something which should be borne in mind by those who contend that the law of supply and demand should have no place in our economic relations. 

And we do hear such expressions from time to time from certain people who would like to correct inequalities among people by ignoring such fundamental principles as may interfere with their purpose. Just as we always will have to accommodate ourselves to the vicissitudes of the weather, so we always will have to accommodate ourselves to the vicissitudes of human relations and conditions, where people individually and in groups will be seeking to adjust themselves to the ever-changing and over-lapping cross-currents of human interests and activities.

It probably is true that most of the trouble with respect to human relations can be traced to the great variety of misfits among people - on either or both sides - rather than to any fault of the principles upon which our economic and social activities are operating. It is what people do, or refrain from doing, that brings comfort and happiness, or sorrow and distress, to themselves and to their fellow-beings. This applies to any and all human relations. 

It is only because a civilization demands such a stupendous array of things, rights, conditions and service of every kind and character, that so many people are able to participate so extensively in the comforts and joys of civilized life. As all of these things have to be paid for there is occasioned a constant redistribution of buying-power in corresponding volume. If there were no human wants to be supplied there would be no economic relations among the people.

The replacement which is made necessary or desirable because of the daily consumption, wearing out and destruction, in ordinary times, constitutes and supports vast, and more or less constant, markets for produc-

tion, service, things, rights and conditions. 

That is to say, consumption, wearing out and destruction will have a constructive effect upon business to such extent as they pave the way for further production, service and exchange, and open up new markets assuming that there is adequate available buying-power. 

Anything which induces new production and gets it marketed and paid for will promote business welfare on the part of the masses.

Whether a product is used as a necessity or as a luxury, or is wasted, will be of little consequence so long as it is paid for, consumed and replaced by a new product. It is the use of things which inspires new demands and creates new business from which the general public will profit. Superficial and thoughtless buying contributes substantially to the flow of buyingpower, thereby helping to support the multitude of activities involved in industry and commerce. 

Please bear in mind, and never forget, the distinction between the individual or his group, on the one side, and the general public, on the other. In some respects there will be a mutuality of interest, but in all other respects their interests will be opposed. The difference may be of vast importance on one side or the other, or it may be of little or no importance. 

A very conspicuous factor contributing to the great prosperity growing out of and following the World War was the tremendous buying-power of the

American people finding expression in the desire to travel, and particularly in the purchase and operation of automobiles.

The motor vehicle in its various forms, from the automobile to the truck, has been put into the hands of the people upon a basis which has enabled them to avail themselves of its advantages, for both pleasure and business, to such an extent that it has revolutionized the lives of the masses.

Probably one of the most prodigious extravagances in the 

United States, in the period since the World War, has been that which pertains to travel, induced and stimulated by the development and easy availability of traveling facilities, in conjunction with the natural fascination which travel has for most people. The success of the motor vehicle business is more or less due to the fact that it lends itself so extensively to the rota-

tion of production, marketing, consumption, replacement and consequent flow of buyingpower, particularly as the rotation or cycle is completed so largely within the United States. 

The great caravans of those vehicles winding back and forth throughout the length and breadth of the land involve a continuous process of wearing out, destruction, consumption and maintenance - to say nothing of the countless things that people do just because they have the automobiles.

Industrial and commercial activities are thus provided for multitudes of people in the carrying on of a vast production and distribution-not only of automobiles, motor trucks and accessories, and the materials entering into their construction, but including as well the construction, maintenance and operation of plants for the manufacture of an endless variety of other commodities - the building and maintenance of roads, and the many provisions that have become necessary or desirable for the accommodation and regulation of this new form of travel.

In other words, the production, destruction and replacement involved in and growing out of automobile travel has been a stupendous factor contributing to the exchange of buying-power among the people. 

Every automobile, wherever found, whether used for pleasure or for business, is performing its share of this great process, contributing to the welfare of the general public even though it may be exhausting the resources of its owner. 

The development of the automobile has opened up a vast array of new enterprises, and promoted the welfare and happiness of millions of people - but the automobile business is not monopolized.

Railroading is also a substantial factor contributing to the economic welfare of our country. 

The product of a railroad is transportation service-conveying people and things from where they are to some other place. The carrying on of that business necessitates stupendous investments in land, buildings, equipment and man-power. The man-power is largely a current operating expense; the buildings and other structures represent value only as long as they are useful, and are continually being replaced by new construction. 

Every movement of rolling stock involves the process of consump-

tion, wearing out and destruction; and railroads are among the most extensive and active purchasers of both labor and commodities of all kinds and grades in America. The extent and activity of railroad operations depend directly or indirectly upon the extent and activity of every branch of industry and commerce. The importance of railroad activities is so great that the prosperity of both the country and the railroads may well be considered to depend to a large extent upon each other. Here again production, consumption, destruction and replacement cause a continual shifting of buying-power in enormous volume, and a widespread re-distribution of wealth.

Without such a stupendous demand the mass production could not have been supported, and without the mass production it would not have been possible for prices to be so low. Prices are normally at the point of balance between production or supply on the one hand, and demand on the other.

This is an example of how the supplying of human wants develops and nourishes economic activities. Generally speaking, wants are a product of the mind, so it is easy to see how a 'state of mind', in conjunction with available facilities, may be responsible for business prosperity - which may be expected to proceed approximately in balance between the state of mind and whatever the available facilities may happen to be. 

On the other hand, a state of mind in itself may bring about a business depression, as when people refuse to buy, regardless of what the available facilities may be; a condition which developed on such a large scale after the close of the World

War. It is apparent, therefore, that outside of the realm of necessities economic wants, and the resultant activities, will take on temperamental characteristics. 

Supply and demand will vary along with the natural, normal activities of the people, according to the seasons of the year, weather conditions, crop conditions, and the locality—or, the relation will vary due to the effect of market manipulation on the part of individuals, groups, or by governmental activities, however well-intended they may be.

A change of weather will create certain wants on the part of certain people, which in turn

will create markets for mate- thrown to that extent out of rials, man-power, mechanical power, transportation, communication and so the ramification spreads. The condition precedes the market. 

If the condition does not occur the market will not come into existence. If railroads had not been developed the various markets growing out of railroad activities would not have developed, and so with the automobile and the radio, and so with all modern equipment. The development of the condition develops the market to supply whatever may be needed in connection with the condition, affording opportunities for private enterprise- whereupon jobs are created. 

It is not enough that there should be large productionthere must be also a profitable market to absorb it. As long as the two exist in conjunction with each other a low range of prices can be maintained. Likewise, it will not matter what the 'production facilities' may be if an adequate market does not exist, and cannot be created. 

When production, and facilities for production, expand beyond the absorbing power of the trend of the times, then industry and commerce will be balance. 

The process of exchange is not always mutually and spontaneously reactive. Usually it is preceded by a function known as 'marketing' or merchandising, where the seller induces a willingness on the the part of the buyer to buy. Marketing becomes a motivating impulse in the process of exchange-without it there would be relatively little production and service flowing through the channels of trade. The power of 'sales influence' is one of the most important forces or factors in economic prosperity.

So, in addition to the natural or normal demand as for necessities and the ordinary comforts, together with all of those things which, of themselves, have a natural fascination or appeal inducing a 'desire to possess', there will be an artificial demand, induced by sales promotion or stimulation of buying interest.

The creation of a market will be as important a function as that of production. The development and growth of advertising has been going forward by leaps and bounds, and its influence has constituted a very large contribution to the flow of buy-

ing-power-promoting general prosperity. Indeed, that artificial stimulation of buying interest has proceeded so far that it has become a large proportion of the business of the Country. 

The power of advertising or sales propaganda may be highly beneficial or destructive, as when it breaks down sales resistance on the part of people who in their own interests ought not to buy. Vast numbers of people would be more comfortable and secure if they had been better able to resist the ravages of sales propaganda to which they have been subjected. 

A vast amount of selling is accomplished due to the insistence of the seller and the yielding of the buyer. Usually the buyer is the important factor in the trade. The seller will sell when he can get a price which is satisfactory to him- it is the buyer who has to be cultivated.

When a business is to be carried beyond the supplying of things which people have to have as a matter of necessity, or which they want on their own account, its further growth must be cultivated-involving all that the term may signify; and sometimes sharp bargaining and heartless exactions will be subordinated in some degree to more subtle methods designed to create a favorable attitude of mind on the part of the buyer. 

It is the 'over' - doing of things that causes mischief. We may reasonably assume that most vices come into existence through the over-doing of something. The principle is fundamental because it is in harmony with the laws of nature; for example, to such extent as a strain becomes excessive there will be a weakening of the structure, and when the excess reaches a certain point a break occurs; or when something grows too rapidly it develops weakness. It is clear that the principle applies to economics as well as to mechanics, vegetation and to animal growth. Whether the economic activity is excessive or not will depend largely upon the trend of the times. 

The trend of the times, over a long period, represents a gross market which will be limited in capacity because the general public cannot be properly regarded as comprising a vast cornucopia, to be drawn upon without limit, except through inflation of credit. When money has been spent for one purpose it cannot be spent over again by the same person for some other purpose.

Just as the passing of time cannot be hurried, so the gross capacity of a market cannot be exceeded without an ultimate slowing down for an adjustment with the trend of the times. The law of balance has a wide application in the affairs of this world. People cannot live on past markets, any more than they can get power out of water which has passed beyond the dam, or until it finds another sufficient drop in level. When a market is over-worked it will reach a spent condition earlier than under the normal flow of business. 

Down through the years certain people, one generation after another, have been learning something about the worries of over-production. It is not to be expected that people in their right mind, and expecting to remain in business will go ahead for very long frenziedly producing things which nobody is going to buy. 

The slogan 'buy now' is likely to result in the curtailing of future markets, which might have brought higher prices. The invasion of future markets provides business activities to-day, but it is likely to bring a natural reaction in the form of slower operations later on. Then, when people wonder why the market 

is so slow, the answer may be that the business 'has been done' that they have already had and enjoyed such prosperity as was coming to them out of the current trend of the times, and which might have been spread out over a longer period. 

When the demand is supplied the market passes out of existence, whereupon it may become necessary for business to wait and mark time until the slack can be taken up, so to speak, by the trend of the times, before another period of prosperity can develop in that particular branch of industry or commerce.

It naturally will have its effect also upon other lines of business, which may or may not have prospered in the earlier activities. 

Extravagance with respect to future markets, and their usurpation, either to supply a present profit, or to satisfy an unbridled enthusiasm, is an evil which logically has much to do with the creation of boom peaks and depression dips.

It was the uncontrolled spending by too many people, and the wasting of their resources in the buying of things which they could not afford, that helped to support the boom which preceded the crash. The tendency was to create an inflated demand, thus building up an un-

stable peak which sooner or later 'had' to collapse. If the people had not done so much crazy spending the boom would not have risen to such a high peak, and the depression would not have made such a low dip-if even there had been any depression at all. 

And there are those, high in the councils of the nation, who would try to effect a cure by applying more of the same kind of medicine that caused the trouble; that is to say, they would reach out into the future for relief through the immediate construction of certain public improvements and other projects which naturally might be expected to come along at some future time. 

In prosperous periods there may develop many activities which will have to be curtailed or abandoned when prosperity recedes and adversity develops. 

The movement will be perfectly natural, both forward and back. 

Along with the expansion of production facilities there has developed a stupendous amount of sales activity on the installment basis. When people overreach themselves in the mortgaging of their future production, or earning-power, through installment buying, or other forms of debt, in order to acquire buying-power for present use, a question naturally arises as to how they will provide for their future current requirements for necessities, comfort and luxuries. 

The condition will be doubleedged, so to speak, for when they are without resources with which to buy, the general public will be deprived of just that much of a market which otherwise they might have had during that future period. 

Particularly in these modern days of intensely aggressive sales effort, together with the very natural tendency of people to spend in a period of prosperity and easy credit, it becomes relatively easy to stampede the masses into extravagance. The invasion of society by those who 'have something to sell' may become as harmful as a pestilence, flood or other misfortune, where the resources of certain people are spirited away from them, leaving them illprepared to provide for their normal comfort and economic safety. When intensive selling results in the wasting or confiscation of other people's resources, it becomes an evil.

It is not good engineering when people build up standards or levels of living which cannot be supported by their economic resources. With an ample foundation of resources the standards of living can be anything that people see fit to make them but not otherwise, with safety or propriety. 

Supply and demand can go on climbing together as long as they are supported by adequate buying-power, but when the buying-power is built up on a basis which involves too much unsound or over-extended credit, it becomes a weak element in the economic structure, and must be expected to collapse whenever the strain overbalances such strength as it happens to possess.

We are living in an era of very marvelous scientific and mechanical development, which opens up so many new things to be done, and so many ways in which to do them, that it becomes difficult to restrain the impulse to go on and on, at an ever faster pace but over-indulgence of that impulse will be not only at our own peril, but also at the peril of certain other people who may be drawn into our activities. 

It is not good engineering to develop a super-structure to a point beyond the strength of the foundation which is to support it, nor to introduce elements which will have a tendency to weaken the structure or interfere with any of its essential functions. It is logical to expect that manipulation of economic relations and activities will induce reactions as varied in character as the people, conditions and

An arbitrary manipulation of prices and values would be in the nature of a regulation which would lead away from a natural balance rather than toward it. 

The wealth of a nation grows, not through the regulation of trade by arbitrary direction and control but rather through a natural interflow of economic relations. Despotism and economics are not natural companions. 

The limitation and standardization of profits is not logical because of the vast difference in the value of things and conditions in the interchange of human activities. All people are not doing the same things, nor doing them in the same way, nor at the same time, hence they will have different values to different people at different times.

Ideals and theories are worthless unless they can be co-ordinated and matched up with the realities of life. This is just as true with respect to business as it is true that the various sciences grow out of natural laws. 

The sentimental or emotional phase of human relations is of vast importance to civilization, not only on account of its refining influence, but also because it induces the flow of a stupendous volume of buying-power inspired by philanthropy, love, sympathy, patriotism, duty, etc., and in support of political, religious and social relations and activities. It serves to create that much more business. If all of the activities of life were based upon reason alone, then nobody would ever spend a dollar except upon that basis-it would make a tremendous difference in the flow of buying-power. 

While sentimentality is a great business producer, it also causes many people to overreach their economic resources, leaving them ill-prepared to take care of responsibilities grounded in substantial reason.

The holiday trade, in all of its phases, is a very large factor contributing to the flow of buying-power, and the consequent promotion of commerce; and so with weddings, funerals, ceremonials, monuments, and enterprises of various kinds founded upon human interest or civic pride. Every dollar spent in

behalf of victims of disaster, as in the case of fire, famine, flood, earthquake or epidemic of disease - becomes a contribution to economic activities inspired by human emotions.

It will not matter, however, if certain people do profit by the flow of buying-power into emotional activities, as long as the activities are not sponsored and carried on primarily for the purpose of creating more business for certain people who are interested just because they have something to sell. It is to be expected that somebody's business will benefit by whatever the enterprise costs but obviously there should be a comprehensive distinction between current business and profiteering. 

Fancy is also a very subtle and potent factor contributing to economic activity and the consequent flow of buying-power,

Prosperity and adversity are effective economic forces. Those people who have but limited resources cannot afford to despise small economies. Moderation in all things is a wonderful precept, and is akin to the principle of civilization. People can have as a rule just as high standards of living as they can produce for themselves or can pay for; beyond that point they will have to accept what somebody else sees fit to provide, or what may

come to them through the trend of the times. It will not matter how high the standards of living may be for those people who have ample resources the more they spend the better it will be for the general public. 

We not only have a responsibility to refrain from wasting the resources of other people, but we should also refrain from doing things which will tend to break down or undermine their moral character or to defile or corrupt such degree of refinement as they may have acquired. 

Much of the modern advertising and sales activity will have a tendency toward enticing or intriguing people into extravagance which they cannot afford. 

While the spending of money will tend to promote the welfare of the masses in general, yet the prosperity of the individual will depend upon 'how' his own money is spent - whether it is wasted in improvidence or whether it furnishes him with proper necessaries and comforts of life, or is otherwise so invested and safeguarded that a competence will be built up to provide for his future requirements.

Competition The process of balancing supply and demand naturally develops competition, which may occur between the buyers as well as between the sellers of both commodities and servicewhere demand competes for the supply, or where supply competes for the demand. Competition in selling will cause prices to decline, while competition to buy will cause prices to advance. 

Although competition may have a more or less destructive influence upon the fortunes of various individuals and groups yet its normal tendency will be to equalize or balance supply and demand, and thus it forms a basis for the establishment of prices, and becomes a stabilizer of human relations so far as it prevents people from taking undue advantage of each other's necessities, and so far as it safeguards against various abuses which in so many instances have accompanied a monopoly or combination.

When prices for commodities or for service appear to be high, or appear to be low, it is likely to be due to an unbalanced condition with respect to competition, among either buyers or sellers, as to an unbalanced condition in the matter of supply and demand.

Structure of Wealth 

T will be of outstanding importance at this point to develop a comprehensive understanding of the distinction between 'wealth' and 'money', for apparently there is a vast misconception in regard to that subject. Furthermore, a great deal of confusion will be avoided if we guard against using the word 'money' as a generic term for wealth or buying-power.

Contrary to what appears to be a popular idea, gold and other types and kinds of money, "in and of themselves', do not constitute wealth. If all of the gold and silver in the world should disappear we still would have that condition of human relations which we call wealth, just as, at the opposite end of the stick, so to speak, we would have that other condition which we call poverty. 

Gold, silver and other kinds of property are more properly 'manifestations' or 'evidences' of wealth, and then only when they are reduced to ownership and possession by man - for over and beyond the property with which it is associated, wealth is essentially in the nature of a right or force which has to do with the balancing of supply and demand, and grows out of the vast array of interwoven and overlapping relations and activities among the people, wherein individuals and groups acquire certain rights, and become responsible for certain obligations which have an

economic value. The obligation whom it serves. Trustworthy of one man constitutes a right in the hands of another man.

The economic value of the right will depend upon the nature of the obligation, the prospect for its fulfillment, and its status in the channels of trade.

While it is one of the functions of government to supervise and control the issuing of money or currency in order to safeguard its identification and standardization, and further to provide some form of circulating medium to facilitate the exchange of goods, rights, service, etc., among the people, yet 'wealth' is not created by act of law nor by a decree of government, except as some law, decree or ruling by the government may make available various opportunities to be exercised by certain people. Safeguarding the identification and standardization of money is, if anything, more important than supplying a circulating medium, in a country where banking facilities are so highly developed as in the United States. Integrity is one of the most important precepts or precedents through which a government can hope to establish and maintain the confidence and support of the better element among the people standards of value are as important to the people as trustworthy standards of weight, length, capacity, etc.

A government has no economic resources which it does not get from the people. Gold has to be purchased before it can be coined. The paper currency which is issued against the gold is in the nature of borrowed resources every dollar of such currency constitutes a 'promise to pay' upon demand - it represents a government I.O.U. for the payment or redemption of which the gold has been pledged.

All paper currency which is not backed by gold or other available commodity of adequate value, is issued upon faith and confidence in the integrity and the financial responsibility of the public — in effect it is in the nature of 'borrowed money'.

Government resources, as rule, come from taxation, and by borrowing-pledging the public credit for payment. Sometimes they may come through conquest

A supposed justification for high taxes is that the money is paid out again by the government to the people - but it is not returned to the same people. Here again it will be appropriate to draw attention to the distinction between the interests of the individual in contrast to the interests of the masses, or general public. The benefit to the general taxpayer is so remote, intangible and contingent as to be negligible. It will be like using the public money to enable certain people to buy things from somebody else taxing the masses to create business for a few. And there is nothing new about that idea, for in the early history of our country it is found that much was said about 'taxation for revenue purposes only'. I like to sell the goods which I manufacture, but draw the line at giving people the money with which to buy them. It is one thing to pay taxes for the purpose of defraying the cost of government, but it is not to be recommended as a business investment. 

Wealth comes into existence in many ways, largely as a result, directly or indirectly of human enterprise engaged in an infinite variety of production, service, buying, selling, accumulation and exchange-to provide things and conditions which will be of value to mankind, and in making them available for human use and getting them marketed and paid for, at every stage of their travel through the channels of trade.

It becomes apparent that it is not the property, in and of itself, but the right of ownership, possession, use, occupation and control of the property, which forms the basic element of wealth. The property itself has no rights of any kind the rights belong solely to the people who happen, from time to time, to own the property. The element of wealth resides in the ability of certain people to use the property for such purposes as may be desired. 

It is the right of ownership which is transferred, in consequence of which the property itself may, or may not, change its location or condition, or its value, or it may or may not change in other respects. The substance or thing or condition is something with which the

wealth is associated. When nobody wants it there will be no element of wealth associated with it. 

It does not matter what the subject of the ownership may happen to be. It may be cotton or pig iron, a leasehold on a warehouse or a store, the right to operate under a patent, or a franchise, or to operate a utility.

The wealth may consist in the right to collect dues, or to control some certain enterprise or activity. Certain rights of ownership may be more or less intangible and contingent. The value of those rights will depend upon the desire of other people to acquire them, and how much they would be willing to pay for them. 

The ownership shifts back and forth between people as and when a man having say fifty thousand dollars in gold may exchange his gold for land. Assuming that the land is worth what he paid for it, he still will be worth fifty thousand dollars, but his wealth will be in the form of land instead of gold. He may dispose of the land and buy an interest in a steamboat - he may dispose of his interest in the steamboat and buy stocks or bonds in a railroad- he may sell his railroad securities and buy an interest in a steel mill 

he may take his resources out of the steel mill and put them into a department store, a leasehold, a franchise or other propertyright; and if at each step he acquired property which was worth as much or more than he paid for it he will still have his original amount of wealth, though it has resided from time to time in various forms of property. 

Money and other property serve as concrete, tangible things wherein rights of ownership may be stored away, so to speak, until such time as the owner wants to transfer his ownership into some other thing or service, or whatever else he may desire. It is the right of ownership, use, occupation or control which constitutes the element of wealth.

Whether or not certain things represent wealth at all will depend upon their relation to the forces of supply, demand and buying-power, in conjunction with the 'buying spirit' on the part of the people. The elements upon which values are based are relative in point of time and place, and in respect to many other conditions which are more or less constantly changing, hence there can be no such thing as fixed and unchanging, or stabilized values-but the 'money units' will serve, so to

speak, in the role of an impartial umpire to indicate the fluctuation in prices and values. 

The only thing which gives an economic value to anything is the fact it has certain characteristics which make people feel that they want it, or want to acquire certain rights with respect to it, and are willing to pay for those rights. When the characteristics change, or when the attitude of mind changes, or when the market does not have enough current vitality, then, to that extent, the value declines or passes out.

Wealth thus grows out of an attitude of mind-not particularly on the part of one person, because something which one person would prize very highly might be regarded as worthless by everybody else but the attitude of mind must be on the part of enough people to give the object of the desire a standing in the realm of supply and demand. In this sense psychology becomes a very considerable element in the structure of wealth. 

Business activities will not be calibrated to exact or uniform performance - there is nothing constant about supply and demand, nor available buyingpower, the buying spirit, nor the manner or timing of the marketing process. There is probably no proper reason why there should be. there should be. It is only the money units, serving as standards of value, which remain constant, as would be the case with respect to other standards of measure, such as weight, length or capacity. Even though some phase of supply continues at a hitherto normal rate, there may result either an over-supply or a shortage due to changes in the attitude of mind of the people, as their fancy changes from one thing, or from one condition, to another. 

Prices and values will vary according to who the seller or the buyer happens to be, and his attitude toward the sale. 

They will vary according to the marketing ability and marketing facilities of the parties to the transaction, and their market relation. The value of property may change as its ownership changes - changes as, for example, between the manufacturer or merchant on the one hand, and the consumer or user on the other; but it is a characteristic of 'money' that its value does not change when its ownership changes. 

---

Values will tend to adjust themselves with respect to each other except where the price or rate is controlled by contract, by law, by decree, or by custom, or where the price is influenced by manipulation on the part of individuals, groups or by governmental activities. 

Values change-increasing or declining as things, service, 

rights, etc., pass in exchange among the people. For example, cotton changes in value as it passes from the plantation to the city warehouse, because of the handling and transportation service, and because of its change in ownership from the planter, who had a relatively limited market, to the cotton merchant who has a much more extensive market and better marketing facilities. We must not overlook the fact that market position and the marketing facilities have much to do with the increase and decline of prices and values.

As some certain commodity passes from one marketing stage to another its market relation may change as completely as though it had become a different commodity due to a degree of processing or fabrication, or it may take on new characteristics 

due to its new location and environment. 

Cotton changes in value at each stage of its progress from the raw cotton to the finished cloth put into the hands of the ultimate consumer-subject always to the influence which supply and demand and the trend of the times have upon values, wherein they rise or decline due to market conditions, regardless of the cost of production, fabrication, handling, etc. 

Potatoes on hand in a certain market will be worth more than others of equal grade or quality which are too far away to be available for supplying a present urgent market. 

To a purchaser in the open market it will not be a question of how much it cost the farmer to produce the corn in the field, or how much time, work and expense were involved in getting it to the market, but rather what the corn is worth when it arrives at the market. But to the farmer the relation between the selling price and the cost of production and marketing will be a matter of very great interest and importance.

The 'cost' of production, including material, service, equipment, maintenance, etc., is passed along into the hands of the general public at the time

the process of production is going forward, in payment for whatever they may have furnished. But the 'value' of the product, out in the channels of trade, will depend upon trade conditions- the mental attitude of the buyer and the seller respectively, according to the urgency with which the buyer wants to buy and the seller wants to sell. The market value at some period, in some locality, and under certain conditions may be exceedingly great, or it may become worthless, without regard to what the cost of production or the cost of transportation and handling may have been in the channels of trade. That is exactly how it happens that property-wealth may become so extremely valuable, or so worthless, according to the state of market conditions. 

The cost of production is paid for in dollars, which do not change in value but the value of the product will fluctuate from day to day, or from period to period, and according to its location.

The elements of value will vary with respect to certain things: Some things will be bought or sold on the basis of their present yield of incomeothers on the basis of their potential value tential value or probabilities with respect to future developments, and others on the basis of their present market value, which will be whatever price they will bring in exchange today. 

Aside from money or credit, or collectible debt in the hands of the creditor, wealth is not fixed and stable in volume, like things which occupy space. No values are permanent, but all may fluctuate along with the trend of the times and the relation and reaction of the people who happen to be associated with the property to which the values attach. 

Wealth consists of 'rights' with respect to certain things which are supposed to represent value. Neither rights nor values occupy 'space', any more than a beam of light occupies space in the sense that if it is not in one place it will be somewhere else. 

Wealth may either increase or shrink through redistribution. 

Things will have a greater or a lower value in the hands of some people than in the hands of others, because of what various people will do with them. Some people will have greater econom-

ic foresight, and greater ingenuity in combining, fabricating, etc. Some will have better marketing relations and facilities, and better marketing ability, and there will be a difference in reaction to the trend of the times, and ability to recognize and utilize various conditions as they arise. Some people do not have 'any' judgment in the handling of economic resources. 

In the hands of certain people various things representing property-wealth will be exposed to market conditions which are of a higher or lower order, and the value of those things will increase or decline accordingly.

All of which means that a socalled redistribution of wealth taking it away from certain people and giving it to other people, would be likely to bring about a drastic decline in wealth.

The wealth so distributed would not remain constant and stabilized in the hands of those who receive it, but would flow naturally into consumption, wearing out and destructionits normal function. It is not to be expected that the people from whom the wealth had been taken would be likely to go ahead and build up new wealth-to be taken away from them as fast as it is produced. It is easy to see how anything which would tend to discourage the building up of new wealth will bring about ultimately, lower standards of living for the whole country. Wealth is made up of many things which depend for their value upon who owns them, and what he does with them. It is the ideas which are developed and executed by different people that have the effect of building up or destroying wealth.

It will be obvious that the thing in which wealth is supposed to reside must be located where it can be made available for use. A ton of gold hidden away in the bottom of the ocean, where no one can get it, would not represent wealth. Foodstuff located so far away from market that it could not be delivered before becoming unfit for use, or until after the demand for it had passed, would have little or no value. Time and place, and the cost of transportation and handling are important factors in determining values. This is one reason why so many business transactions are so profitable, and why others are attended by loss.

It is largely through the daily activities of the people engaged in an infinite variety of production, service, distribution and exchange-growing out of every

conceivable form of enterprise, large or small, that the buyingpower or wealth of the masses is created and replenished in the hands of individuals and groups. 

Every building, private or public, that is erected, and every improvement to property automatically brings into being a new market for the commodities and service which happen to be involved in its construction. 

So, on every hand, in every community, are evidences of such markets or sales opportunities many of them of stupendous proportions that have been created by business enterprise, private and public convenience and civic pride. As we gaze up and down the streets of our cities, large and small, and contemplate the rows of buildings, old and new, we are confronted with the fact that every dwelling, church and schoolhouse, every mill, factory and warehouse the street improvethe street improvements, the parks and the river and harbor improvements brought into existence a market for both the labor and the material employed in its construction, and stands now as a monument representing business activities reaching back into the past.

Many of them continue up to this time to afford more or less of such a market for their maintenance and repair, and with the exception of public property, and religious and educational property, all of those buildings and improvements are contributing to the support and maintenance of the community through various kinds and methods of taxation. As it is only private property which is subject to taxation, it follows that the more public property there may be from time to time, the less private property there will be upon which to levy taxes. 

Those structures which remain represent the markets which have passed out after having played their part in providing certain economic opportunities for the masses and countless fortunes have been sunk in projects, which ultimately failed. Scattered over the country are to be found an endless array of monuments to blasted hopes and ambitionsmonuments in the form of idle and abandoned mills, factories, warehouses and other buildings which once housed industry and commerce which sometime were, if you please, 'market places' for the sale of 'human effort' brain-power and manpower, as well as for the commodities required in their various functions or activities. An idle factory affords no current

market for man-power or for anything else. Even the plant itself represents but a phantom value, as compared with other things which have assurance of a more ready market sometime.

This naturally would be true owing to the contingency of many conditions, such as location, adaptability, etc. A ghastly blight seems to settle down over an idle plant- and it becomes just something to inspire boys to throw stones and break the windows. Shattered windows serve as a mark or emblem indicating a lifeless building, a spent enterprise, and they will be recognized here and there on all sides. It is the business which is carried on in a plant that gives life, character and value to the whole establishment.

The value of the buildings and improvements is whatever it is, in the light of present conditions; many have ceased to have any value, and many have already been destroyed to make way for other improvements; but whatever their value, those which remain belong to whoever paid for their construction, or who may have since acquired them in due process of business.

The money that was paid for the labor and for the commodities entering into their construction passed out into the hands of the public at the time the buildings were constructed. 

Things which once represented wealth may lose their value through the wear and tear incident to use and occupation, through destruction or decay, or through obsolescence due to the trend of the times. Every structure which falls into disuse or is destroyed by fire or other means becomes an example of the passing of the wealth which it once represented.

A question has been raised as to the distinction between 'wealth' and the 'ownership of wealth', but it may be said that there is no such distinction, because ownership is a primary characteristic of wealth. For example, a fishing boat may go out on the ocean where there are millions of fish, but the fish will have no economic value, and therefore will not represent wealth, until after they have been captured and reduced to possession. It is not until then that the elements of wealth and ownership come into effect; they will be nobody's fish until after they are captured. It will be a matter of passing chance as to which fish happen to be caught; those which are not caught never will figure in the economic resources of man. Even after the fish are reduced to

possession, the degree of wealth which they represent will depend upon the state of the market with respect to supply and demand in conjunction with the available buying-power, and the attitude of mind of the people who might be buyers. If the market happens to be over-supplied the fish may represent 'no' wealth, regardless of how much buying-power is available, or how much labor and expense were involved in catching the fish and bringing them to the market. The status of the market will be influenced also by such other things as may be in competition with the fish. This will be an instance of the distinction between 'things' and 'wealth'. 

Obviously it must be presumed that in the matter of exchange people will give something which they have a right to give-something which belongs to them as distinguished from something which belongs to somebody else. It becomes necessary, therefore, to acquire and accumulate such things as will be acceptable to other people for purposes of exchange. Even in barter one must have something to give in exchange for whatever he wishes to acquire each element of the exchange represents buying-power in the hands of the respective traders. 

Having something to give in exchange for something else is 'capitalism' in its most elementary form. For the reason that buying-power, wealth and capital are synonymous, and because buying-power is so intimately interwoven with supply and demand, it follows that capitalism becomes naturally a component element of civilized relations. 

Buying-power is as essential to the proper functioning of economic activities as the various forces of nature are essential in the realm of physics; the stability of a three-point base is as fundamental in economics as it is in mechanics. One might as well try to make a stool stand by itself on two legs as to try to operate a business enterprise without buying-power of some sort or character to be used in the exchange of supply and demand.

Those people who spend their lives waging war against capital, apparently overlook the fact that they are thereby fighting against their own interests. It would seem like the height of absurdity for people to try to destroy or discourage the customers who constitute the market for their effort and skill, and for the products, commodities, equip-

ment, etc., which they must expect to sell in order to get the things which they will need for themselves. Unless demand is equipped with buying-power in the form of something to give in exchange, it will be unable to avail itself of the supply which it seeks to acquire. It is buying-power that helps to lift man out of his primitive state of existence where he would be obliged to produce, by and for himself, everything required for his existence. such as fire or water or any of the natural elements. All down through the development of the human race abuse has been a common characteristic, manifested in all phases of human relations. Relief from abuse does not lie so much in its elimination, for that obviously would not be possible in this complex world, but rather in a suitable degree of regulation and control, and in the cultivation of a spirit of common decency among people in line more or less with the spirit and purpose of the golden rule.

Wealth is only one of the subjects in respect to which human relations have been wont to clash, just as love and hatred, veneration and indifference, religion, politics and human control have always served to affect human emotions. Our hearts may well go out in sincere sympathy to the multitudes of people in various walks of life, whose welfare is dependent upon, or who are burdened with the responsibility of supporting and living with, or associating with other people who are worthless, irresponsible or in- reasonable degree of control. compatible.

The evils which surround wealth grow more essentially out of its 'abuse' what people do what people do with respect to wealth, just as many other abuses occur with respect to the misuse of things, It is unquestionably true that much of the antagonism towards wealth develops out of a spirit of jealousy in the minds of certain people. Jealousy, envy, covetousness, vindictiveness and malice are natural human characteristics, and like poisonous gases are to be controlled or avoided. Iron castings are the finished product at the foundry, and raw material in the machine shop and steel rails, structural shapes, wire, sheets, strips, etc., are the finished product at the steel plant. 

A similar rule applies to all natural resources, so far as they are reduced to ownership and control by man, including products of the soil, animal husbandry, etc. And so the process goes forward- all of these various finished products becoming raw material in countless other industries and enterprises. 

As they pass on through the channels of trade they may be changed in form and characteristics and united with other materials, and made into new designs, or adapted to new uses, resulting in a vast expansion and distribution of opportunity, opening out like a fan, creating new markets for man-power, brain-power, mechanical power and equipment, transportation, communication and financemarkets where more and more people may exchange their effort and skill, and the things which they have produced or acquired, for whatever else may be essential or desirable to satisfy their own wants. own wants. All of which becomes a labyrinth of human activities, affording a livelihood for the masses, enabling growth, development, expansion, education, culture, comfort and pleasure away beyond what was available to the people of earlier days.

It is through such activities that the principles and forces of economics perform their varied functions - wherein wealth is produced, distributed, accumulated and destroyed, and where the human element will range anywhere from the highest order of honor and culture, wisdom, thrift and frugality, down to blundering stupidity, improvidence and reckless shiftlessness.

It has been widely claimed that, in the end, everything has to be paid for by the consumer class-with a subtle intimation that the consumer class is made up essentially of wage-earners. 

In the first place, while more and more people are getting into the wage-earner class, a vast proportion of the total purchasing population is essentially outside of that class - individual

consumers who are not wageearners, and do not get their resources from the pay envelope. 

Many of them will have vastly more buying-power than those who depend upon wages earned. 

The greatest factor in the production and distribution of new wealth is the interchange of economic activities among business enterprises supplying the wants of each other through the building and equipment of mills and factories, business blocks, and the equipment and supplies used in transportation, communication, merchandising, finance, etc. It is only through the functioning of private enterprise that it becomes possible for the masses to make use of those facilities. Private enterprise puts buying-power into the hands of the greatest number of people because it operates on a larger scale than an individual.

Under ordinary or normal conditions the greater the activities of private enterprise the more wealth will be produced and distributed.

The buying which is done by private individuals will be small in comparison, because the consumer demand, particularly among the wage-earner class will be on a smaller scale. Some private enterprises, of course, will depend absolutely upon the business which comes from the wage-earner class. Those people will naturally be greatly interested in the prosperity of their customers, and may contribute more or less influence and activity in their behalf.

The financing of business extensions and expansions does not come from the wage-earner class, but it does come from 'accumulated resources and from borrowing'-usually through stock and bond issues, etc. And those borrowings are not repaid, immediately or remotely, from proceeds received from the wageearner class except as wage-earners, along with other people and institutions, invest their resources in stocks and bonds.

Some of the debt is paid from earnings of the enterprise, but the balance simply passes out into oblivion through the fluctuation in the prices for securities, and through repudiation, mergers, etc. A vast amount of debt is never paid. The wealth which it once represented has ceased to exist. 'Private enterprise' will find its application throughout the entire range of economic activities, involving service, produc-

tion, marketing, transportation, communication, research, etc. 

Service may be individual, group, corporate or institutional in form. Any of these forms of service, or combinations of them, may become the subject of exchange between people, and therefore will come under the operation of the broad principle of supply and demand, except where sentiment or the emotions sometimes serve to bring about a shifting of otherwise natural conditions, so as to promote, or defeat, the ever-varying purposes and interests of mankind. 

When we turn our attention to the number of private enterprises in and about such places as New York, Chicago, Philadelphia, Los Angeles, Detroit and Cleveland, and along through the hundreds and thousands of cities, towns, villages and country crossroads activities throughout the length and breadth of the country- and including the vast extent of agricultural, mining, exploration and research activities we find private enterprise rolling up to stupendous proportions alongside of the activities of those people who may be classed more strictly as wage-earners.

It may be assumed that almost everybody starts his career as a wage-earner of some sort, but it is not at all essential that one should always remain wage-earner in a country where there are so many opportunities and incentives for engaging in private enterprise. 

Every activity of private enterprise creates a market wherein other people have an opportunity to sell things, rights or service, or to participate directly or indirectly in such resulting profits as there may happen to be. Wealth fluctuates in volume and value in the process of marketing.

To such extent as private enterprise flourishes there will be automatically an increasing distribution of buying-power through the hands of the wageearner and all classes of people. * * * 

Both the increase and decline of wealth are most rapid and in greatest volume through fluctuations in the value of those things which constitute 'capital' property, such as land, stocks, bonds, etc. The rise in security prices between March 1st and 

August 1st, 1933, is said to have added between sixteen and seventeen billion dollars to the

value of the stocks and bonds listed on the New York Stock 

Exchange-to say nothing about the increase in the value of other property during the same period. All of that increase in value caused a corresponding increase in the purchasing power, or working capital, of the masses or general public and a very substantial increase in business activity and up-swing in the trend of the times. 

When it is protested that those higher values were not real, the answer will be that they were as real as any other 'market' values, because any of the stocks or bonds could have been sold at or about the August 1st prices just as hats, shoes or blankets could have been sold at current prices and the proceeds would have been available for passage through the channels of trade in the payment of taxes and other debts, or for reinvestment, or in the purchase of commodities, etc. If sold on the basis of those so-called paper values, the proceeds would be just as real as though they had resulted from the sale of something else at the current market price or on the auction block.

As a matter of fact, it does not matter how many of those securities were sold, or were not sold, for whatever is on the market has to be owned by 'somebody'a change of ownership may not be a matter of consequence to anybody but the parties to the transaction. The really important thing is the psychological effect which will be produced upon the morale of the people due to the rising prices for capital-property. The consciousness that one is now worth more than he was before, and has a more substantial backlog of financial resources, gives him a feeling of confidence and the spirit to expand his business activities, which, in turn, naturally would have the effect of stimulating the demand for land, buildings, equipment and for commodities, services, etc., whereby other people will have greater opportunities for doing business with an expectation of increasing their own earnings.

Decline of Wealth 

Wealth loses its character as such to the extent that it gets into things which have a lower order of value, or as current values decline, or through the evervarying attitude of mind and the circumstances of the people from day to day. 

Wealth passes out and ceases to exist along with the processes of consumption, wearing out, obsolescence and destruction.

The property itself may continue, and the property right, or ownership, may continue, but the economic value of each will decline to the extent that the property cannot be sold or exchanged for something else. 

Economic wealth may be in the form of accumulated resources, more or less enduring in their nature; or it may have but a current passing existence, as in the case of things which are consumed or destroyed approximately as soon as produced, such as some forms of food, for example or as in the case of a passing service, entertainment or pastime. Service may have no value beyond the moment of its completion, like running an er- 

rand, carrying a bag, etc. or it may enter on a prolonged journey through the channels of trade. 

Buying-power may be sustained legitimately through production and exchange, through exploration, research and discovery, through development of natural resources, through gifts and inheritances; and, in the case of a government, by taxation or levy of duties; or it may be sustained temporarily by borrowing. Borrowed resources represent temporary wealthwhich eventually must be either repaid, or repudiated. 

When buying-power represents borrowed capital out of proportion to vested resources or earning power or productive ability, then repudiation in whole or in part may be inevitable.

There are many things which certain people can do to protect themselves against the vicissitudes of their economic relations, as well as against the inclemency of the weather - just as, on the other hand, many will be able to take advantage of weather conditions and the trend of the times to promote their economic welfare.

When buying-power is exchanged for something which is presently consumed or destroyed, or for something which cannot be subsequently sold or exchanged for as much or more than it cost, then the collective or gross wealth of the country will be reduced by that much. 

As every dollar of 'indebtedness' represents just that much 'wealth' in the hands of the creditor, it follows that the repudiation of debt destroys a corresponding amount of wealth belonging to somebody else. A vast amount of wealth passes out of existence by that process. 

For the reason that wealth is continually passing out of existence through various processes it becomes necessary to create new or further wealth in order that the affairs of the people can continue to be carried on. The replacement of consumption and waste forms a very large part of the economic opportunities and activities of the people. If there were no consumption, wearingout or destruction, there would be no continuing 'demand' or outlet for 'production'. For example, assuming that our wants are all supplied and that nothing is consumed or wears out, and that nothing is destroyed, then naturally there would be no market for further production or service other than to provide for such increase in population as there may happen to be, and to provide for the utilization of new developments. It is the growth of economic resources that enables a healthy expansion of living conditions. It is as essential that the gross wealth of the country should be continuously reproduced or replenished, as that vegetation should be reproduced.

The conditions which affect values, causing them to increase or decline, are so varied and widely scattered that the resulting gross wealth will be about as variable and uncertain as in the case of anything which depends for its status upon weather conditions. There will be no such thing as total gross wealth, except in a strictly academic sense, any more than there could be any such thing as total gross 'weather'. The total which will be available to some individual, or corporation, group, association or government will have nothing more than a contingent working connection with the total gross wealth of the mass, because of the difference in the fundamental relations between the individual, or a group unit,

and the rest of the world. Statisticians can play around with the idea of total gross wealth by limiting themselves to the consideration of certain kinds or classes of wealth- and even then the relation of those certain items will be so changeable and uncertain that they may have a very far-flung influence upon other items or phases of wealth. 

When I make use of the term 'masses' it is intended to mean the same thing as 'general or total population'.

The destiny of wealth is exceedingly precarious, due to the character of its flow. There is no assurance that fortunes built up through hard work and the practice of self-denial will not be used up, frittered away or exhausted in gambling, or through various other forms of loose living. It may happen on the other hand that fortunes acquired through evil methods will be employed in very righteous and worthy purposes everything everything will depend upon who happens to own or control the wealth during its period of existence. 

The wage-earner's 'daily effort' is a passing asset, as precarious in its value to him as a perishable commodity in the hands of a merchant.

If some- body did not work yesterday that opportunity, that potential asset, has been lost forever. If he works tomorrow and does the things he might have done yesterday, it will be 'tomorrow's' work which he will be selling, not 'yesterday's'. A person may have escaped the doing of a certain amount or kind of work, but unless he has made some other valuable use of his time and talents he will be that much poorer than he might have been. 

He will have lost the opportunity to convert that much of his earning power into economic resources. It is not to be expected that people will want to sell the last degree of their effort and skill. Some will need to sell a larger portion than others. It will be a matter to be governed largely by individual necessity, desire or opportunity, having due consideration with respect to ability, physical endurance, aims and objects in life, family and other responsibilities. This rule applies to all types and phases of human service. Consideration for physical fatigue, health and endurance would apply also to animal service. But it is not logical or reasonable that human or animal effort, speed or endurance should be calibrated in terms of mechanical equipment.

71 It would be a travesty upon the natural freedom of mankind and upon vested human rights, if the terms and conditions attending the conversion of service into buying-power should be regulated by law, custom or a coercive invasion on the part of various groups of people. It would be an unwarranted restriction upon human liberty, and an over-stepping of the normal domain and function of government and the comity of human relations in the realm of civilization. The terms and conditions of service should be subject to the voluntary control and agreement between the people involved just as the prices, terms and conditions of any other purchase and sale should be subject to the voluntary agreement between the parties to the transaction, as long as there is no evidence of fraud or misrepresentation. There may be no more reason for regimenting the wage-earners and the farmers than that other business activities should be regimented. 

The earning power growing out of use and occupation is as precarious and perishable as that which is associated with manpower, because it passes out along with the passing of time, just as happens in the case of any power or force which is lost because it is not used. Every vacant dwelling, store and office space every idle mill and factory-together with idle equipment pertaining to production, transportation, communication or other purposes, represents facilities for use and occupation, and a degree of earning power, which is lost with each passing day that it is not used. The same rule will apply to the loaning of money. No new wealth is created out of business which is not done.

The value of property-wealth will be whatever it happens to be from day to day, depending upon how the attitude of mind of the people falls into balance as between buyers and sellers, borrowers and lenders, etc. The wealth which is represented by money will depend upon the government administration in conjunction with the trend of the times. The wealth which is represented by credit will be even more flexible, depending upon the readiness and ability of the debtors to discharge their obligations and responsibilities.

Hence the total gross wealth within the nation will not be easily estimated, nor susceptible of any accurate computation be-

cause of its more or less precarious and uncertain state of fluctuation, and due also to the questionable character and temporary existence of propertywealth and credit. That is to say, none of those forms of wealth will hold still long enough to be measured and correlated in conjunction with each other in terms of a grand total. 

The wealth of the world billows up and billows down from time to time, in one locality or another, with a degree of variation and uncertainty that is more or less comparable to changing weather conditions. 

This of course is something is something which must be expected, due to the variable activities of the people as they engage day by day in the development and discharge of rights, opportunities, obligations and responsibilities. 

Buying-power, or wealth, as long as it lasts, must be owned by 'somebody', and hence will accumulate here and there in the possession of individuals, groups, corporations, institutions, etc., and flow hither and yon under the influence of the various activities of the people more or less as water accumulates in reservoirs and lakes, and flows from place to place drawn by gravity, with the ocean as its great ultimate storage reservoir.

All of the wealth of the masses or total population will be invested in or consist of those things which constitute property-wealth, or money, or credit; and the credit will consist of all of the vast array of debts and financial obligations and responsibilities of other people, corporations and institutions, together with the bonded and other debts of government, federal, state and municipal bearing in mind the fact that every dollar of indebtedness represents that much wealth in the hands of the creditor. resources. 

Whatever the total amount of wealth in a country may be, it will have little significance beyond its potential status as a huge impersonal back-ground of 

More or less of it will be subject to taxation by the various political authorities of the country, but, because of its vast diversification of individual and group ownership, it will not be subject to the use or control of the people as a whole. 

Portions of it will be available to support, or to defeat, some certain activity or ideal to such extent as people unite at any time for such purpose.

Its use and control will be essentially

in the hands of those individuals and groups who happen to own it each one according to what he has, and what he wants to do with it. 

The wealth of past ages was consumed or exhausted in feeding, clothing and sheltering the people of each passing period it was used also in the development and support of literature 

and art, music and architecture, refinement and culture, and in the support of religious and political activities, the waging of wars and the building of empires, and it passed out and ceased to exist along with the people, the things and conditions with which it had been associated.

Incentive, Flow and Accumulation- 

OTHING ever happens in this world without the operation of an impulse, an instinct or incentive of some sort, or through the functioning of other laws or processes of nature which if carried far enough would amount to the same thing.

This will be true in the case of inanimate things as well as within the realm of life, growth and decay. A shell placed upon a ledge of rock would stay there for countless ages unless removed by the impulse of some force, if in the meantime it did not disintegrate through the operation of some natural chemical reaction, or wear away through the process of erosion. 

The incentive or impulse may not always be reasoned out, or planned - something may happen as an accident resulting from a combination of circumstances; but the combination of circumstances will be the impulse which caused the accident to happen. 

Impulses and reactions have been provided in human and animal life just as they have been provided in the realm of physics, manifesting themselves in more or less constantly recurring cycles, alternating and overlapping, such as, for instance, hunger and satisfaction, comfort and distress, joy and sorrow, necessity, goodwill, and the fascination of adventure, exploration, research and the glory of accomplishment.

Instinct will be comparable to a chemical reaction or other process of nature operating spontaneously whenever certain conditions come into conjunction with each other. 

Just as water and electricity must flow in order to do any work, so also that form of wealth which is known as 'circulating medium' consisting of money or credit, will perform its most essential service when travelling through the channels of trade. 

No matter how much buyingpower may be in existence in the hands of individuals, groups and institutions, it will have only a potential value, so far as the public is concerned, until it is passed along into the channels of trade through buying or lending, gift or inheritance, through the payment of taxes, or in support of various projects — economic, civic, religious, benevolent or educational, or for exploration, research, etc. 

It is the circulating medium which serves as a flux between supply and demand. It facilitates the purchase and sale of those things which constitute 'property-wealth'. Obviously, both property-wealth and the circulating medium will be flowing actually or constructively at the same time, but in opposite directions. In the channels of trade both will have the characteristics of buying-power-each will be used for buying the other. 

Water flows by gravity, due to the difference in elevation if there were no difference in elevation there would be no flow. 

There must be a motivating impulse or incentive to cause the movement of 'anything' 

- SO, in the realm of economics, those things which are passing in the channels of trade will flow in the direction of the greater demand. The demand will be influenced by the fact that the thing, service or right will be worth more to the buyer than to the seller, just as on the other hand the money or credit will be worth more to the seller than the things, service or right.

Thus it is apparent that both the buyer and the seller has his own individual incentive - - otherwise there would be no exchange. 

Buying-power in motion is going somewhere — somebody gets the use of it, and like the flow of water or of an electric current it can be utilized for turning the wheels of progress and performing work at every

stage of its movement. It can also be likened to the process of irrigation, nourishing growth and production wherever other things are in favorable conjunction. 

The first and most impelling economic incentive lies in the necessity for man to make a living-to engage in production, service, accumulation and exchange, for pay, gain or profit, in order to provide food, clothing and shelter and such other things as may be necessary or desirable for promoting the wellbeing of himself and others who may be dependent upon him, as well as to promote, protect and develop such other activities and conditions as may be of interest to him. The incentive will be supplemented and enlarged by the vast array of human interests and aspirations such as ambition, pride, honor, philanthropy, curiosity, love, hate, greed, etc. Incentives may be voluntary, or they may be induced through necessity, discipline, coercion, fear or some form of despotism. The coercive appeal may be expected to affect the emotions rather than reason. 

A so-called 'living' may be on any scale between the two extremes, depending upon the resources, tastes and inclination of the people. A living may range from bare subsistence on up to the most affluent circumstances. 

A scale of living which would be satisfactory to one person would be regarded as starvation by another, or affluence by still another. Two persons may be approaching the same level of existence-one coming down and the other moving upwardwith very different emotions. So far as the individual is concerned, everything depends upon his attitude of mind. 

But it is not enough that buying-power should flow the next, or other, most important thing is that it should accumulate and grow, developing greater volume, so that it may do more work, render greater service, and enable the transaction of business on a larger scale by a greater number of people, and also that it may provide for the replacement or replenishment of consumption, wearing out, destruction and waste. or In the same sense that a reservoir of water represents latent potential possibilities of power, so a reserve of wealth or buying-power represents latent or potential economic possibilities. Such accumulation not only brings security against

want, but usually provides a means of acquiring further resources. In the United States that is a right which is open to all-to each person according to his respective abilities and reaction to his opportunities. The storing up of economic resources develops 'potential' power, but, like any other force it does not perform any work until it flows. 

While the storing up of economic resources will develop power in the hands of individuals and groups, its flow, on the other hand, promotes the welfare of those people into whose hands it goes. If water were not stored up behind dams, valves, etc., we never would have the benefit of water-power in excess of that derived from the current flow of the stream. 

When one spends his money as fast as he gets it, he never will develop a reserve of economic power, but will have to depend upon whatever the subsequent flow if any may happen to be as it passes through his hands.

Assurance of a continuous adequate flow of buying-power, however, would be comparable to 'accumulated wealth'. person will get beyond the treadmill stage until he accumulates economic resources. Buying-power flows through the hands of some people just as No water would flow through loose, sandy soil.

People are learning about the importance of regulating and controlling the flow of water, and its conservation, not only where it is to furnish power to be used in the service of man, but as well for flood control and protection against the ravages of erosion and other forms of flood waste - millions of dollars are being spent for the purpose of correcting that sort of waste. 

The loss of soil through the process of erosion has its counterpart in economics-just as an excessive flow of water may cause a wash-out, about the same thing may happen as a result of an excessive flow of buyingpower. A vast amount of soil has found its way to the bottom of streams, lakes and the ocean through the process of erosion, and a vast amount of wealth has passed out of existence through extravagance in consumption, wearing out, obsolescence, destruction, unproductive activities, the failure of business enterprises and the repudiation of debt. There is no fixed and enduring stability of either soil or wealth.

In the redistribution of soil through the process of erosion, some of it will be deposited

where it can be used again by the people who happen to have the use of the land where the silt was deposited, but all the rest will be lost there will be that much less soil available for the use of man. About the same thing will happen through a socalled redistribution of wealth, according to what becomes of it - whether it flows into growth, or into economic waste.

It will be much easier to regulate the flow of water, however, than to regulate the flow of buying-power, especially that which belongs to other people, because, where there is such a widespread equality of 'rights', as in a democratic form of government, there will be less respect for coercive control which might interfere with the normal and natural right of the people to manage their own affairs. 

Water remains in some places for a long time, because there is no chance for it to seep away.

This condition has its counterpart among people whose wants are relatively few, and there is no urge to spend their money. 

They may not necessarily be hoarders or misers, at that they will spend their money freely enough for whatever they want, but if their wants happen to be limited they will not permit the fact to worry them.

As the various forms of wealth or buying-power develop very rapidly, and to great volume, during periods of unusual business prosperity, it becomes should be some more or less important that there practical means for safeguarding those resources against untimely and unnecessary waste.

This subject will be discussed further under the title of 

'Debt'. 

Wealth passes out of existence at such a stupendous rate that its replenishment will have to be at an equal rate in order to neutralize the shrinkage. 

Buying-power or wealth may be stored up in one sense, and flowing in another sense, as when it is invested in securities; so far as the holder of the securities is concerned it is stored up, but the price paid for the securities passes along and will be active in the hands of other people—just as in the case of bank deposits, where the resources of the individual depositor will be stored up, subject to his call, yet in the meantime they will be scattered in the channels of trade. Buyingpower will be either stored up

or flowing according to what the people, individually or collectively, find occasion to do with such resources as happen to be at their command.

The flow of water is replenished through the rotation of certain processes of nature, such as evaporation and condensation, resulting in rainfall; whereupon it flows by the force of gravity through such channels and courses as may be available, tending always toward lower levels. Wealth is renewed through the functioning of human relations, involving production, invention, discovery, marketing and various forms of service, when, and to the extent that people engage in the process of supplying the wants of each other, and through the shifting relations between supply and demand. Individual wealth is increased also through the conversion of dollars into propertywealth on rising markets, and is conserved by transferring property-wealth into dollars on falling markets.

The flow of buying-power in some instances is similar to the movement of the tides, where it flows out and returns through substantially the same channels, as in banking and in various commercial activities.

All down through the ages vast suffering has been caused by the exhaustion of the supply of water. The effect has been disastrous in many ways. And the same thing will be true with respect to the exhaustion of economic resources. The distress will affect individuals, groups and nations. 

Neither water nor electricity will increase or renew itself merely by flowing, and the same thing will be true with respect to wealth. 'Property-wealth' is the only kind or phase of wealth which changes in value-it is bought at one price and sold at anotheranother higher or lower. And those values do not change in the process of flowing-the change in value occurs in the hands of the current owner or holder, before or after the flow that is to say, between the successive flows. The circulating medium may change in volume, but not in its unit value — except as standards of value are changed by arbitrary governmental action, whereupon the new units will represent greater value or less value in relation to gold or other basic values. The units do not change in value in the process of trade, as in the case of property wealth. If the unit value did change in the

course of trade, then the change would have universal application, whereas any apparent change will apply only to those things which have 'changed in price' - everything else will be on the same basis as before the apparent change. This characteristic will be treated more in detail in the discussion 

'Standards of Value'. 

of The term private enterprise applies to business activities which are privately owned, operated and controlled, in contrast to public-owned or operated activities. 

Private enterprise is a producer of wealth, while a government, on the other hand, consumes or redistributes the wealth which it gets from the people. 

Public money is not likely to flow, to any great extent, into things which will grow into greater economic wealth. 

Private enterprise is supposed to be self-supporting out of the proceeds, profit and new wealth arising out of the business itself it will be a wealth-producing activity. In governmental activities, on the other hand, there is not likely to be any outstanding incentive for economic profit for the benefit of the tax-paying public who will have to bear the cost-it will be supported wholly or in part out of taxes rather than its own earnings. In public enterprises the element of economical and profitable operation will be subordinated to civic and political purposes - wherein the money of somebody else is being spent, and where there will be no goal of individual ownership in the resulting accomplishment.

Where something belongs to 'everybody', as in the case of public property, it may be said. that it does not belong to 'anybody' there will be no incentive, as in the case of private ownership, to inspire or support a spirit of responsibility with respect to its safe-keeping, preservation or enhancement in value. 

Conservation and accumula tion is an individual responsibility. The errors, extravagance and waste on the part of the individual, group or corporation, contribute to the enrichment of the masses. The wealth is lost by the individual, but the replacement of the property creates business for the masses.

Public waste becomes a burden upon individuals. The loss of wealth resulting from waste or the destruction of the property may, or may not, be a serious matter, depending upon the economic status of the individual. 

This is another instance illus-

trating the distinction between the individual, or his group, and the rest of the world. 

The economic incentive for pay or profit belongs essentially to the individual as distinguished from the collective or impersonal general public. In that respect the corporation, group, association or institution will be in about the same position as the individual. Private incentive is a natural force which cannot be ignored any more than we can afford to ignore any other force or process in nature. Public reaction to private gain, and individual reaction to public gain will be, as a rule, more or less disinterested with respect to each other. This is a very natural condition which of course will have many exceptions, due to the influence of other incentives and forces.

So, in the matter of public activities it may be well to avoid confusion between probable incentives-whether they are based primarily upon a desire for public welfare or for private gain.

It has been argued that a government-owned or controlled enterprise can operate more economically than a private enterprise because, among other things, it does not have to pay taxes. The public enterprise is not supposed to make a profit, hence it follows that the public does not get any revenue out of the enterprise in the form of taxes, but frequently has to use tax money collected from other people to support the enterprise. 

The public enterprise becomes, therefore, just something more to be supported by the tax-payers, and it will have the effect of holding back the production of new wealth.

The movement of public money represents a forced circulation of buying-power-like the forced flow of water by the process of pumping and distribution. The money is collected by force through certain forms of taxation, fees, licenses, etc., and is sprayed out again over the masses in payment of the manifold expenses of government and political projects and experiments, subsidies, pensions, the support of the army and navy, certain forms of transportation, communication and other things which are supposed to promote the public welfare. very large amount of public money is paid out each year for interest on the public debt which has come into existence because various governmental bodies and

organizations have in the past reached out into the future through the process of borrowing to procure money for their current use.

When government money flows into things which have a lower subsequent value, or into things which are presently consumed, worn out or destroyed, there will be a redistribution of wealth along with a probable decline in the gross resources of the country. 

Public buildings and other public works may have cost vast sums of money, but regardless of how much they cost, or how great their civic or other values may be, their economic value will depend upon their current 'exchange relation' in the channels of trade-according to what use can be made of them, and what somebody would be willing to pay, just as in the case of anything else. A monument or courthouse would not have a market value in the channels of trade which would be comparable with that of a business block or a factory building. 

Municipalities and nations will be faced with the problem of deciding how far they can afford to go in the sacrifice of economic values in behalf of vaposes 

rious civic and political pur- -just as individuals, families and groups will be faced with a similar problem with respect to their economic resources and living or cultural conditions. Of course, it is something which will vary according to the sufficiency and security of the economic status. 

The higher order of living and cultural conditions will be desirable, but economic resources will be an unavoidable necessity as a physical support for whatever other conditions may be desired.

The spendthrift sacrifices economic security for passing comfort and enjoyment, while the miser sacrifices comfort and enjoyment in behalf of economic gain. Individuals and nations will be encountering similar problems.

There will always be a question as to whether one will be satisfied to live within his resources, or whether he will endeavor to live up to his ambitions. One way will mean security, while the other may be extremely hazardous.

Aside from some element of chance, management is unquestionably the most important factor in the development of new wealth, through the medium of

business activity, as when a man with vision, courage and initiative conceives a business enterprise, arranges for the necessary capital, and with it buys certain material at the current market rate, and employs certain labor also at the current market rate; then by bringing the money, the material and the labor together he causes something new to be produced — something which did not exist before-something which he may be able to go out into the world and sell, at a profit. It is this profit or increase in resources that constitutes the new wealth that has been created, and it was he who created it, through his initiative in first conceiving the business enterprise, then by bringing together the necessary factors involved in its accomplishment, and marketing the product under conditions which yielded him a profit. If he does not have the necessary working capital he will be obliged to arrange for the use of the resources of other people. It is the spirit of business sagacity on the part of individuals, groups and corporations which determines when and where various activities should be carried on.

Wealth is also increased or destroyed through the fluctuations in supply and demand, and those changes in value will grow out of what people do or don't do, and what their attitude of mind may happen to be along with the passing of time and circumstance. In other words, there would be no economic values except for the interplay between human relations. 

The only wealth which is created by public enterprise, as a rule, is that which accrues to private enterprise through the sale of things, conditions and service to or on the behalf of the government. But taxation overdone has the effect of sapping the economic resources of the people. While the wealth so collected is paid out again by the government, it is not likely to go to the same people who paid the taxes. Such personal advantage or recompense might accrue to the individual taxpayer out of the prosperity of other people, or of the general public, will be intangible, and is not likely to be even remotely proportionate to what he pays out in taxes it will be lost in the impersonality of the public. 

This will be an example of the difference or distinction between an individual and the mass or total population. The individual relation or attitude will be as

personal with respect to his own property, in contrast to something which belongs to an impersonal general public, or to other people.

The individual freedom which people are supposed to have under a democratic form of government should logically carry with it the responsibility of selfreliance, except for a reasonable comity of relations between individuals and the general public in harmony with the spirit of civilization. In a democratic country paternalism obviously should be held to a reasonable minimum. There is always a question as to the justification of taxing the public for the purpose of promoting the welfare of certain private enterprises, or of certain portions of the people. 

The absence of an economic incentive involving the prospect of a profit, may be regarded as a most logical reason for the vast amount of wastefulness which so frequently occurs in Government work. 

Economic activities growing out of various incentives may be more or less interwoven and dependent upon each other, and it is important that they should have ample opportunity to function through and by the natural forces and processes of supply and demand, restrained only by an orderly sense of decency and fair dealing. It is always desirable to have a substantial flow of buying-power passing through the hands of as wide a range of people as possible - particularly in a period of business depression, but it is not essential that it should be confined to any particular class. 

There will be no great element of equality in the results, because there will be no equality in the functioning of the people, nor in the conditions under which they operate the results will be according to what each person happens to get, or fails to get, out of the current flow of events. The status of the people is so varied, interwoven and overlapping that the prosperity and happiness of some will be promoted by conditions which will bring distress to others.

The destruction of certain property may be a hardship upon the owner, but will bring a profit to those other people who sell service or materials employed in its replacement.

The flow of buying-power becomes an impelling force which stimulates production so far as there is a prospective market,

and production' and consumption in turn tend to induce the flow of buying-power. We are thus brought into a rotation of natural impulses which vitalize and nourish a civilization. 

Unequal Distribution of Wealth

HE very marked inequality which always has existed in the distribution, or ownership, of wealth in any civilization has been, and probably always will be, the subject of much discussion. In the first place, wealth is not something to be 'distributed', in the sense that corn is thrown to the chickens, or the passing out of rations to the hungry. Economic wealth has to be created, largely through production, service, marketing, finance, development, transportation, etc., and as long as it survives it passes along from one to another in the process of exchange-buying and selling, borrowing and lending — and through gift, inheritance, payment of taxes, etc. It varies from time to time in volume, value, rate of flow and accumulation according to place and the ever-shifting relations of people with respect to each other. In order to maintain an equality in its distribution it would be necessary to make a re-distribution every day. The impracticability of such a distribution and re-distribution is obvious. 

It is obvious, also, that one must have something to sell in order to get the money or buying-power-which he needs or wants hence he must produce, serve, guard and accumulate his resources. It is not to be expected that the general public are going to supply very much for the benefit of other people without being paid for it. Money flows according to the wants of the buyer and the seller respectively.

If it were possible to have an equal distribution of all wealth

it might be expected to do more toward breaking down the character of the masses than to improve it. If one knew that he would participate in a free distribution of the wealth of the country every day he would have no incentive to engage in any wealth-producing activity.

Without necessities to be provided for, or comforts or pleasures or ambitions to be gratified, relatively few people would participate in very many of the manifold activities which are necessary for the ordinary functioning of a civilization. With out such an impelling influence it is not reasonable to expect that they would put forth the effort, or assume the risks involved in such undertakings. 

Economic activities would lose their motivating incentives. All of those things have to be done by 'somebody', and they usually will be done by people who either 'have' to do them in order to make a living, or by others who see an opportunity to make a better living or to acquire greater wealth and the multitude of advantages which it makes possible. Just as in the case of air, weather conditions, etc., whatever is free to everybody would have no exchange value. 

If somebody should suggest 'a stabilized income to everybody'- to be derived from current economic activities, the answer would be that no such thing would be possible because those activities do not function with uniformity or stability, their purpose being merely to serve the variable current wants of the masses. Business in general cannot be calibrated to produce specific or predetermined results except in a more or less relative degree. Where conditions are variable the results will be variable also. Any compensating elements will have to come later on as another operation, to use a machine shop expression or it may become a function of another enterprise. 

A further difficulty would be found in the vast array of interwoven, overlapping and conflicting natural or normal rights of such a great number of people. 

Economic activities will not always be stimulated, however, by the flow of buying-power. 

For example, when some people have acquired sufficient resources to enable them to live in accordance with their conception of comfort and happiness, they will relax their productive effort.

This will be an absolutely natural and logical reaction, and it is one of their normal rights. 

Some will reach that point at a lower level than others. It is amazing to find what a low level will satisfy some people. Many may wish they could have a higher level, if it did not involve any additional effort, self-denial or discipline.

It is probable that most people are not likely to do a great deal more than they have to do in order to accomplish their purpose. This is in harmony with the theory that action and reaction are equal- and that the effort will balance with the desire. It probably is given to relatively few people to appreciate the joy of work for its own sake, for the pleasure and satisfaction of accomplishment. 

People will naturally forge or drift into their normal or logical economic or social level, according to their varying abilities, individual characteristics and reaction to their environment and opportunities except as they are aided, or held back, by external conditions or circumstances.

Each one will have his own peculiar problems of life to be worked out, and it must be done, to a greater or less extent, in his own way, with each individual finding his own level or status.

As some fish will work their way upstream against the force of the running water, so certain people will struggle up through the various forces in civilization because of the motives which attract or impel them. 

Many appear to have no desire to develop themselves or to improve their circumstances, particularly if it calls for effort or sacrifice upon their part- and in some instances even where effort or sacrifice would be unnecessary.

Enthusiasm may be just as ardent whether inspired by emotion or founded upon fact so many people will rush in to do things which are contrary to natural laws-things which God 

Himself would not undertake such as attempting to bring about a universal equality among human beings. 

Apparently there are relatively few things in this world which have any substantial degree of equality in the matter of distribution, particularly outside of gravity and certain characteristics of various natural forces and processes. Water is not equally distributed, for one reason, because of the topography of the earth. It is a

characteristic of water that it flows to the lower level, as electricity flows to the lower potential, and as other things flow in the direction of the least resistance or the greatest pull. There is no equality in the distribution of electricity, or vegetation, or the weather. There may be no reason why there should be any equality in the distribution of certain other things including wealth, or economic resources, particularly when one considers the varying characteristics of the people, their environment and the different kinds of forces and incentives to which they are subjected.

We may ponder over the differences in human conditions and relations, and try to figure out a blending which would tend to eliminate or reduce those differences. But when the process is completed there would likely be no comprehensive or workable classification of 'people'. It would be a classification of conditions, rather than of people.

Furthermore, human relations could not be stabilized in accordance with such a blending of classifications, because people are not likely to remain for long in any regimented relation to each other in a country which recognizes the principle of individual and group freedom. 

Of course, there always will be a certain amount of irresponsible regimentation or classification in the matter of society, politics, religion, fraternal association, etc., among people who are drawn together because of certain interests which they hold in common with each other in the case of certain systems of caste which exist in various parts of the world. 

or It is because wants and methods differ among people and places that wealth or buyingpower accumulates here and there in varying degree, like the 'balance of trade' between localities or countries. 

A distribution of wealth is going forward continuously through the flow of buyingpower back and forth throughout the world, in payment for production, service, materials, taxes, inheritances, benevolences and the multitude of other things which form the subject of exchange among people.

Every church and every monument, bridge and city street, every mill, factory and warehouse represents a distribution of buying-power among the people in exchange for the materials, equipment, service, etc.,

which were involved in its construction. 

During the past few years enough people have been working, serving, producing and exchanging thereby acquiring money, property and credits, which they have been spending for a multitude of different purposes which have created economic activities for other people, so that there has resulted a prosperity never before equalled in this Country. This resulting prosperity has been wide-spread because the spending has been wide-spread for things which have been consumed, worn out or destroyed, or otherwise taken out of the market, or for passing comforts, pleasure, entertainment, education, travel, etc.

Or, the money has been paid out in taxes or in aid of public enterprises, or for the support of charities and philanthropies of various kinds. It is only because the process has been carried along on such a large scale that general prosperity of so great a magnitude has been possible.

The aim should be to make possible enough diversity of economic activities so that facilities may be available to all of the people to carry on their ordinary or normal activities without unnecessary or unwarranted interference or control on the part of other people. Then, as a rule, people will take care of themselves through production, service and exchange wherever they find an adequate market, among themselves or elsewhere, for such activities. The greater the opportunities and the incentives for private enterprise in any country the larger population that country will be able to support.

There are other natural forces constantly at work which tend to promote the distribution of wealth-as, when certain people come into possession of greater resources than they have been accustomed to have, their appreciation of their value declines, and wealth does not remain long in weak or inattentive hands. This tends to prevent those people from becoming too rich, and makes it easier for others to acquire the escaping resources. Many people spend their resources as though each day was the last they expected to live. 

If people had a greater dread of the horrors of adversity it does seem as though they would take greater precautions to avoid doing the things which are likely to bring on adversity. The prob-

ability is that they do not look ahead nor take enough interest in their future to select a safe path to comfort and security.

An amount of wealth or resources which would enable some people to live in comfort, would make a fool of certain other people. It is wonderful to see how little it takes to cause some people to lose their self-control. 

It is not possible to eliminate poverty by destroying its habitation.

In any great civilization there will be all of the different kinds, degrees and grades of people, from the highest order of human development, culture and character, down to the lowest, the most sordid- the waste dregs of humanity. Probably most 

Probably most people will rank somewhere between those extremes. Some of the lower order can be improved but the results will have natural limitations-first, by the ability, spirit and character of the people engaged in carrying on the work of improvement; second, by the intelligence and character of the people who are supposed to be benefited-it it is not to be expected that they will rise very far above their own limitations; and third, the environment may or may not be suitable for getting the desired results. Education, of certain kinds, may help enormously, but it is not to be expected that it will be effective in developing, rebuilding and reconstructing human intelligence and character beyond certain limitations. 

Education does not always develop intelligence. An educated fool will be more of a menace than one who is uneducated. It will not matter how well educated an automobile driver may be if he is irresponsible - he will be more of a menace in traffic than somebody who is not so well educated but who has a greater degree of responsibility - who has better judgment and who will exercise greater care in driving. About the same thing will apply all up and down through human relations. Education is a power, and like any other power or force it will be subject to use, misuse and abuse.

There never has been, and never will be any wide-spread uniformity in human development, nor in human relations. 

It could not become universal. 

People are not uniform in their reactions, because of differences in quality and texture exactly as in the case of inanimate things with respect to mechanics, chemistry and electricity.

This will be just as true with

respect to the so-called 'uplift' work there has to be an elimination of the unfit and unworkable. It is possible to do things for and with certain people which would be impossible in the case of certain other people. We cannot afford to treat some people with the degree of consideration which we would like, because of the assurance that it will be used only as a stepping stone, so to speak, to see how much further they can go. In other words, many marvelous results can be accomplished upon a limited scale which would be impossible if attempted upon a universal scale. 

Ideal conditions are greatly to be desired. We may strive for them, and thank Fortune for the degree in which they are achieved but it is not in the province of man to have them everywhere, all of the time, because human activities cannot be generally calibrated to mechanical or scientific precision. 

Millions of dollars are being spent every year in welfare and rehabilitation by people-individuals and groups who have a kindly spirit of interest in behalf of other people. It does not have to be in the form of charity - it may be just a wholesome desire to share with other people who have been less fortunate. There are great numbers of people who get a thrill out of giving to other people the means of development, so that they may provide themselves with greater comforts and a higher standard of living.

This is a very large subject it is well understood, and it may not be necessary to go into it further at this time, except to show that it accomplishes a substantial redistribution of wealth.

All of the money spent for such work goes somewhere- somebody gets the benefit of that much more business. And that increased business, as it passes through the channels of trade, provides vast numbers of still other people with opportunities to make a living, and to accumulate a degree of wealth on their own account.

Our hearts will go out to a vast number of people-individuals and groups, who are in distress. Our sympathy and interest, however, will vary according to our conception of their worthiness. Towards some people our attitude will be very tender and sincere - but that is something which must be expected to be selective. We cannot take upon ourselves the responsibility of seeing to it that 'everybody' lives up to certain standards, re-

gardless of what they do towards providing for their own welfare. 

It is not reasonable to ask, expect or require the thrifty and the frugal to bear the burdens of the lazy, the shiftless and the improvident it does not accord with the spirit of the golden rule. 

The so-called 'bountiful life' is not for everybody. It is not for the shiftless and improvident, whose propensity for wasting is greater than their ability to provide - unless somebody else wants to provide for their welfare. "Bear ye one another's burdens" to be consistent, should be predicated upon the assumption that each one is doing what he can in his own behalf to carry his own burdens.

When sharing the burdens of other people, the giver unquestionably should have the right of selection. If such sharing is accomplished through coercion, the gift will be robbed of its glory and joy-the flavor will be spoiled.

From the nature of things, a gift must be voluntary-anything else would amount to some form of confiscation of human rights. A person may be free to give away his own resources, but there is no proper reason why they should be confiscated by others and scattered among the masses. 

Another fallacy with respect to an equality in the distribution of wealth is manifest in the fact that its popularly supposed benefit to mankind would be impossible of accomplishment for the reason that the condition would be only reversed; that is to say there would be a fixed distribution to meet the variable requirements of the people.

Without an equality in the characteristics and reactions among people nothing would be accomplished by an equality in the distribution of wealth; and it is obvious that such an equality in human characteristics would not be attainable, particularly in civilized life. 

Furthermore, it is doubtful if the advocates of an equality in the distribution of wealth would feel disposed to have that equal distribution extended to include all the people of the world; and one may be left to his own conjectures as to just where they would be satisfied to have the line drawn.

There are many people who are graciously generous in giving certain of their resources for the benefit of other people. 

But one is not likely to hear

much from them about a maldistribution of wealth which they are trying to correct. That criticism and complaint seems to come most insistently and loudly from people who want to see somebody else's resources broken up and scattered.

Inordinate and over-reaching avarice and conceit have occasioned the downfall of many a business, social, political and domestic relationship.

While a substantial diversification of buying-power manifestly would be desirable because, if for no other reason, it would promote general business activity, yet it makes a great difference as to how that diversification or distribution is to be accomplished, whether by confiscation, restriction, suppression of opportunity and incentive, or by the promotion and stimulation of enterprise and the development of business opportunities. 

When a person has worked, and produced something, or has acquired by purchase, inheritance or gift something which somebody else has produced, there could be no element of justice in requiring him to relinquish his possession to other people beyond his natural obligations to those who may be normally dependent upon him, as in the case of domestic responsibilities, or in the matter of taxes for the reasonable maintenance of government. 

There would be no spirit of civilization in the idea that just because somebody has been more successful than I have been, the fruits of his his accomplisment ought to be taken away from him for my benefit, or for the benefit of anybody else, or which would ruthlessly take from one person or group for the benefit of others merely on the pretext of equalizing economic resources would be exactly on the same level as the barbaric instinct of helping one's self to the fruits of another's efforts just because he wants it. The same criticism would apply to the taking of the resources of certain people for the creation of business opportunities or other advantages for some group, as through taxation for some uneconomical or unnecessary public purpose. In other words, it can be no worse for the savage to appropriate to his own use the fruits of another's effort than for the same thing to be done in the name, and under the prestige, of 'civilization'. Many atrocities are committed in the name of civilization.

Rights involving economic resources, together with social rights and customs which have been developed over a period of time, should be respected and protected against usurpation by newcomers of today. This will be one of the duties pertaining to the normal functioning of a civilization.

The contention that the world owes every man a living is obviously a fallacy. The 'world' owes no man anything. The resources of the world are here, available for the use of man to such extent as he converts them to his use - just as every other living creature has to do. 

But a 'civilization' owes to man such protection in his normal and reasonable rights as will enable him to live, to acquire and hold property and to seek happiness according to his ability, his effort and reaction to his environment and the trend of the times.

To such extent as the masses are protected from exploitation, and are given a chance to be selfsupporting, the better will be the morale, and the higher and more substantial will be the character of the community and of the nation. 

Care should be exercised to avoid confusing 'principles of civilization' with enterprises for the exploitation of the public or for monopolizing, grouping or controlling business activities so as to make them inaccessible or unavailable to worthy portions of the people, thereby depriving them of their natural and normal right to participate in the economic activities of the world in which they live.

Prosperity has varying effects upon the people some will safeguard their resources and increase their growth, while others will turn to extravagance and wastefulness.

Much will depend upon the thrift, frugality, judgment and ability of the individual-his reaction to his opportunities and environment and the trend of the times. It follows that it never will be practicable to have any individual equality in the division, or ownership, of existing wealth. As stated elsewhere, the nearer people approach to universal poverty the nearer they will come to economic equality, but the farther they get away from poverty the greater will be their economic inequality.

It is because private fortunes belonging to individuals, and those of corporations or organizations for profit, break up eventually by natural processes, that they are safer for the masses than fortunes which are

tied up in perpetuity. Wealth in the hands of individuals is being constantly readjusted and re-distributed. In this respect most people are contributing more or less to the trend of the times. 

The administration of government should be logically in the nature of a policing function to afford protection against abuses and unfair practices to such extent as it may be able to do so having due regard for the natural and normal rights of the people to control their own activities as long as they do not unduly interfere with the basic rights of other people. 

Government sometimes may be regarded as something ideal, impersonal and immaculate, incapable of doing wrong; but every government has to be administered by 'people', possessing human virtues and human weaknesses, which will be like those of other people. No government will be better than the character of the people who happen to be charged with its administration, in conjunction with the influences to which they may be subjected upon the part of certain other people, and the trend of public sentiment. The quality of a government will be in the nature of a resultant or composite of all of these characteristics.

Laws are not ideal and immaculate, for similar reasons. 

Too much, therefore, should not be expected with respect to the character and operation of laws. 

Law observance, and respect for the law, are not likely to be on any higher plane than the human element involved in the making or the administration of laws - or in a cross-section of the people. The tendency will be for laws, like people, to stand or fall in accordance with their merits, although in some instances certain laws may carry along over a period through the influence of emotion, leadership or group psychology.

Respect for certain laws and disrespect for others cannot be said to be altogether a matter of selection of the laws which are to be observed and those which will be disregarded. Such laws as prove to be unpopular always will be difficult of administration. Laws will be respected when their character commands respect, and there is a great deal of difference between commanding and demanding. In other words, the laws should be worthy of respect. Those which are designed to safeguard the normal rights of the people, individually as well as collectively,

are fundamental and therefore always will have the respect of the masses in general. 

If man-made laws are to rest upon any stable foundation they must provide for the balancing of conflicting rights, responsibilities and obligations—in accordance with the great scheme of nature and the precepts and principles of civilization. 

It cannot be presumed that every business should be operated primarily for the benefit of the public, but within the realm of civilization it may be expected that the public should be accorded reasonable consideration at the hands of business, then the business will be more likely to enjoy greater permanence of prosperity and good-will. It is equally logical that business should be accorded reasonable consideration at the hands of the public. 

A nation never will be able to eliminate destructive influences completely, and the most that can be done will be to keep them within reasonable regulation. 

Sometimes a difficulty may arise in determining whether something really is destructive or not -owing to the over-lapping of interests among individuals or groups. In such cases it is probable that the decision should be based upon the manner or the degree in which it balances with the normal rights of people in general, together with its effect upon the rational development or progress of civilization. 

When people undertake to make use of the prestige of 'governmental impersonality' and authority to advance certain interests or ideals, the effect may be highly constructive and beneficial to the public, or it may become deplorably destructive just as the value or the hazard of any force depends upon its direction, application and control.

When natural human incentives to produce and exchange, or to participate in other ways in economic activities, are discouraged or stifled by governmental restrictions, confiscation of profits, or lack of protection with respect to property rights, or other natural human rights, or when such restraint is accomplished through the selfish and unwarranted interference other people - individuals other people of or groups then the trend of public welfare to that extent will be backward.

The greater the prosperity of the people the greater will be

the resources available through taxation for the support of governmental activities with less hardship upon the people. nation 

That community or nation will be most prosperous which provides the greatest freedom and security to individuals and groups to acquire and hold economic resources through the various processes of production, service and exchange.

In view of the magnificent prosperity which has been achieved in the United States, there appears to be abundant justification for continuing the policy of protecting the people, individually and collectively, in respect to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness according to their own ideas of what they want to do along with the right to prosper through doing business with each other according to their ability and reaction to their environment- as long as they maintain a reasonable attitude and decent behavior toward other people.

Arbitrary and coercive regimentation opens the way to class exploitation whereupon the destinies of the people will depend upon whatever the intelligence, spirit and character of the exploiters happens to be.
