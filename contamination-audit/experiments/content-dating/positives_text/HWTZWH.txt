An Experiment in Education For Family Life* A. INCEPTION OF THE WORK 

UTCHESS County during the years 1929 to 1932 was the Dscene of an experiment in education for family life undertaken by various organizations in the county with the cooperation of Vassar College. In 1928 the Women's City and County Club of Poughkeepsie had established a fellowship for "work in the field of social studies especially designed to further the welfare of Dutchess County through the cooperation of the Women's City and County Club and Vassar College." The Fellowship, founded in the memory of Margaret Lewis Norrie, a former president and active worker, was planned for a six year period, a different field of study being undertaken every year, or at most every two years. It provided that a Vassar graduate of not more than five year's standing should undertake a study of some phase of welfare work in Dutchess County, and that the field work for this study might fulfill in part the requirements for a master's degree at Vassar College. In 19291930 the Norrie Fellowship was awarded to a member of the class of 1927 for work to be done in the field of pre-school child welfare. 

The results of the year's work were sufficiently valuable to make it advisable to continue and enlarge the project. In 1930-1931, by means of a grant from the Carnegie Foundation which the college was instrumental in procuring, the work was carried on another year, and then further developed in 1931-1932 through the interest and cooperation of Vassar College and various groups of parents in the county. For the first year as Norrie Fellow the work was leveled particularly at the pre-school child and his parents. In the succeeding years, 1930-1932, the emphasis became parental education, and the group reached included parents of children of all ages.

The work in the Euthenics Seminar was centered around the establishment of a play group at Lincoln Center and the work with parents in such a group. The topic selected for Physiology 500 was a study of the relation of diet to colds, and to resistance to infection. 

The Vassar Nursery School children were used as subjects, and the results were written up by a student assistant in the Nursery School whose work the Fellow directed. The Child Study 380 problem was a study of the effect of Daylight Saving Time upon the sleep of young children. The subjects in this case were the members of the youngest group at the Children's Home in Poughkeepsie. 

Work in Nursery School-Vassar 

In addition to taking these courses, the Fellow worked in the Vassar Nursery School, supervising the luncheons with all the details of planning menus and ordering food which were involved. She held conferences with the parents of the children about food problems their children presented, and in all, found the assistance at the Nursery School an invaluable contact with pre-school children and their parents. 

Trip West 

The Fellow made a trip to Chicago for the Third Conference of Nursery School Workers, called by the National Committee on Nursery Schools, which proved a most valuable type of preparatory work. On the way out to Chicago a visit was paid at the Merrill- 

Palmer School in Detroit, one of the oldest and best centers of preschool education in the country. 

On this trip to the West several very definite things were accomplished. The contacts made with leaders in the Parent Education and Nursery School fields were invaluable. The chance to [ eight ]

visit other schools was helpful. The discussion group on Parent 

Education was particularly enlightening to one who was very new in the field and the method of conducting the whole conference, by discussion, was most useful in subsequent work. This Parent Education discussion group seemed to crystallize vague ideas and make clear some of the aims toward which all workers in the field are striving. 

Trip to Washington

These conferences having proved so valuable, others were arranged for with similar purposes in mind. During the Thanksgiving vacation conferences were arranged with Dr. Louise Stanley, chief of the Bureau of Home Economics of the Department of Agriculture, Dr. Edith Hawley and Miss Hildgarde Kneeland, both of the same Bureau. Dr. Stanley suggested seeing Miss Anna E. Richardson, of the Journal of Home Economics, because she was in touch with Parent Education programs as they are connected with Home Economics extension programs throughout the country. All four of these women were most cordial and interested in the Norrie Fellowship plan. Miss Richardson gave most practical suggestions as well as mimeographed material from Texas showing what is being done there, and the subject-matter covered. Dr. Hawley's and 

Miss Kneeland's suggestions could not be carried out since they involved special research problems and it had been decided that the emphasis in the field work in Euthenics would be mainly educational. 

The Fellow was always on the lookout, therefore, for material which showed what others were doing in similar circumstances: State 

Extension work, for example, rather than for suggestions for special research projects for the County groups. Miss Richardson suggested Mrs. Dillon, at the Washington Child Research Center, on ways of presenting information on child development to parents. 

A conference with Mrs. Dillon was held during the Christmas vacation. Her work was in the formative stage, however, so that not much help was gained there.

Visits to Schools 

Another sort of preparatory work which proved of great value was a week's visiting in New York progressive schools and a trip to Cornell for consultation there. Mrs. Henry Morgenthau, Jr., chairman of the Norrie Fellowship Committee, arranged most of these visits. About ten days were spent on the trip. Monday morning was spent at the City and Country School and the Bureau of Educational Experiments; the afternoon with the National Council of [ nine ]

Parent Education; Tuesday, at the Lincoln School; late Tuesday in Macy's Toy Department; Wednesday, at Teacher's College; 

Thursday with Miss Anna Eva McLin visiting three schools of the

Child Education Foundation; and Friday at Public School 41, "The 

Little Red School House" experiment of Miss Rachel Irwin and 

Miss Kearny. Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday morning of the following week were spent at Cornell University in Ithaca. 

To digest it all took a long time. In fact even now, bits of information gleaned from this trip, prove valuable. In each case the question was asked "What do you do in the way of parent education in your school?" Notes were taken also on the play equipment and the techniques used in each place and any other useful details were jotted down, as for example, the system of serving noon luncheons in each Nursery School.

The value to the work of such a series of visits lay primarily in the point of view about children and parents which was impressed upon the Fellow by the contacts made. To regard the child as an entity, not part at home and part at school, well summarizes this point of view.

Summer at Institute of Euthenics, Vassar College 

Six weeks of the summer of 1930 were spent at the Institute of Euthenics. This was the most valuable of all the year's preparatory experience and laid the foundation for the work in Parent Education which was carried on in 1930-1932. The courses elected included the Lay Leadership Training Class given by Dr. Ruth Andrus and 

Miss May Peabody and the course in the Family given by Mrs. 

Beatrice Bishop Berle. Part of the time was spent in observation and in attending the staff meetings in the school for little children. 

The Fellow also "listened in" on Dr. Smiley Blanton's course in 

Mental Hygiene.

Reading 

In connection with all this preparatory work, the Fellow was becoming acquainted with the wide field of reading matter focussed on the pre-school child and his parents. In the preparation of her talks and the conduct of her discussions, references were constantly made to this reading. A bibliography constantly employed by the 

Fellow and field worker is added to this report. 

E. CONDUCT OF THE WORK Parent Study Groups 

The first step in forwarding the plan of organizing study groups [ ten]

for mothers was to make known throughout the county the general aim of the Norrie Fellowship work for the year. This was accomplished through a notice in the Sunday Courier on October 13, 1929. 

The Dutchess County Health Association News Letter for October, carried a full page notice also. Invitations to speak at Parent- 

Teachers meetings soon came in to Women's City and County Club and to the Fellow directly. These first meetings might be termed preliminary or interest arousing. Through them the most responsive individuals in the County could be reached who might later serve as nuclei for regular study groups. 

Organization 

The fact that the first talks were given, for the most part, to Parent-Teacher groups meant that most of the women present were not necessarily mothers of pre-school children. The speaker, therefore, had to keep in mind these aims: (1) to help those present to see the usefulness of a study group for mothers of pre-school children (2) to give a "sample" of the kind of thing which might be discussed in such a group and (3) to treat the subject under discussion in such a way that the mothers present might go away with the feeling that they had gained a little for their own lives. If these purposes were attained even in small degree, it was felt that the women who took part in the preliminary meetings might help later in forming regular study groups for younger mothers. At any rate these meetings spread knowledge of the plan. 

In an ideal situation the mothers of younger children should be reached at a first meeting, either through personal visits of the leader, or a local chairman, or by means of letters of invitation and other publicity. Any means which are usual in the locality should be used. Sometimes a public health nurse may help with organization. 

Speaking to parent-teacher associations first, before any contact is made with the women one wants to reach eventually, delays organization, although it does not necessarily make it less effective in the end. It must be remembered the Norrie Fellow of 1929-1930 knew very little about Dutchess County and knew very few strategic peɔple, so that all of the preliminary work was a learning process for her as much as for the groups to which she spoke, or even more so. 

It was three months before she felt oriented at all in the situation. 

The general aims the speaker had in mind in these preliminary talks were (1) to develop a learning attitude on the part of those present (2) to supply information which might be of practical value [ eleven ]

to the mothers present (3) to bear in mind and help to solve individual problems which the mothers from time to time hinted at or mentioned directly.

Conduct of the Parent Study Groups 

As a result of these preliminary meetings, two interested communities, Pawling, and Pine Plains decided to organize groups at once. A third group of mothers was organized in Poughkeepsie in connection with the Lincoln Center Play Group for pre-school children. With these three groups upon which to concentrate, the Fellow approached the second phase of her general aim: the organization and actual conduct of a series of conferences with parents of pre-school children. Three methods of approach and presentation were attempted. Periodic meetings were held in Pawling every two weeks. A concentrated series of meetings (or Institute) was conducted in Pine Plains. Individual daily visits were made to the mothers of the Play Group children in Poughkeepsie. Pawling

Community Fire House assembly room, at half past three o'clock; (4) that if possible an undergraduate from the College should come to each meeting to help care for those children who could not be left at home; (5) that the first meeting should be held on February 24, 1929. 

Ten mothers met on that date. Katherine Gordon, Vassar '30 took care of the six children who came. "Playthings for children" was the subject of the discussion. At the close of the meeting, the leader asked for suggestions for future meetings. It was found that feeding problems constituted a major interest. Accordingly, three following meetings were devoted to this subject. First, essential foods for a good diet were discussed following by a study of feed- [ eighteen ]

The influence of clothing and of shoes on posture and health was stressed at the fifth meeting in Pawling. A clothing exhibit was included. OUTLINE FOR TALK ON FEEDING DIFFICULTIES 

There seem to be three types of anorexia, or lack of appetite. 

The child who is negative.

2 The child who dislikes certain foods but on the whole eats well. 

3 The child who really has no appetite. 

Stop and think of your own child's dislikes and see whether they come under one of these headings. 

The second type has two varieties, (a) a real physiological inability to use certain food elements, for example, a protein sensitivity, and (b) a dislike which has grown up through imitation of parent's dislikes or through some incident which has occurred at mealtime which has been unpleasant. 

What methods would you suggest for dealing with these three types? 2 For the child who is negativistic, who refuses food because he is told he must finish: a. 

Relieve pressure. b. Show you do not care whether he finishes or not; let him go hungry and do not feed him later in the day to make up for the small meals he has eaten previously. (One caution is important to remember, however; by eating less food the the child may need watching because his resistance is lowered; give him an extra wrap if the weather is cold so that he will not catch cold; don't let him feel any anxiety on your part because you will defeat your own ends if you do.

For the child who has some food dislikes-(some suggestions). 

Prepare the dish which he dislikes in a new way. a. b. Serve the food he dislikes on a special dish. C.

Make a party of the meal at which this old food served in a new way is given. d. Remove pressure. e.

Serve small helpings and expect those to be finished. f. Slowly increase the size of the helpings as the child gets used to the food, and praise him when he has finished the serving. g. 

If you or your husband dislike a food, try not to show your dislike before your child. h. Avoid losing your temper with the child during meal time; let the meal time be as pleasant as possible. i. Serve the food the child dislikes first in the meal. j. Let the child help himself. 

3 For the child who has no appetite. a. Find out first whether there is any physiological cause for the lack of appetite by a thorough doctor's examination. b. If there is no real cause which the doctor can correct, then, [ twenty-two ]

C. reduce the size of the servings so that the child can finish these; let the child get in the habit of clearing his plate first, then increase the amounts slowly.

Do not hurry the child through his meals, but on the other hand, do not let the meal drag out too long; keep a time record for a while to help you in deciding upon the length of the meal. d. Have as few people in the room as possible during the meal; cut down on the distractions for the child. e.

Don't give the child all he wants; let him feel a little hungry for the next meal. f. Let the child help himself to all of the foods. 

The parents' attitude is most important in all types of feeding difficulties. Keep calm; the world won't come to an end if your child does not eat; letting him help himself to the amount he wants to eat is one of the best ways of handling lack of appetite because it puts the decision of amount upon the child.

Several difficulties presented itself in connection with the study group in Pawling. One was the care of the children. The Fire 

House has on the second floor, one large room and several smaller ones. The latter are used in various ways. The Library is in one, the Firemen's smoking and lounging quarters in another and so forth. There is a kitchen next the big room and it was here that the children were expected to play, since the adults were to use the assembly space. What the children were to play with was another problem. The Fellowship committee generously gave fifteen dollars to buy equipment, which helped considerably. Still the play space was poor. The girls who came to care for the children were most ingenious and seemed to make the children happy. The railroad is close by the Fire House and several times the children were interested in watching the trains go by. Most of the time they used crayons and drawing paper and the blocks we had provided, as well as the modelling clay. The whole plan for the children was not as fine a demonstration of what might be done for little children, at such meetings.

Attendance is one criterion of success. In this matter the situation is somewhat different in a small country community from that in a city. Most of the women, all except one, in fact, did their own housework which made an extra meeting a burden sometimes. Also, in all of the communities in Dutchess County one hears "we are over-organized." The same people belong to all the clubs and societies; there is always a small group which does everything. The older, long-established organizations had first claim on the women's attention. This new thing which we were trying to do had to take second place, often times, in the minds of the people who belonged to it.

And of course whenever a child was sick the mother had to stay at home to take care of him. When one begins with a group of ten it is easy to see how the number attending the succeeding meetings could vary. The redeeming feature of this attendance question, [ twenty-five ]

however, lay in the fact that all but two of the first ten attended at least four of the meetings; on the whole, the same ones came back each time. In other words, the work held the interest of the group when once started.

The Fellow felt that it was important to know the families of the women in the group but she had very little time for visiting that first year. It takes about an hour to drive to Pawling and after the meetings were over it was usually necessary to hurry back to Poughkeepsie in order to get the students with her to the College in time for dinner. The distance was a drawback in getting students to come because a whole afternoon, practically, had to be given over to each meeting. The roads were often bad in the spring and on two occasions there was snow to cope with. 

The women who constituted the Pawling group found little time for reading. At first this seemed discouraging but when one is keeping house and doing all the laundry work for a family of from three to seven people there just is no time for extra reading. The 

Pawling Library had no new books on child development so it was necessary to borrow books from the Elizabeth McCormick Memorial Fund and pass them about the group. Another reason for so little reading being done was the fact that these women were not used to reading, and admitted that it took good hard concentration "to get anything" out of the books they did try to read. All of which indicates that such a study group was a very new thing in their lives and that if they could be given a point of view and an attitude of mind about their children something of value was accomplished. 

That this was done, and a start made toward community-mindedness, is shown by what Miss Taber said the following year of the women who had been in our group. She said "Whenever we have a project under way here in Pawling we find we can always count on that group (meaning the study group members) to have open minds about it. In any work of this kind it is very hard to see tangible results. The whole movement of Parent Education is in just that state. People are asking all the time "What do you accomplish?" and all workers in the field are striving to find ways of evaluating their "product."

In attempting the Institute plan in Pine Plains it was felt that the women would respond to the idea in the same way that their husbands do to that of the Farm Bureau Institutes. The children were no problem in Pine Plains. When they came they played outdoors by themselves with the toys which we supplied. 

The arrangement seemed satisfactory enough, partly because of the weather and partly because, next to the Hall where we met, there was an enclosed grassy space in which the children could be safely left.

Establishment of Play Group at Lincoln Center 

As a part of her third aim, the establishment of contacts with pre-school children in work that would be of benefit to them and to the adults who worked with them, the Play Group at Lincoln Center, Poughkeepsie's Settlement House, was started. It had these purposes in mind (1) to give the Fellow further contact with pre-school children; (2) to give the Nutrition Seminar class at Vassar subjects for study as well as contact with pre-school children of a different social background from that found among the Vassar Nursery School Children; (3) to give the Norrie Fellow intimate contact with families of a different social and economic background from those with which she was already working; (4) to give the Norrie [ twenty-eight]

Fellow a third method of approach to parents of pre-school children; (5) to be of service to the mothers of the children in the Play 

Group. The emphasis in the latter was to be upon nutrition, for the most part.

Because most of the students in the Nutrition Class had never done any family visiting and the Fellow had had very little experience in that direction herself, she asked Miss Dorothy Phelps, of the Associated Charities, a trained social worker, to speak to the Class before any visits were made. Miss Phelps succeeded in setting attitudes for the class and in giving them a healthy point of view. 

She stressed the point that in our case the visiting would be easy since in our first contact we were issuing an invitation to each mother and her small children. We were inviting them to join our 

Play Group. She warned us that there might be some resistance to the idea of letting such little children go out to "school" every afternoon but felt that we would surely find enough interested mothers in the neighborhood of the Center to form a workable group. 

From Mrs. Lansing and from Miss Reed at the Center, names, ages and addresses of children in the very near neighborhood were obtained and eleven visits were made before the personnel of the group was finally decided upon.

The points we tried to cover on the first visit included (1) our invitation to the mother for her child: could the child come to our 

Nursery Play Group five afternoons in the week from one-thirty until three-thirty? (2) the means of bringing the child to the Center and taking him home (3) the child's usual nap time since it was important, of course, not to interrupt any routine which the mother [ thirty]

On each of the preliminary visits the Fellow was accompanied by one or two members of the Nutrition Class, never more. If the mother was not fairly interested she was not urged to send the child.

The buying of equipment and the planning of the routine was done with the help of Miss Gertrude Athearn of the Vassar College Nursery School, through the courtesy of Miss Wagoner. The schedule for students was so arranged that two and usually three members of Miss Wheeler's nutrition class were present every day. 

Each student came twice a week. These girls were also in the advanced class in Child Study and gained helpful experience for that work.

The purposes of the Play Group as written at the time the experiment started, were: (1) to provide for residents of Poughkeepsie near Lincoln Center an opportunity to send their children to an organized Play Group (2) to provide in the program of this Group as many suggestions for better health as possible; this made it necessary, with the limited time available for work, to stress health rather than play and to make the play period only half an hour long, equalling the nap time and the lunch period; (3) to provide subjects for a nutrition study of both the individual child and of his family, for the advanced class in Nutrition at Vassar College if the families were interested in making such a study with us.

On March tenth the first "school" was held. It continued uninterruptedly through May, with the exception of the College Spring Vacation. The Fellow stayed with the students the entire time during the first week, in order to give the program continuity and in order that each new girl might understand fully what was expected. of her. After the first week, the regular visits to the parents were started, and the students had entire charge of the children. 

A tentative schedule of work was made out and after two weeks [ thirty-two ]

trial each girl was asked to write a job analysis of her assignment and to make suggestions for changes.

Records were kept of the nap period, of lunch, and of evacuation; notes were taken from time to time of the play period. Dr. 

William Conklin very kindly agreed to make the physical examinations and one of the students kept a record of weight and height. 

The examinations were made in the morning since we did not wish to use the "school" time for this. Dr. Conklin's kindness in giving his time on two different days, was greatly appreciated. 

The final personnel of the group, after one or two had come and later dropped out because the child or the mother was not completely satisfied, included eight children, three boys and five girls; four were four years old, and the rest were three. In the spring a five year old came for the play period and a younger child, two and a half, sometimes came in. These two we hated to turn away. They could not go through the whole routine because we did not have enough equipment, but they could join in some of our activities. 

The hardest part of the routine for the students to handle was the nap. While the children seldom went to sleep, they were quiet most of the time. The problem was not an ordinary one because the children were not used to taking naps at home and they felt that what we were trying to do was almost an imposition. They gradually became used to it, however. In May, when the sun was out so many days, we let them rest outdoors and so get sun-baths at the same time.

One of the rules was that the sandwiches brought by the children must be made of plain whole wheat bread and butter with no filling. Since most of the families did not buy whole wheat bread ordinarily we hoped other members of the family might be benefited as well as our children!

The use of evaporated milk instead of fresh was another educational measure. The nutritive value is the same; the cost considerably less and we planned to recommend it to the mothers in our dietary studies. The students mixed it with an equal part of water, stirring it vigorously with a Dover beater and although several refused it at first it was amazing how quickly the children learned to like it.

It was a joy to see these children in the play periods. Most of them had never had much to play with. They were apt to stand around at first but it did not take long for them to become quite at ease in their play. In contrast to the children at the Vassar Nursery [ thirty-three ]

School, they were subdued even at the last. They were slower with ideas of things to do, though that might have been due, somewhat, to the more meager equipment. When we realized the homes from which they came and the small play space in those homes, we felt we were really being of some service to the mothers as well as to the children.

Daily Visits to Lincoln Center Mothers 

The daily home visits were easy to manage since the families all lived within short walking distance of the Center. When one mother was particularly communicative and when, by staying longer, we felt we could learn more or give more help we did not hesitate to omit some of the other visits, although our aim was to see all the families every day.

We needed information about the child because we hoped to make the mid-afternoon lunch supplement the home diet and every mother was willing to tell us, as correctly as she was able, the family menu and the amounts the children had eaten. They seemed to enjoy the frequent visits and as soon as the habit was established they would almost complain if we missed a day. They even helped sometimes giving the week-end menus. In the course of the conversations data were given on income, rent, gas, electricity, heat, amount spent on food for the whole family per week, and so forth. 

This information came out in unexpected ways, often times. Only students who were going to use the data in their studies were allowed to see it and they were cautioned not to divulge names. Each student who was working in the Play Group had one family for particular study. Sometimes there were two girls working on the same problem, from a somewhat different angle. 

A friendly attitude was maintained always and through the close and continued contact with these families real friends were made. Since the first aim of the educational program with the 

Lincoln Center mothers was improvement in diet, most of the help was along that line, although as occasion offered, other suggestions that seemed welcome were given. 

Research on Sleep

Another contact with the pre-school child was made at the Children's Home in Poughkeepsie where research was carried out on the sleep of young children. For a month, two weeks during 

Standard Time and the first two weeks of Daylight Saving time, two trips a day were made to the Home to record the rising and retiring time of all the children in the younger group. The purpose of this [ thirty-four ]

study was to determine whether the actual sleeping time of young children is decreased during daylight saving time as compared with standard time. By making the study in an institution, it was felt that in such a situation many variable factors which might be found in a home study would be eliminated and the factor of increased light would be the only change.

Five girls and eight boys were chosen as subjects. They ranged in age from two to five years. With one exception, all had been in the institution long enough to be accustomed to the routine. 

The room in which the subjects slept was long and narrow, about thirty-seven by fifteen feet. The five windows faced east. 

These had heavy green shades and outside blinds. The beds had five inch cotton mattresses, with ordinary stretch springs, and crib sides. Each child had one heavy woolen blanket, one lighter weight gray double blanket, one extra blanket and one blanket across the bed which was used to keep the covers in. Each child slept in flannel pajamas or night gown. The fourteen beds were arranged down the long sides of the room. The amount of light reaching each bed varied but since the children slept in the same places throughout the study, the light factor was constant for each child; it varied only with the weather and with the change to daylight time. 

Method 

Twenty-six observations were made in all. Half of these observations were made during standard time and the other half during daylight time. The period of observation began April fourteenth and ended May tenth, 1930. The children were put to bed at 7:00 p. m. and were awakened at 6:00 a. m. The observant went to the institution daily at 6:30 p. m. and at 5:30 a. m. The following data were recorded daily for each child: 

I time asleep 

2 time awake or awakened 3 loud noises outside and inside the children's rooms 4 disturbing situations such as visits from older children or punishment 5 approximate time at which darkness was complete 

6 arrangement of the window shades 

7 ventilation of the room 8 approximate temperature indoors and out-of-doors 

9. weather conditions 

10. changes in number of blankets used.

The children were not allowed to talk while undressing so that the [ thirty-five ]

room was quiet the half-hour preceding the bed time. The four children suffering from enuresis were awakened at a definite time each night to be put on bed pans.

In order to eliminate the personal factor of an outsider as much as possible the observer entered the sleep room at four minute intervals. The rounds were started four minutes after the day nurse had left the room and were continued thereafter until all the children were asleep. A final visit was made four minutes after the last child was asleep. Sometimes, when the observer felt that the children were waiting for her to return, she allowed eight minutes to elapse between visits.

An Ingersoll watch was used as the timepiece. In order to keep it accurate it was set night and morning by a Naval Observatory- 

Western Union Clock.

eyes closed

It will be a compilation from the years 1930-31 and 1931-32. 

The introduction in Parent-Child Relationships sums up very adequately the purposes the authors had in compiling the material in just the form they did and states the case for the discussion method of conducting study groups for parents. It also suggests ways of using the outlines.¹ Harrison Elliot's book, The Process of Group Thinking2, is another valuable source of information on the discussion method used frequently throughout the year.

A suggestion that the mothers think over the day's contacts with their children usually started the ball rolling. Many of the women who came to these meetings were not accustomed to expressing their ideas as was asked for here, but they did have ideas. 

Companionship seemed to loom large in their minds as one element in a satisfying parent-child relationship. An example of this given in one of the groups shows how homely and simple the anecdotes usually were: The mother had six children, covering a wide range of years. One day the three youngest were out playing in the yard. The mother suggested that all turn to and help rake the leaves. When this was done the mother played in the yard with the children and then raced with them to a tree. The children seemed very happy at this show of abandon on the part of their mother and one of them said "You are just like a little girl, aren't you, Mother?" Another example given under the heading of companionship, (and stories of this variety were given on several occasions) was of the joys of going off for a day in the car with the whole family. Everybody puts on old clothes and spends a day or an afternoon in the woods, cooking a picnic lunch and playing games in a group. The mother who told this story said that both she and her husband "unlaxed" and really played.

Another factor stressed as important in promoting satisfying relationships was interest on the part of the parents in the child's activities. In one group the women were agreed that they did not have much trouble in taking an interest, the children so often came running home to tell them all about their school activities. When the question of how they showed the interest was suggested as the important thing, we felt we were getting more at the root of the matter. If they felt interested in what the children did, in what was of immediate importance to them, they were going a long way toward making a really happy parent-child relationship. Examples were talked about of parents who were indifferent to the child's hobbies, followed by a discussion of the effect of this indifference upon the children. In one group the following illustration was given : 

A child of four came in to his mother with a grasshopper in his hand and said "Look what I've found." The mother admitted that she had turned away horrified and said "Take that horrid thing away." Then she added the question "Was I wrong not to take [Forty-one]

an interest in the grasshopper when I hate them and all bugs of any variety?" The leader did not undertake to decide for her, but as the discussion went on the woman had her question answered by the general attitude of the other mothers. It is just such occurrences which convince us that there is a good deal of value in the discussion method and, too, in the system in Parent Education of holding study groups for mothers for the sole purpose of focussing their attention upon family relationships. If its aim is to help change attitudes rather than to give formulas, success is more frequent. 

In several groups we turned from considering the point of interest in children's activities to the element of confidence in the parent-child relationship: How confidence, so admittedly an essential element in the parent-child relationship, should be fostered. Refusal to be shocked at what the child does was considered an important factor in winning and keeping confidence. The story of a little girl, aged four, who wore long drawers and who cut a triangle out of them so that they would fit better and would not have to be folded over the ankles, was given as an example of this point. The mother said that when she discovered the child's act she was horrified, but remembered that she had better inquire into the little girl's motives before scolding her. When the explanation was given, the mother realized that the child had not been willfully destructive but had carefully figured out a method to eliminate the discomfort of the long drawers folded at the ankle. She said she explained to the child that it was a good idea but requested her not to do it again. 

Contrasting examples were given of the unfortunate effect of showing shock and surprise at a child's behavior. 

Long sermons were inveighed against as discouraging confidence. The mothers cited examples in which the child, after a long "talking to", had made a remark indicating that he had not benefited by his mother's words.

As a further aid to confidence, the mothers felt that explanations should accompany requests and demands as often as possible. One woman said "I think you oughn't to give too many commands either." The leader then cited the example in Arlitt's book, The 

Child From One to Six, of the little boy who was brought to a clinic because he did not obey at all. The mother was helped with her difficulty by an observer who kept track of the number of commands the mother gave in a two hour period: forty-seven commands to do and seventy-three to stop, one hundred and twenty in all, and not one was obeyed. [Forty-two]

"Get the whole story" from the child if possible before punishing. "If you fly off the handle immediately the child will be afraid to tell you things and so you will lose his confidence." Getting the child's point of view was considered another way of understanding him, and of keeping his confidence. "Using intelligence instead of emotions" was another expression the mothers used. Always telling the child the truth they felt would be important in building and keeping confidence. The story used to illustrate this point was that of the little boy whose parents told him they were not going out one evening. Later he was standing in the window and saw them going down the walk, evidently dressed for a party. He turned to his nurse and said "There go two of the biggest liars I know." Linked closely with this point of not telling lies to children was that of acknowledgement of mistakes on the part of parents. One woman put the whole question of keeping confidence into a phrase which sums it up very well. She said "regard your children as people."

There was a general concensus of opinion that being a parent took patience; that that quality was an invaluable element in a satisfying parent-child relationship. Oftentimes this point was brought out negatively; that is, when we were discussing unsatisfying relationships the mothers seemed willing to agree that fatigue caused impatience and consequently altercations with their children and an unsatisfying relationship with them!

At one of the meetings, other elements in a satisfying parent-child relationship were mentioned; for instance, sympathy, tolerance (closely related to patience), encouragement (closely related to interest in the child's activities) and love.

In several groups integration of purpose between husband and wife was mentioned as a very important element in a happy parentchild relationship. The mothers would say "one trouble we have is with our husbands who don't agree with us in all we do with the children; we don't seem to agree on discipline." This not only points to the great need of reaching fathers in the Parent Education movement (and all who are working in the field are very conscious of this need), but it also indicates that something even more constructive must be done than reaching men who are already fathers; it suggests that boys as well as girls in high schools and colleges might well profit by discussions of family life and its varied relationships. 

Physical health was mentioned as a factor in a satisfying parent-child relationship. The mothers were conscious that when a child is sick for a long time or is delicate, the relationship takes on differ- [Forty-three]

erent aspects. Examples of overprotective parents of children with handicaps were given to illustrate this point. 

Another principle frequently brought out in discussion was variously described: "treat your child with respect" or "without ridicule" or "regard him as a person" or "seek to win the child's confidence" and a typical illustration was given by a mother who had just returned from the hospital with a new baby. The oldest child, a boy, had to sleep in the same room with his parents temporarily, while the trained nurse was in the house. The small boy talked a great deal to his mother because she had been away for some time. Finally he said "Mother did you love Daddy when you were a little girl?" She said she did not because she did not know him. Then he asked whether his Daddy had loved her when he was a little boy and again the mother said he had not because they did not know each other. The little boy then said "Mother, I love a little girl now." 

The mother's reply was "Isn't that nice, Jack, who is she?" The child replied "You could never guess!" Then the mother started naming several of the girls in his class and to each he said "No, not her." Finally with great glee he said "It's Mildred!" The mother did not laugh, nor make any comment other than a genuine “Isn't that nice, Jack!" She did not ridicule him, it was brought out; she had regarded him with respect. 

Discussion based on the second unit in Parent-Child Relation ships which deals with the influence of the parent's own childhood experiences were fewer in number because so often need for discussion of something else seemed to the group more pressing. In those meetings in which the second unit was used the effect of early childhood experiences upon the present parent-child relationship were admitted to be great. One outstanding example was given by a woman who said that in her early experience her mother had been "strict" in her management of the children and that she was not going to make her own children feel the way she had felt as a child. "Mother and father expected their word to be law and we were not to have any ideas of our own. When I wanted to have a job in 

Poughkeepsie they would not let me. Another time they punished me for doing something for them that I thought was very nice and I've never gotten over the feeling of injustice I had from that episode."

The complications which arise from the presence of grandparents in the household was brought out in this discussion also. One woman moved from Poughkeepsie to a smaller town in the county [Forty-four]

to live with her parents so that the children could live in the country. She now says she regrets the move because the grandmother is always interfering with her methods of handling the children. She finds that the children "shop around" from one adult in the family to another when they want something. She finds her mother insisting on one kind of obedience while she insists on another. There is friction almost continually and she blames it upon the three generation situation. Other mothers comment on the awkwardness of carrying out their disciplinary measures when they have one or another grandparent visiting them. The characteristic attitude of these grandparents is that instant obedience from children is essential.

They seem to oppose the point of view of the majority of the group members, of regarding children as people to be treated as courteously as one would treat an adult.

The discussions of brother-sister relationships, the third unit, always interested the women. The underlying causes of unsatisfying relationships among brothers and sisters were usually summarized as three: fear, jealousy, self-centeredness. Examples of quarrelling among children were given. The mothers decided that some “quarrelling" is harmless and that if the children are “let alone they can fight it out' among themselves. An example of jealousy between brothers was given by a mother of two boys, one of whom lived at home all the time, the other, who was away at school, during weekends only. The young boy who lived at home was most malicious when his brother was present in the family circle. It was obvious that he was the center of attention during the week and disliked having to share attentions over the week-ends. He would not let his brother play with his things, would fight him on the least provocation and even admitted that he didn't like his brother. 

Uncertainty of position in the family constellation was felt by a seven-year-old adopted cousin in a family consisting of two other children and a father and mother. This seven-year-old girl was younger than her boy cousin but was in the same grade with him in school. She would bid for attention at home by stating that she could do better work than did the boy in school; that he had done so and so at school which had called down the wrath of his teacher; that she should be allowed to stay up as late as he because she was just as big as he and was in the same grade, etc., etc. Her tattling was frowned upon by the mother, especially when it concerned her own son. She interviewed the teacher and found that the child was always working to get ahead of the boy in school and that she con- [Forty-five]

tinually "told on him" in classroom situations which the teacher missed. The two children quarrelled in loud tones at home.

The family council plan was suggested as a method of helping the various members feel a part of the whole and an important part. 

It was urged that this would help each grow in respect for the others. One mother, who had used this plan for several years, urged its effectiveness. She felt that the mutual respect which her children had for each other was due largely to their system of discussing matters of family moment with all present and with all given a chance to express their own views.

The position of the child in the family, whether oldest, youngest, middle or "only", was a topic which brought out examples that opened the eyes of many of the women to their responsibility in making each child feel his position a real and secure one. Preparing the first child for the arrival of the second was discussed, and the value of doing this ahead of time was brought out several times. 

Playmates of the same age were mentioned as important factors in an only child's life. In all this discussion of family relationships, concrete examples were given by the mothers every time. 

The matter of sex education often came up in this unit in connection with preparing the first child for the second. Sometimes it came out in other discussions. It always revealed opposing attitudes and for this reason was a dangerous subject to discuss in an early meeting of any group. We tried to steer clear of it until the group had reached a stage of impersonality in approach to problems of child development. De Schweinitz' Growing Up was usually recommended as a useful handbook for sex education and was shown to the group, and parts of it read to them, in order that they might see the new point of view in sex education. The modern attitude toward sex was a shock to some of the women and they could not quite grasp it. Treating it in an unemotional way was hard for them to understand. The eagerness with which they all took down the name and price of the De Schweinitz book showed that they had been stirred to action and some perhaps to a changed attitude. 

In the discussion of the Laws of Learning, Unit Four in the Andrus-Peabody material, the phrase "development as a continuous process" was hard for most of the women to understand. We usually changed the first question in this discussion and asked "How do we learn things? How does anyone learn?" The phraseology seemed easier to grasp and brought out a good many examples. They [Forty-six]

said we learn by observation, experimentation, experience, study, imitation. The next question was "But what attitude of mind do we have to have before we can learn anything?" Various phrases such as interest, willingness, desire, were used to express the first Law of Learning. The term, "readiness," was given by the leader. The second step in learning was brought out by the question "after we have a desire or readiness to learn, what do we do?" The idea of practice or the term, "exercise," was always forthcoming. 

Often this whole discussion would center around an example of a learning situation for a child. It was amazing how often the mothers chose as the example for discussion some personal habit, such as learning to brush one's teeth. The ultimate feeling connected with a learned habit was asked for. The word "satisfaction" was emphasized usually.

The question of obedience was linked with the discussion of the Laws of Learning because of the fact that several times the mothers said dissatisfaction was connected with a learned habit. 

They saw the connection between punishment and this dissatisfaction. Forms of punishment were usually discussed at this stage and following this, methods and principles of obtaining obedience. Isolation, spanking and deprivation were the methods most often mentioned as punishments.

The question of parents' attitude toward obedience brought forth an amazing number who felt that their own parents' point of view, of instant and unquestioning obedience, was harsh and unfair. This suggested that a swing toward more liberal parenthood was in process of taking place. "Willing obedience" was a term used several times. It was interesting to note here how often the questions brought up in one unit of study overlapped and recurred in subsequent discussions. The myriad interrelationships of family life were always coming to the fore.

Simplicity in the rules for dont's in a family was stressed, in discussing disobedience. Treating broken rules in a uniform way was emphasized. The Laws of Learning were brought up here and it was pointed out that only by consistent "dissatisfaction" would a bad habit be broken. Regularity in routine of sleeping, eating, resting, etc. was mentioned as a factor in obtaining willing obedience in childhood. One mother said she had found this to be so because fewer opportunities for conflicts arose when the child knew what was expected of him. The Family Council plan was usually brought up in this discussion as well as in others. Even if no Council were [Forty-seven]

held regularly, talking over a plan of action with the child before it was to be carried out was given as one good way of obtaining willing obedience. The leader mentioned the example of child who refused to play the piano before her mother's guests because it was suddenly "sprung upon her" without any warning. If she had been asked ahead of time, even a few minutes before, or if she had been asked by herself, not in the presence of the company, she would probably have done it. The value of a warning in calling a child in from play for meals, etc. was brought out by several mothers. Others were not so sure this was wise. In discussion, such points of view might be changed. When examples of a method were given and shown to be satisfactory, the mothers who held back and felt that perhaps instant obedience was important usually veered around to the more liberal attitude. Consistency and firmness in the warning plan were stressed as necessary, however. The women seemed to learn more themselves by discussing actual examples than by having the leader merely state that psychologists have found a warning before any action, the wise way to obtain satisfactory results. It seems almost absurd that so simple a fact needed to be discussed in these groups but the attitude of a great many people toward children is still not one of regarding them as people who react in much the same way as do adults.

One of the most fruitful discussions we ever had was on the subject of family integration (the fifth Study Unit). The ways in which homes differ from houses were brought out and the leader discussed with the group ways in which the home could supplement the school and the community by activities in which the whole family could be interested. Music and a better use of the radio was suggested: science hobbies which could be carried on at home were mentioned and various forms of handicraft, such as carpentry or weaving, were described as possible. The question of pets in the family was touched upon. The value of various types of books and magazines formed another topic. The arrangement of rooms in a house or apartment was emphasized as important in family integration. A place to entertain guests uninterrupted by other mem bers of the family was pointed out as one of the essentials for comfort and for reducing friction. The ideal that each individual should be considered in the family as an important member of the family circle was brought out by the group. A separate room for each member was considered ideal; where this is impossible, at least a cupboard of one's own to be regarded as inviolable by the rest of the [Forty-eight]

came up. family, was agreed to be essential. The Family Council plan again 

We had one member who had tried it and felt it to be a valuable way of making family decisions. She felt that her family succeeded in the plan because they had learned, through several years of experience in joining in the discussions, to make a decision and then to stand by it all together because each had contributed toward its making.

There were three men present at this meeting and the discussion turned from the Family Council plan to the father's part in the family. The twelve women rather overshadowed the men and the fathers felt at the end of the meeting that they were somewhat backward in their contributions to family life. There was some joking about this and the men said they would have to do something about their laxity. But the discussion really seemed to open their eyes. The first thing mentioned when the question was asked "What is the father's responsibility for home-making?" was "financing." Next came "backing up the mother" in her handling of the children. This was the same point made in our first study unit discussions of satisfying parent-child relationshships: Integration of purpose between husband and wife. Some of the women said they wished the fathers would "show some interest in the upbringing of the children." Companionship with the children was mentioned. An example which was very much to the point was given here. A boy of sixteen said he had "kept straight" because he did not wish to disappoint his father who often gave up social engagements with other men in order to go fishing or golfing with his son. The matter of allowances and the father's part in them was brought up. The value of making an allowance and of adhering to the original decision as to amount was stressed. An example of a child who could wrangle a ten-cent piece out of her father at any time was given, to show how unfair this uncertain method of allowance-giving could be. The child would not grow up to understand salary or income limitations. The common sense attitude of this appealed to the men present. 

Other discussions of this unit ran along the same lines. In one the mothers seemed puzzled about the continual running away from home by their adolescents. Factors in home life which might tend to increase this swing away from the family to outside attractions were listed and compared to those things which kept the young people at home in the days before automobiles. One point brought out in the discussion, that of arrangement of rooms in the house, surprised several of the women who had never thought of the influ- [Forty-nine]

ence of a factor so apparently slight. 

In two groups in which we discussed family integration, the women felt that something of the new attitude they were coming to believe in, that of regarding the child as a real person, ought to make some difference in the attitude of the children toward home. 

The importance of making home a place where the children, especially the adolescents, could have fun, was brought out. Mothers who allowed "parties" of an unexpected and informal nature, who allowed the gang to drop in and to feel at home when they did werc praised.

Examples of excellent discussions by the various groups could be cited almost indefinitely. Yet there were times when the work was discouraging, when only four people came to a meeting, when discussion dragged, or integration seemed impossible. When a really good meeting took place, with integration in the purpose and in the thinking, some principle of value to the mothers was brought out, and the leader went away feeling that something had been contributed to the cause of family life education. 

Two of the groups, at various times, wished to discuss childhood fears and some of their causes, and a special discussion of this subject was planned. Examples of various fears were offered and their causes searched for. Conditioning was explained and several fears which were presented could be accounted for on that basis. The outward signs of fears were discussed and desirable ones mentioned. 

The undesirable ones were listed according to causes (conditioned, imitative, those fostered by over-protective or over-solicitous parents, protective, physiological and so forth). The treatment of fears was discussed next. As a final summary of the meeting, it was concluded that there is no single cause of all fears and hence no single, simple treatment. That many factors need to be taken into account was stressed.

The subject of freedom often followed naturally after discussion of obedience and discipline. As she grew more experienced the leader tried to come prepared with a good many concrete illustrations of the principles under discussion. These were supplemented by other illustrations given by members of the group, but when it was impossible to draw appropriate illustrations from the group, the leader tried to be prepared with a good many various sources in order to get the discussion started. In the Poughkeepsie High School group the subject for the year's work was adolescence. During the first year of the High 

School Study Group, 1930-1931, it was conducted by a combination of the lecture and the discussion methods. Hollingworth's The 

Psychology of the Adolescent was used as the main guide for the lectures supplemented by other writings in the field. Hollingworth's book was chosen because it presents the subject in a simple form and was written for parents.

In discussing the physical changes which take place at adolescence, the group compiled a tentative list, which the leader completed. The attitudes of parents toward these physical changes were discussed as Mrs. Hollingworth presented them. In the second year of work on the subject a method used by Dr. Andrus and Miss Peabody was followed: the women were first encouraged to draw their picture of an adolescent; then, beside it, to list the characteristics of an adult; and finally, to discover what major adjustments the former must make to become the latter. They covered, without any trouble, the five main adjustments which Mrs. Hollingworth lists in her book: (1) psychological weaning (which, by the way, the mothers usually called "untying mother's apron strings"); (2) finding a vocation; (3) seeking a mate, (or, to use the phraseology of most of the groups, boy and girl relationships); (4) achieving a point of view and (5) finding the self. 

The High School group was the best integrated, the most interested and the best attended of all the groups held that year. The women enjoyed it very much, and the work seemed to meet a real need. In the third year of work, 1931-1932, several of the groups, after using the material in the Andrus-Peabody book for a while, shifted their emphasis to adolescence even though they were not mothers of adolescents. They gave as their reason, "we want to know what is ahead of us, we want to be prepared for adolescence." Six of the groups conducted that year made this change. 

This is a very significant fact, since it showed an eagerness to be prepared as well as a kind of fear of the future. This attitude is partly a result it may be of the continued talk about the younger generation "What are we going to do when our girls stay out late and we don't know where they are? What are we going to do to understand this sophisticated youth? What are we going to do about parties for our twelve-year olds?" These are some of the questions which bother the mothers of school children who have [Fifty-one]

talked with mothers of high school boys and girls. Therefore, mothers of younger children felt they might get some help in their group discussions along these lines. The same material which had been used the year before in the first adolescence group was enriched and enlarged for use with these groups. The High School group which continued to meet in 1931-32 rounded out detailed discussions of the major adjustments of adolescence, and went on to further discussions of marriage and of how parents might help prepare their children for it.

Pawling 1930-1931

The Committee, still headed by Mrs. Harrie Wright and backed by Miss Mary Taber, felt that individual visits to a suggested list of people in Pawling would start the organization there. Two days were spent at this, and with the help of Mrs. Wright and Mrs.

Each one of these occasions seemed to cement the bond with the women pro- [Fifty-two]

posed for the study group; it also gave them a chance to meet the field worker. The first meeting was called for October tenth in the 

Fire House Community room, following the arrangement of the year before. An attempt was made to sound the keynote of the meetings for the coming year, and some of the time was spent in giving a background for the County program, its tie-up with the State and with Vassar College. The discussion was then opened with the question "What do you hope to get out of these study group meetings? What are you thinking about as parents?" Such problems as these were proposed for study: feeding, psychology, justice in discipline, teaching consideration of others, bribery, handling of fears, the selfish child, temper tantrums, allowances, punishment, self-discipline.

From this unassorted list it is plain to see that there were plenty of things these women wanted to talk about. It was obvious too that in some cases at least, they wanted ready-made answers or formulas. 

It was pointed out that such formulas do not exist, that what we were trying to do in our study groups was to discuss certain principles of the parent-child relationship and that as time went on we would find help and even answers to some of these questions. 

Points of view or attitudes of mind in attending study groups were discussed also. The most important of these listed by the group were impersonal, scientific, experimental, learning and self-critical attitudes.

This method of beginning a study group may have some drawbacks, but at least it gives a group a chance to get used to the discussion method when many of them have never used it and have never tried to talk in a meeting before. It also helps them begin to be self-critical and somewhat analytical. From the discussion of attitudes we turned to a more detailed discussion of the method we would use in subsequent classes. The value of concrete examples was brought out and the mothers were asked to remember incidents which might happen in the interims between meetings in order that they might use them in the discussions to follow. They were asked to use the device, in telling of these incidents, of saying "I know of a mother and child who" rather than saying "My child and I did so and so." They were also asked to note not only their own children but any parent-child relationship which would serve to throw light on principles.

Despite the fact that this foundation of impersonality was laid for the group, it was often hard to steer them clear of the morass [Fifty-three]

of personal anecdotes. There was a conscious effort on the part of several to take the suggestions, however.

REPORT OF THE MAIN POINTS COVERED BY THE SPEAKERS AT THE PARENTAL EDUCATION IN- STITUTE HELD AT VASSAR COLLEGE MONDAY, 

MAY 18, 1931.

Dr. Elizabeth Gardiner, Director of the Division of Maternity, Infancy and Child Hygiene of the New York State Department of 

Health was the first speaker on the program. She said that “Parent 

Education means the acquisition of knowledge about children by parents." "New York State has put forth more effort than any other state, perhaps for children, but all these activities need to be coordinated. Parent Education, or the program of parent education, rather is the beginning of this coordination." She went on to show how progress had been made in Health work. She mentioned Dr. 

Thom's statement that "we had struck a gold mine when we started all this work for children, but we needed a method to get at the ore." 

Dr. Gardiner said that this meeting showed that we had found a method.

Dr. Ruth Andrus, Director of the Division of Child Development and Parental Education, New York State Department of Edu cation, was the second speaker. She talked about "What parent education does." Her main points were as follows, although the delightful examples and stories which she told cannot be reproduced here: 

Parent Education she said, (1) induces a sense of humor (2) helps us think of children as children and as individuals (3) deals with parents of children of all ages (4) helps parents understand what sorts of materials children need to play with (5) helps parents understand themselves.

(6) focusses some attention upon modern educational methods (7) helps parents understand the effect of the community upon their children. 

The discussion led by Miss May Peabody, Assistant Director of the Division of Child Development and Parental Education, New 

York State Department of Education, centered around the questions "How can Parent Education be undertaken?" "How are we going to get it over in Dutchess County?" "What is your interest in Parent 

Education?" "What does it mean to organize?" Following is a summary of the main points which seemed to be forthcoming from the group: (1) the name "parent education" stops some people; (2) an

Institute such as the one held today might be repeated three times a year, at the beginning, middle and end of the year; (3) fathers might [Sixty]

be interested in several ways, (a) mothers can talk things over them, (b) library reading (c) a man leader for the discussions (4) sectional conferences might be held to arouse interest in Parent 

Results

Such an Institute as the one held in Dutchess County had several purposes which we felt were realized by this meeting. (1) to arouse greater interest in Parent Education itself, i.e. to arouse a number of people to consider what Parent Education is and how it can function best (2) to bring together all agencies interested in children. that representatives from each might think together on the subject of Parent Education. (3) to interest individuals who feel they need help with their children; to show them how they can get this help (4) to arouse interest in study groups for the following year (5) to sum up, or rather to bring to a head, all the work carried on during the current year, (especially to use the Lay Leaders Class) 

(6) to utilize all possible local resources in connection with the meetings. 

Plans for 1931-1932

In the spring of 1931, it was decided that having the worker carry on the nutrition work at the Nursery School along with the parent education program was no longer wise; that money from the 

Nursery School and Physiology budgets might better be used for a full time nutrition worker and that parent education might better be carried on full time also. Dr. Andrus and Miss Peabody, invited the four District Superintendents in Dutchess County to discuss the possibility of supporting a Parent Education worker from the public school funds. Vassar College agreed to join the project to the extent of appropriating $600 from Institute of Euthenics funds and each Board of Education of a Union Free School District was to be asked to contribute $50. The City of Poughkeepsie was to be asked for $500 through its Board of Education and the City of Beacon for $200 through its Board. In order to make a $2500 budget for salary and traveling expenses for the worker, the Board of Supervisors was to be asked for the remaining $400, since, if each Union Free School district were to appropriate $50 each $800 would be raised. The District Superintendents were hopeful and felt that they [Sixty-one]

could get most of the Districts to join in the plan. All this planning took place in the early part of June. Most of the budgets for the coming year are voted upon in July or in August, but since parent education can come under the State Equalization Law and can be put in the budgets under the heading of health, it was felt that there would not be much trouble in getting them through even though they were not brought up in July budget voting. It was also felt that if the Boards could be persuaded to appropriate the money before the first of July, it would come under the past year's equalization quota. The Superintendents of Schools in Poughkeepsie and in 

Beacon were also in favor of the idea and were willing to present the matter to their respective Boards of Education. The Board of 

Supervisors was approached through their secretary, who stated. that under the Laws of the State of New York the Supervisors were not allowed to appropriate money for education unless there were a definite statute on the books authorizing them to do so; since there is no such ruling for parent education, some other plan had to be evolved for raising the $400 originally assigned to them in the first budget made by the District Superintendents and Dr. Andrus and 

Miss Peabody. That each School trustee in the small rural schools should be asked for five or ten dollars toward the joint support of a parent education worker for the County was the final method decided upon to meet the amount which the Board of Supervisors could not appropriate.

The first step in connection with raising the joint budget was left in the hands of the District Superintendents. Before July first one superintendent had persuaded one Board of Education to appropriate the money asked for, $50. The other superintendents were interviewed and the worker spent a day with one of them interviewing all of the Presidents of the Boards of Education in his district. 

These Board Presidents were favorable in varying degrees, from a lukewarm approval to positive enthusiasm. Each president promised to bring the matter up at the next Board meeting. A plan to attend the Smith School of Social Work was given up by the worker in order to be available for conferences, but little was accomplished. 

There was nothing to show for the efforts made except that Rhinebeck, Stanfordville, Millbrook and Hyde Park had agreed to join in the Parent Education plan. The summer at the Institute of Euthenics which the worker attended was, however, a valuable experience.

F. RESULTS OF WORK AND EVALUATION 1. Development of Parent Education 

A program of education for family life arose in Dutchess County as a result of the three year's work. The work was carried on in widely separated places, and an extensive area in the county covered. Twenty-seven study groups for parents were held in the three years, and there were numerous visits and conferences with people interested in the work. During the first year alone some two thousand miles were covered on the roads of Dutchess County; thirty-seven talks of various kinds were given, and one hundred and forty-two individual conferences of one kind or another were held. The three groups of 1929-1930 grew to twelve in 1930-1931, of which nine were new, and to twelve in 19311932 of which five were new groups. The meetings (1) focussed the attention of the mothers on the better care of their children, (2) [Sixty-nine]

made available in a very tangible form some of the newer scientific information about the care and training of children, (3) gave the mothers a first contact with a movement of growing importance, Parent Education. The backing of Vassar College, the Women's City and County Club, and the Parent-Teacher organizations in the county which cooperated, helped immeasurably in the development of this work. There was evidence that the college-county relationship was put on a firmer and happier footing as a result of this cooperative enterprise in establishing Parent Education. Due to the present financial condition of the country, the movement has suffered a slight setback, but there is established a firm foundation from out of which further work may develop. 2.

Establishment of a Procedure in Organizing Groups 

Interviews The record of work done in planning for the year 1931-1932 was reported in this paper in some detail, and indicates one of the essential elements in the procedure for organizing groups, that is, the interviewing of key people in the county. It is a question as to whether this part of the procedure should be undertaken by the field worker, who as a stranger in the community, may lose valuable time in trying to make these contacts herself. 

Preliminary Talk 

Once a group is assembled, a preliminary talk presents to them the purposes of a study group, the possible topics for discussion, the requirements for joining such a group. 

Care of Children

The care of children provides a problem which has remained as yet unsolved. It is poor policy to have the children come to the meetings, because they must usually stay indoors. It is extremely hard to find students from the College who are free at times when the meetings are held and who can afford to give up much of an afternoon as such trips require. On the other hand, if the mothers did not bring their younger children, they could not themselves come. 

The ideal situation would be to have a demonstration Nursery School in these towns and to use it as the center for Parent Education work. 

At least, it would be helpful to have demonstration Play Groups on the afternoons of meetings. However, it is a question whether such a plan is financially possible at this time.

Scheduling Meetings 

There were three general plans of scheduling meetings, (1) hold- 

[Seventy]

ing periodic meetings, as those in Pawling in 1929-1930, and in most groups in 1930-1932; (2) conducting a concentrated group of meetings-an Institute-as in Pine Plains in 1929-1930; (3) making individual daily home visits to mothers as in Lincoln Center in 1929-1930. 

Of the three methods of approach used in the family life education program, the periodic meetings would seem best for the type of mothers we reached in most of the county groups. For the Lincoln 

Center families the individual visits were better since the mothers were unable for the most part to leave their homes. This method is expensive of the worker's time and energy, but it is an invaluable way of gaining a deep insight in problems of family living. As between the periodic meetings, and the Institute form of meeting, undoubtedly the periodic meetings were better. The method of conducting the meetings were the same in both cases, the lecture-discussion plan, and the material covered the same. The only difference lay in the arrangement of dates for the meetings. Where the contacts with the members spread over a longer period of time, it was possible to come to know the group better, and to get more successful cooperation with them. Contacts in Pine Plains where the week's Institute was held were more fleeting. It takes more than a week to reach a free and understanding basis with people if one is a comparative stranger in the town. The Institute plan can, however, be used to advantage if there have been a series of periodic meetings which might be summarized and in some ways enlarged upon in a series of meetings held for one or two days. For a small country community so long an Institute as was tried in Pine Plains seems inadvisable. The plan in Pine Plains was not a total loss, by any means, because the few women reached were most appreciative and responsive, and a foundation was laid for work there the following year. But, it seems that on the whole, choice in the matter of scheduling meetings might better be made between periodic meetings and individual visiting, the decision to depend upon the time available, and the class of mothers to be reached. 

The wide geographical distribution of the points visited and the necessity of holding meetings at times convenient to the local groups (sometimes at night) presented a slight difficulty in the matter of scheduling meetings. This together with the condition of the roads. in the winter and the spring, require that the person undertaking this work have robust physical strength. In planning for the future, care might be taken to integrate the program of meetings to avoid need- [Seventy-one]

less expense of time and energy in getting from group to group. 

From this work which has the great advantage of never being confining or dull, one should attempt to eliminate all that is needlessly tiring. 

3. Development of Techniques for Conducting Groups 

There were two general plans for conducting groups, the one most generally used being the lecture-discussion method. A detailed account of how this worked has been reported in this paper. The leader came prepared with an outline of work to be covered, and with questions ready, which would open the discussion. In one group, however, the High School Class in Poughkeepsie, a text was used, and the usual plan for the leader was to discuss the various chapters of the book by the lecture method. The success of the adolescence group, conducted as it was more on the lecture than on the discussion plan, rather shook the leader's faith in the lecture-discussion method. But as time went on and the ability of the leader developed more, and her familiarity with the material increased, she found the lecture-discussion method easy to handle. It proved a method which brought out the mother's own particular problems in a more informal way than could be accomplished by the lecture method alone. It was a method by which the material could be more easily moulded to the needs and demands of the group. D. 

Selection of Material to be Used for Groups

A first perusal of the Andrus-Peabody book, Parent-Child Re lationships, on which most of the discussions were based, made one aware of the need of fitting these general discussions to the particular groups to be led. A friend, familiar with the people in Dutchess 

County, who had worked with them for several years, was consulted and said, “You'll have to modify these, won't you?" As a matter of fact, however, it was used successfully with little modification. It was found valuable, however, to introduce from time to time other topics of discussion as they were brought up by the parents themselves. 

5. Development of Work with Two Pre-school Groups Lincoln Center Play Group (1929-1930) 

In evaluating the project at Lincoln Center, the organization and establishment of a Play Group, these points might be brought out. 

In the first place, we were successful in gaining the cooperation of a group of women in this district who were willing to have their children enter the group. Several recommended it to their friends so that we had a group from which to choose, the second year the Play group was conducted. In the second place, the educational facilities and [Seventy-two]

suggestions we brought the children and their mothers were worth while, and though not everything was accomplished that we planned for, noticeable progress in the right direction was made in each family. Thirdly, the organizing, managing, and conduct of the group provided valuable experience for all who were working on the project.

The many sources of error in the data collected in connection with the Play Group at Lincoln Center the first year are to be regretted. Many of the details of the routine with the children could have been improved. For example, better continuity in the project the first year would have been achieved had the same person been available for the work every afternoon, instead of our having a different pair of students in charge every day, as happened that year. 

Some of these faults were corrected in our second year at the Center when two Euthenics Seminar students conducted the work. 

Research on Sleep

The research on sleep which was reported on in full in this report, has appeared in Child Development, Vol. III, No. 1,

March 1932. For the Fellow this contact with a children's institution, which was gained as a result of the study on sleep, added to the general store of knowledge and understanding of the pre-school child.
