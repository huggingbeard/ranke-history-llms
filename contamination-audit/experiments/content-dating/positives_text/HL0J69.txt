Gross National Product. Although the strike in the automotive industry reduced economic activity in the last quarter of 1964, the gross national product nonetheless set new quarterly and yearly records. The preliminary estimated rate for the fourth quarter was $634.6 billion, and raised the annual rate for the year to an estimated $623 billion. This would be 62 percent above the 1963 annual rate of $584 billion, and even after adjustment for price changes would be a 5-percent gain over 1963. 

Disposable Income. Bolstered by the tax cut enacted in February, consumers' disposable income climbed last year to a record annual rate of almost $432 billion, 7 percent higher than the 1963 rate. Consumers spent most of the additional income on nondurable goods and services. Durable goods expenditures and savings deposits also increased, but to a lesser extent. 

Industrial Production. By the end of the year the Nation's industry had overcome the effects of the automotive industry strike, and the index of industrial production moved to a new yearly high of 137.0 percent of the 1957-59 average. Durable goods manufacturing showed a larger relative increase than any other industry category.

Sales. Total sales of manufacturing and trade in the first 11 months of 1964 were estimated to be almost 7 percent higher than in the same period of 1963.

Small Business Population Now Exceeds 4.6 Million The U.S. Department of Commerce estimated the Nation's business population at 4,910,000 at the end of the third quarter of 1964, including about 3

4,660,small businesses. The business population had grown by 60,000 since the third quarter of 1963, with small firms accounting for virtually all of the net increase.

Record Total of Incorporations 

Dun & Bradstreet reported a record 197,724 new business incorporations in 1964, compared with a previous high of 193,070 in 1959. The 1964 total represents the fourth consecutive annual advance, and is 6 percent higher than the 1963 figure of 186,404.

Analysis of business incorporations by geographical regions shows an increase last year in eight of the nine regions, with gains ranging from 

4 to 8 percent.

The only region where incorporations dropped is the Mountain Region-comprised of Montana, Idaho, Wyoming, Colorado, New Mexico, Arizona, Utah, and Nevada. Here there was a decline of less than 1 percent. 

Failures in 1964 

Lowest Since 1956

There were 13,501 business failures in 1964, according to Dun & Bradstreet. This was the smallest number of failures since 1956, and the third straight year in which failures declined, reflecting the Nation's continuing prosperity. 

Failures last year were 6 percent lower than the 1963 total of 14,374, and 21 percent below the postwar peak of 17,075 in 1961.

The trend in the failure rate is even more significant than the trend in number of business casualties, since it relates failures to size of the total business population. It measures them as a ratio, that is, the number of failures for every 10,000 firms listed in the Dun & Bradstreet Reference 

The failure rate per 10,000 firms dropped to 53 in 1964 from 56 in 1963. As with number of failures, the failure rate has declined consistently since the postwar peak of 64 per 10,000 firms in 1961. 

Compared with 1963, failures decreased last year in all major industry divisions except construction, where they increased by less than 1 percent because of a rise of 9 percent in casualties among general building contractors. The manufacturing division had the fewest fatalities since 1955. 

However, in a few industries, particularly apparel and chemicals, failures were higher than in 1963. 

The commercial services industry experien the greatest decline in failures last year, a red tion of about 11 percent, the largest decrease of five major industry divisions. The marked provement is particularly noteworthy in view the industry's poor record in 1963, when it was only major category that suffered an increase failures. Among subdivisions of the commer services industry, failures decreased significan in passenger and freight transportation, mis laneous public services, and cleaning, dyeing, repairing.

Income of Unincorporated Firms 

Increases for Fourth Year 

Data for the first three quarters of 1964 indicate that income of unincorporated enterprises in the major divisions of industry (excluding agriculture, forestry, and fisheries) reached approximately $38.5 billion last year. This would be a gain of almost 4 percent over the 1963 income of $37.0 billion. From 1962 to 1963, the increase in total income was approximately 3 percent. 

The services, retail, and wholesale trades are expected to account for the major portion of the 1964 gain, as they did the previous year. In 1963 the income of these major industry groups made up over 70 percent of the total for all unincorporated enterprises. From 1962 to 1963 income of the retail and wholesale trades increased by 2 percent, from $12.2 to $12.5 billion; income of the services rose by 5 percent, from $13.6 to $14.2 billion. Manufacturing income also increased by 5 percent, but for unincorporated enterprises totaled only $1.5 billion in 1962 and $1.6 billion in 1963.

Income of the mining group showed no change from 1962 to 1963, while transportation, communication, and public utility enterprises suffered the only decrease, a very small decline of $4 million.

Uptrend in Mergers

Continued Last Year 

The merger movement continued unabated last year. Furthermore, it is apparent that the number of mergers in previous years was greater than reported, and consequently the Federal Trade Commission is now revising its recording procedures. 

Based on the revised procedures, a preliminary estimate indicates that about 1,800 mergers took place last year. Under the previous procedures, 1,402 mergers were recorded in 1964, an increase of 91 over the 1,311 recorded in 1963. The 

1964 estimate based on revised recording procedures cannot be directly compared with previous yearly figures as yet. However, FTC is planning a revision of totals for the years prior to 1964, an this will make possible a comparison.

The trend to mergers may significantly affe numerous industry structures, with possible detr mental effects to their small business segment 

According to the FTC, more than 200 of the 1,00 largest manufacturing firms of 1950 have disap peared via the merger route. This has eliminate many sources of potential competition and r sulted in increased concentration and oligopoly.

The 200 largest manufactu ing firms of 1962 had acquired, since 1950, mo than 1,800 companies with combined assets of a most $14 billion. These mergers were significa factors in the growth of many large companies. 

It should be emphasized that the acquired com panies were generally successful, established enter prises. The Small Business Administration in 1964 gave the greatest measure of disaster loan assistance since the start of the agency. Disaster struck during the year at many points-a devastating earthquake in Alaska, floods in the Ohio Valley and other sectors, tornadoes in the Midwest, hurricanes in the south. The President, Congress, and the Small Business 

Administration all took action in 1964 to strengthen and improve the Small Business Investment Company Program. President Johnson supported and signed legislation liberalizing key provisions of the Small Business Investment Act, and SBA followed up with several major changes in its regulations. Result: The outlook for the young SBIC industry is much brighter. 

At year's end, more than 700 SBIC's were in operation in all parts of the country. They had combined assets of about $750 million, and had invested over a half-billion dollars in more than 10,000 small companies. Establishing Programs for Community Action 

The Economic Opportunity Act provides for broad-scale Community Action Programs to eliminate poverty and its causes-a mobilization of community services to develop employment opportunities, productivity and motivation, and to improve conditions of life, learning, and work. 

As an incentive and aid, the act authorizes the Director of the Office of Economic Opportunity to allot funds among the States to help finance de velopment of Community Action Programs. 

In practice, Community Action is made up of various public and private programs, including those undertaken by the Small Business Development Centers (SBDC's) that SBA has planned.

What SBDC's Are, 

What They Do

As planned by SBA, the primary function of a Small Business Development Center is the de velopment of new business opportunities that will aid community growth.

Typically, the SBDC has a board of advisers representing the community's business and trade associations, professional societies and labor organizations, the community college or university, minority groups, churches and human relations councils, the local government, and SBA. 

The SBDC also has an operating staff consisting, as a rule, of at least two persons who are skilled in finance and business management, and secretarial help. 765-046 0-65 59

Loan Terms

To stimulate the formation and expansion of small businesses in needy communities, the Economic Opportunity Act calls for loans in these areas on more liberal terms than apply to SBA's regular business loan program. Accordingly, in administering the small business loan program au thorized by Title IV of the Economic Opportunity 

Act, SBA has adopted these policies: 

On loans for modernization, conversion and expansion of existing businesses, the agency gives full weight to the borrower's character, ability, and financial responsibility, as well as the available collateral. • On loans for new enterprises, SBA permits a borrower to provide less than half the cost of starting the business, especially if it is one that will provide a family a livelihood.

The act itself establishes these conditions for loans: 

1. There must be reasonable assurance of repayment.

4. The loan, together with other available funds, must be sufficient to assure completion of the financed-project or purpose. 

5. Projects which would involve relocation of businesses from one area to another or would enable subcontractors to take up work previously performed elsewhere are ineligible. 

6. SBA may require that a borrower take m agement training before or after disbursement a loan.

SBA made a comprehensive study of its operations to achieve maximum efficiency and economy. The study brought major changes in functions, delegation of greater authority to field offices, and plans for full decentralization of activities.
