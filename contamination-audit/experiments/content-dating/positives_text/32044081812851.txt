For those who love to reflect on the intimate and inspiring stories that depict the early history of our country, I have gathered here facts peculiar to this locality. For the benefit of the Quaker Street Monthly Meeting of the 

Religious Society of Friends, I have recorded in the following pages, as true and accurate accounts as is possible to give from the meager records at my disposal.

Much of this material I have gleaned from the faded old papers, diaries, essays and articles written by my Grandmother, Mary Jane Hoag, who during the long years of her ministry made an exhaustive study of the Quakers. Meager as the records were she drew from her wide acquaintance, facts that we find priceless today. As thoroughly as possible she familiarized herself with the local history of the Religious Society of Friends. With gifted pen she painted a picture truly worthy of those fine old pioneers, which is an inspiration to their descendants and others who carry on. 

In loving memory of her, I dedicate the following story.

FRIENDS MEETING HOUSE 

ANY and fascinating are the stories that have come down through the years regarding the pioneer settlers of this section. In my opinion our immediate locality presents some of the most vivid pictures in our country's early history. 

As will appear in detail elsewhere in this story, most of this section was a part of the original “Duane Grant" which it is believed was a gift from King George III of England to Anthony Duane, father of James Duane who later inherited it. (Judge) James Duane as he was more familiarly known was an early Mayor of New York City.

Here among these hills he hoped to build a great metropolis. 

Many famous names were recorded as guests here of James Duane and chief among them appears that of his close friend, George Washington. 

Most interesting are the stories concerning the life of this famous and esteemed family whose name long ago became synonymous with this section and is the pride of each generation that follows. 

In the town that bears their name (Duanesburg) stands the ancestral home, the historic old "Duane mansion," the proud home today of a direct descendant, Duane Featherstonhaugh.

America an unbroken forest, the immigrants of early days had to hew their way to a livelihood, first, in order to clear the land and then to rear a habitation and to raise the necessary crops. 

It was into this section called Duanesburg, that the people called "Quakers" began to arrive feeling a strong desire to advance further the prinicples of 

George Fox, founder of "Quakerism."

Pioneers' House 

They migrated here largely from the vicinity of Dutchess County and laid out farm homes, many of them it would appear on leased land from the large Duane holdings, settling as near as possible along one main roadway of Worship or street. This settlement so located was soon to be known by the quaint and unusual name of “Quaker Street"-the name it still bears. 

The yokes of oxen now appeared, accompanied by parties of Quaker pioneers, hauling the newly felled forest trees to the site chosen for their log Meeting House. This we know was their first House of Worship in this locality. We have no record of its exact location, but from what we have learned, it was located a few yards south of the present location of the Meeting House near the point where the Schoharie Turnpike and Route No. 7 now meet. 

Shortly thereafter the Religious Society of Friends (Quakers) acquired the land on which the Meeting House and Burying Ground are now located. 

This too was a parcel of land from the original "Duane Grant"-but this was not leased. This came through purchase. James C. Duane, son of James 

Duane, later inherited in part the portion of his father's land on which the Meeting House and Burying Ground are now located. It was finally from him that the Friends received full title to the property.

A Meeting for Suffering in 1806 raised the deficient sum of $885 (here the word dollars appears). This finished paying for the Meeting House. 

After the manner of the more commodious Friends' Houses of Worship, the Quaker Street Meeting House was built with a gallery. In those days they also had a folding partition through the center, raised and lowered by means of pulleys and ropes, because the men and women usually entered and sat on opposite sides of the room. 

During that early period they rarely conducted their Business Meetings jointly. Except for the addition some years later, of the porch which now extends across the front, the exterior of the building has never been changed. 

This addition it should be recalled here, created quite a "concern" for it was considered by some, very forward and almost too "worldly" a change. 

Strictly speaking it was "out of plainness." Soon however its protection and general usefulness became so apparent that even the more conservative bowed to its advantages. Again we remember the Discipline of the Society called for the strict observance of simplicity, both in their homes and everything pertaining to their houses of worship and religious services. 

Thus it naturally followed the building's design was of the plainest, with no evidence of decoration inside or out. The windows, tiny panes of crudely made glass were placed high above the heads of the seated members assembled, that they might not be distracted from their purpose, of quiet meditation and worship. 

10-

Thumbing the old iron latches, the heavy wooden doors on either side of the front swung on great wrought iron hinges, as one entered the spacious room. With its walls of white plaster, its plain wood trim, painted a soft soothing gray and the raised "facing seats" for Elders and Speakers, all shared its quiet charm. Years later cushions were added to the plain wooden benches with single board backs, that served the assembled Friends. The same rich silence pervades that room today with a peacefulness that is so characteristic of old Friends Meeting Houses. 

From their first log Meeting House they brought and installed the same cast iron box stoves that grace the old Meeting House today. They are still the only means of heat and are fully adequate. Primitive and plain of structure, they radiate not only warmth, but bring back memories of the charm and dignity of those early Meetings.

Martin Hoag and Benjamin Estes, young men of the Meeting at that time were among those names mentioned who helped in the construction of the Meeting House. The frame was of hand hewn timbers each from a full length tree, fastened together with mortise and tenon joints. The walls were of hand split lath held in place with hand wrought nails, then covered with white plaster or panels of plain wooden boards. Careful seasoning of the lumber and construction so perfect, has kept the building these many years as sound and firm as when it was built. 

On 1st mo 1807 this item appears-"a deed of uses for the Duanesburg lot mentioned for several months past reports as attended to." This we believe marked the completion of the entire Meeting House building transaction.

Some years later there were quite marked changes made in the interior and on 28th da of 2nd mo 1881 (according to my Grandmother's diary), work was begun on the remodeling of the gallery to form an upper floor, which it was decided should become a Temperance Hall. That alteration was made at an expense of $191.00. Later that season it became the new home of the "Independent Order of Good Templers Lodge" and served that worthy organization for many years during its activity in Quaker Street. 

Recorded again on “27th da of 10th mo 1883 a stone walk was laid in front of the Meeting House at a total cost of $81.83 of which $66.80 was expended for stones, the balance for hauling and labor." 

It does seem strange to us that no mention was made in any record we have been able to find, of the first Meeting held in the present Meeting House, no expression of their satisfaction and thankfulness and pleasure in occupying their commodious House of Worship. Yet that in a large measure, is characteristic of the Friends of those early days. Symbolic of the sweet simplicity of their quiet lives. Never unduly exalted by any honor or good fortune that might fall to their lot. Neither was there evidence of their being cast down by adversity, holding always to the even tenor of their ways, faithful to the light they held.

Friends boarding and day schools were also established in many of the neighborhoods where Friends Meetings were located and Friends were very liberal in their support. Not only the smaller schools under the care of the 

Monthly Meetings, but as early as 1802 this Meeting of Duanesburg appointed a committee to raise £3 12s toward discharging the debt due the boarding school at Nine Partners. Friends have always endeavored to extend care and assistance to those of the Society whose circumstances seemed to require it. 

In 5th mo 1808 we find the record-"Oliver C. Smith, Jonithan Soule, Zaccheus Mead, Jonithan Sheldon, Thomas Wing and Ichabod White are appointed to apportion a quota of money to be raised for the Meeting's use and the keeping of such poor as may fall to this Meeting." This charity was usually done in such a quiet, delicate way that many of the membership at large did not even know who were being helped. 

The Discipline of the Meeting called for very rigid observance of Friends' principles in those early days and if there were known violations of those principles and stepping aside from the path of rectitude, the case was usually reported to Elders of the Meeting and one or two of the Overseers appointed to visit the offender to counsel and advise and tenderly admonish him, that the offense might be corrected. The First Day School connected with this Meeting was organized in the late summer of 1878 with 40 pupils. Mary Jane Hoag was appointed Superintendent with 5 teachers. Very few of the pupils were children of Friends as the membership of younger Friends particularly, of this Meeting, had become much smaller by that time, due to deaths and removals to other Meetings through the passing of the years. But the children came from all about the neighborhood and certainly much good was accomplished through this agency, not only in spreading the testimonies of Friends-but in helping to build strong moral and religious characters. 

The First Day School continued for nearly 30 years, closing only for need of more teachers. Mary Jane Hoag continued as Superintendent to the end. 

It was during the year the First Day School was organized that she requested membership with this Meeting on 25th da of 9th mo 1878 and not long after began her ministry which service she faithfully followed according to the light she had for more than 30 years and was still a member of this Meeting at the time of her death 6th da of 9th mo 1920.

The Underground 

Railroad The abolition of slavery has always been to the Society of Friends a part of its religion. Inspired by this firm conviction the Friends conducted what was known as "The Underground Railway." The purpose of this was to transport fugitive slaves from one Friendly haven to another, until the black men had reached Canada and freedom. It was called "Underground" becuse the unknowing could not understand how such a transportation could take place, except below the surface of the earth. 

A Quaker in New Jersey was the first voluntarily to free his slaves and reimburse them for their labor. One year later, 1776 many others of this group freed their slaves and before the end of the century there was practically not a slave holder among the Quakers. This was 75 years before Abraham

Lincoln issued his Emancipation Proclamation. It naturally followed that during the Civil War the Friends in this section were conspicuous in their efforts to assist the "Underground Railway." In various homes throughout this locality they fed and sheltered the runaway slaves from the south who were endeavoring to gain their freedom. They made their way here from previous harbors around Coeymans and Renselearville among other places of secret protection. Here they were kept until it was felt they could be transported with safety to their next refuge, which of course was another settlement of Quakers. This was the little settlement known as Charleston 

Four Corners. From there they were further aided in their escape, eventually reaching the Canadian border and freedom. "Charleston Four Corners" I should like to recall was once the home of another sizable Quaker Community. The very old Meeting House probably

In 8th mo 1907 the Centennial was celebrated here commemorating the building of the present Meeting House. A series of meetings was held during the week which were largely attended and greatly enjoyed by all. 

To the north and rear of the Meeting house extends the old "Friends Burying Ground" where lie many of the fine old families who were among the earliest members of the Meeting and pioneered this little village. Gone these many years are those sweet and serene old faces that radiated a peacefulness from within, which somehow seemed characteristic of those who sat on the "facing seats." Today their descendants are proud indeed to join in quiet meditation here with other Friends, on this hallowed ground.

Adjoining the Meeting House on the east and attached to it is the long low shed which has sheltered many fine teams and carriages from sun and storms, resounding down through the years to the tramp of horses' hoofs. 

Today it stands more as a memorial to those cherished days. Much of it now is outlined and practically hidden behind quantities of wild grape vines. 

Yet it remains sound and intact and one can almost feel proud of its heritage. 

In contrast today, the Friends of those early days conformed strictly to a definite type of dress. Simplicity again being the outstanding characteristic, with materials often of the finest quality. 

The men can still be remembered by some, in their handsome broadcloth suits of black or brown and broad-brimmed hats that matched. 

The dear old ladies will be recalled in white fichus or kerchiefs draped carefully across their bosoms, completing the dresses of finest black silk or Quaker gray (a gray with a brownish cast). 

Their poke bonnets so meticulously made were examples of expert millinery art. A picture of one of the original bonnet molds is shown in this collection. 

Those serene and beautiful old faces that some of us are privileged to remember did as much, I sometimes believe, to inspire and create a desire for higher and nobler living, as some of their softly spoken words. One could not be in their presence without being the richer for it.

Quaker Building

Gets Certificate 

"Possessing exceptional historic or architectural interest and . . . . being worthy of most careful preservation," the old Friends' meeting house at 

Quaker Street has received a certificate attesting to its selection by the advisory committee of the Historic American Buildings Survey as one of 700 old buildings in 16 counties photographed.

The certificate is the first to arrive in the county from the Department of the Interior. It is signed by Harold

L. Ickes, Secretary of the Interior, and 

Andrew L. Delehanty, district officer.

A complete set of architect's drawings, together with photographs of the building as it is today, have been placed in the Library of Congress, and duplicates may be obtained through the library. 

Wilfred E. Smith directed the historical building survey in Schenectady county.-Clipping from a local newspaper.
