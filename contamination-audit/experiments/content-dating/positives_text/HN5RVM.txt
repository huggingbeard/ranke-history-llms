CHAPTER I 

HE above verse is from a vaudeville ballad very popular in the nineties, voicing the public belief in, and worship of the then pugilistic idol, John L. Sullivan, The Boston Strong 

Boy, American heavyweight bare-knuckle king, holder of that proud title for ten years and considered by most fistic critics the world heavyweight champion. 

That wasn't the only song celebrating John L's prowess. Plenty of doggerel ditties were written around his magnetic personality and bellowed across the footlights by vocalists sure of getting responsive roars of applause from the audience. No matter if the performers were rotten and their stuff trash, so long as they put over the appeal of that magical Sullivan moniker, the house was with them solid!

I can recall no other instance of a fighter getting such a grip on the popular imagination, nor does fistic history provide an example. There have been many great scrappers, but song-writers didn't go haywire over them, nor were their names remarkable for the power of stimulating enthusiasm among vaudeville audiences in the days when that form of stage entertainment was widely patronized. 

Not even those latter-day sport idols, Jack Dempsey and Joe

Louis, ever exercised such a lasting spell of fascination on the public mind as did the picturesque, indomitable biffer from Boston. When the ring-followers of his time called him "The Champion Of Champions," it was no idle phrase they really meant it.

In their eyes Sullivan had attained the status of a god, all-powerful, superhuman: the world's greatest fighter with bare fists or padded mitts.

I am not concerned with argument as to whether John L., considered solely as a fighter, was superior or inferior to some of his successors. That's just an academic query, a question never to be satisfactorily answered, a matter of opinion. At all events, millions of folks ranked him as the greatest living scrapper when he was in his prime, and they can't all have been wrong. 

Quite apart from the vexed problem of his actual fistic rating, there can be no mistake in listing John L. Sullivan as the most romantic figure of all time in pugilistic annals. He brought to boxing the potent appeal of personal attraction and vivid color when the game was badly in need of a crusading champion. His deeds established him as a national hero, made him the theme of a glamorous legend that was not even dispelled by the aftermath shadows of defeat.

To him, more than any man in American ring history, the sport of boxing owes the popularity it has enjoyed for the last half century. His is the credit for altering the style of fighting from the brutal bare-knuckle and skin-tight gloves periods, in which everything went from wrestling to gouging, to the Marquis of Queensberry contests whose rules form the basis upon which modern boxing bouts are conducted. 

John L. was the last heavyweight champion under the old 

London Prize Ring rules and the first to be dethroned under the Queensberry code. Thus he appears as the central figure in the transition era during which bare knuckle combats gradually gave way to glove-fighting. 

From the shadowy past of 1719, when James Figg emerged as the first fighting champion, down to the present, only a few gladiators stand out prominently. And among these, as grimly impressive as the Sphinx of Egypt towering above the hot desert sands, the massive outlines of Sullivan's magnificent form loom up_sharply.

The hardest drinker in a long line of battlers who boozed as recklessly as they fought, the convivial, brawling, hot-tempered pug who eventually saw the error of his ways, foreswore the flowing bowl and turned temperance preacher, John L. Sullivan occupies a unique niche in the Hall of Fame. Artists painted him, sculptors modelled him, authors made him the hero of many a thrilling tale, playwrights dramatized him, the sports of many nations feted him, the aristocracy did homage to him, royalty received and welcomed him.

Undeniably, the Boston Strong Boy, who was as proud of his Irish parentage as he was of his American citizenship, ranked as a master of his profession. He was as rare in his chosen field as a 

Michelangelo or a Rembrandt in the world of art. In his day, no man on earth was more widely known. And even at this late date his memory is kept green by sport-writers who find abundant material for stories in the rip-roaring, devil-may-care life of John 

L. Sullivan.

During a career on three continents, he figured in more scraps, both in and out of the ring, and scored swifter victories than any other pugilist. Also he held the heavyweight title longer than any man before or since. And as a money-maker he outscored all his contemporaries. Sullivan's earnings were the highest in pugilism, until the advent of the modern commercial years that saw Messrs. Dempsey, Tunney, Louis, et al, piling up plutocratic wealth. 

In tours extending from Canada to Florida, from New York to San Francisco, from British Columbia to the Sandwich and Samoan Islands and the shores of Australia, New Zealand, England and France, he was accorded greater ovations and came into more direct contact with people of every social stratum than any athlete, male or female, of the past or present. 

John Lawrence Sullivan was born on October 15, 1858 in

Concord Street, Roxbury, Mass., in the Highlands of Boston. 

He came of good old Irish stock, his father, Michael, hailing from

Tralee, County Kerry, Ireland and his mother from Athlone, County Roscommon. His paternal grandfather was a noted Celtic wrestler and champion performer with a shilleleagh, a weapon of more deadly efficiency when wielded by an expert than a locust club in the fist of a New York cop. His father was agile and pugnacious, of diminutive stature, but big of heart, as savage as a wildcat if crossed, and well able to hold his own among the hod-carriers among whom he worked for a living, as tough a crowd of Micks as ever drank damnation to the enemies of Ireland. 

In an article written for a New York newspaper, Sullivan paid the following tribute to his esteemed progenitor : "I've often been asked from whom I inherited my strength. I believe I got it from my mother. She was five feet, nine inches tall, and scaled 189 pounds. My dad is responsible for my love of athletics, but my physical qualities come from mother's side of the family. Her folks were all big, while father's people were of the small or medium build order. Dad himself was only five feet, three inches, and weighed about 130 pounds. But he could give away weight and hand out a licking to men twice his heft in a rough-and-tumble and nobody could equal him for wild enthusiasm over athletic exploits. "In fact, he got so enthusiastic at times that he forgot actual figures, especially where Irishmen's feats were concerned. He couldn't get excited about anything that occurred in America. 

In his estimation, any record made in the United States ranked below one made in Ireland by some great native athlete. And he'd prove it to you, even if he had to invent the time, place and figures necessary to back his argument. He'd lie his way out of any controversy, and make you believe him, at that! He'd boost the old sod and its people if he had to risk his life to do it. Sullivan received his early education in the public schools of 

Boston. Most of the fighting fraternity of that day were happygo-lucky, tough, illiterate devils. So-called reformers opposed to boxing were wont to describe everybody connected with the sport as belonging to the plug-ugly, ignorant brand of humanity, and of course included John L. in that category, he being the most prominent target and easiest to hit with printed slanderdarts.

But the better class of sportsmen and others who knew Sullivan personally were well aware that in point of intellectual culture he was far above the average citizen, and able to meet and converse with brainy men and women on equal terms. He had by far the best education of any pugilist before him, and few who followed were his superiors in that respect. 

Although a bright scholar, he had no liking for class-room work but he took to athletics as a duck takes to water. His specialties were baseball, in which he was proficient, and boxing. So good a ballplayer was he that for a while he turned professional, and in later life frequently donned a uniform to play with the Boston team, or with local clubs wherever he chanced to be.

As might be expected, John's school days were brightened frequently by the flashing interpolations of many fist-fights. He never earned the reputation of a bully, but as he grew older and heavier, his victories were so numerous that the bigger lads kept clear of him, and he was tacitly accepted as cock of the school.

Pugnacity was ever prominent in his makeup and inevitably helped to color his boyish visualization of life, both as regarded history and the present. It is related that when he was eleven, the members of his class were asked to name the presidents of

John L. is said to have selected Lincoln, because he had read how, before becoming President, old Abe used to throw all comers in wrestling bouts in the Indiana county from which he hailed.

When he completed his high school studies he attended Boston College for a time. In view of what destiny really had in store for the student, it injects a touch of comedy into the situation to learn that John's loving mother intended that her husky offspring should enter the priesthood, and the definite object of his entry into the Hub seat of learning was to prepare him for that holy vocation.

But John L., despite the deep respect he always had for Mother 

Church, had no notion whatever of forsaking the World the Flesh and the Devil for a religious career. He had pretended to comply, simply to please his maternal parent for whom he cherished an unfaltering affection. But the time came when he could no longer keep up the deception. She was naturally disappointed when he told her of his decision, but being a woman of sound common sense, made no attempt to force him into a sphere of action for which he felt himself to be unfit. 

With his abandonment of the goal of priesthood, Sullivan also quit college. He had sixteen months of it, and found the life too dull and confining. He had, however, benefitted by the study of two subjects which attracted him most, the drama and elocution, both of which were to prove advantageous to him in the remote future, when he took to the stage, and later, to the lecture platform. 

For several months after leaving college he was at a loose end. 

He entered four amateur boxing tournaments and came out ahead in every instance, winning some repute as a heavy puncher. Few of his opponents lasted three rounds. This was relaxation. For the more serious purposes of life, John proceeded to learn the plumbing trade.

As a budding plumber he seems to have fared all right until an argument with a captious foreman frightened away the dove of peace and ended in a declaration of war. The foreman, a bulky person, fancied himself as a scrapper, and forced a fight upon the youth. At least, that was how John L. described it to the head of the plumbing firm next day, when the foreman was regretting his recklessness and nursing a broken jaw in bed. One suspects the loser in that battle hadn't to do much forcing of Sullivan's inclinations. That was probably what the plumbing executive also thought. At any rate, he dispensed with John's services. 

Jobs were plentiful then and Sullivan had no trouble finding new employment. Plumbing had palled upon him, and for a change, he turned tinsmith. For eighteen months he stuck to the tinning trade, but that also got monotonous. When it did, he threw down his tools and betook himself to his great hobbybaseball. Signed up as a semi-pro, he sometimes drew as much

One night Sullivan attended a boxing benefit performance at the Dudley Street Opera House, Boston Highlands. Tim Scan nel, a boxer of local repute, swaggered to the edge of the platform, and, directing a meaning stare at John L., who occupied a front seat, made a boastful speech, offering to stop anyone in the house in three rounds. 

The sports knew Sullivan by this time, and as everybody felt that Scannel had spoken with reference to the youth, yells of "Sullivan!" were heard from all parts of the house.

John L. 

was nothing loth. He removed his 16 inch collar, pulled off his coat, rolled up his sleeves, and declared himself ready for action. 

Scannel, wearing green tights and a confident smile, pranced lightly up to Sullivan, with his guard up. Sullivan, thinking that his opponent would first shake hands, as was the custom in the amateur tourneys, extended his right glove. Instead of acknowledging the courtesy Scannel let go a left swing that landed on 

Sully's cheek and staggered him.

Scannel laughed aloud at his own cleverness. But his merriment was short-lived. John L., furious over what he considered an act of treachery, charged like an enraged bull, smashed through Scannel's defense, and slammed a sizzling right on the jaw. The sledge-hammer force of the blow knocked Scannel clean off the platform. He crashed into the orchestra circle and lay there, senseless.

It did not really need the kayo of Scannel to convince Sullivan that his proper forte was fighting. He had known that for some time past. It was not, however, as easy a task then for a likely scrapper to get a good start as it is in these days. But at nineteen, he had determined to get ahead in the slugging game at all costs. He attended Professor Bailey's shows constantly and issued challenges to meet any heavyweight. He boxed exhibitions at benefit performances, he spared no effort to get and keep in contact with the promoting fraternity.

But as he was still an unknown, except among his own personal following, it was difficult for him to obtain a match. Yet he did not cease training. When the opportunity for action arrived, he meant to be ready. "All things come to him who waits," says the adage, and in 

1878, John L.'s lucky hour struck. He was signed for a bout with Johnny, better known as "Cocky," Woods, to take place in 

Cockerhill Hall, Boston.

Woods, a tall, well-built fellow, was so well thought of by the experts that he had at one time been matched to fight John C. 

Heenan when the latter was champion, but the battle did not

come off as scheduled on account of interference by the authorities. 

Woods was a good two-handed fighter, equipped with a heavy punch. A tried veteran, the gamblers made him a favorite over the novice. But John L.'s friends were present in force and backed their man generously to win. Nor were they disappointed. 

Sullivan, after sizing up his man, lost no time in preliminary feinting. Disdaining Wood's attempt to counter his rush with a straight left, John L. closed in, and caught his opponent with a terrific clubbing right on the jaw. Woods dropped, and lay without moving a limb. He was out cold. 

The Woods victory launched the youth definitely on the pugilistic ocean. He filled several exhibition engagements and in the following year had all the better of a bout with Dan Dwyer in Revere Hall, Boston. As Dan was the recognized champion of 

Massachusetts, the fact that Sullivan slapped him around lightheartedly, was a big feather in the youngster's cap. He followed this up by decisively whipping Tommy Chandler, a well known and formidable heavyweight. 

People were beginning to talk about young John L. He attracted particular attention from two famous Boston sports, Tom Farley and Jim Keenan, who were willing to, and did back him heavily whenever he put up his hands in a ring. While the professional stars were fighting battles in the newspaper columns, Sullivan was plugging merrily along in active competition, scoring a kayo here and there, challenging any and everyone. 

As the big fistic stars shouted from the housetops, wrangled and howled defiance at one another, claiming laurels they wouldn't fight for with their fists, they were blissfully unaware that Nemesis awaited them all within the cultured circle of the Hub, in the shape of a young Hercules who was one day to reign as the undisputed King of Swat.

Sullivan's big chance that opened the way to a dazzling future came when Professor Mike Donovan, then world champion middleweight, came to Boston to give an exhibition in the latter part of 1879. The professor, who later held the post of boxing instructor at the New York Athletic Club, was deservedly rated as one of the most scientific boxers of the day.

Sullivan begged to be allowed to go on with Donovan. The latter, knowing that the youth had little experience, at first refused. Donovan, always conscientious where his public was concerned, did not want to inflict a novice upon the spectators, who might be incapable of putting up a decent showing. But Sullivan persisted and at last Donovan gave in, after being assured by William Muldoon, the champion wrestler, that Sullivan was not easy picking. For Muldoon had seen Sullivan at work with the gloves! 

John L. rushed Donovan all over the ring, and the only thing that saved the veteran from being knocked out was his ring craft

and generalship. After the bout Donovan accused Sullivan of trying to put him away. John L. only laughed. "It's like this," he said. "It wasn't that I wanted to knock you out, so much as I wanted to convince you that I could fight, so 

I cut loose with all I had." 

Donovan grinned in return. "Well," said the veteran, "since confession's the order of the day, I don't mind admitting that I tried all I knew to stop you-and I couldn't do it. You're the goods, young fellow, and I'm betting you'll go far in this game!" 

Besides William Muldoon, the latter's friend, Billy Madden, had seen Sullivan fight. The two were staging a variety show in 

Boston and they agreed to put John L. on in an exhibition bout. 

Joe Goss, once claimant of the heavyweight title, was to appear 

Sullivan during a sparring session, using the old style, fingerless gloves with wide, protective padding. in a benefit arranged for him at the Hub, and Madden induced him to take on Sullivan for four rounds. 

Goss was clever and tricky, but despite all his cleverness, he was clearly outclassed. Sullivan dropped him with a right on the mouth in the second round. Joe was so badly dazed that he refused to continue, unless Sullivan promised to let up on him. This 

Sullivan did, and the bout went the limit. Said Goss at its conclusion: "I didn't think anyone could do that to me. You've got the kick of a mule in your fists and you're going right to the top, son. I'd hate to tackle you in earnest!"

Following the Goss affair, Sullivan earned new prestige by giving George Rooke a terrific beating. Rooke was knocked down seven times in quick succession and the bout was stopped to save him from further punishment in the second round.

BY CHAPTER II 

Y now Sullivan's fame had spread to America's sporting centers. He was much in demand. Self-taught, picking up all he had learned about boxing in hard fights, he had thrust himself into the public spotlight by sheer force of will and hard punches. Overnight, he had become a sensation in the kingdom of fistiana.

They were talking about him-East, West, North and South. 

Macon McCormick, America's ace sports reporter, then editor of the Cincinnati Enquirer, was an ardent fight-patron and made up his mind that this new knockout artist must show his wares in the mid-West.

Macon made a special trip to Boston to see Sullivan for himself and offer him a match in Cincinnati with Ohio's leading heavyweight, John Donaldson. Sullivan accepted at once. This looked like real recognition. It was the first time he had ever left his home town to go on a scrapping trip. It was the first of many such journeys that were to take him to every quarter of the civilized globe.

Now Donaldson was a veteran of many battles, and had no fear of Sullivan. He had heard those tales of coming young champs often before, and none of them had amounted to anything. Therefore it was with perfect confidence that he answered the call of time and smiled as the scowling youth from the East opened the proceedings with a rush. 

Donaldson jabbed, tried to counter, was swept against the ropes, hurled from one post to another and was downed by a shattering volley of punches that came from all directions. He arose shakily, clinched, tried every trick he knew, but all in vain. The bout degenerated into a farce, with Donaldson desperately holding on and Sullivan battering him relentlessly.

In the third round, Donaldson decided that he had had enough and quit. But the crowd grew hostile and threatened him with worse things outside the ropes, if he didn't continue. 

The beaten man ruefully consented to resume sparring, but a moment later he threw up his hands and quit again. He addressed the crowd, pleading that he was not in condition to do himself justice with such a battler as Sullivan, but promised that he would fight the Eastern man in a return match when fully prepared. 

To do Donaldson justice, he kept his word, although a man of his experience in the ring must have known that he was outclassed by the husky young Irish-American. Two months after the Cincinnati affair, they faced each other in a contest, without gloves, held in a back room of the Atlantic Garden in the same city. 

Donaldson brought every trick in his fistic repertoire into play to avoid mixing matters with his slugging adversary. He sidestepped, ducked, clinched and frequently avoided punishment by dropping to the floor, or sprinting round the ring, with the Boston

Terror pursuing him vengefully. The bout had virtually developed into a foot-race, but in the tenth round Sullivan cornered his elusive opponent, and knocked him cold with a terrific right on the jaw.

The fight made a great deal of talk around Cincinnati, which stirred the indignant authorities into hostile action. The following day Sullivan was arrested on the charge of engaging in a prize fight. He was brought into court, with Judge Moriarty presiding over the proceedings. Incidentally, it may be mentioned that the worthy Judge himself was an ardent fight-fan, and never missed a good fight, if he could help it. Furthermore, he had been one of the most interested spectators at the ringside when John L. battered the agile Donaldson into submission.

The courtroom was packed when the trial started. The first, and only, witness called, was Johnny Moran, who was one of several spectators of the contest caught in the police net. "What is your name?" asked the judge. "Johnny Moran, your Honor!" "Were you present when this alleged prize fight took place last night at the Atlantic Garden?" "I was!" "Do you recognize any persons in this room, besides the defendants, who were among those present at the Atlantic?" "Faith, I do, your Honor. But I'm afraid if I answer, you may hold me for contempt of court!"

The answer provoked general laughter and even the judge indulged in a furtive grin at this verbal stab, which hinted that he, the representative of the law's majesty, had also been among the law-breakers. "Did you see a prize fight?" demanded the judge. "Oh, no, your Honor. I saw a foot-race." "Very interesting," commented the autocrat of the bench. "Who was ahead in that race, Mr. Moran?" "Well, Donaldson is a fine sprinter. He was mostly in the lead by a quarter of a mile, but Sullivan was hot on his trail." "Did Mr. Sullivan catch him?" "He did just that, your Honor. Sullivan got right on his heels, and then he barely touched him, just to let him know that he had caught up!" "What happened then?" "Oh, that was the finish, Judge. Donaldson was tripped up, and couldn't continue. He hit the boards with an awful thud that took all the speed out of him. So he had to stop running and call it a day!" "A very clear, lucid account of this athletic pastime," commented the Judge. "You possess splendid powers of observation and description, Mr. Moran!"

Then, addressing the court in dignified fashion, Judge Moriarty delivered his verdict.

"The excellent testimony of this keen-eyed witness, who apparently saw everything that happened, convinces me that no prize fight took place, and consequently no breach of the law occurred. 

No doubt remains in my mind, therefore, as to the legality of the entertainment. It is plain that these men merely engaged in a foot-race, which cannot be listed as a violation of the law. The defendants are discharged!" 

Roars of laughter and applause greeted this notable judicial decision, and Sullivan was set free.

Sullivan had now proved to the entire satisfaction of all the experts that he could fight like a tiger, was absolutely fearless, fast, and the proud possessor of a punch that carried T.N.T. behind its impact. That he was well started on the road to national fame could not be disputed. 

He returned to Boston, where he gave several glove exhibitions with Joe Goss, which drew capacity crowds. The money was beginning to pour in. Billy Madden, one of the most capable members of the pugilistic managerial brigade, past or present, now took John in tow in real earnest. Billy had projected a cross-country tour for Sullivan, which he rightly figured would prove that the Boston Strong Boy was entitled to a shot at the heavyweight title then held by the redoubtable Paddy Ryan. 

Madden's first move in his promotorial campaign was to visit 

New York City with Sullivan. For John's debut, he selected

Harry Hill's famous dance hall and boxing emporium on Bleecker Street, near the Bowery, the acknowledged rendezvous of America's most prominent habitués of the world of sport. 

Billy created a decided sensation at Hill's one night by making an offer of $50 to anyone in the house who would undertake to stand up four rounds against Sullivan. This challenge was directed particularly against Paddy Ryan, who was present, but the champion saw fit to ignore the defiance.

The offer was then accepted by Steve Taylor, a bare knuckle artist of considerable repute. But Taylor was outclassed, even as 

Joe Goss, George Rooke and Donaldson had been. John L. charged at Steve like an infuriated bull, hammered him all over the stage, and stopped him in two rounds. The consensus of opinion was that the fifty dollar bonus was going to be hard money for anyone to win!

Sullivan made a splendid impression on Harry Hill's customers, and no wonder! He was such a fine-looking, rawboned youth, with a clear bright complexion, brimming over with vigorous health and self-confidence. At this stage of his career, his was a personality to admire and long remember. A modest, unassuming lad, rather reserved than otherwise, not even inclined to be oversociable with strangers, he was a far different gladiator from the John L. of later championship days who became noted all over the globe for his love of conviviality and his tendency to engage in a brawl on slight provocation.

Sullivan looked every inch the true fighter, a modern Hercules whose strength was apparently inexhaustible. In the straight boxing sense of the word, he wasn't a "clever" performer. But he was something more than just a tough slugger. There was a certain amount of what might be termed "natural science" about his ring tactics. In his prime, he was not so easy a target to hit as some writers would have us believe.

He was lightning fast in every move he made, and this natural speed, combined with his terrific wallop, were the qualities which made him such a dangerous adversary to tackle. The late Marshall 

Foch's favorite maxim, that—"the best defense is a bold offense!" might well have been applied to John L. in his best days. 

It was this same faculty of speed that made Jack Dempsey so formidable an opponent. Nobody ever tagged Jack as a clever boxer, but the almost irresistible fury of his attack served to put most of the clever ones on their respective backs when opposed to the Manassa Mauler. Unlike John L., Dempsey wasn't a one-punch knocker-out. But there was dynamic force in the blows he dealt out with machine-gun rapidity that completely outgunned the scientists. Even Tunney, clever as he was, came within an ace of losing his crown under the blasting fire of Dempsey's murderous assault in the never-to-be-forgotten "Battle of the Long Count!"

In Billy Madden, his first manager, Sullivan had a perfect master of ballyhoo and an adviser of phenomenal shrewdness. 

It was Billy who made Sullivan's name virtually a household word in America, and for that matter, all over the world. Folks who had never seen, and perhaps had no wish to see, a prize fight, yet were well aware that Sully's name represented the very essence and ideal of savage combat. 

That standing offer of fifty dollars to any man who could stay four rounds with Sullivan sounded an entirely new note in fistic advertising. Such a sum may seem insignificant in these days of fantastically large purses, but though a modern champ would sneer at the notion of indulging in a slugging match for that sort of chicken-feed, it was good money in a less sophisticated age. 

Madden knew well the psychological value of waking up the public with something that had never been done before. The story of the fifty bucks reward went through the press like wildfire, and the fact that nobody ever earned it by sticking four rounds with the Boston biffer didn't lessen its publicity value. 

Sullivan's first important fight around New York State took place on a barge anchored in the Hudson River, near Hastings. 

John Flood, known as the "Bull's Head Terror," was his opponent. Five hundred sports paid ten dollars each to see the battle. They fought with skintight gloves, under London Prize 

Ring Rules, for a stake of $1,000, of which $750 went to the winner, and $250 to the loser. Sullivan's seconds were Joe Goss and Billy Madden. In Flood's corner were the well-known pugil-

ists, Barney Aaron and Dooney Harris. Joe Elliott, sports editor of the New York Herald, held the stakes, and Al Smith officiated as referee.

The fight lasted sixteen minutes, with Flood down in every round. The latter's seconds threw up the sponge in the eighth, acknowledging defeat. Paddy Ryan was at the ringside, and at the conclusion of the bout, the heavyweight champion promised Sullivan an opportunity to fight for the title. 

Madden then took John L. to Philadelphia and Chicago. In both cities he gave boxing exhibitions and made good his manager's defiance to all-covers. The Quaker City fans saw him knock out Fred Crossley in less than a round, and he whipped two well known sluggers for the edification of Chicago ring patrons.

These latter victims of the Sullivan punch were Captain James Dalton, a tugboat skipper, who, besides winning many ring battles, had licked every man along the river-front who dared tackle him rough-and-tumble style; and Jack Burns, a strapping big fellow, known as the Michigan Giant.

Dalton, abnormally tough at taking it, managed to survive three rounds, but was nailed with Sully's pile-driving right in the fourth, and knocked out. Burns was pounded silly and took the full count before two minutes of the first frame had elapsed. 

When Sullivan and Madden returned to the East, negotiations were well under way for John's title-shot at Ryan's crown. These were concluded on October 5, 1881, when articles were signed for a fight to a finish with bare knuckles, to take place the following February, for $2,500 a side, and Madden deposited a $500 forfeit with Harry Hill to bind the match. 

Sullivan's battle for the heavyweight championship, with Paddy Ryan, was held at Mississippi City on February 8, 1882, the Boston Strong Boy winning the title by stopping his man in nine rounds. Public interest in the match was at fever heat. Thousands of enthusiasts from all parts of the country, anticipating a real pugilistic treat, had either journeyed South to watch the men training, or had reservations on one of the several special trains chartered to carry ring patrons to the battle ground.

Every hangout of fight fans throughout the U. S. was the scene of rabid bickerings over the gladiators' merits. Pugs, gamblers, tavern owners, actors, lawyers, merchants, prominent judges, legislators-representatives of every social grade, high and low rich and poor, were embroiled in fervid arguments as to the respective abilities of Ryan and Sullivan. 

For the first time in American journalistic history, the newspapers hired famous novelists, dramatists, and even members of the clergy, to write their impressions of a prize fight. Among those so employed were Henry Ward Beecher and the Reverend 

T. De Witt Talmadge. 

Nat Goodwin represented the dramatic brotherhood in this capacity. Oscar Wilde, then on a lecture tour of the United

States, accepted an assignment to write a story for a British publication.

At the ringside mustered leaders of the underworld, as well as the elite of society. Prominent in the tough lineup was Red 

Leary, the notorious bank robber, regarded in the East as the most successful practioner in the safe-looting industry. Others of the O'Leary clan clustered around their chief. 

Standing out in bold relief among the gun-toting outlaws of the West were the James brothers, Frank and Jesse. The latter were, so to speak, traveling incognito, for big money rewards were then being offered for their delivery into the hands of justice. 

Yet, although there were many present who recognized the widelyknown death-dealing riders of the range, the James boys felt quite safe in their environment, for they had plenty of friends in the crowd. It was that sort of a gathering! 

Only two months later, Jesse James was shot in the back and killed by his nephew, Bob Ford, and Frank surrendered to the 

Governor of Missouri.

The Sullivan-Ryan battle was exciting, and yet a rather onesided affair, as Sullivan held the upper hand almost from the very start. He took all Ryan could hand out in the way of punishment, but stood his ground, refused to back up, and mixed matters with savage determination.

Sullivan, the more powerful of the two, had much the better of Ryan in the wrestling mixups permitted by London rules, and threw Paddy frequently. On one occasion, both went down while struggling for a fall. John L. was awarded "first blood" by the referee, a decision that elated his followers, who had wagered heavily on it.

In the fourth round, Ryan was bleeding profusely and was badly winded. When time was called for the fifth, Paddy had to be helped out to toe the scratch. He was puffing like a porpoise, although still full of fight, but Sullivan sprang upon him tigerfashion, giving the champion no rest. 

The eighth round was the beginning of the end. In a final rally, Ryan summoned all his strength and skill and went into a furious, give-and-take mixup. This was an episode electrical in its effect upon the on-lookers. 

They slugged away madly, their bodies glistening with sweat in the glowing rays of the sun, their jaws grimly set with intensity of purpose, the muscles playing like writhing snakes under their bronzed skins as the tide of battle surged to and fro. 

Then came the crucial moment! True to the mark as a sharpshooter's bullet, Sullivan's terrible right crashed on Ryan's jaw.

Paddy staggered back and toppled to the ground as stiff as a dead man. He was carried to his corner, where his seconds worked frantically over their limp principal in earnest efforts to enable him to come to scratch for the ninth round.

But Paddy Ryan had shot his bolt, and there wasn't a kick left

in him. When time was called, he could scarcely move. Yet his indomitable spirit forced him forward again, and he staggered out gallantly to meet his inevitable doom.

Sullivan wasted no time, but tore in like a human batteringram. His first blow struck Ryan's temple, then his ponderous right crashed home behind the left ear. Ryan crumpled up, and hurtled downward like a falling tree. His face had turned gray. 

His legs and arms were outstretched, motionless.

A new champion had been crowned. John L. Sullivan was now the world's bareknuckle king!

Thereafter, for ten long years, the Boston Strong Boy met allcomers in the heavyweight division except colored men, whipping a veritable host of opponents, and running up a list of victories unequalled in the records of any of his predecessors to the title. 

Among those he defeated during that period were such notables as Charley Mitchell, Herbert Slade, Johnny Laflin, Jack Burke, Alf Greenfield and Jake Kilrain. Only three of his battles resulted in draws. On July 17, 1882, "Tug" Wilson, a British heavyweight, succeeded in staying four rounds with the champion. 

Wilson evaded a knockout by continually clinching, hugging and falling to the floor, without being hit. Nowadays, he would have been disqualified, but they were less critical regarding fouling in those times, and "Tug" got away with his unorthodox tactics. He was wise enough, however, to refuse a second trial with John. As a matter of fact, Wilson never fought again. 

With the money thus earned in inglorious fashion, he opened a public house in England, and prospered commercially. 

Charley Mitchell, who took an awful beating with the gloves from Sullivan on May 14, 1883, when he was stopped in the third round in old Madison Square Garden, in a bout halted by the police to save the Briton from further punishment, held John L. to a thirty-nine round draw in a bare knuckle battle in France, in 1888. 

In the preceeding year, Patsy Cardiff got a six round draw with the champion, but this was due to the fact that Sullivan broke his left arm early in the bout and finished with only one hand in commission. 

With the foregoing exceptions, Sullivan could boast of a clean slate of victories, until that fateful day of September 7, 1892, when he was dethroned by James J. Corbett in the unforgettable 

Battle of New Orleans. 

Between the period when he won and lost the title, Sullivan mingled world-tours, boxing exhibitions, meeting-all-comers stunts, and vaudeville appearances with his regular professional battles. The most notable contests in his career, other than the 

Ryan mill, were his thirty-nine round draw with Mitchell at 

Chantilly, France, his 75 round kayo of Jake Kilrain, the last bare knuckle championship contest, and his defeat by Corbett.

CHAPTER III 

T was after Sullivan had been touring America with marked schedule for a change. On October 27, 1887, he boarded the 

Cephalonia, and sailed for Liverpool. Queried by a reporter as to the object of this cruise, he replied: "I'm going for money, glory and revenge, particularly the last.

I've been abused in the papers, I've been lied about and condemned by men who, for commercial reasons, wanted to see a true American, a son of the Stars and Stripes, whipped by some foreigner. "So now, I'm intending to get even by unfurling Uncle Sam's victorious flag in the land from which my enemies brought men they hoped would conquer me. If there are any fighters abroad who think they can whip me, I guess I'll give them a chance. 

That goes especially for Charley Mitchell and Jem Smith!"

Thus did the belligerent John L., filled with patriotic zeal for the land of his birth, leave Columbia's shores for his historic tour on the other side of the Atlantic.

It is a matter of record that no prominent American athlete ever received such an enthusiastic welcome from British sportsmen than did Sullivan. During the train-journey from Liverpool to London, he was the subject of one continuous, ardent ovation. 

Once, when the train stopped at a station, a crowd of small boys climbed up on top of the railway carriage in which the champion was seated, and could not be dispersed until police arrived and scattered them.

At Euston Station in London, the crush of eager spectators was so great that Sullivan's party had to fight their way toward the vehicles waiting outside the station for them. 

John himself, in an effort to avoid the mob, leaped into the first cab he reached, and then the real fun began. Everyone was crazy to shake his hand, and seven persons, among whom were two leading heavyweight boxers, rushed the vehicle. Some got inside, others clung to the sides. This was too much for the stability of the cab. Its floor caved in, and Sullivan, along with several of his impulsive admirers, hit the cobblestones in a bunch. 

However, nobody was hurt. The Boston Strong Boy roared a deep-chested laugh, and the incident passed off good-humoredly. 

That night he appeared in a sparring exhibition where he made the acquaintance of Jem Smith, claimant of the British heavyweight title. Smith, in turn, introduced him to the audience. "I consider it a high honor" said Smith, in his speech, "to be the means of introducing this great American fighter. I now understand why his countrymen idolize him. His popularity has been transferred to England, and it is a pleasure I'll never forget to present to you the Champion of Champions-John L. Sullivan!"

Jem Smith's eloquent oration stirred up his hearers to a tempest of applause. But they were not prepared for the faux pas pulled by the guest of honor immediately after the English boxer had finished his speech.

Sullivan was never much of a diplomat. All his life he had been accustomed to speak his mind out, without in the least caring whether he trode on anyone's corns or not. He spoke as he fought, aggressively, when he thought the occasion so demanded, and the compliments paid him by Smith hadn't softened his belligerent disposition in the least. 

When the cheering had died away, he bowed his acknowledgments, and then proceeded to lay the law down in typical Sullivanesque manner. "Gentlemen," said the champion, "I am more than grateful for the fine reception you have given me, which is far beyond my deserts. But I must state that I have come all the way from my native land for the sole purpose of licking Jem Smith and Charley Mitchell, the men who have repeatedly challenged mebut at a safe distance of 3,000 miles. Now I'm here, right on their own camping ground, and I'll give 'em a chance to make good on their boasts!"

He pointed successively to Smith and Mitchell, who was also present, and as he concluded, the crowd burst into mingled cheers and laughter. It was something new for a British crowd to hear two of their athletic favorites defied in such fearless fashion by a visiting rival, and what surprised them still more was the contrast between Smith's address of laudation, and Sullivan's disdainful reply.

But the big moment was reached when John L. turned to 

Smith, who was standing close by, and looking him squarely in the eyes, roared in a voice whose bass accents rumbled in every corner of the building: "Yes, Mr. Smith-that's what I'm here for. I want to lick you, just as much as you want to lick me!"

That was the climax of all the speech-making, and it gave the crowd another unaccustomed and strange as it may seem, a welcome thrill. Probably few other than Sullivan could have swayed a British gathering by such straight-from-the-shoulder uncompromising talk. But it was characteristic of the champion that his outspoken frankess, while it sometimes made him enemies, won for him friends in greater proportion. He went on with his boxing exhibition, which was warmly applauded, and from that night on he was trailed wherever he appeared by ever increasing crowds of admirers.

There were banquets galore for the champion, but in the midst of all these festivities he did not lose sight of the main object of his visit. A few days later, he issued another defiance through the press to Smith, as proof that he was sincere in his stated desire to get the Englishman into a ring with him.

His challenge read as follows: 

"In America it is customary to put up the money first, and do the talking afterwards. My forfeit money is posted for a fight with Smith, but he hasn't covered it. Either he must put up, or shut up. But I'm afraid that neither Smith nor Mitchell want to meet me between the ropes!"

Sullivan was right about Smith, who declined the issue. But before he left England, John L. had signed articles to defend his title against Mitchell.

In both countries the reception given him exceeded all expectations, establishing the unprecedented potency of John L.'s name as a drawing card.

It had been Sullivan's intention to fight Jem Smith first, after his arrival in England, but when Jem declined the issue, The Boston Strong Boy had no alternative save to take on Charley Mitchell. His friends in the Emerald Isle and the U. S. were pleased over the making of the Mitchell match, more so, perhaps, than if Smith had been signed up.

For Mitchell had treated Sullivan rather scurvily, hurling challenges at him when Sullivan was in America; and the Briton did not spare insults, as at the time there did not seem to be any likelihood of John L.'s visiting the British Isles, and it was safe to taunt him at a distance. So Sullivan's numerous admirers were happy in the thought that the big fellow was getting a chance to get even.

Much of Mitchell's rancor was caused by the memory of the defeat he sustained at Sullivan's hands in Madison Square Garden. 

The Englishman figured that he would have a better show with Sullivan under London rules, where a large ring might enable the lightfooted Charles to do some timely sprinting and keep away from the heavier man.

A glove bout would assuredly been a better speculation for John L., judging by what he did to Mitchell with the mitts in their former encounter. But Sullivan was glad enough to be able to take a wallop at Mitchell under any conditions, and consequently accepted the challenge to a finish bare-knuckle battle. 

The site selected was Baron Rothschild's estate at Chantilly, France, a very picturesque place. Most of the spectators were 

British, and owing to the secrecy maintained in order to avoid interference and arrest by the authorities, the crowd was strictly limited in number. Only a mere handful of sports saw the contest. Among these was Sullivan's chère amie, Ann Livingston, dressed as a boy, a role that talented young lady had frequently filled on the stage.

The ring was set up in the rear of a stable, on ground utilized as training quarters for the Baron's racing steeds. For 36 hours a heavy rain had been falling. It had stopped when the fighters squared off, but left the ground so soggy that the battlers were ankle-deep in mud. Intermittent showers persisted after the combat got under way, a fact which did not add to the gladiators' comfort.

Sullivan's colors were an American flag, with a spread eagle in the center, and a shamrock, Irish arms and a monogram in the corner.

Mitchell sported white colors, with a blue border, the center showing the initials "C. M."

The fight did not come up to expectations, but it was so important that the result was eagerly looked for in boxing circles all over the world. There was a wide difference of opinion regarding the showing of the men. The Sullivan cohorts insisted the champion had by far the better of the milling, and based this assertion on the fact that the Englishman had often fouled by going down without being hit.

On the other hand, Mitchell's adherents declared that the latter proved his superiority, and that the decision to call off hostilities at the end of the thirty-ninth round, and make the affair a draw, was a fortunate occurrence for Sullivan.

The muddy condition of the ring was in Mitchell's favor, as Sullivan's greater weight was a handicap on such heavy footing. 

Mitchell was foxy, tricky and knew how to make use of every inch of the big ring. Sullivan knocked him down fifteen times, but Charley didn't appear to be a whole lot the worse for this, and came back to scratch gamely after each fall.

Frequently during the proceedings, the principals were compelled to stand against the ropes and rest, this being caused by the unstable footing, and their consequent exhaustion. But they carried on desperately. However, when darkness set in and the condition of the trampled, rain-soaked turf was such that it was dangerous for the men to continue, it was high time to call a halt. 

At the close of the thirty-eighth round, both Sullivan and his opponent had fallen down from sheer weariness. So the officials prevailed upon him to agree to a draw in the thirty-ninth session, and it thus listed in the records. They had fought for three hours and eleven minutes.

Upon returning home, Sullivan was given a great ovation by the fight fans, despite the somewhat unsatisfactory finish of the fight with Mitchell. Their confidence in him was unshaken, and he was frequently the guest of honor at banquets held in the East, with Boston specializing in these festive tributes to the champion. 

It was during one of these social functions at the Hub that Sullivan was informed Richard K. Fox had awarded a belt to Jake

Kilrain, designating the latter as world's champion. Of course, Fox had not the slightest right to bestow a title on Kilrain, but the crafty editor and publisher of the Police Gazette attained his object, the luring of John L. into a match with Kilrain. 

Now Sullivan had no objection personally to Jake Kilrain, as square and game a scrapper as ever answered the call of time. 

But he did have an intense hatred for Jake's sponsor, Fox. This dated back to Sullivan's New York debut, when he visited the 

Gazette office with Billy Madden, his manager.

The Boston Strong Boy wasn't favorably impressed by Fox, whom he considered too "high hat." Fox on his part, took umbrage at Sullivan's independent, don't-care-a-damn demeanor. The editor was accustomed to fighters who truckled to him, for the Police Gazette was all-powerful then in the sports world, and could do much toward the making or breaking of a pugilist, or, in fact, most any kind of athlete.

Billy Madden told the writer some years ago, that Sullivan's initial meeting with Fox made enemies of the men right from the start, and that he was glad to get away from the office without the brewing storm actually developing into a furious gale of destruction. "Fact is," said Madden, "Richard K. Fox had a very sarcastic tongue, and he dropped a couple of remarks that got Sullivan on the raw edge. Now, mind you, John wasn't a quarrelsome chap by any means, no matter what was said and written about him. At this time he was just a young, rather unsophisticated fellow, who didn't hardly drink at all, and peaceable-minded, as long as he was let alone. "But he was no fool either, and nobody could insult him, even in a roundabout way, and get away with it. Something Fox said got under his skin, and from then on till we left, I was afraid of worse happening. "It wouldn't have taken much more to make Sullivan manhandle him, and that would have been a fine kettle of fish. The whole trouble was that Richard K. Fox was used to having all the fighting men kowtow to him, and Sullivan, who had a way of looking straight in your eyes when he spoke, stared at Fox in a manner that meant plainly he was the equal of the editor or any other person. When Fox tossed off ironical comment, Sullivan got right back at him with real, unadulterated contempt, and I know Fox sensed danger and was mighty glad when John walked out without noticing the publisher's outstretched hand. "I was sorry things worked out as they did, for Fox was a good friend, but a bad enemy, and if he had it in for a sporting man, could, and would, do him a lot of damage. The Police 

Gazette was a mighty influential paper those days, and don't think that it didn't put the kibosh on Sullivan in lots of ways! "For years Sullivan was attacked by the Gazette, and while his many friends ignored what they knew to be unfair journalism, these attacks hurt him a lot with folks who didn't know what a square shooter John really was. It was Fox's inuendoes and accusations in print that started most of the big dailies after Sullivan, too. He was always good copy, on account of his national importance, and he was also a good target on which the spacewriters could practice!"

CHAPTER IV 

ULLIVAN agreed to give Kilrain a shot at his title. But he indignantly refused Fox's offer to present the victor with a belt. He would have none of the Police Gazette trophy, which he designated as "Richard K. Fox's dog-collar!" The newspapers picked up the phrase, which tickled the public fancy, and made 

Fox boil over with helpless rage. He could only wait and hope that Kilrain would obtain vengeance for him by whipping Sullivan. The fates declared otherwise! 

That Richburg, Miss., fight of July 8, 1889, immortalized as the last bare knuckle championship mill, saw John L. Sullivan reach the zenith of his pugilistic popularity. For months previous to that date, the world of sport buzzed and hummed with talk of the coming Sullivan-Kilrain battle. From all parts of Uncle Sam's domain special trains carried ring patrons to the scene of action. 

The ring stood back from the railroad tracks on a small knoll, situated in a three-sided amphitheatre, with seats ranging tier over tier. These were filled to capacity, with prices ranging from $50 to $200 for choice locations. It was extremely hot, and the hatless, coatless spectators suffered the tortures of Hades under the burning sun.

But once the fight got under way there were no complaints. 

Referee Fitzpatrick took charge and the bare-fisted gladiators went into action. It was a fast, thrilling battle from the start. 

First blood was scored by Kilrain with a punch which ripped

Sullivan's ear in the seventh round, but immediately afterwards Jake dropped without being hit, and Sullivan protested. 

Starting with the twelfth, Sullivan began to play for the body and Kilrain showed signs of distress. Both men were grimly determined and the battle was waged with unrelenting ferocity, Sullivan having the better of matters, and so it continued, round after round. Often the principals seemed on the verge of utter collapse, due to the fast pace they set under the flaming rays of the sun.

Kilrain retreated frequently under Sullivan's bombardments, and John L., on these occasions, growled at him to "stand up and fight like a man!" This Jake did not see fit to do, and his clever evasions more than once brought explosions of temper from the riled champion. Kilrain was knocked down a number of times, but invariably came back heroically.

Still, barring accidents, it was plainly Sullivan's fight. From the fifty-first to the sixty-ninth round, Jake made spasmodic rallies, but then had to resort to milling on the retreat in order to stay on his feet. He occasionally got home on Sullivan with left and right punches, but the blows lacked steam and were ineffective.

It was the sixty-eighth round that spelled eventual disaster for 

Kilrain. Sullivan, after hammering Jake's ribs, feinted, and nailed 

Kilrain with a stunning right uppercut that toppled him over.

Though he tottered through seven more rounds with unexampled grit and endurance, his hopes of victory had vanished. 

The end came in the seventy-fifth round, when a right to the jaw sent Kilrain down. Thereupon Charley Mitchell, his chief second, walked to Sullivan's corner and proposed a draw. This was refused. Mike Donovan, the other second to Kilrain, then threw in the sponge, ending the fray. The fight had lasted 2 hours, 15 minutes, 25 seconds. 

That decision_left the mighty John L. Sullivan still champion under London Prize Ring rules, an honor never wrested from him, inasmuch as the battle was the last championship affair to take place according to the bare knuckle code of the roped arena. 

With the victory came also the realization by the fistic experts that no man could beat Sulivan in a straight, face-to-face, standup fight, because of the Boston Strong Boy's terrific hitting powers. They agreed that if he was dethroned, it must be by a man of unusual scientific cleverness, who might manoeuver so that the champion could not get in close enough to make his strength and punching power felt at their best. 

It was just such a boxer who wrote finis to Sullivan's spectacular ring career. He was James J. Corbett, a young ex-bank clerk, who hailed from California. Corbett had run up a highly creditable professional record, and went on Sullivan's trail after he had gotten something of a line on the champion's methods in a four round exhibition with the great slugger. 

He knew Sullivan could punch with sledge-hammer force, but figured out that the big fellow would not be difficult to jab, and might be made to miss frequently, gradually lose his strength and endurance, and lose in a wearing-down battle. And as it turned out, the youth from the Coast had made a very correct diagnosis of Sullivan's weakness.

Sullivan and Corbett signed articles to fight at the Olympic Club, New Orleans, on September 7, 1892. This was to be the third event and grand climax of a Fistic Carnival Week, with three championship titles involved. On the opening night, George

Dixon, featherweight champion, kayoed Jack Skelly in eight rounds; and the second program offered Jack McAuliffe, lightweight champion, defending his title against Billy Myers of

Streator, a bout won by the champion in a 15 round knockout. 

But the great attraction was the much-heralded meeting between the undefeated champion, the one and only John L. and the agile, magically skilled young boxer who hailed from the 

Golden Gate. As might have been expected, the Big Fellow was top-dog at heavy odds in the betting. But gamblers, fistic prophets of the newspapers, and nine out of ten ring followers were completely flabergasted when their predictions went crazily blooey. 

For Jim Corbett knocked out John L. Sullivan in twenty-one rounds!

Train loads of visitors from far and near poured into New

Orleans to attend that memorable battle. Each carried its quota of straight sports, gunmen, pickpockets and gamblers. But so keen was the public interest, that the gents of society's shady side of life were greatly outnumbered by respectable citizens of good standing.

The Olympic Club was dressed in gala style for the big occasion. The wide tarpaulins that formed the roof were removed, leaving only bare rafters, so as to better ventilate the place. Above the bright stars twinkled down upon the spectators. But before the fight started, a heavy downpour of rain drenched the spectators, and the tarpaulins had to be hurriedly replaced. The ring floor, contrary to present-day practice, was formed of turf, and had become wet and soggy. To remedy this condition and protect the fighter's footing, sawdust was sprinkled heavily on the turf. 

When the men entered the ring it was plain to discerning eyes that the challenger was in far finer condition than the champion. 

Corbett, lean, supple and wiry, was the living personification of physical perfection. Sullivan's heavy body was impressively massive, and suggestive of brute strength. But John L. carried a lot of excess fat, and was certainly a long way from being in the shape our modern standards would demand of a champion about to defend his title.

It was to be speed, youth and scientific generalship against bulky muscular power slowed down by age and fast living, a gifted exponent of a new style of boxing against old traditional slugging methods; and archaic milling tactics were doomed to defeat. One hour and twenty-three minutes later, the cool, smiling youth from California would be crowned the new world heavyweight champion, in the first battle staged for that title under Marquis of Queensberry rules with gloves.

That tensed expectant crowd was to be given an unforgettable thrill in the twenty-first round, by the sight of John L. Sullivan lying helpless on the ring floor, knocked out for the first time in his spectacular career, his autocratic reign of ten years as fistic monarch finished!

From the start of the battle to its end, there was no doubt as to Corbett's superiority over the great battler whose punching powers had won all down the line, whose terrifying presence and thunderous voice had scared many a wilted pugilistic aspirant into submission.

For John L. was clearly outclassed at every angle. He was outgeneraled, outpointed, outspeeded by the cleverest big man that ever stepped into a ring.

The scientist triumphed over the exponent of brute force. Corbett displayed cleverness such as the excited spectators had never before witnessed. Thousands of dollars changed hands on the result, with the losers in a huge majority, as the Sullivanites had bet heavily on their vanquished hero, and they far outnumbered the canny, but comparatively few, fans who backed the Californian.

Time and again Sullivan rushed his elusive opponent, delivering his famous thunderbolt right, but he might as well have been striking at a shadow. Corbett was always out of range. 

When John L. leaped forward furiously in attempts to corner 

Corbett, his young adversary invariably sidestepped with agile grace, and the Big Fellow blundered aimlessly into the ropes.

If The Boston Strong Boy did manage to get in close, and cut loose with both hands, he found that Corbett had a knack of either blocking the punches, or tying Sullivan up in knots. Meanwhile, Jim kept up the steady, unmerciful rain of jabs that Sullivan could not escape or block, and which gradually wore him down to a point of exhaustion.

In the twenty-first round, with only one minute and a half to go before the call of time, Sullivan led with a weak jab. He jumped back, rushed in again, to be halted in his tracks by a rapid succession of straight lefts to the face. Sullivan's nose bled profusely, as Corbett started his final, devastating assault. 

Rights and lefts to body and face stung the helpless John L. 

Corbett closed in, smashed a right on the ear, and followed with a sharp, stunning left hook to the jaw. Sullivan reeled and fell.

He made a feeble effort to arise, but the struggle was futile, and he collapsed. It was a clean, undisputable knockout!

When John L. came to, he arose, faced the crowd and said: "I fought once too often. But I'm glad that it was an American beat me, and that the championship stays in this country!”

That terse, manly acknowledgment of defeat was characteristic of the man. He never fought again, but thereafter confined his public activities to appearances on the stage in acting roles and vaudeville, and as a lecturer.

New Orleans. And all over the world in front of newspaper bulletin boards, in the streets of New York, London, Paris, Boston, Chicago, San Francisco; in Melbourne and Sydney, Australia; in Capetown, Johannesburg, Africa—wherever the boxing game flourished, men and women of all grades of the social order crowded to read the awesome news of John L.'s Waterloo, and many of them wept unshamed.

For Sullivan's downfall was something far more to the millions who admired him than the present generation can easily understand. He had become a sort of national symbol of glory in which even those who had little interest in the prize ring somehow felt was a tribute to America's greatness in one field of heroic endeavor. None of his successors on the heavyweight throne, not even the colorful, popular Jack Dempsey, ever equalled Sullivan as regarded the latter's hold on the public's affections. 

Napoleon Bonaparte's grim, hard-bitten veteran grenadiers wept bitterly when they bade farewell to their dethroned general and 

Emperor when he left them for exile to Elba. And it isn't stretching the probabilities absurdly to assert that Sullivan exercised a

similar magnetic influence over his devoted adherents. In both cases we see the adulation naturally given to the fighting spirit which had always reigned dominant in the human race. John 

L. was most assuredly the Napoleon of the fistic brigade in his day.

I have referred to the excitement caused in the big cities of the universe by his defeat. But that tremendous surge of public feeling was by no means confined to metropolitan circles. It extended to every humble hamlet and burg in these United States. 

As an instance of how widespread and deeply centered was that belief in the legend of John L.'s invincibility, it is well to mention what occurred at a mining camp not far from Peublo, Colorado. They were all, without an exception, rabid Sullivan admirers in that locality. Nobody there ever dreamed that their idol could be toppled from its pedestal by any living man. 

So when the news of The Boston Strong Boy's fall flashed across the wires, the surprised and indignant miners insisted that it was all an infernal lie. Indeed, their anger reached such a mark of fury that they stormed the telegraph office from which the story emanated, wrecked the place, smashed the telegraph instruments, and threatened to lynch the luckless operator. The latter might have been sacrificed to the vengeance of the mob, had not some cooler heads among them helped him to escape through a back window. Not until newspapers reached the camp, confirming the sorrowful tidings of disaster, were the miners convinced.

JOHN CHAPTER V 

OHN L. SULLIVAN made several very successful crosscountry tours, the most lucrative of which was that of 1883, following his victory over Paddy Ryan, by which he won the heavyweight crown. He grossed $227,000, splitting $189,000 with his manager, Al Smith.

In that one year he made and virtually gave away a fortune. It was a protracted season of wild money-spending and wine-drinking by John L. While most of the money was foolishly wasted, much of it went to charity. According to the official figures supplied by his business manager, he had given to recognized charities, as well as to members of the down-and-out brigade, a total of $29,700. He didn't merely toss it, he hurled it away with his big generous heart and hand!

In every city he visited, the Sisters of Charity never failed to call upon him. Sullivan was a devout Catholic, and Catholic institutions never had to ask him twice for alms. He responded lavishly. 

In Chicago, two Sisters of Charity approached him. Sullivan put a fifty dollar bill in the hand of one of them, remarking: "Here, sister, you're far more worthy of this than is John I. 

Sullivan. I'm sure you'll put it to better use than I would!"

On that same day the manager of the leading theatre in Houston, Texas, wired him, offered an engagement for the following Friday night. Sullivan had previously appeared there to capacity houses for one week. The guarantee offered was $3,000 for a one night stand.

John L., glancing at the calendar, noted that the date on which his services were requested was Good Friday, and telegraphed back: "Not on your life. I never box on a Good Friday!" 

With such lightness did the Big Fellow throw away a chance to make three grand! 

Like Jack Dempsey of this generation, Sullivan's generosity was often imposed upon. Tramps, hangers-on, broken-down boxers, ordinary beggars, derelicts of all races and classes-he was an easy mark for them all. None went away from him empty-handed.

On his first boxing tour Sullivan carried along nine men. Among the boxers were Mike Donovan, Pete McCoy, Steve Taylor, Mike 

Gillespie, Bob Farrell, Herbert Slade. Al Smith officiated as his manager, Jake Munzinger as treasurer, and Frank Moran, who later succeeded Smith as manager, handled the publicity department. Moran's duties also included trying to make John L. behave and keep things running smoothly, by no means a soft assignment. 

It was purely a boxing troupe, with Taylor, Donovan, Slade and 

McCoy acting as Sullivan's sparring partners.

Many men tried to earn the $1,000 Sullivan offered to anyone who could stay four rounds with him, but none succeeded. It was a good paying tour, but there were rough times for Moran and Smith, who had their hands full keeping John L. from tearing loose

on his own convivial account and indulging in the gleeful pastime of painting the town red. He was a sort of mature Peck's bad boy, brimful of the joy of living, and impishly careless regarding the future.

They drew record-breaking houses in Boston and journeyed on to Denver. When the show opened in the Colorado city, the building was so jammed that spectators were hanging high up on the rafters of the ceiling. Not another person could get into the place. 

From there, the troupe went to Butte, Montana, always a good fight town, where the miners turned out en masse to welcome the conquering hero. And, as was his wont, Sullivan spent the better part of an afternoon in one barroom after another, with the result that he acquired a generous jag by the time he was due to appear in the theatre.

Nevertheless, as the management had advertised the usual offer of $1,000 to any man who could last four rounds with Sullivan, the champion had to make good, even though he was not fighting-fit. 

A big miner of local fighting repute had determined to take a chance for the money and was egged on by his friends, who thought him capable of giving Sullivan a tough argument. Al Smith had a chat with the challenger and concluded that the latter was a dangerous fellow, not to be lightly considered, especially with Sullivan in a half-woozy condition. Smith was really worried and conferred with the champion in the latter's dressing room. 

Al informed John L. that he didn't like the prospect at all, but drew nothing save a growl from the Boston Strong Boy, on whom Donovan and Slade were working, trying to sober him up a bit and get him ready for the bout. Back went Smith to the miner's quarters to hand him the gloves which were to be used in the contest. The manager's opinion that this was a genuinely tough hombre was strengthened when he saw the miner stripped to the waist, and he returned to Sullivan. "Look here, John," said Smith, "I don't like this by any means. 

You're pretty groggy, and if I'm any judge of a scrapper, this big, red-bearded duck is hard as nails and damned bad medicine. Let's not take a fool chance. I'll make some excuse and call the bout off for tonight, anyway!" 

Sullivan's bull-like roar of contempt echoed through the room. "Me call it off, is it?" shouted the champion. "A likely thing, a

Sullivan afraid of a big mining slob! No, no, Al, bring him on. 

T'hell with your red-bearded miner. That beard 'ill come in handy for him to keep the blood from swamping him altogether, after I get through mauling him. As big as me, is he? Well, I like 'em that way. The bigger they are, the easier it is to hit 'em!" 

Al Smith gave up his peace proposals. There was no arguing with Sullivan in his present mood. In fact, his manager's expostulations had but served to arouse him to a fit of uncontrollable fighting rage, for he deeply resented Smith's evident fear lest he, the great John L. might chance to be licked. And Al's reference to

the red beard seemed to heighten John's ill-humor. When Sullivan sighted that hairy appendage, it appeared to act on his nerves much as would the waving of a red rag before an irritable bull. 

The principals were given a great ovation when they appeared on the stage, and the miners yelled encouragingly at their representative as the announcer bellowed: “Gentlemen, it gives me pleasure to introduce Jeff Tomkins, the

Montana fighter who means to relieve Mr. Sullivan of that one thousand dollars by staying four rounds with the champion tonight."

The house rocked with stormy applause, and when it died away, the star was presented as "The Champion of Champions—John L. 

Sullivan !"

Then another tempest of cheers raged, for strong as the spectators might be for the local scrapper, they didn't forget the reputation of the slugging marvel from Boston. Sullivan wabbled slightly on his legs as he tossed up a hand in acknowledgment of the greeting, and the gesture brought a chorus of laughter. The onlookers sensed that Sullivan was under the influence of liquor, and that Mr. 

Tomkin's self-imposed task of holding off the champ might not be so hard to accomplish under such conditions. 

When the bell sounded, the miner left his corner in a rush, met Sullivan more than half way, and cut loose with a vicious swing that just grazed the champion's chin. That woke up the champion! "So you're trying a fast one, hey?" he roared, and let go a whistling right that hit the miner plumb on his Adam's Apple. 

The local man gasped for breath, but Sully gave him no rest, clouted him on the mouth with a left and the blood spurted in streams from cut lips and damaged nose, soaking the red beard beneath to a darker crimson. Again the Sullivan right banged home, this time on the chin. The miner's hands dropped to his sides, his eyes were glassy, his knees sagged. Sullivan stepped back and his opponent fell face-forward on the floor. 

The bout was over in exactly a minute and 27 seconds. Sullivan's stated preference for large targets to aim at had been fully justified. 

The victim in the case delivered himself of a timely bon mot when he regained his senses. One of his mates happened to remark that anyone could see the champ was drunk, when he came in the ring. 

Said the battered Tomkins reproachfully: "Drunk, was he? Thank the good Lord for that. I suppose if he'd been sober, he'd have killed me!"

From Butte the tour continued westward to Victoria, B. C., where Sullivan was given a royal reception. He was not only accorded the freedom of the city, but was guest of honor on a British warship, and at a dinner tendered him by local sportsmen.

The mayor of Victoria, an ardent fight fan, conceived the idea that a huge French Canadian lunberjack, named Boiquet, a good slugger with his fists, but also a skilled La Savate performer, might have a chance of earning the $1,000 offered by the champion to

anyone who could stay four rounds with him. If the pace got too hot for Boiquet, there was a chance that he might slip one over on the champion by using his feet. A kick on the chin wouldn't be playing the game, according to the Queensberry rules, but if it outed Sullivan the result would be sensational anyhow. 

So the wily Mayor proceeded to ingratiate himself with the pugilist by telling him how honored Victoria was by his visit, and then hinted slyly that he had heard that Sullivan's $1,000 offer was made purely for publicity purposes. "Publicity!" snorted Sullivan, his ire aroused by the query. "If you've anyone around here who wants to try his luck with me, just trot him out, and I'll soon show you what sort of publicity it is!"

Sullivan's reaction was just what the mayor wanted. Four of the latter's sportive friends had combined with the city executive to bring about a meeting between the lumberjack and the champion. 

They promised Boiquet $250 if he lost, and if he won, he would receive a handsome bonus in addition to the $1,000 guaranteed the man who could stay four rounds with John L.

Continuing his conversation, the mayor went on to state that their local pride, Boiquet, thought he could whip any man in the world and had boasted publicly that he would lick the champion, if given a chance. on. "So he thinks he's a world-beater ?" queried Sullivan disgustedly. "I've heard the likes of him talk that way before. Well, bring him 

I'll teach him the difference between fighting and bragging!"

So the bout was arranged. A pool was organized and thousands of dollars were scattered around the lumber camps to be wagered at good odds. The mayor and his co-plotters felt pretty sure of making a financial killing. If Boiquet stuck the four rounds, there would be a grand cleanup!

The night of the show Boiquet was brimming over with selfconfidence. Manager Al Smith heard about the wild betting that was in progress, scented a plot, and investigated, with the result that he was convinced some sort of a trap was being laid for the champion. He communicated his suspicions to John L. but, as usual, the latter derided the idea that he could be made the victim of any kind of a setup.

Smith visited the lumberjack's dressing room and watched the latter's handlers rubbing their principal's huge body with oil from head to foot. They didn't know who Smith was and informed him gleefully that the purpose of the oiling was to make Sullivan's blows slip off Boiquet's body. Smith was still more interested when they proceeded to wrap several yards of red flannel around Boiquet's stomach. "This flannel," they told Al, "is to protect the belly. Sullivan punches hard, but his body wallops won't hurt so much tonight!"

Smith returned to Sullivan and gave him an account of the precautions taken by the opposition. John L. was amused, and at the same time a trifle riled.

"So it's oil and red flannel, now, hey?" he growled. "Well, I'll make that sucker see red, Al. Come on, let's go!"

The lumberjack was a queer sight as he entered the ring, with his oiled body and red flannel bandage around his mid-section. Sullivan stared at him, as they donned the two ounce gloves. Said the champion, with a palpable sneer: "Why in blazes didn't they give you a chest-protector and facemask?"

John L. had been patronizing the local gin mills in his customary fashion, besides imbibing cheerfully at the numerous parties given in his honor. Consequently, he was in no better fighting shape than when he outed the Butte miner, but the condition question was one that never bothered the easy-going biffer from Boston. 

When the fight started, Boiquet landed a light tap on Sullivan's nose. The champion retaliated with a right to the stomach, behind which he didn't put all his force. Nevertheless, Boiquet dropped his guard and bent over, apparently in pain, and there was a look of agony on his face. He wasn't really hurt, however, but was putting on an act in pursuance of the plans made by him and his backers.

Sullivan rushed and swung his right. As he did so, Boiquet went down on his hands, and let fly his right foot, aiming for the chin, as La Savate fighters do. But he missed by an inch, as Sullivan swiftly back-stepped out of range.

The champion saw through the trick. Before Boiquet could regain an erect stance, the champion's right smashed to his jaw in a terrific uppercut. Boiquet's feet left the floor, he turned in mid air, and his huge figure hurtled downwards to the boards. 

The eyes of the stricken La Savate artist were closed, his mouth was wide open. He was gasping. His arms were thrust up stiffly in a praying attitude, his body twisted in an odd contortion, his legs spread out. Then he slumped into a limp heap, and did not recover consciousness for several minutes. Sullivan stooped, grabbed his fallen opponent around the waist and lugged him to his corner. 

When Boiquet was revived, the champion said: "So you thought that French kick 'ud fool me, eh? Listen, fellow, never try that stuff again. Learn to fight the American way, with your fists!" He slapped the crestfallen gladiator on the back, and bowed in response to the thunderous cheers that shook the building. The humiliated lumberjack stumbled from his corner to where Sullivan was standing, caught the champion's hand and shook it warmly. "You are of all men mos' wonderful, Mr. Sullivan," said the 

French-Canadian in his broken accent. "Me, Boiquet, am strongest man in Canada. I break trees and fences wit' my fists, but you could break stones, is it not so? I am of La Savate the championbut you, I now know why of all champions you are the champion!"

Much elated by his quick victory and the applause given him, Sullivan left the stage to attend a dinner tendered him by the City.

There his scorn of diplomacy was again in evidence. For he refused to join in a toast to England's queen. When the toastmaster requested all present to rise and drink her Majesty's health, Sullivan alone remained seated. Naturally, the loyal subjects of the

British Empire resented his action, but little cared the brusque John L. He arose, walked a bit unsteadily toward the door, stopped half way, and addressing the guests, growled menacingly: "A true Irishman never drinks the health of a British ruler, King or Queen!"

That rash statement, duly published in the newspapers next day, caused a decided commotion, and resulted in something like a nearriot being staged before Sullivan's hotel. Those who had previously cheered him were now clamoring for his scalp, which, under the circumstances, was not surprising. So great was the public agitation that early the following morning, he and his party left Victoria for Texas.

On the way down South, Sullivan got into many other scrapes. 

At Fort Worth, Texas, during the small hours of the morning he got into a heated verbal argument with a bunch of cowboys in a dance hall, which was terminated by John L. hitting one of the group on the jaw and knocking him unconscious. 

As might have been expected, a genuine free-for-all scrap followed. Chairs and fists flew in all directions, and when order was partially restored, several cowboys were stretched bleeding on the floor, the bartender had gone to cover and pretty girl dancers were fleeing to safety.

The champion, with his fighting aides clustered around him, bellowed an order for drinks for the house, swore that there was no hard feelings on his part, and soon had all present guzzling champagne at his expense. This drinking spree lasted till dawn. Al 

Smith arrived and tried to persuade John L. to leave, but the champion was deaf to his appeals.

But later, when Smith was tipped off that some of the cowboys, now well liquored up, and with their guns handy, had planned a drastic revenge on the plastered champion, he induced Donovan, Slade and Munziger to drag John L. to the railway station by combined force, where they boarded a train and made a quick getaway. 

It was a lucky escape, for the chances were about a hundred to one that Sullivan would have been shot and killed, had the cowboys cornered him.

The following day, at Dallas, there was more trouble. It frequently happened that when Sullivan was disporting himself in a barroom, some local fighter would try to pick a quarrel with him, if The Boston Strong Boy chanced to be much under the influence of liquor, with the hope of getting a reputation by besting the champion in an "off-the-reel" scrap. That's what happened at Dallas. 

Jack Traynor, a local heavyweight, was introduced to Sullivan at the bar. John L. bought several drinks in his usual free-handed fashion, and was rather shaky on his legs as a result of many libations. Suddenly Traynor remarked:

"You've got a phoney reputation, Sullivan. You brag better than you can fight. And I'm here to tell you I can whip you right now, without training a minute."

This was a neat introduction by way of starting a scrap, and it served its purpose, although not as Traynor intended. For the words had scarcely left his mouth before Sullivan wheeled about, let go his famous right, and Traynor went spinning to the furthest corner of the room, there to fall, with a wrecked jaw, knocked cold!

The onlookers yelled in a mad chorus of approval, for the local man was a good deal of a bully, and none of those regretted his downfall. As for Sullivan, that convivial gentlemen brushed aside the episode as if it were something that didn't concern him at all, and repaid the congratulations of those present in his favorite way -ordering champagne for the house. 

It would be superfluous to chronicle in detail all of such melodramatic incidents which accompanied the triumphant tour of John L. At this date they seem indescribably funny and were certainly exciting and prolific in thrills. But the members of his party had to be constantly on the qui vive in order to conduct their rip-roaring star safely through the aftermath of consequences bred from his habit of stirring things up.

At all events, the pace proved altogether too fast for Slade, the giant Maori, who sought rest for his too highly tensed nerves by deserting the company at Salt Lake City. The dusky warrior took up his permanent abode there, married a Mormon girl and became a faithful follower of the prophet, Brigham Young.

It would fill a goodly-sized volume to narrate in detall all that happened on Sullivan's initial tour. Decidedly, it was the most remarkable ever undertaken by any fighter before or after his time. 

Victims of all calibres, both in the ring and outside the ropes, fell before the ponderous fists of the Boston Strong Boy. They all looked alike to John L.!

He came, he saw, he conquered, and he left a trail of dollars behind him, wherever he went. He didn't hang on to his big earnings. On the contrary, he threw money away with royal abandon, despite all the agonized protests of Al Smith, who was himself a generous spender, but none the less aghast at the manner in which Sullivan recklessly dissipated the rewards of his exertions. 

The trip lasted until May, 1884. During that time Sullivan put away 59 opponents, and the knockouts were scored in from twenty seconds to nine minutes. Where he so far outclassed an opponent that to carry him along might have resulted in a fatality, John had a knack of stopping his man with a couple of well-directed punches, thus saving him from further punishment. 

In McKeesport, Pa., a big, brawny ironworker, James McCoy, insisted upon coming on the stage and tackling the champion. He had a good local reputation, weighed 217 pounds, and believed, as many had done before him, that Sullivan couldn't stop him within the four round limit.

That was McCoy's mistake. Sullivan was in rare fettle that night. It only required two punches to send McCoy into dreamland, twenty-one seconds by actual timing. So powerful was the kayo wallop, that McCoy was taken to hospital with a dislocated jaw. Asked by a reporter what his impressions of Sullivan's hitting powers were, he replied: "I've wanted to meet the champ for a long while. I though he was over-rated, that most any big, strong chap like myself had a good chance of lowering his colors. My wish was granted, and 

I've changed my mind. I never thought anyone could hit like him. 

What a godawful, murderous wallop he packs! A sledge-hammer isn't in it! It's getting pretty close to plotting homicide to put any ordinary man in the ring with John L. I've had my lesson, and 

I'm satisfied. Anyhow I can boast for the rest of my life that I once fought John L. Sullivan!"

From McKeesport the tour continued to St. Louis. There Jim 

Miles, better known as "Gypsy" Brady, asked for a shot at the champion. Miles weighed around 170 pounds, but looked much lighter, and Sullivan, on seeing him, disdainfully announced that his would-be opponent was too small. "They'll have me fighting bantams next!" commented John L.

As Miles insisted on appearing in the ring, Sullivan, to oblige him, offered to put the St. Louis boxer on with some other member of the troupe, Donovan for choice. But the militant Miles refused this concession. "It's you I want to fight," he declared defiantly. "I know I can lick you in four rounds!"

Sullivan lost patience on hearing this remark, and told Miles to come on the stage that evening and take his medicine. "I'll teach that braggart to speak out of turn," said the champion to Mike Donovan.

The house was packed to capacity when the show started. All the front rows were occupied by the staunch adherents of Jim. 

Miles, from which vantage points they meant to cheer the local favorite on to victory. They did cheer him with a vim, when he appeared stripped for action, and as a further token of their regard, booed and hissed the champion.

That was all needed to send Sullivan's none too docile temper into a fury of rage. He eyed Miles so wickedly that Donovan and 

Smith glanced at each other with expressions that spoke as eloquently as words could have done "This won't last long!" 

Miles stepped forward and feinted with his left. Sullivan let go his right. It came up in an arc and landed squarely under 

Miles' chin. That one blow really ended the bout proper, for Miles went down in a heap, knocked senseless. But it didn't finish Miles' activities that night.

When the St. Louis man recovered, he leaped wildly to his feet, and rushed at Sullivan, who was standing by the ropes. The chief of police, who had entered the ring immediately after Sullivan

landed the kayo, made a grab at Miles, but all he got for his pains was a clout on the jaw that dropped him. 

The referee then took a hand in the game, and tried to catch hold of Miles, who was temporarily deranged by the kayo punch, which had sent him on a mad rampage. But the third man in the ring fared no better than the well-meaning police chief. He too was the recipient of a wallop that stretched him on the stage. 

The crowd applauded frantically. They were getting more than their money's worth, for Miles seemed set to go all night as long as there were jaws to belt. But Sullivan, who had been laughing heartily, decided that the joke had gone far enough.

As Miles came tearing in, the champion set himself and swung his right to the chin. Miles' feet left the stage, and he sailed out through space, crashing into the orchestra, on top of the startled musicians. Two violins were smashed by the impact of Miles' body, and the orchestra-leader, on whom the local pug fell first, was hurled clean out into the pit.

Take it in all, it was a grand night for everybody, except Jim Miles. The latter was out for twenty minutes from the effects of the second and final kayo. Miles took it philosophically enough. "I did the best I could," he said, "but it wasn't anywhere near enough. Sullivan just ain't human, he's more of a gorilla than a 

His first punch took away my senses, and he knocked 'em back to normal when he belted me the second time. Well, there's only one John L.!" man.

Thus the amazingly dramatic series of events continued day after day, month after month after month, until the conclusion of the tour. Drunk or sober, it made no difference; Sullivan always got his man! Even when under the influence of liquor he seemed to steady up and get his bearings once he had the gloves on, more especially so if his opponent landed the first punch. 

That never failed to wake him up, and send him into furious action, out for blood and fully geared to administer a knockout. 

There is no doubt that he possessed a sort of sixth sense when in the ring that guided him safely through the stress of combat. I have seen other fighters equipped with a similar mental barometer, but John L. had an edge on all his contemporaries in that respect.

CHAPTER VI 

HEN Terry McGovern, featherweight champion, was training for his battle with Young Corbett, which took place in 1901 at Hartford, Conn., and resulted in the hitherto invincible 

Terry being kayoed in the second round, an incident occurred which was curiously characteristic of Sullivan's irresponsible actions when the Bostonian was off on a tear.

Terry and his staff, with Joe Humphreys in charge, were staying at Stratford. The great little Brooklyn boxer had no more ardent admirer in the world than John L., who saw in the terror of the featherweight a miniature counterpart of himself.

Sullivan, who had been residing with Doc Needles at Bridgeport, where the Big Fellow was appearing in the play, "The Man From Boston," suddenly decided to pay a visit to McGovern's camp. 

He was hardly in fit condition for making a social call, as he was in an extremely belligerent mood, having had an all-night session with John Barleycorn.

It was four in the morning when Sullivan and two friends drove to the camp in an old hack that had bounced them around considerably en route. Arriving at their destination, the hackman found John L. fast asleep, and proceeded to awaken him. And then the fun began!

Suddenly aroused from slumber, as the hackman shook him, Sullivan came to life with a snort of anger, and shot a right to the luckless cabby's chin, dropping him like a log. Out of the cab leaped the Bostonian, dragging the limp body of the hackman, and heaved him into a mudpuddle. John's growl of indignation merged into a roar of laughter at what he considered a huge joke on the driver, who came to in time to see his truculent passenger disappearing into the hotel. That it was pitch dark, and Terry and his handlers sleeping peacefully, didn't faze the uninvited guest.

As Sullivan ranged about in search of Terry's sleeping quarters, he saw the Irish cook dozing on a bench. She was entirely bald, and customarily wore a wig, but this had been temporarily removed, and in the shadows Sullivan mistook her for a man. 

Grabbing her roughly by the arm, he ordered: "Up with you, get a move on. Show me to Terry McGovern's room, and be quick about it!"

The half-dazed woman gave vent to a shriek of terror that brought the manager, house detective and several guests rushing to the scene. The intervention of the new arrivals didn't stop 

John L. He was more determined than ever to find Terry. Brushing aside all opposition, he clambered lustily up the stairs in a headlong hunt for the featherweight champion. Bellowing as he searched, pounding on bedroom doors, kicking in panels, his rage grew with every kick. But there was no trace of McGovern. It developed afterwards that Terry, awakened by the mad tumult, hearing Sullivan's lion-like roar, and not knowing who the wild.

raider was, naturally enough feared bodily harm, and had discreetly hidden under his bed.

At length Sullivan, wearying of his unsuccessful search, made a bee line for the bar. There was a large cage in a corner of the room, its occupant a snarling wildcat, whose pugnacious attitude apparently exasperated Sullivan. Humphreys and McGovern had appeared by this time, and it took their combined efforts, aided by Sullivan's two friends, to keep John L. from opening the cage and entering into personal conflict with the beast. 

But they finally got him safely to bed. On the following morning when Sullivan realized the damage he had done, as well as breaking up the sleep of a champion in training, he was properly repentant and apologized humbly. He helped out Terry in the latter's gymnasium stunts that day, as a token of remorse for having disturbed the lad for whom he cherished such deep affection. 

On the night of the Moran-Willard fight in the old Madison 

Square Garden, in 1916, Joe Humphreys, the clarion-voiced announcer, introduced John L. to the crowd. They gave him a great hand, and Sullivan, turning to Humphreys, his steadfast pal, commented: "Joe, if it was possible to turn the clock back twenty years, I'll bet I could take on those two dubs and whip both of 'em in one night!"

He meant every word of it. Like an ancient war horse responding to the call of the battle trumpet, the Champion of Champions would have loved nothing better than to have been one of the contestants that night. It was the last time he ever climbed through the ropes.

It had often been asked why Sullivan retained his popularity and hold on the public affection until the day of his death. The answer is simple-because he was the most generous fighter in the history of the American ring, and that assertion includes all battlers, past and present. With the exception of the short time after he retired, when he unsuccessfully attempted book-making at a New 

York race track, The Boston Strong Boy never belonged to the gambling fraternity, nor did he personally indulge in gambling on a large scale.

In his prime, he gave generously to the needy and all kinds of religious and charitable organizations. Despite his numerous escapades outside of the ring, he was essentially a noble-hearted man. 

He did enough for suffering humanity to earn him the respect of all who knew him in this connection. This sympathetic side of his character was so widely different from that of the John L. Sullivan of the roped arena and barroom brawls, as to give him a virtual Jekyl and Hyde rating.

Even when he journeyed over the country, fighting his way into the hearts of his fellow Irish by stopping all comers in one night stands, that strange mixture of vicious and commendable traits was evident in his complex makeup. First he'd knock an opponent

cold, then clean out a saloon bar, and probably end up by handing out gifts to the poor, needy and sick, to say nothing of champagne all around to his erstwhile enemies. 

Newsboys always came in for their share of his generosity. He liked the lads who sold papers, and often stopped to discuss fights with them. Then he'd grab their papers, buy them outright and toss the journals to the bystanders. Sometimes, he'd persuade the onlookers to purchase papers, and whatever they bought, he'd match with his own money. That was the John L. Sullivan whom the people loved unreservedly, because he was one of themselves. 

Sullivan made enormous sums of money in the ring and in vaudeville, but spent his earnings so lavishly that when he retired he was flat broke, and his friends found it necessary to hold several benefits for him.

But he made a memorable and highly successful comeback on the stage, and again, as Fortune favored him, he gave unlimited relief to those in need. Nor did he show any preference in creed. or race. Though he always drew the color line as a fighter, many a colored pugilist and preacher had good reason to give thanks for the timely aid given them by John L. Many touching human interest stories are related regarding his feelings with Negroes. 

When he returned from a tour of the South, the Morning Sun, a New York paper, attacked the champion for his refusal to meet a colored man in the ring. The sports editor saw fit to assail 

Sullivan in these words: "A fighter who calls himself the heavyweight champion, but balks at meeting black leaders of his profession, while accepting matches with men of ordinary talent, cannot be worthy of the title!" 

This insulting paragraph got under Sullivan's skin, and left him in a bitter mood the day he read it. That afternoon he went to

Boston, and on the train met a Negro preacher who was bound for Providence. They chatted for a while, and then Sullivan proceeded to the smoking car. Mike Donovan, who was traveling with him, told Sullivan how elated the preacher was over having met the champion, and quoted the Negro as having said: "I have heard and read so much about that great man that I consider it a high honor to have been permitted to shake his hand!" 

Sullivan listened gravely, then asked Mike to bring the minister into the smoker. As they sat together, the champion inquired about the colored man's church. He wanted to know where it was located, and how many attended it. Learning that the church was heavily in debt, and the congregation poor, Sullivan beckoned to his treasurer, who was sitting in another seat, had him make out a check for $100, and presented it to the Negro. "Take this," said the champion. "I'm glad to have met you. 

You're a good fellow, and the kind of person I like to help!"

The minister rose, took Sullivan's big right hand in both of his, offered a prayer, and then departed, saying-"God bless you, Mr. 

Sullivan, you are a noble man!"

There are many such instances on record of Sullivan's spontaneous generosity when he was at the height of success. But even, later on, when he was not so well fixed financially, he always managed to help a friend in need. 

During a Southern combination vaudeville and boxing tour, Sullivan and his party arrived at Montgomery, Alabama. While the champion and Donovan were standing outside their hotel, along came an elderly colored man, leading a lively stepping horse. Sullivan halted the Negro. "Can I have a ride?" he asked. "You suah can, boss," replied the Ethiopian, and turned over the animal to the fighter.

Sullivan, who always had a deep affection for horses, and could ride like a Centaur of old, mounted the steed and galloped off down the road. Donovan stood watching him, and then, turning to the 

Negro, asked him if he knew who the rider was. "Deed I don't, sah," responded the colored man. "Well, uncle," said Mike, "your horse is being straddled by the great John L. Sullivan. Ever hear about him?" "Oh, suah. I has, but no sah, it kaint be, yo' is just foolin' this chile," chuckled the Negro.

But Donovan insisted that he wasn't joking, and finally the colored man was convinced. When Sullivan rode back and dismounted, the Negro looked him over carefully. Then putting his hands on his knees, he stared into the champion's eyes and exclaimed: "It is him—it is him, suah 'nuff. I done seen his picture on dem walls in dis hotel. Lor' bless me, if 'taint him, Mist' Sullivan, the great champeen!"

Sullivan laughed and made a playful pass at the old Negro, who jumped back in alarm, yelling: "Don't yo' hit me, Mist' Sullivan, I ain't done nothin'!" 66 Sullivan inquired how much he wanted for the horse. 'Taint for sale, massa. I hab a wife an' two chillun, and this here animal makes us a livin'."

Sullivan dug down into his pocket, produced a twenty dollar bill, and tossed it to the Negro, remarking: "Here, uncle, take that, and buy your children some clothes." 

The ebony visage lighted up with a radiant grin. "May de good Lor' bless yo', Mist' Sullivan," he cried. "I ain't nevah seen so much money befoah. Mah chilluns 'ill hab more clothes than dey eber dreamed was in de world, sah!"

That evening at the hotel, a crowd of colored urchins, having heard of Sullivan's generosity, serenaded the visitors with Negro songs. Sullivan liked the music and got the leader to try his hand at playing some Irish tunes. The boy had never attempted to give a rendition of a Celtic ballad on his banjo, or even sing one, for that matter. But he persevered manfully, and finally ground out the strains of "The Wearing of the Green," much to the champion's

delight. Sullivan had a great time. He made the lads do a buck dance over and over again, and scattered handfuls of silver among them at the entertainment's close.

The little darkies leaped for the coins and their instruments were clean forgotten during the ensuing scramble. One lad's muchprized cornet, an evident Army relic of Civil War days, was broken in the rush. But Sullivan made the grieving owner happy by taking him to a music shop, where he selected a brand-new cornet, and presented it to the dusky musician.

It was such actions as the foregoing that endeared Sullivan to the hearts of millions. Granted that he drew the color line, so far as a battle with Peter Jackson was concerned, it can never be said that he made any race distinction when it came to a question of dispensing the milk of human kindness. Negroes or white folksthey all shared in Sullivan's bounty.

While Sullivan's troupe was giving boxing exhibitions at Hot Springs, there were several broken-down scrappers in the city, who had gone there for the baths. When they heard John L. was around, they knew the time was ripe for a touch, and one by one they called on him, and each went away the richer by a few dollars.

That night Sullivan played to a crowded house. The receipts were large, and Sullivan told Donovan to hunt up the former pugs he had assisted, and bring them to a certain saloon in the neighborhood. When John L. arrived at the rendezvous, the fistic derelicts were there in full force.

The champion produced his bankroll, and distributed bills freely among them. Thus, with no axe to grind, and asking for no reward save the satisfaction it gave his own conscience, Sullivan split the receipts of the opening night with has-been members of the profession in which he had won fame and fortune. 

Outside the saloon he saw an elderly man selling papers. The champion conversed with the news-vender, and learned that his name was John Sweetman, an old bare knuckle fighter, and formerly one of John Morrissey's sparring partners. After a pleasant chat, Sullivan left, but first slipped a fifty dollar bill into the veteran's hand. Sullivan said nothing to anyone about the incident, but the ex-scrapper spread the story around, and it was published in the local papers the following day.

From Hot Springs Sullivan and his company went to Memphis. 

A billboard near their hotel announced a match between Dan 

O'Leary, famous pedestrian, and a local race horse. Incidentally, the champion learned that Dan was broke. 

O'Leary came to the hotel in response to an invitation from Sullivan. They discussed sports in general, and Sullivan inquired how it came about that Dan had to resort to pitting himself against a horse. O'Leary frankly acknowledged that he was in pretty low water, and John L. gave him fifty dollars to help out. 

The race took place as scheduled, and O'Leary's share of the

receipts was far better than he had anticipated. Later on, he went to Sullivan. "John," said the pedestrian, "you did me a damned good turn just when I needed it most. But I'm well fixed now, so here's your money back. Use it for some other poor chap that's in want.”

Sullivan protested against taking the money, but Dan insisted. 

A Catholic priest was present, who had been talking to the champion about a benefit about to be staged in aid of a church-building fund. He was anxious for Sullivan to attend, knowing that the news of the champion's coming would greatly increase the number of prospective patrons. Sullivan consented, and the building was packed on the night of the affair. John L. went the rounds, patronized the booths, took one book of $5 from each of twenty-five girls in charge, and when he left, remarked to O'Leary, who had accompanied him: "Well, Dan, the good Father has got your fifty and my seventyfive. Great fun, wasn't it?"

When Sullivan was appearing at Hartford as Captain Harcourt, in "The Man From Boston," it fell to his lot to substitute for a football hero who is put out of action by drugs administered to him by a love-rival. Sullivan as a gridiron star was distinctly funny.

He played the part with rollicking clumsiness, missing the ball as often as he landed a kick on it, but the audience were pleased anyhow, for it was the great John L. who was giving them a laugh, and whatever he did was all right. men.

As a publicity stunt, the theatre manager arranged a parade for 

Sullivan through Hartford's principal streets. The star rode in a carriage, followed by a brass band, trailed by an army of sports- As the procession came within a few hundred yards of the theatre, the carriage stopped, a bevy of pretty girls stepped forward, and one of them handed Sullivan a number of tickets, explaining that the tickets were for a benefit to be held in aid of the widow of police officer Martin, who had been killed by a bandit. 

Sullivan arose to the occasion with characteristic zeal. Leaping from the vehicle, he rushed to a nearby newspaper stand, swept up an armful of papers, and started a sidewalk auction for the fund. "The sale is on," boomed the champion. "I take the first paper for $100. Who'll buy the next for one dollar!"

The effect was electrical. There was a mad rush by the onlookers, who bought up every paper in jig time. A fresh supply was brought, and when the improvised auction was over, the fund was richer by five hundred dollars. When Sullivan finished his task, he handed ten dollars to the newsie who had furnished the papers. 

It turned out to be a fine bit of publicity for the play, although quite unintentional in that respect, so far as Sullivan was concerned. For the friends of the slain officer, acting on the adage that "one good turn deserves another!" saw to it that the house was jammed to capacity when "The Man From Boston" opened. 

I have already stated that Sullivan, though averse to meeting

colored men in the ring, did not carry this attitude into his private life, and among other examples of his toleration as regarded the Negro race, should be mentioned an incident which occurred when he was training to fight Corbett, in New Orleans.

George Dixon, feather champion, was also getting into shape for his bout with Jack Skelly, at the same time. Sullivan liked 

Little Chocolate, and often rode with him and George's manager,

Tom O'Rourke, on a visit to Dixon's camp. The Southern sports objected to this indiscriminate mixing of white and colored folks, and adverse comment on Sullivan's so-called laxity in this respect appeared in the Crescent City newspapers.

As a matter of fact, the Southerners felt that they had made a great concession in allowing the Dixon-Skelly match to be staged at all. It was the first time in ring history that a fight between a colored and white pugilist had been tolerated below the Mason- Dixon line. From that point of view, it was bad enough, if just barely permissible, for a white boxer to battle a Negro; but next door to a crime to condone social association between the races. 

George Dixon, always a peaceable, easy-going little chap, was very sensitive about the rumpus raised by the newspaper criticism, and suggested to O'Rourke that it would be better if he refrained from riding around with his manager and John L., as he didn't think it wise to arouse Southern antagonism by not conforming to their customs. O'Rourke might have adopted Dixon's stand in deference to the interests of peace. But appeasement policies were never in favor with the warlike Sullivan, who proceeded to lay the law down to O'Rourke in his usual bellicose fashion. “George Dixon," roared the indignant champion, "is my friend, and I won't have the boy Jim-crowed! He'll ride with me, and stay with me whenever and wherever I want him to. If any of those grumbling slobs try to interfere, they've got me to reckon with. And that goes for those measly newspaper editors too!" 

In the face of this stern ultimatum, the storm raised by the upholders of racial discrimination soon faded into a peaceful calm. 

Nobody cared to argue with John L. on that, or any other subject. 

So thereafter, Dixon appeared in Sullivan's company whenever the champion desired, and the conscientious objectors were conspicuous by their absence.

One of the most impressive ovations given Sullivan was upon the occasion of his homecoming to Boston, after losing the title to Corbett. The ex-champion returned to the cultured Hub, scene of his early professional triumphs, the starting point of the mighty slugger's spectacular journey along the fighting trail to the inside throne; a greater hero in defeat than he had been in victory. 

The sports turned out in full force to show their admiration for the man who had made the city as famous pugilistically as did the Boston Tea Party historically. The welcome exceeded in unrestrained enthusiasm anything John L. had experienced in the

heyday of his career. The glory of his deeds was not overshadowed by the aftermath of his downfall. 

Sullivan was deeply affected by the warmth of the reception given him when he appeared at the old Howard Theatre, where in bygone years he had made his fistic debut. Tears from a full heart streamed down the ex-champion's bronzed cheeks, as addressing an overflow audience from the stage, he said: "It's the same old burg, and the same old people, and the same solid friends that used to root for me when I was on top of the heap. It's the greatest moment of my life to be able to stand here, while you folks give a 'has-been' such a grand welcome. Men may come and men may go, but old Boston's people never change. 

They're loyal to the backbone! I thank you all from the bottom of my heart!"

When he finished speaking, pandemonium broke loose. They cheered John L. to the echo, they yelled themselves hoarse. It was the kind of demonstration that no money could buy, no scheming politician awaken. Fallen from his high pugilistic estate, the exchampion was still the Man of the Hour in Boston. Let no one tell you that even Jack Dempsey, or any other fighter of past or modern times was ever the subject of such unadulterated heroworship as John L. Sullivan, the Mighty Son of Swat!

As is well known, for many years Sullivan refused to speak to Corbett. Though he had publicly stated that he was glad an American won the championship, he disliked and shunned the new king of fistiana. And throughout the long period before he became reconciled to his conquereor, he never hesitated to say just what he thought of the brilliant young Californian, and his remarks were far from being complimentary. That, of course, was quite in keeping with his straightforward attitude toward all things in life. 

He always wore his heart on his sleeve. What he thought, he said, in fact he roared emphatically, and his uncompromising antagonism toward those he deemed his enemies was as unwavering as his loyalty toward his friends. Whether he was right or wrong in his estimate of Corbett, is beside the point. Being himself, the dogmatic John L., one could scarcely have expected him to have acted otherwise than he did, up until the soothing hand of Time obliterated bitter memories, and he and Jim finally buried the hatchet.

Sullivan and Corbett were different in every respect but one. 

They were as far apart as the North and South Poles, excepting that both knew how to win public popularity, and did so, although their methods varied widely. It is no wonder that Sullivan, worshipped as the outstanding pugilistic idol for so many years, found it difficult to take a back seat when he was compelled to relinquish the throne.

John L. was the fighter, pure and simple. To a great extent he was reminiscent of prehistoric man, the cave-dweller, stone axe in hand, battling indiscriminately, fighting his way to his goal, ful-

filling his desires, good or bad, and conquering by sheer audacity, an irresistible arm and a heart that never knew fear. That is a by no means overdrawn picture of Sullivan during the early eighties.

On the road he faced all comers, never caring who his antagonist might be. They all looked alike to him. Legend had it that he shunned a meeting with Peter Jackson because he feared the great colored gladiator might defeat him. To those who knew Sullivan intimately, such an hypothesis is absurd. John L. feared no man: his pride and self-confidence, developed in his case to the verge of egotism, was too strong for that. He never anticipated defeat, because to him defeat was unthinkable.

He passed up Jackson, as he would have any other colored challenger, because he was obstinately averse to fighting a Negro, and he abided by that decision. When he entered the ring, he asked no favors, and gave none. It was fight to the bitter end, and "let the best man win!" And until the fateful day when he met Corbett, he was always the best man.

It cannot be denied that he was extremely rowdy at times, a most abusive fellow when under the influence of liquor, his besetting sin, but when sober he was remorseful for anything offensive he may have done. Normally, he was not only sociable but very sympathetic and made countless friends. Those who were best acquainted with him, were not surprised at his conversion from a booze-hound to a temperance preacher.

The American public forgave his many faults because of his many homely virtues. He found an established place in the hearts of a huge majority of Uncle Sam's citizens because of his colorful character, his superabundant vitality, and spectacular vim as a fighter.

Corbett was the direct antithesis of Sullivan. He introduced an entirely new style of fighting into the roped arena. The title of "Gentleman Jim," bestowed upon him by the boxing writers, suited his polished personality in every particular. He was every inch the dude, to speak in the slang phraseology of his day.

Each was a hero in the heroic age of boxing. They were in their prime during a period when men of great ability ruled the roost in practically every division. Think of the heavyweight lineup at the time, including such performers as Jake Kilrain, Jem Smith, Charley Mitchell, Paddy Slavin, Joe Goddard, Tom Sharkey, Peter 

Maher, Bob Fitzsimmons, Joe Choynski, Jim Jeffries, and ask yourself if we are ever likely to see such an array of fistic talent duplicated within as short a period of years!

Pugilistic popularity reached its apex when Sullivan reigned. At no time since has it attained such a mark, not even when Jack Dempsey and Joe Louis appeared to set the fistic horizon ablaze with fiery radiance. After all, it can be truly said of only one battler in the history of the American ring that he was "The Champion of Champions." John L. Sullivan deservedly wore that title!

on July 8, 1889, in the last bare knuckle championship battle, he headed North with his party. At Nashville, Tenn., he was arrested on a warrant issued by the Governor of Mississippi for having illegally engaged in a prize fight. Pugnacious as usual, he refused to submit to arrest, until eight officers, armed with clubs and guns, handcuffed and dragged him to prison. 

His backers had him released on a writ of habeas corpus, and, after a long legal battle, during which his lawyers tried every means to prevent trial of charges of conspiracy to break the laws of Mississippi, Sullivan surrendered to the New York police, and was taken to police headquarters by Inspector Byrnes. There he was turned over to Sheriff Childs of Mississippi and returned to stand trial.

The result of the judicial proceedings down South was that Sullivan was found guilty, and sentenced to serve a year in the penitentiary. He was convicted on a charge of prize fighting, and his opponent, Jake Kilrain, was found guilty of assault and battery. 

The jury panel was made up of farmers. 

An appeal was taken by Sullivan's counsel, and won. The Mississippi authorities then offered to forego the prison sentence, on condition that Sullivan should plead guilty, and submit to a fine of $1,000, and this was finally agreed to. Actually, however, the case cost the champion $18,670, before he got through with it. 

Jake Kilrain was less fortunate, so far as his liberty was concerned. He kept in hiding in Baltimore, pending the outcome of 

Sullivan's trial. But a few days before the case was settled, Jake was arrested, tried, found guilty, and served two months in jail. 

When the way was again clear for Sullivan to resume his public activities, he joined a company playing a melodrama entitled, "The 

Paymaster," in which Duncan B. Harrison was starred, and traveled with it for six weeks. The champion did not fill a speaking role, but came out between acts and sparred an exhibition with his boxing mate, Joe Lannon.

It was in the fall of 1890 that Sullivan made his first appearance as a "legitimate" actor. Duncan Harrison had played to capacity houses with "The Paymaster," due to the fact that the public flocked to get a look at John L., the champion being far more important a box office asset than the play itself.

Harrison, a keen business man, then approached Sullivan, and offered him a fifty percent interest in a new venture, a melodrama, "Honest Hearts and Willing Hands." Sullivan accepted, and the play opened on August 19, 1890, at Niblo's Garden, New York

City, and afterwards went on tour, filling dates in all the principal cities of the U. S., through to San Francisco, California.

The drama was especially constructed to show off Sullivan, who played the part of the hero, a blacksmith's helper, and later world's

champion fighter. The scene was laid in Ireland, and the plot dealt with the fortunes of the Daly Brothers, who are the victims. of legal double crossing by the aristocratic Dare family, with young 

Arthur Dare played by Duncan Harrison as the arch villain.

Sullivan made a tremendous hit as James Daly, whose fighting prowess is only equalled by his talent for successful love making, as shown by his romance with a pretty Irish colleen whom he woos, wins, and defends against her traducers. He is also falsely accused of murder, but beats that charge triumphantly. 

The big scene of the play, and of course the one which drew the most plaudits from the enthusiastic fight fans, was the ring encounter in which the champion won on a clean knockout, despite the foul endeavors of conspirators to defeat him by slipping a loaded weight into his opponent's glove.

It was exactly the sort of unsophisticated drama that carries mass-appeal, with its exposure of rascality, romantic trend, vice defeated and virtue triumphant, and last, but certainly not least, the exciting lure of the glove combat. And it was a huge box office success, with Sullivan's magnetic personality dominant, the beau ideal of an athletic youth braving misfortune, battling heroically, victor alike in the field of battle and the lists of love. 

Also, it revealed Sullivan as the possessor of unsuspected histrionic ability, due partly to the fact that the role fitted his athletic person like the proverbial glove, and did not make too pressing demands upon him as regarded the subtle distinctions of changing, finely modulated moods. He had only to be what resembled a second edition of himself, a youth beating down all opposition, and with a candid dislike of crooked work, such as was John L. Sullivan in real life, and as he was always at ease in public, without the faintest notion of what "stage fright" meant, his performance was all that his myriad friends could have desired. 

While on the Pacific Coast with "Honest Hearts and Willing

Hands," Sullivan boxed a friendly four round bout with James J. 

Corbett in the theatre. This was on June 26, 1891. They did not even strip for action, but boxed in their shirt sleeves, with their coats removed, and Sullivan probably did not attribute the least importance to the affair.

Yet Corbett afterwards stated that it was in that four-rounder that he gauged Sullivan's style, and learned that the Bostonian was easy to reach with Jim's educated left, so that in the hour of real battle he knew just how to counter the Big Fellow's rush, and straighten him up with effective jabs.

At any rate, that bout was the precursor to the Battle of New Orleans, which took place a little over a year later and resulted in 

Sullivan's dethronement.

Other dramas in which Sullivan was starred were "Lottery of 

Life," "The Man From Boston," "A True American," "Uncle

Tom's Cabin," and "The Two Orphans." He also figured in vaudeville monologues, but none of his theatrical ventures, though

uniformly successful, brought him such big financial returns as did "Honest Hearts and Willing Hands."

While playing in the latter melodrama at Henck's Theatre, Cincinnati, Sullivan's audience included no less a noted actress than the famous Madame Modjeska, who watched John L.'s performance from a box, and applauded him vigorously. She was quoted in a newspaper interview as saying: "I enjoyed the play immensely. Mr. Sullivan speaks his lines naturally, and I like his bluff, hearty manner, which happens to be quite in keeping with the demands of his role. Gracefulness of gesture would be altogether out of place in playing such a part, and he has the good sense not to attempt anything of the kind. 

Consequently, his whole performance is true to life, and his gruff demeanor and powerful voice carry unfailing audience appeal. The only thing I feared was that he might injure somebody badly. He goes about thrashing his opponent just as if he actually meant to cripple him. It's a bit too dangerous!" 

During a Chicago engagement of "Honest Hearts and Willing

Hands," Sullivan was in the habit of taking a morning sprint to improve his wind. While out on the road one day, he encountered the beautiful Lillian Russell, who was also playing in the Windy City, and was taking a constitutional down Michigan Boulevard. 

The champion halted and saluted, in response to Lillian's gracious smile. "Rather early for you to be up, Miss Russell," said John L., somewhat dazzled by the vision of radiant loveliness.

Lillian smiled again. "Let me grasp the conquering hand of the great John L.," America's beloved stage beauty requested.

Sullivan grabbed the slender hand extended to him and squeezed it energetically. 

Lillian uttered a silvery scream, and John released her hastily. "I hope I didn't hurt you, Miss Russell?" queried the champion anxiously. "It's all right, Mr. Sullivan," laughed the actress. "You don't know your own strength, but after this, I think I'll leave the task of shaking hands with you to British royalty, or the men you meet in the ring!"

From Chicago the company visited Philadelphia. Sullivan, informed by his manager that the members of a Quaker society had arranged to call upon him at his hotel, attired himself in what he deemed would be a fitting costume for the occasion. He bought himself a full outfit of Quaker garments, and just before the guests arrived, strolled down the street wearing a long, drab coat, and a wide-brimmed hat. It was letting-out time for the school children, who, attracted by this bizarre getup, paraded in force behind, in front and alongside the champion. Duncan Harrison came by at this time, halted in amazement, and demanded an explanation of the masquerade. "Ain't you an actor?" queried Sullivan. "Don't you know it's

always the thing to dress your part correctly? If I was in Rome, why I'd do as the Romans do. When I'm in the Quaker City, I do as the Quakers do. It's just plain courtesy to the good old Society of Friends to adopt their costume, so here I am!"

Early in 1891, Sullivan decided to go to Australia with his company, stopping off at other ports of call en route. First he visited 

Hawaii, where thousands flocked to see his boxing exhibition, which was patronized by Queen Isabella, sister of King Kalakau. 

Duncan Harrison preceded him to Australia to prepare for the opening of "Honest Hearts and Willing Hands." Meanwhile,

Sullivan went to Samoa, and from thence to Sydney, New South Wales. There, after a week's rehearsal, the play made its Australian debut, to be followed by a tour of the country. 

This tour of the antipodes did not turn out as profitably as had been expected. The fact was that the Australians, all bred-in-thebone sports, would have preferred to see John L. Sullivan fight, rather than act, or merely box exhibitions with his sparring mates. 

Nor was the press friendly to him on this account. Editorial comment was to the effect that Australia wasn't interested in Sullivan's ability as an actor.

The champion, as was his wont, spent money freely wherever he went, and his generosity, as shown by various acts of charity he performed, won him the favor of the very papers which had previously criticised him, and several articles were published expressing sympathy for the lack of public support as regarded "Honest Hearts and Willing Hands." But when all was said and done, the 

Australian trip had to be listed as a financial failure. 

If Sullivan had traveled through Australia on a meeting-allcomers tour, as he did in America and England, there is no doubt he would have reaped a fortune, for the Australians admired him immensely as a fighter. For years they had been hearing about and reading about the deeds of John L. Sullivan. They had longed to see him in action, and had looked forward to the day when he would visit their shores in the role of fighting gladiator. 

When he disappointed them by assuming a Thespian role, they resented his action. John L., the actor, was a person in whom they did not feel, nor manifest any interest.

Back in America, Sullivan began another trip around the country, commencing at Sacramento, California. The company not only performed in the leading cities of the U. S., but took in Manitoba and British Columbia as well.

When he reached New York, Sullivan was so pleased with himself in a dramatic capacity that he signed another agreement with Duncan Harrison, calling for appearances in Brooklyn, Philadelphia and Boston. It was at Boston, on June 4, 1892, that he made his final appearance at the Athenaeum before an immense crowd. 

It was real homecoming for the City of Culture's Strong Boy! 

In speaking of his stage career, Sullivan stated that there were only two roles he liked, the blacksmith's helper in "Honest Hands

and Willing Hearts," and that of Simon Legree in "Uncle Tom's Cabin." Those parts suited him best because they gave him an opportunity to roar and use rough tactics to his heart's content. 

Said John L.: 

"I was given a royal welcome whenever I came out on the stage as the hero of 'Honest Hearts and Willing Hands,' and I could put all I had into the role. In 'Uncle Tom's Cabin,' I was the villain of the piece, and the audience's sympathy was naturally with poor Uncle Tom, who I thrashed with a blacksnake whip. "Yet I got a great kick out of that part, liked it, and might have continued to play it indefinitely, if it hadn't been for the brazen demands of the man who played the role of Uncle Tom, which caused me to give it up." "Uncle Tom's Cabin" had a run of two years before it closed one night in Springfield, Ill., in 1902. There were rumblings of discontent among the members of the company, who maintained that they were underpaid. Speaking of the final revolt, Sullivan said: "The fellow who played Uncle Tom was a very mediocre actor who was put over on me by a theatrical agency. He got $25 per week, and wasn't worth it. But he thought that because the audience hissed me when I whipped him, and gave him applause, that he should get more money. So he held me up for $40 per week. 

I gave him the increase, and no sooner did he get it, than he was demanding ten more. That was too much for yours truly, and I wouldn't stand for his nonsense. "It was my dough that was invested in the company, dough that I made with my fists. And there wasn't a damn soul in the whole company that people came to see, except myself. I was really the whole show, the only attraction. Yet that bum actor led the whole tribe of ham-fatters in a strike to stick me up for bigger salaries. "On the night we disbanded, this trouble-maker asked me for additional money again. He wore a wide leather band around his belly, and of course every time I belted him with the whip it sounded like I was murdering him, and so I suppose many in the audience thought. "Suddenly, in the middle of our big scene, where I made him get on his knees and lambasted the daylights out of him, the crowd went wild. Mingled hisses and cheers, the cheers for Uncle Tom, the hisses for me, echoed through the building. Some of the women present even yelled for me to stop slaughtering the poor chap. 

"As I struck him, I asked:

The crowd was cheering Uncle Tom and booing me vigorously. As the applause was dying away, this duck sort of raises his head and says:

When Sullivan abandoned the legitimate stage, he also gave up drinking forever. He gave lectures on the evils of liquor, preached temperance and Americanism at boys' clubs, and even spoke before women's organizations. Because of his somewhat lurid past, some editorial writers derided this change of attitude, others, with keener insight, pointed out that no living man was better qualified to denounce the Demon Rum; and opined that he was doing a lot of good for the rising generation.

In 1886 Sullivan and the wrestling champion, William Muldoon, toured the country in a very popular vaudeville act called "Living Statues." They joined the Lester and Allen Minstrels, and were a great sensation. Both men were splendid models of physical beauty, and wherever they appeared, packed houses resulted. Despite the fortunes Sullivan earned in the ring and with his theatrical enterprises, he had little money left in the later years of his life. The farm on which he died, represented the bulk of his

estate. Most of his income then came from his temperance lectures. 

He had ridden on the water wagon for over a dozen years, during which liquor never passed his lips. In fact, shortly before his death, he was trying to float a motion picture company for the purpose of making educational films, depicting the dangers of drink. 

But he passed away before the project reached the experimental stage. He was, of course, to have been starred in these productions. 

Opposition always made him fight the harder, and one reason he was particularly anxious to get these pictures on show was because of the snubs he received from certain patriotic societies, which had turned down his offer to have the films exhibited before soldiers and sailors. These short-sighted people objected to Sullivan because of his past record as a heavy drinker, in which they erred. Sullivan was right in his contention that he could exercise a good influence upon thousands of men, particularly lovers of boxing, young and old, with whom he was popular to the last. "Next to my dear friend, Teddy Roosevelt," said the ex-champion, "I'm aces with the common people. I talk their language, I'm one of them. Young red-blooded fellows will listen to John L., in preference to anyone else."

However, the enterprise came to nothing, because of lack of financial support. Sullivan's story of how he reformed is best given in his own words: "I was sitting in a hotel, flat broke and feeling rather rotten.

I had made over a million dollars fighting and on the stage. Now, I was nearly down and out. "That's the time a man starts thinking, when he's got a big head, is busted and no one seems to care what becomes of him. A big head is the best temperance lecturer in the world, only people won't listen to it. Here I was, the champion fool of the world. 

All these years trying to lick John Barleycorn, and never having sense enough to see that he couldn't be beaten, that he could drop me for the ten count whenever he liked. "I was sucker enough to figure I had a million friends. Where were those friends now? Gone, vanished, like the million plunks 

I threw away! "Then along comes a chap and asks me to have a drink. I said 

I'd take a pint of wine. The waiter brought the champagne. I poured it into a big glass, and held it up to the light. It looked pretty, and I thought of the old gag-'the imprisoned laughter of the peasant girls of France'! "Yes, it was a laugh, all right, I was getting the horse-laugh, so to speak. I stood up and poured the wine into a cuspidor. "What's wrong, John?' asked the fellow who bought the drink. 'Ain't the wine good?' ""Oh, everything's jake,' I told him. 'Nothing's wrong with the wine, but there's a whole lot wrong with John L. Sullivan. But 

I'm telling one thing right now-I've had my last drink. I'll never touch alcohol again in any form-never!'

"Well, the boys standing around laughed a lot at that one, thinking it was just a joke. But I meant it, and I had the last laugh. 

John L. had reformed for keeps! "Booze got most of my pals, there aren't many of them left. 

And it would have finished me if I hadn't quit when I did!"

On the lecture platform Sullivan frequently repeated that little sermon, and wound up with the declaration: "I've had money, plenty of it, but I've little left now. I spent most of it on liquor. Champagne flowed freely when I was spending. Now that I've sworn off, I'll bet it made a deep dint in saloon revenues. I have my good health and I own a farm. Jock Rockefeller doesn't get any more pleasure out of his big estates than I do from my little place. He has plenty to eat, so have I. But I can eat anything that's set before me, while Rockefeller and lots of other rich folks must choose their victuals carefully, or suffer. I gave up drink just in the nick of time."

The first time he made a temperance talk at Toronto, Canada, several newspaper men, curious to know if the ex-champ really practiced what he was preaching, sent one of their number to Sullivan's dressing-room. The messenger invited Sullivan to join him and the other scribes at a nearby saloon, and have a few drinks. 

The former pugilist eyed the devil's advocate with the black scowl that had scared stiff so many aspirants to fistic honors. "Look here, fellow," roared Sullivan, "have you had such a rotten education that you don't understand English? You listened to me talking temperance, and then have the nerve to come here and ask me to have a drink. Outside for you!"

He grabbed the young man by the seat of his pants, raised him several feet from the floor, and tossed him through the doorway. 

Then, turning to his companions in the dressing-room, he said: "It takes violence to convince some fools that a man means what he says. I wouldn't dream of hurting that ignorant ass, beyond manhandling him enough to let him feel I'm not the sort of person to make a mock of. And I'll bet that's one story he won't want printed!"

CHAPTER VIII 

JOHN L. SULLIVAN, though a man of the world, and no moralist, was by no means “a lady's man" in the sense applying to such ring Romeos as Kid McCoy and others of that ilk. He had very few relations with women. In fact, only three women figured in his life, from a strictly love angle. He was twice married, first to Annie Bates, of Centreville, Rhode Island, who became his wife in 1882; after he obtained a divorce from her, he married 

Kate Harkins, a childhood sweetheart from Roxbury, Mass., in 1908.

From 1883 until 1896, Sulivan was deeply in love with beautiful Ann Livingston. Rumors of a marriage between the champion and 

Ann Livingston were frequently circulated, but always denied by both parties concerned. Eventually, Ann wed another man. 

The Livingston episode constituted the one great romance of Sullivan's life. Stories of his passion for her, their journeys through Europe, and finally of his supposedly cruel treatment of Ann, who was a celebrated burlesque queen, filled the newspapers. 

She died in Bellevue Hospital, New York City, on September 12, 1896, after a lingering illness. It was only by chance that an executive of the Actors' Guild learned that Ann's body was in the city morgue, from which it was destined for burial in the Potter's 

Field. Through his intervention, the girl who had shared the spotlight with Sullivan in this country and abroad was saved from a pauper's grave.

Much of Sullivan's fortune had passed through Ann's slender hands, during the early part of her career, yet she died penniless. 

John L. fell in love with her years before, when he was attending a Boston college. But when he grew to manhood, he married 

Annie Bates. Sullivan's intimates were well aware of his affection for Ann Livingston, who later became famous on the stage. 

Ann Livingston was tall and lovely, with bright blue eyes and a wealth of golden hair, a most alluring personality whose beauty attracted a host of ardent admirers. 

Sullivan's marriage with Ann Bates turned out badly. The pair were incompatible in the real sense of the word, quarreled repeatedly, and indeed, led a regular dog and cat existence. They separated after eighteen months of far from wedded bliss. During that period John L. frequently faced a magistrate on charges of assault and battery brought by his wife. When they parted, Mrs. 

Ann Sullivan went to live with her folks. 

Meantime, Ann Livingston, while playing at the Athenaeum 

Theatre, Boston, had a love affair with the booking agent, Fred

Anderson, which resulted in marriage. A daughter, Eva, was born, and for two years Ann retired to domestic life and cared for her child. But the lure of theatrical life was too much for her, and she returned to the stage.

In her matronly maturity, she was more beautiful than ever,

and became the standing toast of Boston's young bloods. She was a great drawing card, her fame spread, and soon she tasted the sweets of popularity in New York, Philadelphia, Washington, and other metropolitan centers. At that time, she was probably the most talked-about actress in the profession. 

While filling a Washington engagement, she met Sullivan for the first time since both were in their teens. It was a case of love at first sight, and they were continually together. Rumor had it that each would get a divorce and marry, but both denied this allegation. However, she separated from her husband, and took her child with her. She joined the champion, and thereafter they travelled together, and were seemingly inseparable. 

When a Boston paper published a report that Sullivan and Miss Livingston intended to wed, Sullivan raged at a reporter who interviewed him on the subject. "I am still married to Ann Bates," roared the champion, "and one venture in matrimony is enough for me. Can't a man be in the company of a charming girl he has known from childhood, without being subjected to a lot of lying gossip?"

Ann Livingston was equally emphatic in her denial of matrimonial intentions. "I would like to box the ears of the scamp who sent out that report," said the lovely blonde. "John and I are just good friends.

We have known each other ever since our schooldays. We're not married, nor will we be. Neither of us is divorced. I am still Fred 

Anderson's wife."

Yet they continued to live together. When Sullivan started on his European tour, Ann accompanied him, leaving her child in the care of her parents at Providence. Ann was constantly with the champion, even in his training camp. When Sullivan fought 

Mitchell she was present at the ringside, dressed as a man, the only female among the spectators. 

Again, when Sullivan fought Kilrain at Richburg, Mississippi, in 1889, Ann Livingston was the only representative of her sex to see that famous battle. Clad in male attire, which she was well accustomed to wear on the stage, none but a few of her intimates knew that the handsome youth who cheered enthusiastically for the champion was a girl.

Through all those years nobody exercised such influence over 

Sullivan as did Ann Livingston. When William Muldoon and

Duncan Harrison, his most trusted allies, were unable to control John L. and keep from breaking training, a few words from Ann served to subdue the obstinate champion. 

But all such infatuations have an end, and as is usually the case, the man was the first to tire of the connection. They quarreled frequently in 1889 and the year following. After he defeated Kilrain, Ann was the recipient of a gift of $14,500 from the champion, as well as presents of valuable jewelry. But then Sullivan began drinking heavily, and Ann could not restrain him.

One night in New York, Sullivan was drunk and accused Ann of being unfaithful to him. She tried in vain to calm him and he knocked her down. This quarrel was patched up, but a few months later, Sullivan again beat her, while intoxicated. This time when he regained his senses, he apologized, but told her frankly that his passion for her had faded, and it would be best for them to part. 

He informed Ann that he wished her only good fortune, that the jewelry and money gifts he had presented her were her property forever, but they had come to the parting of the ways, and she must not cross his path again. Ann resigned herself to the situation, and departed, never to see John L. again. 

She went back to the stage, but not to new triumphs. She had become prematurely aged at thirty-five, her magnetism was a thing of the past. A failure before the footlights, where once she queened it over all, she had to seek other means of support. 

Ann opened a theatrical boarding house on Third Avenue, near Chatham Square, in New York. For a while she prospered, then patronage fell off, and she was obliged to vacate the premises. She became quite ill.

The woman who was sheltering her, Fanny Reynolds, knew nothing about Ann's past. After the latter's demise, Miss Reynolds told the police Ann Livingston was merely a casual acquaintance to whom she had given a spare room, and that she had not the faintest notion who her celebrated guest was, until the crisis came which resulted in the sick woman being taken to the city hospital. 

Then came mutual recognition. Miss Reynolds had been a variety actress in Boston. She and Ann played in boy roles at the 

Athenaeum Theatre, and they had been rivals for the affections of 

Fred Anderson, whom Ann later married. On discovering Ann 

Livingston's identity, Miss Reynolds tried in vain to communicate with Sullivan and Anderson. The next day Ann died. The rescue of her body from Potter's Field already has been narrated. 

Like Sullivan, she had let a fortune slip through her fingers.

The thousands of dollars she earned, and the money she received from her lover, were squandered in fast living. It was estimated that, outside of the gifts from John L., Ann earned over $500,000 during her career.

Her daughter went to live with Anderson, who had become a candy manufacturer and prospered in the commercial field. He came to New York later, and opened a theatrical agency in partnership with William Harris. He never became reconciled to his wife. 

Ann Livingston scored her biggest stage triumph in "The Black 

Crook," playing a boy's role. She tried to get Sullivan to join that company, but the champion refused. With Lilly Sawtelle, she introduced a novelty in vaudeville. In this act, she appeared first as a boy, then as a girl, and wound up with a jugglery and ledgerdemain exhibition that proved a great sensation. 

Her beauty was statuesque and many compared her to the worldfamous Lily Langtry. In fact, on more than one occasion, she was mistaken for the English beauty. In her day, she was considered the "handsomest boy" on the American stage.

Sullivan was deeply affected by the untimely death of the girl he had once loved so ardently. He was greatly enraged over the stories published about him, stating that he had deserted her in her hour of need, and allowed her to die wretchedly. To the editor of a Boston paper, who had been particularly vindictive in his attacks in this regard, Sullivan sent the following telegram: "You published a piece about me in this morning's issue that is as untrue as it is dastardly. If you have any manhood, you will meet me and take your thrashing like a man. When I heard of her death, I wired to have her body properly buried, but that already had been attended to by the Actors' Guild. I have reimbursed the Guild for the expenditure."

It was shortly after the death of Ann Livingston that Sullivan

gave up drinking and started to preach temperance. Twelve years later, in 1908, he married Kate Harkins, with whom he found real happiness. He bought a farm, and settled down to a genuine home life. Now and then he left to make a lecture tour, but when not so engaged, spent all his time with his second wife, whom he survived by a few years.

In 1917 the former Kate Harkins died. Her passing was a severe blow to Sullivan. Between 1910, and the World War, he had lost most of the money he made, with his farm remaining his sole asset.

He endeavored to get the United States Government to permit him to act as a recruiting agent, but this was refused, and the former champion suffered much from gloom consequent on the belief that he had nothing further to live for in this world. When he became ill, he said to his friend and former secretary, George Touhey: "George, if the good Lord should call me now, I'm ready. I've seen it all. I've lived from A to Z, traveled the world and met all kinds of people. But nothing matters any more now. I'm through. 

But I can't understand why my services, those of a fighting man whose name can still thrill thousands of folks, should not be accepted by a fighting nation!"

A few days later the ex-champion's condition became definitely serious. He lapsed into unconsciousness, and thus passed away. 

So died the most colorful fighter in all ring history!

Thousands came to weep at his bier. He had been a rough-andready fighting man, a heavy drinker, and abusive when in his cups. 

But he was charitable to a degree never equalled by any other prominent figure in the sporting world, and that redeeming virtue atoned for many trivial shortcomings.

They laid him to rest on a cold, wet day in February, 1918. It was raining heavily, the roads were slushy, deep in mud. But the inclement state of the weather did not prevent the mourners from mustering in force at the funeral, though cars and pedestrians alike had a hard time progressing through the melting snow and ice. 

It was an imposing cavalcade, guarded for more than a mile by cohorts of police lining both sides of the streets through which it passed.

Slowly the procession wound its way through Boston's busy. thoroughfares, to its goal of God's Acre Calvary Cemetery, where all that was mortal of the Champion of Champions was reverently placed beneath the sod. Old John L. Sullivan had gone to join the ranks of other glorious gladiators-high up in Valhalla.
