6. RANK AND INSIGNIA 

Enlisted men of the Army range in rank from private to master sergeant. Each grade above that of private confers extra pay and responsibilities appropriate to the grade. The same grades apply to all branches of the service, and the pay pertaining to each grade is the same in all branches. 

Responsibilities of each grade are similar in all branches, but duties vary in the different arms and services. A corporal in the Cavalry, Infantry, Field

Artillery, etc., is usually in charge of a squad. A sergeant may be a section or platoon leader. Staff sergeants are usually battalion or squadron sergeants major. First sergeants are senior noncommissioned officers of companies and similar organizations. Technical sergeants, who rank with first sergeants, generally perform highly specialized tasks in the various technical services-the Engineers, Signal Corps, etc. Master sergeants fill the post of regimental sergeants major and regimental supply sergeants. 

Noncommissioned officers of some branches,

while not always required to exercise command over personnel, perform important duties which entitle them to the higher pay appropriate to their grade.

A private, first class, draws extra pay and wears a chevron.

The service stripe does not denote rank. Worn on the left sleeve of the blouse, each is a badge of service that indicates that its wearer has completed three years of service.

8. RETIREMENT 

Every man who completes thirty years' service with the colors is eligible for retirement from active service and to receive, as long as he lives, three-fourths of his active duty pay at the time of retirement, plus certain specified allowances. All service with the Army, Navy, and Marine Corps counts toward retirement.

There is maintained in Washington, D. C., the 

United States Soldiers' Home for the benefit of enlisted men who as a result of illness or injury suffered in the line of duty are incapacitated for further military service, or who have served honorably in the Regular Army twenty years or longer. 

Men who retire after thirty years' service are also eligible for admission. 

9. INSURANCE 

All soldiers of the Army of the United States are eligible for benefits of National Life Insurance. 

Applications for this insurance must be made within 120 days after entering the service.

The insured may designate as beneficiary of his insurance a person or persons in one of the following categories: wife, child (including an adopted child, step-child, or an illegitimate child) parent, brother or sister. If the beneficiary is under thirty years of age at the time of the death of the in- 14

sured the benefits are payable in 240 equal monthly installments of $5.51 for each $1000.00 of insurance carried. If the beneficiary is over thirty years of age, the benefits are payable in 120 equal monthly installments.

The payment of premiums is waived if the insured becomes permanently disabled after taking out National Service Life Insurance. 

When the insured completes the forms required by law, deductions are made from the monthly pay of the insured as long as he remains in the service. The insurance may be continued after the insured has left the service by paying the required premiums.

All National Service Life Insurance is issued on the Five-year Level Term Insurance Plan (prem ium remains at the same rate for five years). After one year, and before the expiration of five years, the plan may be changed for one of the 

Ordinary Life: Provides maximum protection for a minimum cost. Premium payment continues during the life of the insured unless he becomes totally and permanently disabled. 

Twenty-payment or Thirty-payment Life: Payment of premium continues for twenty or thirty years, depending on type selected. At the end of the twenty or thirty year period the insurance is paid up for the face value of the policy. 

No medical examination is required of those 15

who desire to take out National Service Life Insurance. It may be taken out in amounts ranging from $1,000.00 to $10,000.00.

10. ARTICLES OF WAR 

The written portion of military law consists, in part, of the articles of war enacted by Congress. 

Other sources of military law are the Army Regulations, other military laws, and general and special orders issued by the War Department, and by corps area, department, post, and other commanders.

The articles of war provide that a selected list of the articles shall be read and explained to every soldier upon his enlistment and once every six months thereafter. 

Article 1 includes definitions of the words "officer," "soldier," and "company" as used in the articles. 

16

Article enumerates the persons who are subject to military law.

Article 29 provides that, in the event a soldier has been acquitted after trial by a court martial, the court shall immediately announce the result in open court.

Article 54 provides that any person who fraudulently enlists and who receives pay and allowances under such enlistment shall be punished as the court-martial may direct.

Article 58 provides that any person who deserts, or attempts to desert, the services of the United States shall, if the offence be committed in time of war, suffer death or such other punishment as the court-martial may direct, and if the offence be committed in time of peace, any punishment, except death, that the court-martial may direct.

Article 59 provides punishment similar to that for offences committed under Article 58 for any person subject to military law who advises, persuades, or assists another to desert.

Article 61 provides for the punishment of any soldier who absents himself without leave. 

Article 62 provides. for the punishment of anyone subject to military law who uses contemptuous language or disrespectful words against the 

President, Vice President, Congress of the United 

States, the Secretary of War, or the governor or legislature of any state or possession of the United States. 17

Article 63 provides for the punishment of any. one who is disrespectful to a superior officer. 

Article 64 states that any person subject to military law who assaults or wilfully disobeys his superior officer shall suffer death or such other punishment as a court-martial may direct. 

Article 65 provides for the punishment of any soldier who is insubordinate towards a noncommissioned officer.

Article 66 provides for death or other penalty for mutiny or sedition.

Article 67 states that any officer or soldier who knows of an attempt to mutiny and does not try to prevent it shall suffer death or other punishment.

Article 68 provides for the punishment of any person who refuses to obey a lawful order to abandon a quarrel, fray, or disorder.

Article 69 provides for the arrest or confinement of any person charged with a serious offence and also provides penalties for breaking arrest or escaping from confinement.

Article 70 requires that a person subject to military law sign the charges and specifications against an accused; that the accused be given a prompt trial; and that no soldier shall be tried by a general court-martial until after an impartial investigation and an opportunity to question witnesses against him and to present testimony in his own behalf. 18

Article 73 provides punishment for releasing a prisoner without authority.

Artciles 75 to 82, inclusive, define and provide punishment for war offenses. Death, or other punishment directed by the court-martial, is provided for any person who misbehaves himself or runs away before the enemy, tries to compel his commander to surrender, makes known the countersign to unauthorized persons, overcomes guard stationed to protect property, or assists the enemy. Failing to turn over to the United States any property captured from the enemy, or dealing in captured or abandoned property is liable to a punishment other than death. The article also provides the death penalty for spies in time of war. Article 83 states that any person subject to military law who wilfully or negligently loses, damages, or wrongfully disposes of military property shall make good the loss and in addition suffer punishment. 

Article 84, like Article 83, provides punishment for soldiers who sell, lose, or damage any equipment issued to them. 

Article 85 provides punishment for being drunk on duty.

Article 86 provides punishment for any sentinel found drunk or sleeping on post. Death may be the penalty in time of war. 

Article 88 forbids the abuse or intimidation of any person bringing supplies to an army station. 19

Article 89 requires that all persons be orderly and provides punishment for any soldier who destroys property; it also requires that reparation be made to the injured party. 

Article 90 forbids any reproachful or provoking gestures.

Article 91 forbids dueling. 

Article 92 provides the death penalty, or imprisonment for life, for any person found guilty of murder or rape. In time of peace the trial will be held in civil courts unless the offence be committed outside the limits of the continental United States.

Article 93 provides punishment for various crimes such as manslaughter, burglary, robbery, larceny, perjury, forgery, and assault.

Article 94 provides for the punishment of any person subject to military law who is guilty of theft or embezzlement of government property.

Article 96, a general article, states: "Though not mentioned in these articles, all disorders and neglects to the prejudice of good order and military discipline, all conduct of a nature to bring discredit upon the military service, and all crimes or offences not capital, of which persons subject to military law may be guilty, shall be taken cognizance of by a general or special or summary court-martial, according to the nature and degree of the offence, and punished at the discretion of the court." 20

Article 104 allows organization commanders to award company punishments summarily within certain limits. The soldier, however, has the right of trial by court-martial if he prefers. 

Article 105 provides for the stoppage of pay of soldiers to reimburse owners of private property for damages caused by the soldiers' fault. It also provides for a board of officers to determine the extent of the damage and to assess the damages. 

Article 106 makes it lawful for civil officers to arrest deserters.

Article 107 requires a soldier to make good the time lost in desertion, while absent without leave for more than one day, while in confinement, or as the result of his own misconduct.

Article 108 provides that no soldier shall be discharged without a discharge certificate, or before his term of service has expired, except by order of the President, the Secretary of War, or the commanding general of a department (corps area), or by sentence of a general court-martial. 

11. GENERAL ORDERS 

Orders for sentinels on interior guard duty are of two classes: general orders and special orders. 

General orders apply to all sentinels. Special orders apply to particular posts and duties. 

Sentinels are required to memorize the following general orders. 21

My general orders are: 

1. To take charge of this post and all government property in view. 

2. To walk my post in a military manner, keeping always on the alert and observing everything that takes place within sight or hearing. 

3. To report all violations of orders I am instructed to enforce. 

4. To repeat all calls from posts more distant from the guardhouse than my own. 

5. To quit my post only when properly relieved. 

6. To receive, obey, and pass on to the sentinel who relieves me all orders from the commanding officer, officer of the day, and officers and noncommissioned officers of the guard only. 

7. To talk to no one, except in line of duty. 

8. To give the alarm in case of fire or disorder. 

9. To call to the corporal of the guard in any case not covered by instructions. 

10. To salute all officers, and all colors and standards not cased. 

11. To be especially watchful at night and, during the hours for challenging, to challenge all persons on or near my post, and to allow no one to pass without proper authority. 

12. DECORATIONS, MEDALS, BADGES 

Medal of Honor: This medal is presented by the 

President, in the name of Congress, to members 22

of the Army who distinguish themselves conspicuously by gallantry and intrepidity, at the risk of life, above and beyond the call of duty while in action involving conflict with an armed enemy.

Distinguished Service Cross: This medal is awarded to persons who, while serving in any capacity with the Army of the United States, distinguish themselves by extraordinary heroism in connection with military operations against an armed enemy.

Distinguished Service Medal: This medal is awarded to persons serving with the Army who distinguish themselves by exceptionally meritorious service to the government in a duty of great responsibility.

Medal of Honor or the Distinguished Service Cross.

A wound received in action and which necessitates treatment by a medical officer is considered a singularly meritorious act of essential service.

3) Members of the U. S. Navy and Marine

Corps while serving with the Army of the 

United States. 

4) Members of the military, naval, and air forces of foreign governments while serving with the Army of the United States.

SECTION II General Information 13. MAPS 

A map is a representation of a portion of the surface of the earth on a plane surface such as paper. 

It is desirable that maps used for military purposes show such important features as elevations and depressions, towns, roads, rivers and streams, buildings, and railroads.

Conventional Signs: Data on maps are depicted by conventional signs, i.e., symbols representing buildings, streams, bridges, etc. Conventional signs must be learned, just as new words are learned, if the map-reader is to understand what he sees. On terrain maps (topographic military maps), conventional signs appear in four colors: 

BLACK: for works of man and for military grid lines.

BROWN: for contour lines. 

BLUE: for water and for water-covered swamps.

GREEN: for wooded areas and for other vegetation.

Distance: On maps of the United States dis tance is measured by the English system of miles.

Contour Lines: A contour line is a line on a map which represents an imaginary line on the ground, every point of which is at the same height above sea level. Contour lines are used to indicate on maps the elevations and depressions of the surface of the earth.

The vertical distance between contour lines on a map, i.e., the number of feet you would go up or down in moving to the next highest or next lowest contour line, is called the contour interval. 

The contour interval is given on the margin of each contoured map. 

Direction: Direction on maps is usually represented by arrows. Two arrows are usually shown. 

The arrow with a full barb or star at one end points toward the true north (north pole). The arrow with but half a barb points toward what is called the magnetic north. The magnetic north is some distance from true north and has certain magnetic qualities which attract the needle of a compass. 28

Orientation: A map is said to be oriented when the north and south arrow on the map points north on the ground. This makes all lines on the map parallel to the corresponding lines on the ground. A map reader is oriented when he knows his own position on an oriented map and the directions on the ground.

To orient a map: 

1) Using a compass, allow the needle to settle so that it points to the magnetic north. Turn the map so that the line pointing to the magnetic north is parallel to the line indicated by the compass needle. 

2) Using two points on the ground that can be located on the map, draw a line on the map between the two points. Stand at one point. 

Sight along the line on the map and turn the map until the line of sight points exactly at the other point on the ground.

Military Symbols: Military symbols are signs that are added to maps by pencil or ink, and sometimes printed on them, to show such information as the location of companies, battalions, and other units, trenches, emplacements, barbed wire, machine guns, artillery, mortars, and many other kinds of military information. If put on the map in colors blue is used for friendly forces and red for enemy forces. Like conventional signs, military symbols must be memorized. 29

14. FIRST AID First Aid "Don'ts": 

1. Don't get excited; act quickly but quietly. 

2. Don't be rough; handle an injured person gently. 

3. Don't try to do everything yourself; try and get medical aid as soon as possible. 

4. Don't move an injured person before medical aid arrives unless it is necessary to do so in order to provide warmth for him. 

5. Don't let an injured person suffer from cold or chills; even on a warm day it is sometimes necessary to use blankets and other covering for the injured. 

6. Don't pour liquids into the mouth of an unconscious person. 

7. Don't try to bring an unconscious injured person to consciousness; let him be quiet, keep him warm, stop his bleeding, and get medical aid quickly. 

8. Don't touch a wound with anything that is not sterile; use the first aid packet, if available.

First Aid Packet: A first aid packet is a part of every soldier's equipment. The packet consists of two sterile dressings, each wrapped in waxed paper. Each packet consists of a gauze bandage (4 inches wide by 84 inches long), to which is sewed in the middle a "compress," or pad of 30

gauze. The dressing is arranged so that the compress (folded) is on the inside and a roll of bandage is on each of the two outer sides. When the rolls are pulled, the compress on the inside opens. 

This packet also contains two safety pins wrapped in waxed paper. 

To apply the first aid packet, carefully remove the waxed-paper wrapper and carefully remove the paper from one of the packages without unfolding the compress or bandage. Hold the compress and bandage by grasping the outside rolls of bandage between the thumb and fingers. When ready to dress the wound, open the compress by pulling on the two rolls, being careful not to touch the inside of the compress with the fingers or with anything else. Still holding one roll of bandage in each hand, apply the compress to the wound, then wrap the bandage around the limb, or other part, and tie the ends together or pin with the safety pins. The second compress and bandage may be applied over the first or it may be used for a sling, if the arm is wounded.

Fractures: In no injury is the outcome more influenced by the character of first aid than in fractures. Improper handling or immediate movement may produce or aggravate shock and deprive the patient of all chance for recovery.

A simple fracture is one in which there is no wound extending from the broken bone through the skin. A compound fracture is one in which 31

the broken bone has pushed through the skin; the wound is therefore exposed to danger of infection. 

A complicated fracture is one in which there is injury to adjoining large blood vessels, nerves, or muscles from movement of the edges of the broken bone.

A splint should be applied to a fracture as soon as possible and without moving the patient. Temporary splints can be made of shingles, boards, bayonet scabbards, etc. The splints should be well-padded on the inside and snugly bound to the limb. The splint should be bound above and below the break, but not over it.

In applying the splint, the limb should be gently straightened out and the broken bone aligned quickly but firmly; the alignment should then be maintained by the application of the splint.

If there is a wound, it should be bandaged first before the splint is applied; if possible, wait for the arrival of medical aid before applying the splint. 

Poisoned Wounds: Snake bites should be promptly treated, since snake venom acts quickly.

To prevent the circulation of the venom, apply a tourniquet above the wound, then get the poison out of the wound by cutting it open and sucking (if there are no cracks or sores in the mouth or lips). Keep the patient quiet. Do not give him liquor but give him strong coffee if possible. 32

Mad dog bites should be carefully treated, though the venom does not act as quickly as that of snakes. Do not apply a tourniquet, but keep the patient quiet and give him strong coffee. 

Bites from scorpions, spiders, centipedes, and other insects are treated locally by applying a solution of ammonia or baking soda.

Fainting: Place the patient on his back, loosen his clothing, lower his head and raise his feet. If the patient is in a comfortable position in a warm place, allow him to come to of his own accord. If necessary to bring him out of his faint, apply cold water to his face. Wait until after he has recovered consciousness before giving him a stimulant. 

Poisoning: If there are no signs of acid or burning such as burns on the mouth, an effort should be made to empty the stomach and bowels of the patient. He should be given a laxative and should be made to vomit. Two teaspoonsful of mustard in a glass of warm water, or a tablespoonful of salt in warm water will help make the patient vomit; epsom salts, castor oil, or Rochelle salts are good laxatives. After the poison has been evacuated give stimulants, apply heat and rubbing externally. 

When the lips are burned, do not give anything to cause vomiting. Give castor oil, olive oil, cottonseed oil, or milk, and also the proper antidote if you know it. 33

Freezing: The symptoms of frostbite are cold in the part, then pain, then complete numbness.

The affected part becomes white or bluish white. 

The part affected should not be warmed before a fire. Rub it instead with snow or with a cloth dipped in cold water and wrung out. After the part has been well rubbed dry it thoroughly and smear it with grease. If the part becomes swollen or changes color medical attention is necessary. 

If a person becomes unconscious from cold, carry him into a room without fire, if possible. 

Remove his clothing, and rub him hard with snow or with a wet cloth until signs of returning circulation are evident. When warmth and consciousness return, give him a hot drink, cover him up warmly, and let him remain quiet. 

Sunstroke: In sunstroke the face is flushed, the skin hot, the breathing labored, and the pulse rapid. The patient may be unconscious. Place him in the shade, loosen his clothing, and try to cool him by applications of cold water or ice, and by fanning him. 

15. HINTS FOR FIELD DUTY 

1. Marching: Make certain that your pack and other equipment carried are snugly adjusted. Take advantage of rest periods by relaxing. 

2. Shoes: Do not wear new shoes on field exer- 34

cises. Break in new shoes by wearing them one or two hours each day for at least a week before starting on any exercise involving marches. 

3. Water: Do not drink any water except that from a source approved and marked by medical authorities. Fill your canteen before starting on a march and drink from that. 

4. Tents: In cold weather pitch your tent so that the back end is facing in the direction of the prevailing wind. Ditch the tent carefully so that rain will not enter during the night. 

5. Bedding: If the ground is hard, soften it by loosening it with a pick or shovel. Investigate to see that there are no stones or rocks under your bedding. Hollow out a small portion of the ground to fit your hips. Put straw or grass under your raincoat and then place your blankets over the raincoat. This will prevent the dampness from chilling your body during the night. Always arrange your bedding before dark, if possible. 

6. Mess Kits: Be sure that your mess kit, knife, fork, and spoon are thoroughly washed in hot wa- -ter and rinsed in boiling water after they are used. 

Unless this is done in boiling water your utensils may become contaminated with disease germs from the men who used the water before you did. 

7. Clothing: Wash your shirts, underwear, and socks frequently. 35

17. THE FLAG RULES FOR DISPLAYING THE FLAG: 

1. In a procession with another flag the United 

States Flag is on the marching right. 

2. In a procession with a line of other flags the 

United States Flag is in front of the center of the line of other flags. 

3. When displayed with another flag against a wall, staffs crossed, the United States Flag is on the observer's left and its staff is in front of the staff of the other flag. 

4. In a group of flags of states or cities, or pennants of societies displayed from staffs, the United States Flag is in the center or at the highest point of the group. 

5. When flown on the same halyard with flags of states, or cities, or pennants of societies, the 

United States Flag is at the peak. 

6. When the United States Flag and flags of other nations, states, or cities, or pennants of societies are flown from adjacent staffs, the United 

States Flag is hoisted first and lowered last. 

7. When the United States Flag and flags of other nations, states, or cities, or pennants of societies are flown from adjacent staffs, the United 

States Flag is on the right of the line. 

8. When displayed either horizontally or vertically, the blue field is uppermost and to the flag's own right, i.e., to the observer's left. 37

9. When used to cover a casket, the flag is placed so that the blue field is at the head of the casket and over the left shoulder of the deceased. 

10. To indicate mourning when flown from a stationary staff the Flag is placed at half-staff. 

Respect of the Flag: 

1.

Do not permit disrespect to be shown to the 

United States Flag. 

2. Do not dip the United States Flag to any person or to any thing. The regimental color, states flag, etc., will render this honor. 

3. Do not display the Flag with union down' unless as a signal of distress. 

4. Do not let the Flag touch the ground or trail in water. 

5. Do not place any object or any emblem of any kind on or above the Flag of the United 

States. 

6. Do not use the Flag as drapery in any form whatsoever. Use bunting of red, white, and blue.

Saluting the Flag: During the ceremony of hoisting or lowering the Flag, or when the Flag is passing in a parade or review, all persons present should face the Flag, stand at attention, and salute. Those present in uniform should render the right hand salute. When not in uniform, men should remove their hats and hold them just below the left shoulder. 38

18. MILITARY TERMINOLOGY 

Advance guard.—A security detachment which precedes and protects a unit on the march. 

Approach march.-That part of the advance from the point where the zone of hostile fire is encountered up to the point where it becomes necessary to return enemy fire in order to continue the advance without undue losses.

Ballistics.-The science of the motion of projectiles. 

Barrage.—A curtain of fire, usually shellfire, laid on an area close to friendly troops to provide protection by impeding enemy movements or fire, or advancing at a fixed rate to precede an infantry attack.

Bivouac.-A rest area, or temporary encampment, for troops.

Bridgehead.-Position on the enemy side of a river seized by friendly troops for the purpose of protecting the crossing of the river by the remaining friendly troops.

Camouflage.-Measure or expedient taken to deceive the enemy regarding the existence, location, defensive works, etc., of friendly troops.

Center of resistance.-A group of several strong points. 39

Combat group.-The smallest organized tactical locality; usually occupied by a rifle platoon, sometimes less, seldom more. 

Command post. Headquarters of a unit in the field or combat area.

Convoy. A number of vehicles formed into a train. 

Danger space. That space along the line from the gun to the point of strike within which a standing man would be hit.

Defilade. A position (defiladed) protected by a natural or artificial barrier from frontal or enfilading fire.

Deploy. To extend the front of a unit, as from column to skirmishing formation.

Distributing point.-Place at which supplies are distributed to motor trains or to troops. 

Dump.-Place for temporary storage of supplies. 

Echelon.-Formation in which units are placed one behind the other, extending beyond and unmasking one another either wholly or in part; also, a division, such as maintenance echelon.

Enfilade. Condition permitting the delivery of fire along the length of a position or unit. 

Field of fire.—An area of ground exposed to the fire of a weapon or group of weapons. 40

Final protective line.-Fixed band of grazing fire along a predetermined line, so fixed as to cover the front or flank of a position that is to be defended. 

Flank. The right or left of a command in line or in column; also the element on the right or left of a line. 

Fox hole. Individual trench, hastily dug, and permitting fire from the prone position. 

Front. The advanced portion of the field of operations.

Infiltrate. To advance by sending individual soldiers or small groups forward.

Line of departure. The line which all leading elements cross at the time of attack.

Logistics. That branch of military science dealing with the supply, quartering, and movement of troops.

Main line of resistance.-An imaginary line connecting the forward elements of front line combat groups of the battle position. 

Message center.-Point usually at or near command post through which messages are routed. 

Mortar.-A weapon with a high-angled trajectory. 

Objective.—Point or area against which attack or manœuver is directed. 41

Observation post.-Point from which fire, terrain, movements of friendly or enemy troops may be observed.

Outpost.-A covering detachment detailed to secure a resting command or defensive position against surprise by hostile ground forces.

Patrol.-Individual or group detailed to reconnoiter to the front or flanks. 

Point.-A patrol preceding the advance party.

Pontoon.-A flat-bottomed boat or float used in building a floating pontoon bridge for the passage of troops.

Railhead. A point on a railroad where supplies are received for transfer to combat, field or other motor trains. 

Rear guard. A security detachment which protects the rear of a force. 

Reconnaissance.—An investigation of terrain to gain information of enemy dispositions, terrain, or other data. 

Round. A unit of ammunition, as a cartridge or a shell. 

Salvo. One round per gun in a battery, fired in a prescribed order and with a prescribed interval of time between each shot.

Scout.-A soldier sent forward to obtain information of the terrain or of the enemy. 42

Strong point.-A group of combat points.

Tank trap.-An obstacle designed to impede the forward progress of tanks. 

Trajectory.—The curved path followed by a projectile fired from a weapon.

SECTION IV Miscellaneous 24. USE OF WATCH TO DETER- MINE DIRECTION 

Direct the hour hand toward the sun. A bearing half way between the hour hand and the 12 o'clock mark on the watch is true south. 

25. TELLING TIME WITH A COMPASS 

Take the true azimuth (T.A.) of the sun. (T.A.) X 4/60 hours before or after 12 o'clock. 

26. JUDGING DISTANCE 

At 100 yards a person's mouth and eyes can be clearly seen.

At 300 yards the face is indistinct. 

At 500 yards the head and hat can be seen and colors can be distinguished.

At 700 yards it is difficult to distinguish the head. 67

Distance is usually overestimated when— 

Looking over broken ground. 

In a dull light.

The object is in the shade. 

Heat haze is rising from the ground. 

Both background and object are of the same color. 

Kneeling or lying down.

Distance is usually underestimated when- The air is clear and the sun is shining brightly on the object.

Looking across level ground, snow, or water. 

Looking uphill or down. 

27. JUDGING FROM A DISTANCE 

Infantry on the march raises a low, thick cloud of dust. 

Cavalry on the march raises a high thin cloud of dust.

Motor trucks raise a heavy, rapidly moving cloud of dust. 

Tanks raise a heavy, moving cloud of dust. 69 68
