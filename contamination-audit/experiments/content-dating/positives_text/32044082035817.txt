FOREWORD 

Believing that the unhappy state of our nation and its unfortunate business conditions can be corrected only if and when the people of the United States have a clear understanding of the problems which confront us, we are presenting a study of the economy of business activity in such a manner that persons having the most limited education can understand; and understanding, may know that they have an authoritative knowledge of economic law. 

The basic purpose of this study is to discover why the so-called "Capitalistic System" is failing to meet the needs of a very large part of the people of this nation. This study is made with the hope that, after learning the cause of the failure, this knowledge will lead us to correct its defects and so perfect the capitalistic system that it will yield exact economic justice to all people. A system of economy which gives economic justice to each individual would be a perfect system and would eliminate all demand for extreme revolutionary change. 

Through this study, we find that there is only one important defect in the existing system and that, if this one defect were corrected, the capitalistic system would yield prosperity to all people. Therefore, we have continued the study to discover the means of correcting the cause of its failure, and through a simple form of governmental supervision, cause it to operate perfectly.

The writer acknowledges having experienced difficulty during the course of this study due to his preconceived ideas regarding the matters under consideration. We therefore request that you give the courtesy of an open mind during this consideration. 

If this study contributes to a greater degree of happiness and prosperity for humanity, our reward will be a deep feeling of satisfaction in having been of service to our fellowmen.

Chapter I DEFINITIONS 

The following are simple and concise definitions of the exact meaning of certain commonly used words when used in connection with a discussion of economic law. 

The writer asks that you read these definitions very carefully and thoughtfully before proceeding to read the following sections of this study.

Commodity A quantity or parcel of goods; an economic good; the singular of goods.

Commodities-A general term which includes movable things which are bought and sold; that is, goods, wares, merchandise, produce of land, manufactures, etc. Economic goods intended for immediate use or enjoyment.

Natural Things Things in their natural original state, or as created by nature.

Chapter II PURPOSES OF THE STUDY 

This study and discussion of economic law is for the following purposes: 

1. To enable us to discover and identify the fundamental truths of economic law. 

2. To enable us to recognize and observe the operation of economic law and its control of the economic lives of people. 

3. To enable us to recognize and identify, for what they are, the penalties which economic law enforces for its violation. 

4. To prove that violation of economic law is not profitable. 

5. To prove that exact obedience to the dictates of economic law would make possible unlimited business activity. 

6. To enable us to devise means of regulating our economic affairs in accordance with economic law. 

7. To show that the capitalistic system of economy, if based on the fundamental truths of economic law, would make possible the free and unhampered development of our national, economic, and cultural life.

Chapter III THE ELEMENTS OF PROSPERITY 

Before beginning an analysis of the fundamental law of economy and its use in regulating business conditions within our country, we must agree upon just what constitutes national and individual prosperity, or upon the conditions which exist when all the people within a nation are prosperous.

An economist would define a prosperous nation as follows: 

A nation is prosperous when, within that nation, there is an unlimited exchange of commodities.

If within a nation there is an unlimited exchange of commodities, all of the conditions favorable to the exchange of commodities must exist; and any condition which would limit or restrict the exchange of commodities does not exist.

We may agree that a country is prosperous when three conditions prevail within a country as follows: 

First Condition: When within that country there is an abundance of the material means of satisfying human desires. 

Second Condition: When within that country there are adequate facilities for the economical distribution of the material means of satisfying human desires.

Third Condition: When everyone within that country has employment and receives pay or derives a profit sufficient to enable him to buy whatever he needs to satisfy his human desires. 

The first condition now exists. 

None will deny that there is within our country an abundance of the material means of satisfying human desires; nor that we are able to produce these things even more abundantly. 

The first of the three conditions requisite to prosperity has therefore been met and is fulfilled. It offers no problem requiring solution. 

The second condition now exists. 

We may also agree, without argument, that the facilities for the distribution of commodities are here; that is, that the means of transporting commodities are adequate and that our stores are the best in the world, and sufficient in number to handle the sale, exchange, or distribution of the material means of satisfying human desires or "commodities".

The second condition has also been met and now offers no problem, the solution of which is essential to the prosperity of our country. 

The third condition does not exist.

All persons will agree, that everyone within our country is not employed and that everyone does not receive pay nor derive a profit sufficient to enable him to buy whatever he needs to satisfy his human desires.

ELEMENTS OF PROSPERITY 

Everyone will agree that this third condition, essential to prosperity, does not exist in our country; and most everyone will agree that, if this third condition were fulfilled, there would be no lack of consumers' purchasing power. There would be unlimited business activity, and all of our countrymen would consider themselves prosperous. 

Since the problems incident to the first and second conditions have been solved, through the personal initiative of business men and laborers, it seems obvious, that the reason for the lack of prosperity, the cause of the business depression and all the suffering which has resulted therefrom, must be that the third of the three conditions, which we have decided are necessary to prosperity, has not been fulfilled and does not exist within our country. 

It is evident then, that the economic difficulties are due to a lack of consumers' purchasing power caused by people's lack of employment and their not receiving pay nor deriving a profit sufficient to enable them to buy the things they need to satisfy their human desires. 

The solution of our economic problem, the creation of ideal business conditions, the providing of employment for all, and the prosperity of each of us depends upon whether or not we have the intelligence to find, adapt, and make effective a system of economy which will bring this happy third condition into existence, and cause it to exist for all time.

To answer this problem properly and completely, we must find how economic wealth produced by labor, with the assistance of capital, can be so fairly and equitably divided between the laborer and the capitalist that the laborer will receive sufficient wages and the capitalist sufficient profit to enable each to buy whatever he needs to satisfy his human desires.

We will concede that a satisfactory answer to this problem must be a usable measure, an exact yardstick which both the laborer and the capitalist can understand and in which each may have implicit faith.

This discussion has now led up to the fact that there is left only one problem to solve. A solution of this problem will tell how to correct the one remaining defect in the capitalistic system of economy and, if placed in effective operation, would perfect the capitalistic system. 

The capitalistic system of economy thus perfected would meet the material needs of every individual. There would then be no desire or occasion for extreme revolutionary change.

Chapter IV THE PROBLEM 

The one remaining economic problem to be solved is as follows: 

How can we provide everyone with employment which will pay him a wage or a profit sufficient to enable him to buy whatever he needs to satisfy his human desires, and thus remove the one and only condition which limits business activity or the exchange of commodities?

Chapter V THE FUNDAMENTAL LAW OF ECONOMY 

To solve this problem (Chapter IV) for our country as a whole, we must consider all of the commodities produced within the country during a definite period of time, or rather since we use money as a measure of value, we must consider the fair and equitable division between labor and capital of the value in dollars of all the economic wealth produced within our country during a definite period of time or during one calendar year.

The total value of the economic wealth produced in our country in a year includes the following. (a) The value of all products of the basic industries; viz., farms, forests, fisheries, quarries, mines, and minerals (including petroleum). (b) The increase in value of the products of the basic industries due to manufacturing processes, intended to fit them to supply human wants. (c) The value of all construction work or capital investment structures created during the year, for any purpose whatsoever. 

Let us see if by means of a simple diagram, and logical reasoning, we can find this exact measure or yardstick for use in dividing the economic wealth produced within our country in a way which will permit us all to be prosperous and happy, and still permit us to build a greater nation and a greater civilization. 

In order that we shall not become confused, we shall build up a diagram a line at a time with an explanation of each new line, as it comes into view.

In the diagram the horizontal lines will represent values in dollars and will be drawn to scale; that is, the length of the horizontal lines will be in proportion to the number of dollars they represent. 

Vertical lines will be used for demarkation and dividing lines, and will have no value other than that of holding the lines representing the various divisions of the money value of the economic wealth in proper relation to the line representing the value of all the commodities produced during the year.

We may now recognize and identify a fundamental truth of the law of business activity and of economy. 

Fundamental Truth No. 1—Unlimited business activity requires that, when capital wishes to sell one dollar's worth of goods to the consumer, the consumer must have one dollar with which to buy the goods. 

An accumulation of commodities in excess of the amount which the consuming public has money to buy has in the past been called overproduction of commodities or "overproduction". 11 When, however, we consider the things which a large part of our population needs, especially those who are unemployed, and also those who are employed but do not receive pay or derive a profit sufficient to enable them to buy what they need to satisfy their desires, we will at once recognize the fact that there is not overproduction. There is underconsumption due to a lack of consumers' purchasing power or to the fact, that the major portion of our people have not the money with which to buy what they want.

It is true that, if there is to be unlimited business activity or unlimited exchange of commodities, the consumers must have the money to buy all unexported domestic commodities, as well as those commodities, which are imported for consumption from foreign countries.

Chapter VI CAPITAL INVESTMENTS 

In addition to the cooperation of capital and labor in the production of commodities, capital and workingmen cooperate in the creation of capital investments; that is, in the construction of factories, railroads, office buildings, hospitals, churches, universities, mansions, poor-houses, palaces, yachts, club houses, ships, navies, etc. 

All of these capital investment structures are the result of assembling the commodity products of labor into a permanent structure or one which is consumed only after a long period of time. 

Of the examples of capital investments listed above, palaces and yachts are constructed for the pleasure and pride of the wealthy; railroads, factories, and office buildings for the purpose of making a profit; churches for a place to worship God; universities for the education of our children and the advancement of civilization; hospitals and poor-houses for the care of the sick and needy; and navies to protect our commerce from the greed of profiteering nations and to prevent us from being enslaved by foreign peoples.

It matters not, however, for what purpose capital investments are made, be it for pleasure, for profit, for educational, religious, or charitable purposes, or any other purpose; their effect upon the economic life of the nation is the same, for capital investment is simply the spending of accumulated money for the commodity products of labor and for additional labor to assemble these products into a permanent structure.

The money spent for the materials or commodities to be assembled into a capital investment structure goes to the producers of those commodities, principally to labor. The money spent for the labor required to assemble those commodities into a permanent capital investment structure also goes to labor and is thus redistributed to a large body of consumers in the form of wages.

Since the average wage earner seldom acquires sufficient money to make a capital investment, he spends this money for commodities; the necessities of life, food, clothing, shelter, also recreation, entertainment, and the services of other people.

15 A consideration of the Figure 6, shows that capital may take as profit the total amount of money which it needs for capital investments, plus the total or entire value of the excess of exports over imports; and it may do this without limiting the exchange of commodities within the nation, and without causing business depression. 

Profit Which Must Be Redistributed to Consumers

It is likewise seen that any profit taken in addition to that due to the excess of exports over imports must be spent or invested by the capitalists, for that money must be placed in the hands of consumers to enable them to buy all of the commodities which remain in and must be sold in the United States. This statement develops the second fundamental truth of the law of economy. 

Fundamental Truth No. 2-Unlimited business activity requires that profit taken, in excess of that derived from foreign trade must be spent or converted into property for ownership. 

Amount of Profit Which May Be Held Out of Circulation 

A further study of Figure 6, will indicate that the part of the allowable profit to capital, due to the excess of exports over imports, is money which is not needed by the consumers in order to buy all of the commodities to be sold in the United States. The capitalists may therefore hold this amount of money for future investments or out of circulation without limiting the exchange of commodities, and without causing business depression.

Extent and Limit of Profit Under Economic Law 

It is also obvious, that if the capitalist should take 50% more profit and immediately redistribute it to consumers by spending it for capital investments, services or commodities, the consumers could still buy all of the commodities within the United States and business would be unlimited.

It is clear, therefore, that the amount of profit which capitalists may take is limited only by their ability to spend or invest it, or to the amount necessary to enable them to buy whatever they need to satisfy their human desires,

PROFIT UNDER ECONOMIC LAW Total Allowable Profit 

The total allowable profit for commodity-producing capital therefore comes from two sources: first, the amount which the capitalists can spend and invest within the nation; second, the value of the commodities which he can sell in foreign countries in excess of the value of those which are bought from foreign countries. 

Since it is true (See definition of True) that commodity-producing capital can take as profit the entire value of the excess of exports over imports, and in addition all the money it can immediately spend or invest within the nation, it seems that there can be no reasonable objection to limiting it to this amount, for therein lies the solution of all our economic ills.

We now recognize and identify the third fundamental truth of the law of economy or business activity. 

Fundamental Truth No. 3-Unlimited business activity requires that the profit of capital be equal to and limited by the amount which the capitalists can spend or invest within the country, plus the total value of the commodities exported in excess of the value of the commodities which are imported for consumption.

Note: The above means that economic law provides that capital may take all of the profit from our business with foreign countries. In addition to this, it may take all of the money as profit which it can invest or spend for any purpose whatsoever, and it may do this without limiting the exchange of commodities or limiting business activity. Economic law also provides that capital may not take more than this, as will be shown by subsequent discussion.

THE REGULATION OF CONSUMERS' PURCHASING POWER AND PROFITS BY DIVIDING THE ECONOMIC WEALTH IN AC- CORDANCE WITH ECONOMIC LAW, WOULD CREATE UNLIMIT- ED BUSINESS ACTIVITY. 

The above solution of the economic problem allows the capitalist to take all the money as profit that he can possibly spend and invest within the country. In addition to giving him all he can spend or use, it gives him the entire profit accruing from our business with foreign countries. It simply gratifies the capitalist's pride of possession, and at the same time deprives the consumers of the money which they need to buy the products of capitalistic industry.

Chapter VIII  

There are three ways in which economic law may be violated: 

1. By paying labor more for the production of commodities than economic law indicates that it should be paid. 

2. By paying capital investment workmen, for their services, more than the amount permissible under economic law. 

3. By capital's taking money in excess of the amount of profit permitted by economic law.

With the diagram of economic law completed, we can now see what will happen if this simple fundamental law, governing the division of the economic wealth, is violated. 

In order to save space, we will bring down the parts of the diagram which are essential to the study of the violation of economic law, and draw them into an abbreviated diagram. We will then use the Abbreviated Diagram (Figure 7) showing the correct division of wealth for comparison with additional similar diagrams showing various violations of the law so that by comparing the two diagrams, we may see the effect of each violation upon the business life of the nation. 

Violation No. 1

There are, however, certain commodities which are suitable only for capital investments: for example, structural steel, cement, steel rails and ties for railroads, locomotives, freight and passenger cars, all forms of building materials, machinery for factories, etc.; in fact, all of the products of the so-called heavy industries may be included. If the capitalists and business men receive less than they need for capital investment purposes, they would not have the money to buy the products of the heavy industries. These products could not be sold. The factories would cease to produce these commodities and a large percentage of the United States laborers would be without employment. If capital does not receive all the profit it needs to construct capital investment structures, the very large group of laborers engaged in assembling these commodities (materials) into capital investment structures will also be without employment.

Capital can only redistribute to consumers through capital investments the profit it receives from productive labor. If it does not receive the profit it needs for capital investments, it is not only the capitalist who doesn't get his just share of the wealth, but also the laborers who produce the commodities used in constructing capital investments, and the workers usually engaged in assembling these commodities into capital investment structures. 

Adequate Profit Is An Economic Necessity 

It is seen that by labor's receiving more and capital's receiving less than its just share of the wealth, capital is prevented from creating necessary capital investments. The laborers who produce the capital investment commodities, as well as those who assemble these materials into capital investment structures are thrown out of employment. When these producers of capital investment materials and construction workmen are without employment in their own vocation, they must seek employment in some other line of work or in competition with commodityproducing labor. If they are employed by industry, there will be a surplus of manufactured commodities produced with a consequent lowering of prices and of wages. If they are not employed by industry, the increased competition for jobs will result in a lowering of wages of industrial laborers. If they return to the farm, they will produce a surplus of agricultural products with its resultant decrease in prices and in the earnings of farmers. When considering these facts it becomes plain that in receiving more than its just share of the economic wealth, commodity-producing labor has benefited only temporarily. By receiving more than its just share of the wealth, it has compelled others to work in competition with it with the result that it loses all of its temporary gain.

Without an adequate profit, the capitalist would lack money with which to build new and modern factories and buy machinery with which to manufacture new products to improve our standard of living and advance our civilization. Those modern factories and buildings which do so much to create more healthful and improved working conditions could not be constructed. The laborers who build them would be without employment. It is true that, if capital is deprived of the profit which it needs for capital investments, the development of our civilization and cultural life would cease. Industrial and business chaos would result. Certainly this would not be profitable. Indeed it would be a calamity.

Note: Any industrial enterprise under the capitalistic system of economy is a cooperative effort of labor and capital to earn wages and derive a profit, by producing commodities for, or rendering some service to the people of our country, and in some cases, those of other nations. 

Labor, however, has first claim on the earnings of the business; because without labor there could be no business, and consequently no profit for capital.

The method of curtailing the production of commodities or of decreasing the quantity of economic goods produced is now, and always has been, to decrease the number of laborers engaged in the production of commodities, to discharge or lay off a part of the workmen. For the purpose of this study, we will assume that workmen are laid off in exact proportion to the decreased consumers' purchasing power resulting from capital's taking money in excess of the amount prescribed by economic law.

In other words, we will assume that workmen are discharged in proportion to the amount of commodities produced which cannot be sold because capital has taken money in excess of the economic profit and in so doing has deprived the producing consumers of the money with which to buy.

A consideration of this diagram, Figure 11, indicates that discharging the laborers engaged in the production of commodities has decreased the quantity of economic goods produced. It has also proportionately decreased the amount of money distributed to consumers in the form of wages so that with the decreased production, the consumers are even less able to buy all of the commodities which must be sold in the United States. It is clearly seen, that by discharging workers and continuing to take a profit in excess of that permitted by economic law, industrial management has decreased the consumers' purchasing power in exact proportion to the amount of money taken as excessive profit, plus the amount of money which is held out of circulation since no wages are paid to the discharged workers, who cannot now buy.

Industry cannot sell its products to consumers who do not have employment to enable them to earn money or derive a profit with which to buy. The management of industry, by discharging workmen, has not only decreased the amount of commodities produced but has proportionately decreased the purchasing power of the consumers. It has decreased the market for industry's products and has further limited the exchange of commodities or has decreased the general volume of business all along the line from the source of natural products through the manufacturer, the jobber, wholesaler, retailer, and dealers. 

As mine operators, lumbermen, quarries, manufacturers, jobbers, wholesalers, dealers, and retailers cannot pay wages or salaries to people whose services are not required, there must be an adjustment of force, or the number of people employed in the various lines of business must be reduced in proportion to the decreased volume of commodities produced, and the decreased consumers' purchasing power brought about by the discharge of commodity-producing labor.

It is the custom under the capitalistic system to take profit in proportion to the investment or a certain percentage of the investment. 

Since it is a fact that an investment in industry cannot be decreased in proportion to the number of workers who were discharged as the result of capital taking an excessive profit, it now becomes necessary for the industrialist to take sufficient profit from a decreased number of workmen to pay the regular dividend on the full investment used to employ the larger number of men. This, of course, can be done only by reducing the wages of the laborers who are still employed. 

Reducing the wages of the laborers who are still employed decreases the amount of money distributed to the consumers in the form of wages. Therefore, they can buy still less of the commodity products with the result that the volume of business and profits is proportionately decreased.

As this process of taking an excessive profit, reducing wages and discharging laborers and workers goes on and on, the amount of economic goods which consumers have not the money to buy continues to accumulate.

The consumers' decreased purchasing power with its resultant decrease in the volume of business eliminates the need for new buildings in which to transact business, also the need for new factories. In general, it eliminates the need for capital investment and certainly makes it difficult, if not impossible, to invest capital profitably. 

The lack of opportunity to make profitable capital investments will cause the capitalist to retain his money or hold it out of circulation. 

All will agree that, when not in circulation, money is an inert, useless thing which does no good to the capitalist who has it, nor the unemployed workers who need it. 

The industrialist by doing the very human thing of taking a profit in excess of that amount which he needs to satisfy his human desires or in excess of that to which economic law indicates he is entitled, has caused many workers to lose their employment and has decreased the volume of business of everyone from the natural source of raw material to the retailer and consumer. He has not only decreased his own business but has made it impossible for him to invest his capital profitably whether derived from legitimate profits or from money taken in excess of that which economic law permits.

Due to the limited business activity, resulting from capital's taking an excessive profit, all of the excessive profit taken is now invested in commodities which have become property. This is added proof of the fact that economic law requires capital and capitalists to accept, in fixed property, all profit in excess of that which they require for immediate use and enjoyment. goods.

6. Capitalists cannot use and do not want this kind of property.

The power of taxation is the power to confiscate our entire property if we cannot pay the tax assessed and grant ownership of that property to those who have the money with which to pay. This is admittedly the most effective collection system ever devised. 

Capital now decides to make this most effective collection system, the government's power of taxation, its own collection system. 

As the consumers have no money with which to buy the economic goods which they have produced, capital lends its money to the government at a reasonable rate of interest, so that the government may distribute it to consumers to enable them to buy the accumulated economic goods. This scheme of extending credit to the consumer through the government and using the government's power of taxation to collect seems particularly advantageous to capital for three reasons as follows: 

1. It permits capital to take a profit on both the money and the goods. 

2. It increases the number of consumers as the government will distribute the money to persons who are in need of commodities or who are unemployed and do not receive a profit or earn wages sufficient to enable them to buy what they need to satisfy their human desires. 

3. If the consumers who receive the money to buy the accumulated economic goods are unable to repay it, capital through the government, can collect from other people who own property and are able to pay taxes.

The most superficial consideration of this scheme convinces us that lending money to unemployed consumers through the government enables the unemployed consumers to preserve his physical existence, but does not provide him with a means of deriving a profit or earning wages with which to repay the loan.

Chapter II LEGISLATION, PERMISSIVE AND REGULATIVE 

Economic law is a natural law. It is at all times active in regulating the material affairs of man. Without the beneficent influence of economic law man would have come to grief ages ago. For nearly two thousand years, man has denied and made no attempt to obey economic law. It is disobedience to economic law that causes panics and business depression. Economic law is the unseen force which brought us out of past panics and restored our business affairs to normal. However, since man makes no attempt to obey economic law, it governs through the penalties which it enforces for its violation. Economic law permits serious violation before enforcing its penalties, and then inflicts penalties whose severity are in proportion to the seriousness of the violation. 

When the penalties of unemployment, deprivation, business losses and capital losses are satisfied, business activities increase. Thus, we have very wide variations in the activity of business. Capital takes excessive profit. This deprives the consumer of purchasing power. The lack of consumers' purchasing power makes it impossible for industry to sell its products. The inability of industry to sell its products causes unemployment. Unemployment causes a further decrease in consumers' purchasing power. Capital suffers a loss of business. Capital attempts to reclaim the lost business, and in its effort to do so spends or loses the excessive profits which it has taken. When the excessive profits which capital has taken are returned to labor and the consumer through capital expenditures and corporation losses, business is again normal. 

We may either obey economic law or accept the penalties for disobedience. That is our only choice because economic law is a natural law that is now in force and has always been in force. The purpose of the study is to analyze and interpret economic law and learn how to obey it so that through obedience to the law we may avoid suffering the penalties which it enforces for its violation. 

To obey economic law the economic wealth, produced by labor with the assistance of capital, must be divided in accordance with economic law.

If we are to divide the economic wealth in accordance with economic law, we must have a reliable source of information regarding the division which is currently required by that law. Statistical information regarding the economic affairs of the nation is compiled by the United States Department of Commerce. Our Government is now in a position to prescribe the division of the economic wealth agreeable to economic law. The source of reliable information now exists. 49

LEGISLATION 

It will be said that the government should announce the correct division of the wealth so that those who wish to do so may voluntarily obey economic law. The real business men who would wish to do this could not do so and compete successfully with those so-called business men who obtain a profit by imposing on labor or through other forms of fraud. Therefore, we must establish a means of supervising the division of the economic wealth so that those who wish to obey economic law may be permitted, and those who do not wish to obey the law may be compelled to do so.

The legislation necessary to permit governmental supervision of the economic affairs of the nation will be described briefly for your consideration.

Let us assume that Congress would enact a law, No. 1, requiring that the money value of all of the commodities produced by labor with the assistance of capital shall be divided between capital and labor according to percentages prescribed by economic law as determined by the Department of Commerce. That prescribed percentage of the value of the products of labor will go to capital as profit, and the remainder must go to labor to pay for the work done to produce the commodities. Law No. I would permit the government to order us to divide the economic wealth in accordance with economic law, and tell us how to divide it.

To enable the government to enforce its order, that the economic wealth be divided in accordance with economic law, it must be provided with a means of knowing when and by whom economic law is being violated. In order to provide the government with this knowledge, let us also assume that Congress should enact a law, No. 2, making the issuing of money and the maintenance of banks a government monopoly, and requiring the government to buy all banks. All banks would then become branches of the United States Treasury or branches of the United States Government Bank under the direction of the Treasury Department.

Chapter IX TAXES 

All wealth is produced by labor. 

The only source from which wealth may be paid to the government as taxes, is the economic wealth or the wealth which is currently produced.

If money is collected from any other source than the economic wealth as it is produced, it is a capital assessment and not a tax. 

It was explained in our definitions that there are only two kinds of material wealth; i. e., economic wealth and personal or private property. There is only one source other than the economic wealth from which the government may collect money, and that is from the personal wealth. When government collects from the personal wealth, it takes away a part of our working capital and decreases the value of the private property which we own. For the government to make a capital assessment, even though it be done in the guise of taxation, would be confiscation of property without due process of law and would be unconstitutional. For the government to appropriate the private property of its citizens is not only unconstitutional but would be so morally wrong that it cannot be justified.

The government does have, not only the constitutional and moral right but, it is its duty to buy, pay an equitable price for, and own any property which it finds necessary to the proper conduct of government and the prosperity of its people. 

We have not yet experienced a very serious form of capital assessment in this country. Therefore, it is obvious that American taxes come from the only other source from which they can come or from the economic wealth.

American taxes are and always have been a levy of economic wealth; i.e., when capital and labor have produced economic wealth and are in possession of the money value of that wealth, which they have produced, taxation takes a part of that money from them and gives it to the government. 

Regardless of the method or means which the government uses to collect, whether direct or indirect, regardless of the subterfuge and deceit of tax laws, regardless of whether the actual tax payments are made by the rich or the poor, all and every tax is a levy of the economic wealth as it is produced by labor. All wealth is produced by labor. 

There is no other source from which the government can derive wealth. 69

TAXES 

At present the levy made by the various governmental units against the economic wealth produced by labor amounts to between thirty and forty percent of the wealth produced; that is, from every ten dollars earned by American labor with the assistance of capital, government takes three to four dollars through taxation. 

In view of the fact that all taxes are a levy of the money value of the economic wealth produced, it is plain that the just and equitable form of taxation is the income tax. 

If this one method of taxation were used each citizen would then know exactly what his government is costing him. When he becomes conscious of the fact that the government is costing him from thirty to forty percent of his income, he will quickly move to investigate. 

When he learns the extent of graft and corruption in government, he will be horrified and will quickly move to correct the situation. The resentment, which would be aroused by a full knowledge of the situation and of the practices of politicians for which labor is paying, would be so great that it would result in the decimation of the politicians. 

This study of economics has shown that, if economic law were enforced, labor and capital would each receive its just share of the economic wealth as it is produced. If the value of the economic wealth were justly and equitably divided between capital and labor, the government would properly take from each through taxation exactly the same percentage of its income. Under this arrangement the laborer might personally pay more money to the government but it would cost him no more than it does now.

Labor produces all the wealth that is produced, and since this is true labor either directly or indirectly pays all of the taxes that are paid.

When the laborer is taxed a small percentage of his income and the capitalist a large percentage of his income, it is because there has been an uneconomic and unjust division of the economic wealth between capital and labor. The higher tax levied against the capitalist is simply an attempt of government to collect taxes from the capitalist in proportion to the part of the economic wealth which he has appropriated, due to the government's having condoned an unfair division of the wealth. 

If you tax the rich, the rich simply take additional profit from labor and pass it to the government. Regardless of how or from whom taxes are collected the laborer pays them, for labor is the one who produces the economic wealth with which taxes are paid.

Chapter X NORMAL BUSINESS ACTIVITY 

Since the beginning of the business depression nearly all newspapers and magazines publish charts or graphs showing the present state of business activity in comparison with that of some past year or the average of several past years.

The discussion of these graphs is usually based upon the assumption that the business activity of the country was normal during the year or average of years which they select. These discussions invariably contain the further assumption that, when business activities increase to a point comparable to the activity of the past years, we will be back to normal and nothing more can or need be done about it. 

This study has shown that if the value of the economic wealth produced was divided between capital and labor in accordance with economic law the following conditions would prevail: 

1. All persons who are able and willing to work could be given continuous employment. 

2. The consumers would at all times have sufficient money to buy all of the products of labor. 

With the above two conditions existing, the seventy-five percent of our population who have not proper houses, furnishings, clothes, or food would immediately buy those things which they need. With their actual needs satisfied their wants would remain to be gratified. 

Under economic law the business of satisfying the actual needs of our own people would keep all of labor and industry engaged for many years, and when their actual needs are satisfied their wants are still infinite. When we consider the preceding statements of fact, we may know that business has never been normal nor has it ever even closely approached normal, neither in this nor any other modern nation because of failure to obey economic law.

Chapter XI THE THIRTY HOUR WEEK 

This study has shown that if economic law is obeyed, the consumers will have money to buy all of the products of labor which are for sale at any given time. The consumers will have sufficient money to buy all of the products of labor regardless of the amount of commodities produced. The study also shows that under economic law the laborer will receive his just share of the economic wealth he produces. 

This being true, the number of hours labor would work each week would depend upon what kind of a standard of living he wished to maintain for his family and himself. 71

Chapter XII SOCIALISM AND COMMUNISM VERSUS CAPITALISM 

The study of the violation of economic law showed that capital's practice of taking money from business in excess of the economic profit causes the stagnation of business and industry and a lack of employment for labor.

If unemployment became so severe that it could not be condoned and the government confiscated industry and operated it without profit for the benefit of the people the result would be socialism. 

This study has taught us that the amount of profit prescribed by economic law is necessary for the construction of capital investment structures and for the perpetuation and improvement of our civilization. 

Therefore, socialism cannot operate successfully unless it takes and properly utilizes the amount of profit prescribed by economic law. If a socialistic government took and properly utilized the prescribed economic profit it would then offer no advantage over a capitalistic government which enforced economic law and limited capital to the amount of profit allowable under economic law. 

Under socialism, or government ownership of industry, the maintenance and development of the means of producing economic wealth and the maintenance and upbuilding of our civilization would be in the control of our politicians. The modern politician seeks his own aggrandizement and the satisfaction of his personal wants and interests. He proceeds with utter disregard for the interests of the commonwealth except during periods when the votes of the common people endanger his political existence. The modern politician is unworthy of so great a trust.

Under capitalism or private ownership of industry the maintenance and development of the means of producing economic wealth and the perpetuation of civilization is in control of the individuals who own the industries.

The feeling of responsibility for the preservation and improvement of that which they own causes them to do an exceedingly good job of preserving and improving this nation's means of producing economic wealth in abundance.

In perfecting our capitalistic system by enforcing economic law we would receive all of the benefits which socialists promise without entrusting our civilization to unscrupulous politicians. 72

Chapter XIII SUBSISTENCE HOMES 

The study has shown that, if we would perfect the capitalistic system by a simple obedience to economic law everyone would be prosperous, with earnings sufficient to enable him to buy whatever he needs to satisfy his human desires. 

With this result of the study in mind, the idea of asking people, who have all the benefits of civilization within easy reach, to go back to poverty and be satisfied with a mere subsistence becomes too preposterous and ridiculous to merit discussion.

Chapter XIV SAVING YOUTH FROM RADICALISM 

The majority of people in this country are born in poverty, and reared in penury, educated in the public schools, and if they are so fortunate as to receive a college education, they are graduated to unemployment. Regardless of their degree of education or the amount of their wealth they reach the fullness of their youth with their whole being demanding that they fulfil the purpose and responsibility of perpetuating society. When coming to the age of manhood and womanhood, youth examines the possibility of fulfilling this natural responsibility and finds that our modern civilization does not provide the degree of economic security necessary for its proper fulfillment. He finds that the capitalistic system does not provide him with sufficient earnings to enable him to establish and maintain a home necessary for the adequate protection of a mate, the mother of his children. Being intelligent and proud in the fullness of their youth, this man and maid desist from founding a home in poverty and begetting children to penury and misery. Denied the means of decent existence these sons and daughters go forth in the night seeking the cheap and tawdry pleasures which are within their paltry means. The wheel of fortune turns; on and on they go and if good fortune does not favor them with the means of right living, sooner or later, in the fullness of their youth and consumed by the race feeling they mate without benefit of clergy and bring upon themselves a remorse of conscience, a depth of misery worse than the poverty which is their heritage.

A young man is unable to provide a home and protection for a young mother because we of this generation have been so self-centered and selfish that we have failed to acquire the knowledge and have lacked the courage to perfect an economic system that would provide them with the means of right living. We, because of better fortune and the assumed superiority of our age, turn our noses toward heaven and wonder why our sons and daughters think there is something wrong with our economic system. We are slightly stirred from our complacency because they harken to radical teachers who would abolish such a system. 

Common sense would indicate that the way to save youth from radicalism is to alleviate the social condition which is driving them to radicalism, to correct the defect in our capitalistic system of economy which drives them to desperation. The way to save youth from desperation is to make it possible for youth to achieve a decent consummation 74

The way to save youth from Socialism and Communism is to correct the defect in our capitalistic system so that it will be possible for youth to found a home with a guarantee of economic security. It must be sufficient for the needs of parenthood, the protection of motherhood, the rearing and education of children, and provision of economic security for their children when they are grown.

The study of the economy of business activity has proved that obedience of the law of economics would make it possible for everyone who wishes to work to have continuous employment. This proof is especially applicable to youth for modern industry acknowledges the efficiency of youth by giving preference to youth over middle age as it strives to employ those who will yield the greater profit. 

Youth should surely add the force of its votes to those of the farmers, the city workers, and the aged in helping to compel the prompt correction of the one great defect in the capitalistic system. These combined efforts could compel the leaders of this nation to enact these laws, and do those things which are necessary to effect a just division of the economic wealth between capital and labor in accordance with economic law.

Chapter XV SAVING THIS NATION AND CIVILIZATION FROM DOWNFALL 

When studying credit and the use of the power of taxation to maintain, for capital, the privilege of taking money from business in excess of the amount of profit prescribed by economic law, we saw plainly how surely this nation as well as others are progressing toward financial breakdown and the disorganization of modern civilization. 

We have also seen clearly how simple obedience to economic law would bring to us unexampled prosperity and enable all, both capitalists and laborers, to receive their just share of the abundance which God has provided.

If all men received their just share of the wealth produced, none would be unjustly exploited, and we could free ourselves from the fear that poor and oppressed people would rise in indignation and in their wrath destroy us and the civilization that so many centuries of human struggle for better things has built. 

A consummate consideration of the preceding study will prove, and will cause you to conclude that obedience to economic law is even more profitable for the capitalists than for the workers. 

It will be argued by the recalcitrant that so great a change in the accepted practices and principles of business administration, as this study proves to be essential to the salvation of capitalism and the perpetuation of civilization, might wreck our business structure. 

It is admitted that such a change would tax the ingenuity of many and would introduce problems worthy of the best minds of this era of great business men.

The economic problem must be met and solved by business men in the very near future. We have faith that the American business man will accept the challenge and will conquer the problem of the ages. The American business man is the most courageous and adaptable of any in the world. There is no hope of proper leadership from elsewhere.

Chapter XVI HUMAN HAPPINESS 

The preceding study has taught us the solution of the economic problem. There is no other solution. There are many expedients which may be used to ameliorate various unhappy situations but the use of expediency is not constructive. Expedients can only give temporary relief to special conditions and in the end accomplish nothing but confusion of thought. We have had enough expediency. Away with it. 

There is only one correct solution of the economic problem, the rendering to everyone his due. There is only one justice. 

The reason that impels us to solve the economic problem is that we want to be happy. The science of economics deals only in material and physical things. The solution of the economic problem tells us how to provide for everyone an abundance of the material things needed for the preservation and comfort of our physical existence. An abundance of material things would enable us to live in comfort and would help our feeling of importance but could not bring us true happiness, for happiness is a condition of mind. 

The constituents of human life are the material or physical, moral, mental, and spiritual. True happiness could only result from the proper solution of all of the principal problems of life which are physical, moral, mental, and spiritual.

Strict obedience to the law of economy would solve our physical problem; but our moral, mental, and spiritual problems would remain to be solved. Let us see how the solution of our material problems might contribute toward the solution of the problem of our higher life. 

We have seen that obedience to economic law would enable us to provide everyone with continuous profitable employment. This would enable a youth, who will work, to marry his first love, provide a proper home and protection for a mother and children, and thus fulfil the law of Nature decently. This would be a material but very important contribution toward a solution of the problem of biological morals. 

There is yet another and more general form of immorality born of selfishness and a mistaken idea of what constitutes happiness. 

Some believe that happiness lies in human affections, physical pleasures, gratified ambition, material wealth and so on, but these are all transitory. 

The efforts of man to achieve his false concepts of happiness lead him to heap injustice, poverty, anguish, sickness and death upon his fellowmen and are most disastrous to human progress. 77
