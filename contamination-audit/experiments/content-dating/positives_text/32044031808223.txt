PREPARATION OF LEASES 

Introduction 

This article has been prepared primarily as an aid to lawyers without extended practical experience in drawing leases as well as for more mature practitioners who have been away from the law while in military or civilian government service during the recent war years. 

It is intended to be of practical assistance to those engaged in drafting or passing upon leases and not a treatise on the law of leases. 

Nevertheless, it is as tied to the substantive law as a captive balloon to its cable. While intended for general use, the writer is painfully aware how heavily it leans on the law of New York. To treat the subject otherwise would require an erudition to which the writer does not pretend. While there is substantial uniformity among the different states with respect to much of the substantive law referred to, there will be the utmost variation on some matters. 

The rather dogmatic suggestions concerning things to be considered, and how, are intended to indicate the probable intent of the parties or what they would prefer if they considered the matters in question. This assumes they are not satisfied to rely on the relevant case and statutory law. There is nothing objectionable to special provisions agreed on by the parties, and it is desirable that the various provisions be tailored to fit their needs and desires except insofar as these may offend public policy.

A word on forms and form books is in order. It is customary to give reluctant approval to their use, inveigh against following them "slavishly" and warn against the pitfalls they offer. It is entirely impractical to draft conditional limitations, condemnation and similar clauses without the help of good forms. Here one finds carefully worked out, time-tested language as well as a check against omissions. If slavery is involved, let the draftsman be the master. The scissors-and-paste method may assemble a good collection of clauses, and a good lease as well-if they are reconciled by the draftsman rather than a court. Prepared forms do offer pitfalls to the uninitiated; it is hoped that the following pages will be of some assistance in avoiding their danger.

THE PREPARATION OF LEASES The Parties 

The first thing that should appear in a lease, after the date, is the correct names and addresses of the landlord and tenant, their capacity and the nature of their entities. If a landlord is a married man, the joinder of his wife may be necessary to bind her dower, but the instrument should expressly negate an assumption of liability by her.¹ If either party is acting in a representative or fiduciary capacity, his power to act should be ascertained by both partiesone to avoid breach of implied warranty, and the other to assure himself of getting a valid lease. If either party is a corporation, the power to act should be verified and whenever necessary a resolution of the directors should be obtained authorizing execution by a corporate official. If there is more than one tenant, the landlord will expect the liability of each to be joint and several. 

The Premises 

The demised premises should be satisfactorily identified. If the premises are an apartment, its number and street address may be sufficient. If an entire building is leased, a metes and bounds description is desirable. If part of a building other than an entire floor is demised, a blueprint showing the space demised should be annexed to the lease as an exhibit. If part of a cellar, or more than one floor are included, separate blueprints for each are recommended. Whenever precise dimensions are used, the landlord should add "be said dimensions more or less." If an entire floor is leased, use of common halls, stairways, etc., should be limited to a use in common with others. Some appurtenances are included by implication.² In a commercial lease, however, the tenant should obtain precise specification of his rights outside the premises demised. The Landlord's Title 

Where a lease involves substantial sums or an extended term, a thorough examination of the landlord's title by the tenant, or possibly title insurance, is essential. If the term is short and is for residential purposes, it is not customary to make any investigation. The extent of the search should be governed by the circumstances. In some cases, it may be adequate to run a search down from the last mortgage or conveyance made to an institution or other party likely to have verified the title. A landlord's warranty of title is desirable but rarely given by an institutional landlord. If the premises are subject to restrictions or other encumbrances, these should be set forth. 

These as well as local zoning ordinances or an agreement by the landlord, given to another tenant in the building, not to let for a competing use, may limit the tenant's use of the premises. 

The Term 

The term demised should be set forth, preferably with the precise date of the beginning and end of the term. If, on execution of the lease, the premises are occupied by a third person, consideration should be given to the new tenant's right to possession. Under the 

English rule, applicable in many of the states, the landlord is bound to give the tenant actual possession. Under the New York or "American rule" applicable in other states, the landlord is bound to give a new tenant only the right to possession. Consequently, if an occupant remains in possession, claiming neither under the landlord nor paramount right, the new tenant has no claim against the landlord based on his exclusion. Rent A lease should contain an express covenant on the part of the tenant to pay rent and additional rent. Fixed rent should be set forth in amount as well as the time and installments payable. In the absence of an agreement to the contrary rent is not payable until the end of a period and it is customary, therefore, to provide for an annual rent "payable in equal monthly installments in advance on the first day of each and every month during the term." "Rent concessions" are occasionally given a tenant. These take the form of free rent for periods prior to or during the term demised, or allowances to a tenant for making repairs. Unless carefully drafted it may be uncertain if these apply during a renewal term, given under "the same covenants and conditions as the original term," during a hold-over term or during a period when a tenant remains in possession under right given by rent freezing laws."

A lease may impose additional charges on a tenant. If the tenant is to pay real estate taxes the demised premises should be separately assessed. Inasmuch as the tax and lease years will probably not coincide, provisions should be made for apportioning taxes for the first and final years of the term. The tenant should have the right to protest or otherwise seek reduction of taxes at his expense. But if this involves delay in payment, the landlord may need additional security. Tax reductions are usually effected by obtaining a reduction in the assessed value, and the party seeking such reduction generally claims the value of the premises is lower than that fixed by the assessors. Inasmuch as such claim of value, and to a limited extent the assessment, may be competent evidence in other proceedings affecting the premises, the landlord may have reasons to pass on the amount of such claim. 

If the tenant is to pay for water, the tenant's water should be separately metered. 

The lease may require the tenant to supply the landlord with insurance--such as fire, public liability, rent, plate glass, elevator, boiler, or other appropriate forms. The landlord may require delivery of policies therefor, in amounts, form, and companies reasonably satisfactory to the landlord, together with evidence of their payment. If there is appropriate insurance in existence at the beginning of the term, this should be transferred to the tenant and the premiums apportioned in order to avoid short-rate cancellation. 

The landlord generally insists upon these and other comparable obligations being deemed additional rent and enforceable as such, in order to have the same possessory remedies for their breach as for non-payment of fixed rent. 

Percentage Rent

Percentage leases became common during the depression when many tenants were unable to assume a fixed rent equal to the value of the premises. Their use will probably continue because they are often appropriate for a retail business. Although the rent is fixed by the volume of the tenant's business, the landlord will usually require a guaranteed minimum rent, payable monthly. The percen-

If the tenant subleases or grants concessions, the rent should be computed not on the consideration therefor received by the tenant, but on the income of the sub-tenant or concessionaire. 

Sums paid as sales taxes and for merchandise returned should be excluded from "gross income." Sales on credit are generally included. 

Occasionally, the sale of "loss leaders," services rendered at nominal charge, such as repair or alterations of merchandise sold, and articles sold at small profit, will be excluded or fixed at a lower percentage. 

The amount of the percentage rent varies with the type of business.⁹ 

Percentage rent is customarily computed on an annual basis, but the landlord generally requires more frequent statements of sales, verified by the tenant, accompanied by payment of percentage rent. 

These are frequently quarterly or monthly but in some cases may be weekly or daily. The purpose of interim payments is for the landlord's security. His ultimate right thereto must await an annual accounting and adjustment. A highly responsible tenant, therefore, may be permitted to pay all percentage rent at the end of a lease year. At least once a year the tenant will be required to furnish a statement verified by a public accountant, often one specializing in the tenant's type of business. The tenant will be required to maintain books, at the premises or within the locality, in accordance with approved accounting practices, and to permit the landlord's inspection thereof in order to verify statements. Inspection should be limited in scope, to items relative to sales; and should be limited in time, to a reasonable period after delivery of statements, so that old records may be destroyed. The landlord will occasionally require a small store-tenant to deposit all proceeds in a cash register of a type that is sealed, automatically registers and totals all cash deposited, and is designed to prevent tampering. If sales cannot be reliably checked, the percentage lease is impractical.

Tenant's Covenant to Comply With Laws It is common to require a tenant, at his expense, to comply with requirements of law, ordinances, orders of public authority, and insurance requirements affecting the premises. Tenant's Breach and Forfeiture

If a lease permits the landlord to terminate by reason of a tenant's breach, the tenant should be entitled to notice and an opportunity to cure defaults and avoid forfeiture. Not all tenant's covenants are of equal importance. Some breaches are inadvertent. The treatment to be given them should vary accordingly. Payment of rent is vital to the landlord and the "grace period" therefor is usually comparatively short. Longer periods are apt to be given to cure other defaults, but it is sometimes impossible, when drafting a lease, to predict in advance the time reasonably necessary to cure some defaults where delay may be beyond control. For this reason, it is often provided that the landlord may terminate the lease for the tenant's breach of covenant other than payment of rent, within a specified period, with a proviso that the lease will not be terminated if the tenant shall commence promptly in good faith after receipt of notice to rectify the default and shall prosecute the same with diligence and continuity to completion. Termination of Lease and Survival of Tenant's Liability 

A lease may be terminated by a landlord pursuant to a conditional limitation. A lease may also be terminated by operation of law, as in the case of re-entry by the landlord. If a tenant abandons the premises, the landlord may suffer the premises to remain vacant and recover rent for the balance of the term.29 Some jurisdictions hold that if the landlord relets he is merely minimizing damages.3º Others, however, deem it a surrender of the lease by operation of law.31 

Termination of a lease prior to the original maturity, whether under a conditional limitation or by operation of law, ends all obligations of the tenant which would otherwise accrue from the date of the earlier termination.32 This release of the tenant may be avoided by a provision making the tenant's liability survive earlier termination of the lease. Alterations to Premises

A tenant has no implied authority to make alterations or improvements to the premises.40 If changes are to be made by the landlord or tenant, these should be detailed in the lease, preferably by annexing plans and specifications initialed by the parties. Parol agreements of this nature should be avoided because of the likelihood of their being held merged under the parol evidence rule.41 The obligation to perform should be not "during the term,"42 but by a specified time, with reasonable allowance for delays beyond control. If, as occasionally happens in preparing the premises for occupancy, the landlord and tenant are to make changes, it may be advisable to stipulate that both use labor with similar union affiliations. This may also apply to materials used. Extra insurance will probably be necessary during construction. 

The obligation to make changes in accordance with governmental regulations should be specified, as well as the obligation to procure requisite permits. If one party is to do the work, the other should be afforded opportunity to inspect the work and drawings during its progress and to make objections. It may be advisable to make provision for arbitration, or for a disinterested architect or engineer to pass on disputes.

Inasmuch as improvements made by the tenant, with the landlord's consent, may be the basis of mechanics' liens filed against the landlord's interest in the premises,43 the landlord should have protection against this possibility as well as the possibility that installations, which under the lease are to become the property of the landlord, will be affected by chattel mortgages or conditional bills of sale. Landlord's Right to Enter, Inspect, Repair and Exhibit Premises 

The landlord should have a right to enter the premises during the term. This is for inspection and to make repairs and changes the landlord has agreed or is otherwise required to make. It should also authorize the landlord to effect repairs and changes the tenant has agreed, but failed, to make, and charge the tenant with their cost. 

The landlord should also have the right to exhibit the premises to prospective purchasers and tenants. 

Reasonable limitations should be placed on the frequency and hours the landlord may enter for these purposes. If the entry is to perform a covenant on which the landlord claims the tenant is in default, the tenant should be entitled to reasonable notice and an opportunity to object or to perform himself before being charged with expense. The landlord's right to enter during an emergency, with a master key if necessary, should not be qualified. 

The right to exhibit the premises to prospective tenants should be limited to a period of appropriate length immediately prior to the termination of the lease. It should not continue beyond the tenant's effective election to renew.

The rights exercised under a right of entry may exceed the landlord's obligations under the lease. The clause should, therefore, state that it does not impose any obligations on the landlord or increase any obligations imposed on the landlord elsewhere in the lease. Mortgages

Some leases provide that they are subject to all existing and future mortgages affecting the premises. Inasmuch as the holder of a paramount mortgage may cut off a lease in foreclosure,53 the tenant under an important lease may well seek to limit the power of the owner to place mortgages ahead of the lease. The parties may provide then that the lease will be subordinate to any mortgages affecting the premises, the aggregate amounts of which do not exceed say, two-thirds of the value of the premises. For practical reasons, it is

Neither the landlord nor the tenant can subordinate a paramount mortgage to the lease, but the holder of a paramount mortgage may feel he is promoting his own security in inducing the making of a good lease by subordinating his mortgage thereto or agreeing not to cut it off in event of foreclosure. If in these circumstances he forecloses, he will take subject to the lease and will be bound by its provisions. The same is true of any successive mortgagee who is subordinate to the lease. These mortgagees may well be concerned with the possibility that they may be bound by advance rent payments made to the landlord or onerous provisions of the lease. Even if the lease is satisfactory in form, it is not clear to what extent subsequent modifications, made between landlord and tenant, bind subordinate mortgagees. A subordinate mortgagee will therefore want an undertaking by the landlord and tenant precluding any serious modification of the lease or substantial payment of advance rent without his consent. Some mortgages contain provisions extending their lien broadly over existing and after-acquired fixtures and personal property attached to or used in connection with the mortgaged premises. It is doubtful if such mortgages would be construed to cover any property of a tenant,54 but it may be advisable for a tenant who subordinates his lease to mortgages to provide that no property owned or removable by him shall be subject to the lien of paramount mortgages.

The rule, unfortunately one not well known, is that the rent does not abate and the tenant must pay full rent for the balance of the term. 

The tenant is compensated, however, by a claim against the award for the present value of the excess rent he is obligated to pay the landlord after the condemnation. A startling illustration of the operation of this rule appears in City of Pasadena v. Porter69 where this claim of the tenant absorbed about half the award for the fee. The landlord sought, unsuccessfully, to reduce the rent and obtain the award for his interest in the premises. The decision gave the landlord half the award for the fee, gave the tenant the other half as a lump sum compensation for his future liability for excess rent, and left the landlord with a contract claim for rent in future installments subject to the risks of uncollectibility.

Avoidance of Waiver by the Landlord

Landlords generally do not enforce the strict and prompt compliance of all the tenant's obligations included in leases currently in use. 

Some of these are too trivial. Sometimes because of amicable relations between them, the parties tend to disregard others. Frequently the landlord's agent, who may be in actual charge of the premises, waives rights of the landlord. Any of these may develop a course of dealing between the parties that will estop a landlord from the prompt use of remedies reserved in the lease for breaches by the tenant. Sometimes a tenant, after making a complaint to his landlord which may or may not be well grounded, tosses the keys to the janitor and moves out, with the thought that the end of possession is the end of his liability. The landlord's acceptance of the keys without more does not imply an acceptance of a surrender, particularly where the landlord clearly manifests his determination to hold the tenant.138 But retention of the keys and other circumstances may create a factual issue respecting an acceptance of a surrender by the landlord139 or his agent.140 For these reasons, landlords frequently include clauses providing that the receipt of rent with knowledge of a default by the tenant will not condone the default; that waiver of one breach is not to be a waiver of subsequent breaches; that neither acceptance of keys by the landlord or his agents, nor any similar acts, are to constitute an acceptance of a surrender, such acceptance to be only by an agreement in writing signed by the landlord. A provision will be upheld making the acceptance of rent, with knowledge of a breach, no waiver of the breach.141 But a provision requiring a surrender to be in writing can be waived.142

NEGOTIATION OF A LEASE ON BEHALF OF A TENANT 

The foregoing discussion presumes a certain amount of give and take in the negotiation and drafting of a lease, and presupposes some bargaining power on both sides. This assumption may be contrary to fact in some cases. In a landlords' market the negotiation of a lease often is limited to an agreement as to space demised, rent, term, and use of premises. The tenant may find it necessary to sign the lease drawn by the landlord, with little, if any, negotiation of the details and legal provisions.

Leases are generally prepared on behalf of the landlord. In the case of a chain store or other substantial tenant whose business requires many leases, the tenant's counsel may prepare the lease. An important tenant generally can succeed in obtaining changes in the lease of the character heretofore discussed. 

A small tenant leasing a store, loft, or small factory generally will employ counsel to look over a lease prepared for the landlord, to ascertain that the terms agreed on have been carried out, and to determine if the lease, as a whole, is satisfactory to the tenant. This type of tenant cannot prudently accede to everything that may be incorporated in a lease prepared for the landlord. But if the tenant's attorney prepares too extended a memorandum of changes in an endeavor to make the lease entirely acceptable from the tenant's point of view, the following is apt to happen. The landlord may reject the suggestions in toto, question the tenant's good faith in the negotiations and request a prompt decision if the lease is to be accepted or rejected. The tenant, smarting under the rebuff, will relay these complaints to his attorney with interest, explain that he wants and needs the premises and, possibly, that unless the matter is expedited a competitor may obtain the premises. He then asks why all the changes are necessary. His lawyer discusses in detail the intricate possibilities in condemnation and begins to explain the proposed changes in the fire clause; but the client interrupts to inform him flatly that there is no possibility of condemnation in the neighborhood in question, and that he, the client, is willing to take his chances on the other possibilities mentioned. 

In these circumstances the tenant's lawyer must realize that he cannot obtain a lease which, from the tenant's point of view, will
