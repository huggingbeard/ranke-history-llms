FOREWORD 

This material has been used in mimeographed form by several hundred students. It has been revised and published in this form to facilitate its use by other groups. It is doubtful if the lessons have attained their ultimate form in the present edition. It is hoped that both teachers and students who use this book will offer any criticisms and suggestions which occur to them.

The preparation of this material represents a viewpoint at once narrow and broad. Narrow, in that the purpose is not to teach students to speak, to read, nor to write, not even to teach them vocabulary and idioms, but simply to present in a teachable way the more essential facts of grammar, to help in giving students the few tools most necessary for the building of any language capability, whatever the type. Broad, in that no one method of attack is set forth even for the acquisition of these tools and certainly none for their use. 

The material must be adapted to whatever method a given situation demands.

A conscious effort has been made to develop an understanding of the principles underlying certain troublesome distinctions in the language, such as those between subjunctive and indicative moods, preterite and imperfect tenses, and ser and estar. Thus, considerable comment accompanies the presentation of some constructions, which does not, however, preclude the occasional addition of a convenient rule-of-thumb which has proved effective. Such rules have been found more satisfying when thus built upon an understanding of the principles involved.

Altho the aim has been to give some knowledge of the language structure and to provide working tools rather than such construction material as vocabulary and idioms, it is of course inevitable that the acquiring of some vocabulary be incidental to the attainment of the first aim. This vocabulary is selected from the most useful words, the Buchanan list serving as the criterion. No apology is made for the exclusion of any word, however common, which did not seem essential in the preparation of illustrative and drill material. 

There has been no attempt to present all of the words of even the first three hundred in frequency from the 

Buchanan list, nor to confine those used to so small a iii

A very few words of low frequency in the Buchanan list, such as classroom expressions or terms referring to sports, have been included. However, over 95% of the words are from the first 2,000 in frequency and over 87% fall within the first 1,000. The attempt has been to keep the total number of words used for drill material small enough that continual repetition of those words is inevitable. Thus while learning the elemental facts of grammar the student may attain, as a by-product of the process, the mastery of a small number of common words which will serve as a nucleus for the development of Vocabulary through other types of activity. 

No one type of exercise is given for every lesson except the review exercise at the close of each lesson, an exercise which, like most of the others, may be adapted to many uses. In most of the exercises there is a carefully planned repetition of previously learned constructions as well as vocabulary. In the review exercises this system of recall is especially thorough. Some teachers may be surprised at the omission of the usual paragraph of Spanish preceding other exercises in each lesson. It is not intended that this book should form a complete first semester course. It must of course be supplemented by reading material chosen to fit the abilities and aims of the students using it. In the interests of brevity alone, it has been felt that the omission of all reading material not definitely illustrative is thoroughly justified.

While the text has been prepared and tested as an introduction to the language, it might be adapted to use as a synthesis and organization of language facts after an approach through reading. Some have thought that it might prove a practical medium for rapid grammar review. iv

Vowels. Each single vowel is a pure vowel sound, not a diphthong as it frequently is in English. Instead of sliding off into another sound at the end, it is held on the same value until it is cut off. 

This may be accomplished by being careful to keep the vocal organs in the same position while pronouncing a given vowel sound. Note particularly the jaw movements and do not allow the jaw to move when sounding a single vowel. Of course the language does have true diphthongs, the proper pronunciation of which requires just the reverse procedure.

Accentuation. Spanish words ending in a vowel or n or s stress the next to the last syllable.

Spanish words ending in a consonant other than n or s stress the last syllable.

Any word which is not stressed according to rule bears a written accent upon the vowel stressed. Such accent is a part of the spelling of the word and can properly be omitted only when the letter stressed is a capital.

Syllabification. 

A single consonant between vowels is pronounced with the following vowel.

Most consonants followed by 1 or r form a combination naturally pronounced together and therefore are both pronounced with the following vowel, just as a single consonant would be. However, the letters rl, ir, sl, tl, and sr are not pronounced in the same syllable.

Except the combinations naturally pronounced together, the two consonants are divided. 

When more than two consonants come together usually only the last consonant or an indivisible pair of consonants is pronounced with the following vowel. Subject Pronouns. Except for emphasis or to prevent ambiguity, subject pronouns are not expressed. 

However, usted and ustedes, you, are more often expressed than omitted, for courtesy as well as for clearness. 

Usted is not truly a pronoun, although it masquerades as such, for it is an outgrowth of an old expression, vuestra merced, which, like "Your Honor" of court usage naturally requires the third person of the verb and of all other inflectional forms, such as the possessive adjectives. "I think that Your Honor is right in his conclusion". Hence usted and ustedes always govern a third person verb, adjective or pronoun.

Usted is commonly abbreviated V., Vd., or Ud.,with the corresponding plurals VV., Vds., Uds.

The true second person, tú and vosotros, with their corresponding forms in verbs, adjectives and pronouns, are reserved for use among intimate friends and relatives, or in addressing children or servants.

Interrogative Form. If an interrogative word is used it always introduces the question, as in English. 

Otherwise the interrogative sentence is for med merely by inverting the subject and verb as in the English: 

You have. Have you? dows? ¿Quién levanta las ventanas? Who raises the win- ¿Estudia usted las lecciones? Do you study the lessons? ¿Qué estudia usted? What are you studying?

Ordinary statements and questions are expressed in the indicative.

These two general statements indicate the principle underlying the various uses of the two moods. They should serve to synthesize the several distinctions which are drawn to assist in the choice of mood.

RADICAL CHANGING VERBS. 

Some verbs, otherwise regular, undergo certain changes in the stem, similar to the changes of such irregular verbs as poder or querer, where a single vowel is converted into a diphthong when stressed. These are known as radical changing verbs because the radical, or root word, is changed. 

They fall into three classes.

Class I verbs change o to ue, or e to le, whenever the stress falls upon that syllable. The syllable affected is always the last syllable of the stem. The verbs of Class I may be either first or second conju gation verbs. One verb, jugar, changes the u of its root to ue under stress. 

Class II verbs also change o to ue, or e to ie, whenever the stress falls upon that syllable. In addition, they change o to u,or e to i, whenever the stress falls upon an ending beginning with a, ie, or ió.

In Spanish the reflexive pronoun with the active voice is commonly used where in English the passive voice would be used. The passive voice, in Spanish as in English, consists of the verb to be followed by the past participle.

PERO, MAS AND SINO. Pero and mas are really synonymous but mas is less frequently used. 

Sino is used after a negative to state an opposing affirmative, which is usually dependent upon or governing the same verb.

Sino is also used to contrast two predicates having the same subject. If the sentence is very short, containing little more than two verbs, sino alone is usual, otherwise sino que is used before an inflected verb. 

No cantaba sino gritaba. 

He was not singing but shouting.

No trabajaban sin ruido sino que charlaban y reían alegremente.

They did not work quietly but chatted and laughed merrily.

Sino is also used in the same way as the English prepositional but, meaning except. 

Todos fueron sino Carlos. 

All went but Charles.
