CHAPTER I INTRODUCTION 

In this last six-month period, the demands of small business firms for assistance from the Small Business Administration have increased sharply and our activities, which have risen in response to these demands, have registered notable accomplishments. The various programs of the Agency have gained momentum. After one year of existence, a solid foundation has been laid for this first Government agency created to serve and represent all small business, both in peacetime and in periods of national emergency.

Congress has placed upon the Small Business Administration a diverse number of responsibilities within these three areas: 

1. Helping small business gain access to adequate capital and credit, 2. Helping small business to obtain a fair share of 

Government procurement, and 3. Helping small business to obtain competent management, technical, and production counsel. 

In volume and in intensity of demand, the major call upon our time and resources has been in the financial area. The scope of our lending activities widened substantially during the first half of this year. During this period, the Small Business Adminis tration made 551 business loans amounting to $31,000,562. Thus a large number of small business concerns which are vital for our defense needs or for the essential civilian economy have been preserved by being given credit which otherwise would have been denied them.

The backlog of applications for loans is still large but the Agency's procedures are constantly being streamlined to cut down the time for processing loans without, at the same time, failing to fully protect the interests of the Government. 

The delegation of authority to approve business loans up to a maximum of $50,000 under certain conditions, granted to the 

Small Business Administration's Regional Directors on July 6, 1954, also will aid in expediting loans to worthy small business concerns.

833690

It is gratifying to note that an increasing number of firms that call on this Administration for financial counseling ultimately are enabled, through the help of the Agency, to secure their financing from a private source. Frequently, the financing is made available by banks that originally declined to make loans to the firms. Another matter of satisfaction is the extent to which bank participation loans have grown, and, perhaps, even more worthy of note, the degree to which these bank participation loans are of the deferred type. The likelihood is that a good share of the funds obligated to this type of loan may never be drawn from the Federal treasury.

We have continued to press our campaign to acquaint the Nation's banks with our program of cooperation with private lending institutions.

In every manner possible, we have endeavored to make clear to them that our lending facilities are designed to supplement those of private lending sources, not compete with them. The fact that we stand ready at any time to sell any of our loans, once they are "seasoned", underlines our expressed lending philosophy.

We have sought diligently for a means of providing small firms with greater access to equity capital on more reasonable terms than is now generally available. There are many problems involved in reducing the cost of issuing securities of small companies, and we are pleased to report that the Investment Bankers Association of

America is joining with this Agency in its efforts to overcome these problems.

There has been extensive need for assistance to present and former small Government contractors, as the total volume of defense and civilian agency buying decreased sharply. Notwithstanding the smaller total, small business received a larger percentage share of total defense procurement in the second half of the fiscal year than it did during the previous year. 

A highly important factor in obtaining defense contracts for small firms has been the joint determination program of the 

Small Business Administration and the Department of Defense. This program is expected to become still more effective in the future as the result of final implementation by the Department of Defense and its constituent Services of 10 proposals of this Agency designed to increase the volume of military prime contracts for small firms. 

Full benefits from this program, however, must wait on adequate staffing of procurement centers. 

The varied scope of services rendered by our procurement and industrial specialists are discussed in appropriate chapters of this report. The services run the gamut of all types of technical and production assistance, including the handling of thousands of requests

from prime contractors for subcontractors, and the referral in turn, of many thousands of subcontractors to prime contractors. 

No prime contracts have been taken by the Small Business Administration although a few taken by the Small Defense Plants Administration are still being administered by this Agency. 

The prime contract authority is not necessary at this time since financing of small defense businesses which could be accomplished under the prime contract revolving fund of the Small Defense Plants 

Administration can now be accomplished under the lending authority of the Small Business Administration. Even though under present conditions the use of the prime contract authority is not essential, it is our considered opinion that it should be retained in the law for use in a possible emergency.

For some small firms, the most serious problem is not one of obtaining financing or contracts, but is an urgent need to acquire greater management and technical skill. While a considerable degree of industrial and production assistance is rendered daily to small firms through the "know-how" of our field staff, a great amount of management and technical assistance also is furnished through staff specialists in Washington. In addition, the Agency issues a wide range of publications in the management aid field. 

Through reprinting by numerous trade association organs and other media, these Small Business Administration publications gain wide coverage. A cooperative publishing program with the Department of 

Commerce has widened the effectiveness of these publications at a considerable savings.

An education program of great potential worth to operators of small firms was given a trial run during the spring of 1954 in cooperation with two educational institutions. In this program, the 

Agency assists educational institutions in setting up management courses, but does not offer any courses of its own, or participate in any phase of instruction. Pilot courses were conducted in the spring by the University of Wisconsin, at Milwaukee, and by the University of Richmond, at Richmond, Virginia. Both courses were completed in May and were highly successful. It is the purpose of the Agency to co-sponsor some 20 such courses this coming fall. 

The Small Business Administration seeks to keep its operations and programs closely geared to the needs of small business firms. To this end, the Agency has drawn together a representative group of businessmen, bankers, attorneys and other members of the small business community to act as its guides and counsellors at both the regional and national levels. These groups are the National 

Board of Field Advisors and the National Council of Consultants. 

Meetings of the groups have been held recently in Washington and over the entire country. It was, for example, upon the recommendation of the National Council of Consultants that the Regional Directors

were recently given authority to approve business loans up to a maximum of $50,000. Other significant recommendations are expected to be forthcoming from these groups. 

The entire purpose of our staffing program in Washington has been to develop an administrative and professional group of high competence and versatile talents capable of turning with ready facility to the diverse problems that come daily to an agency of this nature. Because of this flexible operation, this has permitted a smaller staff of experts than might otherwise be required. We have tried to effect maximum use of personnel by centralizing such functions as personnel, accounting, etc., which in larger agencies of Government are disbursed in field offices. To the fullest extent possible, we have endeavored to carry over this same principle to the staffing of our field offices. However, there are manifest limits to this possibility. When the volume of work made it essential to concentrate a greater part of our resources upon the lending program, it was necessary that our resources be realigned and that the scope of our non-lending activities be curtailed. It has thus become necessary to forego the services of some of our industrial and production specialists whose training and skills were not readily adaptable to our financial operations. 

Although the Small Business Administration was set up by Congress expressly to cope with small business problems, the area of small business is not its exclusive domain. There are other 

Government agencies which, in the course of their work, must consider the impact of their policies on small business and which influence the economic climate in which small as well as large business must live. Tax policies proposed and administered by the Treasury 

Department, for example, may have a life or death effect on small business. The Federal Trade Commission has set up a small business division and the Anti-Trust Division of the Department of Justice has as a keynote of its program the preservation of small business enterprise. The Department of Commerce offers many services to the business community generally. 

As fully described in Chapter III, Economic Position of 

Small Business, the tide has been running steadily against small business since the end of World War II. By any of several tests that may be taken -- growth of sales, profits, value added, and working capital the gap between large and small business shows an ever widening margin. The basic and underlying difficulties arise out of financial, technological and economic trends which have developed since the war. 

There is no easy solution to the small business problem. but the Eisenhower Administration is taking definite steps toward overcoming it. The establishment of the Small Business Administration was a significant move in this direction. The tax laws have just been overhauled with the specific purpose in mind of giving needed tax relief to small business. The Attorney General's National

Committee to Study the Anti-trust Laws, of which the Administrator of the Small Business Administration is a member, has been created, and undoubtedly protection for small business will be a determining consideration in its recommendations. 

As the advocate of small business in the National Administration, the Small Business Administration is establishing working arrangements with all Government agencies concerned with small business problems. Within a short time, formal arrangements will be established with the Federal Trade Commission, Securities and 

Exchange Commission, General Accounting Office, General Services 

Administration, the Department of Labor and other Federal Government agencies. Such agreements are already in effect with the 

Department of Commerce, the Atomic Energy Commission and the Department of Defense. In this way, the energies of the various departments of the Government will be harnessed to produce the maximum benefits for small business, and the problems of small business will have increasingly effective attention and study from the executive departments.

CHAPTER II DEFINITION OF SMALL BUSINESS In January 1954, the Small Business Administration formulated tentative size standards for defining small business. 

During the past six months of operation, the Agency has tested a more exact definition of small business which recognizes the wide variation in the size structure of manufacturing industries and other types of business activities. The Agency has found that it is feasible, through the use of a definition embodying variable size standards by kinds of business activity, to channel assistance to those "small-business" concerns which are entitled to receive the benefits of its programs, and to exclude larger concerns which Congress did not intend to share in the programs. On the basis of this experience, some modifications have been made in the tentative size standards.

The Question of Affiliation Entirely apart from the question of what should be the specific size standard for a given manufacturing industry or other kind of business activity, there is a problem of determining that a business concern is or is not independently owned and operated. In the Small Business Act of 1953, Congress provided that "a smallbusiness concern shall be deemed to be one which is independently owned and operated and which is not dominant in its field of operation". Therefore, every size determination must be based not only on the size of the firm under consideration but also upon the size of any other affiliated business. For the purpose of making a size determination, the concern under consideration and all of its affiliates must be considered as one unit.

A presumption of affiliation is raised when a significant portion of the concern under consideration is directly or indirectly held by an individual (or individuals) or an associated group (family, holding company or other business entity, etc.) which also holds a similar interest in another concern. Common ownership is the most obvious factor to be considered.

When those who exercise or who may exercise significant control over the concern under consideration can also exercise similar control over another concern, here again the concerns may be said to be affiliated. For example, control may arise through interlocking management, such as common directors or common officers. Where the individuals or associated groups having significant ownership or control of concerns have actual or tacit agreements relating to the operation of the concerns, it may also be said that affiliation exists. Such agreements may include use of common facilities, selling agreements, purchasing, methods of doing business, or other activities.

When any of these conditions--common ownership, common control or common operation by agreement-- are found to exist, the presumption is that the concerns involved are so closely related they must be considered as one entity in determining if they are a small business. The burden of proof in rebutting this presumption is then shifted to the business concern in question. It must introduce evidence to show that the existence of these conditions is not significant and that they are not substantial enough to warrant a finding of affiliation.

As an example of a case in which a finding of, affiliation would not be warranted, an individual may be a director of several corporations but all other evidence may indicate that the corporations are completely unrelated to each other. 

Additional experience of Small Business Administration operating officials should make it possible to define more precisely the degree of affiliation which precludes a concern from being regarded as independently owned and operated. 

Standards for Procurement Purposes 

There has been no change in the definition of small business used by the Small Business Administration in its procurement assistance programs and in cases involving a loan to a concern which has been granted a certificate of competency that is, a certification that the company is competent, with respect to capacity and credit, to perform a specific Government contract. In these cases, any concern, including affiliates, which employs fewer than 500 persons at the time of the submission of the bid is classified as

small. The only exception to this general rule is that in the case of purchases which have been set aside for small business under the joint determination program, average employment may be taken into consideration in specified seasonal industries. An example would be the food canning and preserving industry. 

Standards for Loans and Other Non-Procurement Purposes

As in the case of the wholesale trades, the size standards for the retail and service trades have now been made less restrictive. 

Any retail concern is classified as small if its annual sales are $1, 000, 000 or less. For retailers of general merchandise (including department stores and variety stores), or new and used motor vehicles, or groceries with fresh meats, the standard is $2, 000, 000 or less. 

Likewise, any service trades concern is classified as small if its annual receipts are $1, 000, 000 or less, except that any hotel or power laundry is classified as small if its annual receipts are $2, 000, 000 or less.

Size standards for other kinds of business activities will be established as the need arises. In such cases, the standards will be developed and size determinations made on the basis of information submitted by the applicant and other pertinent data for the particular kind of business activity.

Procedures 

In most instances, the initial responsibility for determining whether applicants for assistance are small businesses lies with the Directors of the regional offices. In case of doubt, or where special problems exist, the Regional Director refers the case to Washington for determination by the head of the operating office whose function is involved in the matter. The applicant can make a final appeal in writing to the Administrator, who acts upon the basis of appropriate recommendations by his Size Standards Committee. 

Although much progress has been made in developing a detailed definition of small business, it is recognized that there may have to be exceptions in special cases because of the unique product line in which the applicant is engaged or the geographical region in which it operates. The Administrator can, in the appropriate instances, modify the size standards to fit these cases. The Agency frankly recognizes that no definition can eliminate possible inequities in view of the great diversification and complexity of our industrial and distribution structure.

CHAPTER III ECONOMIC POSITION OF SMALL BUSINESS The 

The economic outlook for the immediate future for small and large business alike is definitely more favorable than it was six months ago.

The threat of recession which was the subject of so much discussion in the early months of 1954 has now faded away. preliminary estimate of the gross national product for the second quarter of 1954 was $356 billion, about the same as the first quarter rate, bringing to an end the slight contraction of 4 percent since the second quarter of 1953. Personal consumption expenditures during the second quarter are estimated as above the first quarter rate, bolstered during the end of the quarter by heavier retail sales, particularly automobiles. Final figures will probably show an all-time high. New private construction in the second quarter increased from an annual rate of $25.5 billion last year to $27.0 billion. It is now estimated that both total and private new construction for 1954 will be a record figure, exceeding 1953, in contrast to previous predictions of a decline. It is thus evident that our business expansion, which had been momentarily interrupted, will now be resumed. Small business should share in the increase of the gross national product to $440 billion within the next five years, recently predicted by Dr. Gabriel

Hauge, Economic Adviser to President Eisenhower. 

THE BUSINESS POPULATION 

The number of businesses today remains at approximately 4, 200, 000, of which more than 4, 000, 000 can be classified as small. The rate of growth of the business population has slowed down since 1951. However, since the end of World War II, the number of firms has increased by more than 1, 000, 000, or over one-third. 

Many will be surprised to see how prevalent the very small firm is in American business:

About three-fourths of all business firms have fewer than four paid employees.

In manufacturing, more than two-fifths of all firms have fewer than four employees. 

In retail trade, the services, and finance, insurance and real estate, about four-fifths of all firms have less than four employees. 

Firms with 1, 000 or more employees represent less than 1 percent of all firms in each industry division. However, the individual large firm is far more important than the individual small firm in assets, fixed invest ments, employment, sales and income.

WHAT IS HAPPENING TO SMALL BUSINESS? 

During recent months, it has become apparent to even casual readers of financial statements that small business in important industries is not faring well. The merger movement which is active today is due in part to steady deterioration of the financial position of small independent firms. Mergers in the textile industry, for example, have been promoted by inability of small companies to show a profit. In earnings statements from many industries, a clear divergence can be drawn between large and small firms; the former show good profits at reduced capacity, the profits of the latter have been deeply slashed.

This disadvantage of small business which is now becoming more apparent has its roots in the economic and financial environment of the war and the post-war period. Inflation, tax laws, Governmental policies designed to promote expansion, labor union policies and consumer preferences are among the factors which have played a part in shaping the trend. 

For example, large firms expanded at a far greater rate than small firms did during the war period. Certificates of necessity for tax amortization issued by the Government and availability of funds in larger businesses helped this expansion movement. In the immediate post-war period, small firms made out well because demand was strong and they did not have to cope with the tremendous reconversion problems facing large business. However, when those reconversion problems were surmounted, larger businesses were able to edge small firms out of markets in which the demand was weaker. The Korean period saw the same version of industry expansion repeated.

Another factor is that inflation has raised tremendously the amount of money required to do business. Distribution and research costs today are so heavy that small firms have difficulty in keeping up with the race. Further, the diversification movement in American industry has become epidemic. Earnings on Stockholders Equity 

There has been a drop in return on stockholders' equity since 1947, but the drop has been far greater in the case of small firms than for large ones. 

For the smaller corporations, earnings before taxes on stockholders' equity fell from 27. 8 percent in 1947 to 14.7 percent in 1953. Stockholders' equity is composed of the amounts of capital stock outstanding, earned surplus or undivided profits, surplus reserves, and that portion of other reserves which is considered as surplus. The drop in return on stockholders' equity may thus result not only from a drop in earnings but also from an increase in the components which make up stockholders' equity.

Chart 8 shows changes in the share of total production represented by changes in value added by manufacture. The concept of value added is the difference between the total value of shipments made by the manufacturer less the cost of raw materials and containers, fuel, supplies, purchased electric energy and contract work. 

For the purposes of this chart, small plants are considered to be those having fewer than 250 employees.

This chart shows that, for small concerns, there was a drop of 5 percent in the percentage of total value added between 1947 and 1952, a decrease from 40 percent of total production in 1947 to 35 percent in 1952. This occurred despite the fact that the number of establishments with less than 250 employees increased from 240, 881 in 1947 to about 267, 000 in 1952.

In the case of plants primarily engaged in the manufacture of textile mill products; stone, clay and glass products; fabricated metal products, lumber, and rubber products, plants with fewer than 250 employees did maintain their position in their

respective industries. On the other hand, there was a decrease in the share of the total market for the plants of this size in all other major industry groups. 

The decrease in the small business share was greatest for plastics, chemicals, leather, paper, instruments and apparel.

The relative position of the smaller manufacturing establishments was impaired in some of the manufacturing industries which expanded most rapidly after the beginning of Korea hostilities. 

Although there was some decline in economic activity during 1949, the period from 1947 through 1952 was one of expanding economic activity. This is particularly true in the case of manufacturers engaged in the production of chemicals, transportation equipment, instruments and related products, machinery and primary metals. In these industries, the smaller manufacturers were not able to maintain their relative positions. It is impossible to say how much of this loss of economic position was due to the increased utilization of existing capacity and how much was due to the actual increase in capacities of larger plants owned by larger concerns. It would seem though that the changes were due relatively more to plant expansion than to the former factor.

The more rapid growth of larger manufacturing plants can be looked at from another statistical aspect. After making an adjustment for price increases, we have estimated that for the period 1947 through 1952, the total output of the larger plants employing over 2, 500 employees increased 57 percent, compared with an increase of only 8 percent for those employing under 250 employees.

Tax Treatment of Surplus Accumulations--Small, closely-held businesses have long been apprehensive over the application of the Section 102 penalty tax on earnings accumulations. The tax revision provides that the burden of proof in regard to the unreasonable earnings accumulations be shifted to the Government. It eliminates the so-called "'immediacy test", which in the past has required an immediate need or use of the funds in order to justify the retention of earnings. To insure a margin for growth, it exempts the first $60, 000 of earnings accumulations from the penalty tax. 

In contrast with the old law, which applies the penalty tax to the entire retained earnings of a business even though only a fraction is deemed excessive, the bill limits the tax on excessive earnings accumulations to the excess portion.

Research and Experimental Expenditures--Research and experimental expenditures are essential to the success of many small businesses. For the first time, the bill provides a small business lacking a regular research and experimental budget a clear right either to deduct its research outlays as current expenses or to amortize them over a five-year period.

Loss Carryovers--Small business has a keen interest in the tax treatment of spotty and irregular earnings. The bill increases the net loss carryback provision to two years from the one year presently allowed. It continues the five-year carryforward. 

The additional year of carryback not only gives greater assurance of offset of losses, but also speeds up tax relief and makes it available when business is in the greatest need of money. 

5. Relief from Double Taxation of Dividends -- The relief from double taxation of dividends will make a direct contribution to the healthy growth of small business. The present system taxes corporate profits twice--once as earned by the corporation and again when received by the

6. 7. 8. 9. investor as dividends. 

This double taxation has reduced the incentive to invest, particularly in small new enterprises, and restricted the marketability of new equity shares.

Optional Tax Treatment for Certain Partnerships-- 

The bill extends to certain partnerships, in which capital is a material income-producing factor, the option to be taxed like corporations, including the right to plough back earnings into the business subject to the flat corporate tax rate.

Partnership Provisions-- The present tax treat ment of partnership transactions has been inconsistent and confused. The new tax bill provides clear, flexible and equitable rules for handling partnership transactions. This will be of great assistance to many small-business owners in planning their partnership transactions. To 

Changes in the Capital Structure of a Business- 

For years the rules governing the tax treatment of recapitalization and partial liquidation of corporate businesses have been highly technical, rigorous and uncertain. Many business owners have preferred to sell out to a large corporation rather than run the gauntlet of the tax laws. deal with this problem, the bill provides greater certainty and flexibility in the treatment of capital structure changes. Specifically, it permits the issuance of preferred stock dividends to holders of common stock without subjecting such distributions to income tax. Other types of recapitalization which would achieve similar results would also be permitted tax free. These provisions will remove tax barriers to needed financial rearrangements to facilitate new financing or bring in new management. It should remove some of the tax pressures which have encouraged the sale of small companies to larger competitors. 

Redemption of Stock to Pay Death Taxes -- The bill also broadens the present provisions which permit

the tax-free redemption of stock in a corporation to pay estate taxes. The purpose of this provision is to avoid the forced sale or liquidation of a business in order to pay Federal estate taxes. 

These liberalizing provisions help remove a serious hardship on a family whose principal asset consists of stock in small corporations, which might otherwise have to be sold or liquidated to pay death taxes.

The new tax law requires a speed up in tax payments. Under the present Mills Plan, corporations will have to pay half of their taxes on their 1954 income in March of 1955 and the other half in June of 1955. 

Beginning 1955, under a new provision, corporations will have to start estimating the current year's income and paying some of the tax in the year in which it is earned. Small corporations would be hard hit by this necessity to make payments for the current as well as the previous year's taxes. Therefore, the new tax law limits its provisions to corporations which estimate that they will owe more than $100,000 of taxes.

Financing for Small Firms 

The amount of credit available for business has been expanding. On top of already large bank reserves, on June 21 the Federal 

Reserve Board announced that it was reducing the legal reserve requirements of the member banks of the Federal Reserve System in a gradual way over six weeks, June 16 to August 1. The Board stated "The reduction will release a total of approximately $1, 555, 000, 000 of reserves. 

It was made in anticipation of estimated demands on bank reserves in summer and fall, taking account of public financing requirements, including the marketing of crops and replenishment of retail stocks in advance of fall and Christmas sales, as well as the Treasury's financing needs".

This cut in reserve requirements may result in creating an extra $9, 000, 000, 000 in new credit. On the other side of the ledger, however, demand for credit has been on the decline. 

July 21, loans of all Federal Reserve member banks dropped $1, 822, 000, 000 since January 1, compared with a drop of $719, 000, 000 in the corresponding period last year. In the New York Federal Reserve District, loans dropped $1, 198, 000, 000, compared with a drop of $421, 000, 000 in the corresponding period last year.

Lenders have been making concessions to would-be borrowers and interest rates have declined. There has been a cut in the prime rate, which has been reduced from 3 1/4 percent to 3 percent by New York banks without appreciably increasing the demand for credit. The drop in credit demand is attributable to the minor contraction in business earlier in the year, the continued slowness of inventory accumulation, and the reduction in Government procurement which has reduced the need for financing. Many borrowers have financed themselves through short-term commercial paper at 2 percent and under instead of securing a bank loan. 

Slackening of credit demand has not been general. Loans in Houston, Boston and Seattle have been running above fast year's figures, sometimes by sizeable amounts. Further, a seasonal pickup in bank loans occurs during the latter half of the year. 

This can be expected this year as business activity increases and inventory re-accumulation begins. 

In many cases, the drop in credit extended reflects not a drop in applications but caution on the part of lenders in extending credit. When alarms are sounded about business conditions, as they were during the early months of this year, even though ill-founded, there is a disposition on the part of some lenders to become cautious. 

A drop in interest rates, instead of increasing loans by spurring demands for credit, may have the opposite effect of restricting credit by making risks costlier in the eyes of lenders. These factors may have operated to some extent during the early months of 1954. 

The statutory standard under which the Small Business Administration operates requires that all of the Agency's loans be "of such sound value or so secured as reasonably to assure repayment". The Small Business Administration cannot relax its credit requirements at a time of economic decline, nor for particular applications coming from labor distress areas. The Agency loan fund, however, acts as an automatic economic regulator for credit availability, since in any period of economic contraction, a greater number of loan applications which have been turned down by private banking institutions will come to the Small Business Administration. 

On the other hand, in a period of expanding private credit, the reverse influence is expected to operate.

Equity Gapital for Small Business 

Virtually every study of the financing of small business has demonstrated the difficulties encountered by both new and

established small-business firms in acquiring capital through the securities market. Many concerns have sold stock to secure permanent capital, but the securities were purchased in most instances by the owners or their friends or relatives rather than through wide public distribution. Many small-business owners are reluctant to use equity capital, since they do not want to dilute the control or earnings of the business.

However, where small firms are interested in such a prospect, they are deterred by the high cost of flotation. The Securities and Exchange Commission, in a study of the cost of flotation by listed corporations for the period 1945-1947, found that the cost was approximately 22 percent of proceeds for flotations smaller than $500,000, 15 percent between $500, 000 and $1, 000, 000, 11.5 percent between $1, 000, 000 and $2, 000, 000, and then on a steadily decending scale until it reaches 1. 15 percent for flotations over $50, 000, 000. 

A study of costs of flotation for small issues of less than $500,000 between 1945-1949 indicated an average cost of 19. 13 percent. 

Registration costs are only a minor percentage of the total compensation costs. Compensation to underwriters is by far a greater element of cost than registration costs. In an average cost of flotation of 19. 13 percent between 1945-1949, compensation to underwriters amounted to 16. 56 percent, and other expenses amounted to only 2.57 percent. It should be noted that as the major part of these costs are for compensation to salesmen, they seem inescapable. 

A bill to exempt offerings of $500,000 or less from registration requirements of the Securities and Exchange Commission was not passed by Congress at this session even though it was approved by the Senate. Proponents of the change describe the present $300, 000 limit as onerous, since it does not take into consideration the change in the value of the dollar since the limit was set in 1945. 

The Small Business Administration has established a cooperative program with the Investment Bankers Association of America to assist small business in obtaining equity capital. Mr. W. Yost 

Fulton of Cleveland, Ohio, has been appointed chairman of the Association's Small Business Committee, which will study with this Agency ways and means of cutting down registration and flotation costs. 

The IBA also will suggest experts in the investment banking field who will prepare Management Aids on evaluating the need for equity capital in a small-business concern and methods of acuqiring such capital. The IBA also will furnish lists of investment banking sources

which may be willing to finance small firms when the need and eligibility for equity capital expansion is apparent.

CHAPTER IV FINANCIAL ASSISTANCE 

During the period February 1, 1954, to July 31, 1954, there was a marked increase in the activities of the Small Business Administration in the field of financial assistance to small business. 

This applies not only to loans that were made but also to other aids, such as advising small firms on financial problems and helping them to obtain financing through private lending sources, thus minimizing the need for Govern ment credit. The number of loans made under the disaster loan program also increased. 

During this six-month period, the Small Business Administration made 551 business loans in a gross amount of $31, 000, 562 to assist small firms. It made 124 disaster loans amounting to $933, 902. 

This compares with 55 business loan approvals amounting to $3,755,900, and 72 disaster loan approvals amounting to $227,682, reported for the period ending January 31, 1954. It will be recalled that our lending activities did not start until after September 28, 1953, when the lending authority of Reconstruction Finance Corporation expired. 

Inquiries and requests for assistance by small-business concerns have been received in our regional offices at an average rate of approximately 5,000 per month since the first of the year. 

Loan applications, while normally in a slight seasonal decline during the summer vacation months, are still being received at a relatively high rate and the number is expected to rise to a higher level by fall. About 40 percent of the Agency's presently authorized revolving fund of $80, 000, 000 has been committed. The balance is expected to be committed by February of next year.

Much progress has been made in cutting down the time required for investigating an applicant's qualifications for a loan and arriving at a final decision as to whether the loan should be approved or declined. As staff members become more experienced and there is a better understanding of general policies and operating principles, the loan processing period should be reduced still more. 

In the meantime, the procedures are being constantly reviewed for possible simplifications which will enable the Agency to handle a greater volume of loan applications more quickly and with a minimum of administrative expense.

Financial Counseling The 

Many small-business concerns are in need of counsel on financial management problems, but do not have the resources available to larger concerns to obtain this specialized service. 

Small Business Administration operates on the premise that Government funds should be loaned only after all other possible avenues for solving a small firm's financial problems have been explored. It therefore provides such financial counsel and in some instances may represent the small business in financial matters. 

In the operation of the lending program, a loan application is usually preceded by a personal inquiry from the small business representative. This may be by letter or telephone but more often is through a personal call on the regional or branch office. The inquirer is usually asked to provide the Agency with brief background information on his operations for a preliminary review of the company's qualifications for the required financing. 

The preliminary review often shows that a loan is not actually the solution to the firm's problems and that other means of solving its difficulties are indicated. Through financial counseling and active assistance, Small Business Administration financial specialists may be instrumental in overcoming the problems, or, if financing actually is needed, in obtaining the desired funds from private sources.

Some companies which come to the Agency may not need a loan immediately but are seeking information for future contingencies. Other prospective applicants obviously could not qualify for a loan for reasons such as ineligible type of business, hopeless financial condition, inadequate earnings prospects to repay a loan, no collateral, etc. These inquirers are discouraged from filing applications. This saves the company the expense and time required in filing an application for a loan which could not be made and reduces the workload in the regional offices. In every instance, however, financial specialists attempt to find ways to assist the small business, at the same time recognizing the fact that there must be many which cannot be helped because of limits to the Agency's authority or because good credit principles preclude such assistance.

Processing and Disbursement of Loans 

As a new agency, it is normal for the Small Business Administration to have some initial difficulties. Budgetary limitations on personnel, difficulties encountered in recruiting capable financial men, the rapid rise in loan applications received, the time required for handling the many personal inquiries from businesses on financial matters, and the normal "shake-down" of a new operation have all contributed to the Agency's problems. 

The recruiting of a capable staff of financial specialists was a problem which has now been successfully met. Financial specialists have been carefully selected on the basis of their experience in Government and private lending and their reputations for integrity, efficiency and industry. Careful selection was especially important, as budgetary limitation held down the number of people that could be employed by regional offices below levels estimated as required for the workload involved. Moreover, the exact amount of funds for financial specialists that would be available in fiscal year 1955 was not know until late in June. It was possible, however,

to obtain a few qualified people on a temporary basis, and some of these have now been made permanent employees of the Administration. 

One of the more pressing problems is the current backlog of business loan applications which accumulated early this year when the heavy volume of loan applications exceeded the ability of the then more limited staff to keep pace in processing. As of July 31, 1954, 625 applications were pending in the Washington and field offices. As shown in the chart on Business Loan Activity, in June, for the first time, the number of applications pending showed a net decrease from the previous month, a reduction of 44 applications. During July, there was a further reduction of 135 in applications pending. (See page 41).

A considerable number of the pending applications were incomplete when filed and are being held awaiting additional information from the applicants. In most instances, little can be done toward processing the applications until all necessary information has been made available. Delays in final action in these cases are due to failure of the applicant to comply with instructions on the submission of required background and credit data. 

With the Agency's financial staff now practically up to the budget level for 1955, a considerable reduction in the backlog is anticipated during the summer months while fewer new applications are being received. The Small Business Administration has set as a goal the maintenance of no more than a two week backlog of applications in the regional offices. The realization of such a goal is not an immediate prospect, however, because of staff limitations. 

Another situation being given special attention is the speeding of disbursements on approved loans. 

When a loan is approved for a small business concern, a formal authorization is immediately prepared and made available to the applicant. This authorization states the amount of the approved loan; the collateral to be pledged; such general terms as rate of repayment required, and final maturity date, and such standard provisions as are deemed to be necessary for reasonable assurance that loss will not be incurred on the loan. 

The Small Business Administration cannot make any disbursement on the loan until the prospective borrower has met the terms of the authorization. The borrower has four months in which to meet the requirements for disbursement, but may request an extension of this period. 

A review of some of the requests for extension of the disbursement period shows the following situations: 1. 

A required assignment of life insurance was found not to be acceptable by the insurance company as drawn by the applicant's attorney and had to be modified.

2. 3. 

Delay in starting construction of facilities made extension of disbursement period desirable. 

Agreed injection of new capital by principals was delayed. 4. 

Death of an employee who was in a key 5. position in regard to the purpose of the loan. 

Adverse change in borrower's financial condition.

In addition to the above, search of title on property to be pledged, obtaining military approval on sample products for contract to be financed by a Small Business Administration loan, and other situations may delay loan disbursements. In most cases, the 

Agency can do little to expedite final closing. However, procedures have been established to permit prompt disbursement as soon as the borrower complies with loan terms. 

Deterrents to disbursement of a loan are expected to be always present to some degree, but as the number of loan authorizations grows, a balancing of factors involved should bring loan closings to the same monthly rate as loan approvals. The Adminis trator is aware that too stringent loan terms or long delays in making needed funds available to qualified small business concerns can materially reduce the effectiveness of the financial assistance program. For that reason, and to meet the objectives intended by 

Congress, the Small Business Administration is making every effort to effect disbursement on all loans within a reasonable period. 

Service to Small Business to be Improved While it is believed that the Agency has made a good start in serving the financial needs of small business, there is a wide field for greater service, not only in providing direct financial aid but also in assisting small firms to solve their financial problems without the use of Government funds. It is believed that this can best be done by promoting still closer cooperation between banks and the Small Business Administration. 

Meetings of banking groups and Small Business Adminis tration representatives are held throughout the country from time to time to acquaint the banks with the Agency's financial program and to invite their suggestions. Bankers, as well as representatives of other segments of industry, are serving on the Financial Assistance Committee of the National Council of Consultants to give the Agency the benefit of their experience and knowledge of the best means of meeting the financial needs of small business.

It is being made clear to the banks that the Small Business Administration function is to cooperate with, not compete with them. To make participation in the Agency's loans more attractive to lending institutions, efforts are being directed toward simplification of procedures. In this connection, the application and closing forms are presently under review to determine whether they can be consolidated or shortened to reduce the "Government paper work" some banks have criticized. 

It is anticipated that in promoting a closer relationship between banks and the Small Business Administration, the regional financial specialists will be in a better position to represent small business concerns in obtaining more of their financial requirements from private sources. 

The Small Business Administration plans to continue to offer both its direct and participation loans for sale to private institutions. It is believed that, as these loans become more seasoned and their soundness is demonstrated, banks will be more interested in taking them over. This should prove to be particularly true of deferred participation loans in which the bank has already made full disbursement.

Although its immediate concern is to keep pace with loan applications being received and to speed actions on those presently pending, the Small Business Administration is also planning for the day when available personnel and time will permit expanded services to small business.

Toward this end, consideration is being given to means of helping small business obtain needed equity capital, and encouraging local development corporations. The Agency also is exploring activities it might undertake in other areas of financial assistance so that it can play the most effective possible role in strengthening the economy of the Nation.

CHAPTER V GOVERNMENT PROCUREMENT 

As pointed out in the chapter dealing with the economic position of small business, fiscal year 1954 witnessed a steep reduction in the total amount of Government procurement. While this cut was taking place, however, the percentage share going to small business increased in the second half of the fiscal year compared with the previous year. Under the joint determination program, 1,104 procurements were restricted to competitive bidding among small firms during the past six months, a substantial increase over the 637 earmarked for small business the previous six-month period, despite the fact that the amount of contracts screened was slashed sharply. The effectiveness of this program will undoubtedly be increased in the future by the adoption, by the Department of Defense and its constituent Services, of ten Small Business Administration proposals designed to increase the volume of military prime contracts awarded to small firms. 

The joint determination program is only one of the many activities that the Small Business Administration carried on to increase opportunities for small firms in the Government contracting area. The program to obtain all possible subcontracting for small firms showed good results. The Agency developed thousands of requests for subcontractors from prime contractors and referred many thousands of subcontractors to these and other prime contractors. The number of referrals increased over 50 percent compared with the first six months of the Administration's operations.

Cooperative arrangements with various Government agencies, such as the Atomic Energy Commission, have produced many subcontracts for small firms. Information about procurement opportunities also was widely disseminated. The opening of a Government Procurement 

Information Center at Baltimore, Maryland, the publication of a "U.S. Government Purchasing Directory" and the preparation of a "U.S. Government Specifications Directory" are examples of the steps that have been taken to make readily available to small business owners information that will help them in competing for Government contracts.

REFERRAL PROGRAM IN REGIONAL AND BRANCH OFFICES 

The Small Business Administration, by agreement with the Department of Defense, is permitted to recommend additional small business concerns for inclusion in the list of bidders or firms to be solicited for any specific procurement found suitable for performance by small business. 

In carrying out the referral program, the Administration's regional and branch offices notify competent small concerns of Government purchases suitable for small business. Such notifications are made after review of bid sets and attachments received by the regional and branch offices. Selected firms whose facility records indicate that they can supply the item are notified. The referrals are made on procurements covered by joint determination as well as on other military and Federal civilian agency procurements which are suitable for small firms. 

During the period February 1, through July 31, 1954, 65,807 notifications of procurement opportunities were made to qualified small concerns by the Agency's regional and branch offices. This compared to 44, 536 made during the first six months of the Agency's operations and resulted in a total of 110, 343 prime contract referrals made through July 31.

To facilitate this program, a standard mechanized referral procedure, based on the use of die-cut stencils to speed up preparation of the procurement notice and addressing machines to expedite the mailing, is being placed in the Agency's regional offices. Such labor saving devices should reduce the time formerly required to prepare and mail to small firms notices of specific opportunities to sell to the Government. The effectiveness of the referral program is illustrated by the following case: 

In a recent purchase of head-set cords by a military procurement office, joint determination referrals by the Small Business Administration's regional and branch offices resulted in several new small business suppliers bidding considerably below firms already on the bidders list. In fact, the

lowest bid by firms on the bidders list was $2.47 per unit as compared to the successful bid by a new small business supplier of $1.495 per unit. 

One factor tending to reduce the effectiveness of the referral program is that many bid sets are received in the Agency's regional offices only a short time before the final date for submitting bids or quotations. The short time remaining is often insufficient for small firms to obtain bid forms and prepare their bids. This situation should be improved by recent military instructions to contracting officers to "endeavor to allow a minimum of 30 days from date of issue of invitations for bid or requests for proposal to opening or closing date on all procurements".

Another problem is the shortage of bidding forms when new potential sources of supply write to contracting offices for them. When the Small Business Administration is informed of an eligible firm which is having such difficulty, it invites the firm to inspect the bid sets at one of the Agency's regional or branch offices or at another Government office where the bid set is available. this manner, many small firms have been able to prepare bids on their own letterhead stationery, especially for Air Force procurements. Such firms are also advised by the Administration to make immediate application to be placed on the bidders list so that they may receive bid sets for future procurements.

To alleviate the problem of lack of sufficient copies of expensive prints on particular items, it was arranged with certain Navy procurement offices for reproducible prints to be forwarded to Small Business Administration regional offices. New firms wishing to bid on the items may have the prints reproduced expeditiously at their own expense. This procedure will enable the procurement offices to obtain far more competition on certain types of procurements, and will enable additional small business suppliers to compete for the contracts.

SUBCONTRACTING OPPORTUNITIES FOR SMALL CONCERNS The Small Business Administration's subcontracting program is designed to assist qualified small firms to obtain a fair percentage of the subcontracts entered into by prime contractors performing work for the Government. 

To increase the effectiveness of its subcontracting program, the Administration recently prepared new procedures for more effectively obtaining information on the methods and practices which Government prime contractors utilize in placing subcontracts. 

In addition, the Agency is actively encouraging and assisting prime contractors to place more of their subcontracting requirements with small concerns. For example, a large manufacturing concern holding a major subcontract for aircraft armament recently asked the Agency for assistance in obtaining a listing of qualified small electronics concerns which could be considered for diversified subcontract work. Since geographical locati. n of these potential subcontractors was not a major consideration, all of the Administration's field offices participated in locating interested and qualified small firms in their respective areas. 

The passing of the peak period in Government spending has resulted in prime contractors seeking fewer additional subcontractors. Instead of farming out work, prime contractors are keeping it in their own plants or placing a larger volume with allied plants or subsidiaries. The Small Business Administration has helped to meet this challenge. This is evidenced by the number of requests for subcontractors received by the Agency from prime contractors. From August 1, 1953, to January 31, 1954, 2, 684 such requests were received, whereas for the period February 1, 1954, to July 31, 1954, the number of requests rose to 4, 320, an increase of 1,636 over the comparable period of time. During the period covered by this report, the Agency has also increased the number of referrals it has made of subcontractors to contractors. From August 1, 1953, to January 31, 1954, Small Business Administration field offices averaged 804 referrals per month, while from February 1, 1954, to 

July 31, 1954, 8, 292 referrals were made, an average of 1, 382 per month. There were 13, 123 referrals of subcontractors to contractors during the first 12 months of the Agency's operations. 

During the period from August 1, 1953, through July 31, 1954, in order to dig up subcontract opportunities, Small Business Administration field personnel made 2, 095 visits to prime contractors and 3,477 visits to small firms to obtain first hand information on the type and number of machines and equipment capable of doing specified subcontract work.

Prime contractors performing for various Atomic Energy Commission installations have been even more active in calling on this Agency for possible subcontractors during the past months than they were during the period covered by the Agency's 

First Semi-Annual Report. The Administration's regional offices have been receiving and referring to small firms several hundred invitations for bid per month from these sources alone. 

Here is an example of subcontract assistance from a report by the Agency's Regional Director for the Philadelphia area: 

The Agency's Production Specialist received a telephone call from a prime contractor requesting assistance in locating a firm to design and make three sets of punches and dies and deliver production parts at the earliest possible time. The prime contractor had discovered in final test that additional modification parts were urgently needed for a cooling unit in order to meet performance and delivery requirements on a contract for more than $1,800,000. Within one hour, one of the partners of a firm recommended by this Administration was on his way to the prime contractor's plant 80 miles away. He returned with the sample parts and quoted a price by telephone the next day. His offer was accepted, and in five days a sufficient quantity of parts were supplied to enable the prime contractor to proceed. All of the modification parts were completed and delivered in a week, at a savings to the prime contractor of $2,079 over the price quoted by the large business concern which made the original unit.

A major problem facing small concerns is the difficulty in bidding on Government purchases because of inability to obtain or review copies of applicable Federal and military specifications.

CERTIFICATES OF COMPETENCY 

Many small firms, and the Government itself, have benefited through the Small Business Administration's program of issuing Certificates of Competency. These certificates certify small-business concerns as competent with respect to capacity and credit, to perform specific Government contracts. The Small 

Business Administration acts, in effect, as a court of appeals for small firms which are low responsive bidders on Government contracts but which are about to be disqualified by contracting officers because of doubt as to their ability to perform. The Agency's certificates must be accepted as conclusive by Government procurement offices.

Certificates of Competency benefit small firms by enabling them to obtain Government contracts; in many cases also, they benefit both the purchasing agency and the taxpayer through money savings.

Since August 1, 1953, the Small Business Administration has awarded 41 Certificates of Competency, of which two were later cancelled due to withdrawal of application by the small firm involved or for other reason. During the six months ending July 31, 1954, the 

Agency issued 36 certificates of which two were cancelled. A list of the firms certified during the last six months appears in Appendix C. 

As of July 31, 1954, the current certificate cases had resulted in a saving of $282, 133. 24 to the Government through the acceptance of low bids which otherwise would have been rejected. 

Certificates of Competency were a part of the legislation under which the Small Business Administration's predecessor organi zation, the Small Defense Plants Administration, operated. At the time of its creation on July 30, 1953, the Small Business Administration assumed the administrative responsibility involved in 91 SDPAcertified contracts. Of these 31 are still active. 

It is the policy of the Small Business Administration to render decisions on applications for certificates only after careful study of an applicant's resources, as related to the requirements of the procurement in question, and to award certificates only where there is distinct evidence that the applicant can carry out the contract satisfactorily. In this way, a Certificate of Competency becomes, in effect, a stamp of approval which a contracting officer can accept at face value and without apprehension as to the qualifications of the small firm receiving the certificate. The procuring service is thus protected against later failure to receive needed equipment on time, and the small firm is protected from unwisely assuming a contract which might well result in serious financial loss to it. 

The following is an example of the operation of the Certificate of Competency program in a recent Army Chemical Corps procurement of smoke generators. The low bidder was a small Eastern firm, employing approximately 70 people. The bidder's ability to produce the smoke generators was questioned by the contracting officer, because a plant survey of the company's facilities had brought an adverse report. The company asked the 

Small Business Administration for a Certificate of Competency, and the Agency sent a production specialist and a financial specialist

to the applicant's plant. After rigorous study of the company's resources, both financial and technical, viewed in the light of the financial and technical requirements of the contract in question, the Agency concluded that the company possessed adequate productive facilities to manufacture the equipment, had access to the required finances, had on its payroll or could quickly obtain the necessary skilled workers, could obtain the required materials and components, and was in every important respect capable of meeting the delivery schedule specified by the Chemical Corps in the contract. A Certificate of Competency was issued to the company. The applicant has been awarded the contract, and at a saving of over $80, 000 to the procuring service and the taxpayers of the Nation. 

After a contract is awarded as a result of a Certificate of Competency, the Small Business Administration through its field and Washington staff of production specialists, maintains a careful follow-up on the progress of the contract, and stands ready at any time to offer technical assistance should production problems arise which could jeopardize meeting the contract schedule. 

As an illustration of the follow-up procedure, a small manufacturer of automobile tops located in the Western part of the country received an Army Quartermaster Corps contract for plastic snap-in windows for tents, as a result of having been awarded a Certificate of Competency. After the contract was awarded, progress on it was followed closely by the Small Business Administration's regional office production specialist, and periodic reports on shipments and other items of interest were forwarded regularly to the Washington office. During the initial stages of the contract, excessive material was consumed. However, the initial production problems were solved so well that the bulk of the contract was eventually shipped considerably in advance of the contract schedule. There remained, however, a small number of units for which additional material had to be purchased. Under the prompting of the regional office production specialist, the contractor purchased, on special order, the small amount of additional material required and completed the remaining delivieries approximately three weeks in advance of the final shipment date in the contract. This procurement was made at a saving of $3, 000 to the Government. In another case, a small manufacturer obtained a Certificate of Competency on a contract with the Air Force. The manufacturer

experienced difficulty in obtaining suitable castings from his subcontractor, another small concern. The Small Business Administration regional office production specialist visited the subcontractor, a small foundry, analyzed its techniques in making the castings, and recommended procedures to eliminate difficulties which were being encountered. The recommendations will enable the subcontractor to meet his commitments to the prime contractor and will facilitate the successful completion of the contract. 

Ideally, in view of the benefits conferred by Certificates of Competency, all small businesses which are low bidders on contracts and which are disqualified for reasons of competency should be notified of this in time to apply for a certificate. Up to the present time, however, agreement on such notification has been reached with only one branch of the armed services--the Department of the Air Force. Contracting officers of that service, by directive, must automatically notify all disqualified low bidders, thus permitting small businesses to take advantage of the Certificate of Competency procedure in all applicable cases. When similar agreements have been reached with other branches of the armed services and other Government agencies, the Certificate of Competency program will reach its maximum effectiveness.

PRODUCTS ASSISTANCE PROGRAM The Small Business Administration established its 

Products Assistance program in February 1954, when pilot programs were launched in the Philadelphia and Boston Regional Offices. 

After testing the effectiveness of this tool for helping small business help itself, the program was expanded in June to include all Small Business Administration field offices, and is now in operation on a 

Nation-wide basis.

The Small Business Administration provides experienced counsel to small-business firms and individuals in locating a marketable product or new line or type of product, and in locating a market for a product, which may include someone to make it and put it on the market. The program is designed to assist smallbusiness concerns in finding solutions to research and development problems regarding product improvement and new products as they arise from day to day. In this connection, the Administration looks to and obtains the assistance of voluntary, non-profit organizations,

trade associations, research institutions, civic groups and Federal and State agencies in carrying out the purpose of this program. 

The Agency does not give technical advice in the operating details of product development. Instead, by establishing workable relationships with and soliciting the cooperation of the organizations, institutions and agencies referred to above, the Administration is able to draw from the wealth of information and data of a non-confidential nature on research and product development which has accumulated in industry and in government over the years. 

By and large, the small business man does not possess the funds to maintain elaborate research laboratories. As a result, he is placed in an unfavorable competitive position. It has been stated that research and development on new products and processes represent insurance of future profits; that many a company, if it discontinued its research and product development program, would be out of business in a few years. Many companies state that the majority of their present products have been developed in the last decade. 

In fact, research and product development are big business today, with $3 billion being spent annually on them in this country. 

The larger companies and the Government are, in the main, the sources of these expenditures.

In all the time before 1946, American industry spent $4.6 billion on organized research. But in the last eight years, industry has spent $9.5 billion on research, more than double the amount for the previous 170 years. This is in addition to the $11 billion the Government has spent on research in the same time, which was also double the amount spent before 1946. 

A large volume of the results obtained from product and process research has always been available, but not readily accessible, to the small-business concern. The objective of this Small Business 

Administration program is to help the small firm find a solution to its product development problem by furnishing it with the results of such research. Since not every product is marketable and not every marketable product can be produced at a profit, the program has been developed conservatively. It has been borne in mind that the saving to a firm in avoiding a poor product may be as valuable to it economically as developing a good one.

In order to produce maximum results, the program is conducted by the Small Business Administration field offices. This affords the small-business owner a one-stop center in the area served by the Agency's field office, thus obviating the necessity of his coming to Washington in order to find a solution to his problem. 

Thus, the Small Business Administration, by coordinating the operations through the central office at Washington and decentralizing the program operations to the "grass-roots" field office level, provides a clearing house for obtaining and disseminating information on product research and development and on production and marketing techniques. 

On June 30, 1954, the United States Patent Office discontinued publishing a register of patents which listed patents that were available for outright sale or license manufacturing on a royalty basis. 

As a result, the Small Business Administration will continue this service to small-business concerns through its various field offices. 

The field offices will screen the patented product or process to determine if it will lend itself to the objectives of the program, then forward it to the Washington office to be disseminated to all other field offices, thus enabling all of the Agency's offices to have information on the availability of patented products and processes. 

In addition, Small Business Administration field offices are presently maintaining lists of a large number of Governmentowned patented products and processes which are available to small firms, free or with only a nominal charge at most for their use. 

As has been previously stated, in this assistance program, the Agency does not perform any research activities itself. In assisting small concerns, it draws upon accumulated non-confidential information already available. The Products Assistance program acts as a medium to the owner of a small enterprise, busily occupied in operating his business, to help him keep abreast of current technological developments with reference to products and processes in his field.

The Small Business Administration field offices are also maintaining an index of professional consultants in the engineering and management fields who have indicated to the Agency that their services are available to small-business concerns which may wish to employ them.

U. S. Government Purchasing Directory 

This is a highly valuable publication which was issued by the Small Business Administration in May. It is the first complete guide to the military and civilian purchasing activities of the Federal

Government to be placed on sale to the public. Listed in it are approximately 4, 000 items and classes of items covering the more than 5, 000, 000 products and services purchased by the Federal Government. These items are followed by numerical codes indicating which purchasing offices buy them. The listing is not limited to the offices of the Department of Defense but includes other Department purchasing offices as well. 

In addition to listing who buys what and where in the Federal Government, the Directory provides small firms with a wide variety of other helpful information. For example, it explains 

Government purchasing methods and tells how small concerns can obtain listing on agency bidders lists; outlines important clauses in 

Government contracts, and suggests ways in which small firms can locate subcontracting opportunities. 

The Directory is invaluable to the small-business concern that wants to sell to the Government. Through it, the small-business owner is able to have a ready reference to the Government purchasing offices that buy his products or services. Further, by careful study of the Directory, the businessman can locate other items which his plant is not now making but which it can make and supply to the Government. The Directory should result in more bidders on Government purchases which, in turn, should insure more competitive prices to the Government.

MANAGEMENT COURSES FOR SMALL-BUSINESS OWNERS 

The Small Business Administration has strengthened its program to help the owners of small manufacturing and distributing firms to help themselves by bringing them in touch with the latest management policies and procedures. This program was launched in cooperation with educational institutions throughout the country in 

December 1953. 

In this program, the Agency does not offer any courses of its own, but instead assists university schools of business, colleges of engineering, evening colleges, and university extension departments to provide such courses. 

The major problem which the owner of a small business faces is lack of management experience and knowledge of sound management procedures. He has no staff of experts; he cannot afford to retain a staff of consultants. Through experiment and investigation, the Small Business Administration is determining what problems small operators are facing and what kind of courses will provide the best answers in the easiest, most graphic manner. 

During the spring semester of 1954, the Agency cosponsored two experimental or pilot courses: one at Milwaukee,

Wisconsin, where the University of Wisconsin ran a "Small Business Executives Institute"; and one at Richmond, Virginia, where the 

University of Richmond presented a course in "Practical Management for Small Business". Both institutions are planning to repeat their courses this coming fall session. 

The course in Milwaukee ran for a two-hour session, one night a week, for eight weeks. Membership was limited to 15 owners or presidents of small manufacturing plants operating in or near Milwaukee. This course was of the conference-led type, resulting in discussion by those present. Subjects discussed were: 

The Role of Small Business in the Milwaukee Economy; 

Analyzing Current Sales and Distribution; Cost Estimating and Control; 

Production Problems; Effective Managerial Organization; The Future of Your Product--Design, Development, Markets; Your Financial Problems; Accounting Records, Taxation, Financial Statements.

In Richmond, the course was open to all classes of business owners--manufacturers, wholesalers, retailers, and owners of service businesses. They met for three hours at each session, one evening a week, for eight weeks. The first two hours were given over to lectures by university men and business leaders; the last one to discussions in which the manufacturers, wholesalers, and other groups met separately. In this course, the members used textbooks and took notes in class based upon outlines. The subjects discussed were:

Basic Functions of Management; Developing Good 

Executives; Management for Increased Sales; Granting and Receiving

Credit; Uses of Financial Records; Personnel Training; Personnel Motivation; Policy Determination; Business Publications and Litera ture; Problem Analysis.

Currently, a bulletin is being written, setting forth the experience with these two courses as well as three earlier clinics. 

The bulletin will be based upon evaluations by the students, faculty, and Small Business Administration regional personnel who observed the work.

It is the Agency's purpose to co-sponsor some 20 such courses this coming fall, each one adapted to the needs of local small business and operated by a local educational institution. The institutions include such colleges and universities as Temple, Syracuse, Tulsa, Pennsylvania State, and Houston.
