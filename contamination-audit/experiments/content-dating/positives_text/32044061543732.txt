III. LUXEMBOURG TAX CONVENTION 1. PURPOSE 

The income tax convention between the United States and Luxembourg is designed to eliminate double taxation of income, prevent fiscal evasion, and promote trade and investment. It also establishes procedures for mutual administrative assistance between the tax authorities of the two countries. 

2. SUMMARY OF PROVISIONS 

In general, the convention with Luxembourg follows the pattern of other income tax conventions to which the United States is a party. 

As in the case of similar conventions, it contains provisions relating to the following matters: 

(1) Definitions (arts. II and XVII); 

(2) Adoption of principles for determining and taxing business income derived by enterprises of one country from sources within the other country (arts. III and IV); (3) Reciprocal exemption from taxation, upon certain conditions, of various items of business income, including profits derived from sources within one country by enterprises of the other country from the operation of ships and aircraft (art. V); (4) Reciprocal taxation on the basis of net income of real property rentals and natural resources royalties and reciprocal exemption of industrial royalties and like income (arts. VI and VII); (5) Reciprocal tax exemption, upon certain conditions, of interest and dividend income and, in the case of dividends received from sources within one country by a resident or corporation of the other country not having a permanent establishment in the former, reduction of dividend taxes to 50 percent of the statutory rate otherwise imposed, with provision for a further reduction to 5 percent in the case of dividends paid by a subsidiary corporation in one country to its parent corporation in the other country, subject to specified conditions (arts. VIII, IX, and X); (6) Reciprocal exemptions with respect to (a) Government wages, salaries, and pensions; private pensions and private life annuities; (b) compensation for labor or personal services other than fees of directors of corporations; and (c) remuneration or remittances received by teachers, students, or business apprentices in prescribed circumstances (art. XI, XII, XIII, and XIV);

(7) Reciprocal credit provisions and a reservation by each country of the right, without regard to the provisions of the convention, to include in the basis upon which income tax is imposed in the case of certain taxpayers the various items of income taxable under its own tax laws (art. XVI); (8) Reciprocal exchange of information between the competent authorities of the two countries and reciprocal assistance in collection in certain cases, in order to insure that an exemption or reduced rate of tax granted by the convention shall not be enjoyed by persons not entitled to such benefits (art. XVIII); (9) The right of taxpayers to appeal when they can show that double taxation has resulted or will result in respect of any of the taxes to which the convention relates (art. XIX); (10) Provisions relating to exemptions of diplomatic or consular officers, and taxes on other U.S. citizens (art. XX); and (11) Provision to allow the competent authorities of Luxembourg and the United States to prescribe regulations necessary to carry out the provisions of the convention (art. XXI). 

3. EFFECTIVE DATE Article XXII provides for ratification and for the exchange of instruments of ratification and prescribes the effective date of the convention. Pursuant to its terms, the convention is effective for taxable years beginning on or after January 1 of the calendar year in which the convention is ratified and the instruments of ratification are exchanged. It is effective for a period of 5 years, and indefinitely thereafter, but it may be terminated by either country at the end of the 5-year period by a 6-month written notice by one of the governments to the other government, the termination to become effective for taxable years beginning on or after January 1 next following the expiration of the 6-month period.

MEMORANDUM OF JOINT COMMITTEE ON INTERNAL REVENUE TAXATION TAX CONVENTION WITH LUXEMBOURG 

The tax convention with Luxembourg has the general purpose of affording relief from double taxation resulting from the fact that the laws of the two countries impose tax on different bases. It also establishes procedures for mutual administrative assistance between the fiscal authorities of the two countries. 

In the case of the United States, the convention applies only to the Federal income tax. In the case of Luxembourg, the convention applies to the Luxembourg tax on fees of directors of corporations, the wealth tax, and the communal taxes on commercial profits, invested capital, and land, as well as the Luxembourg income tax. 

In general, Luxembourg levies an income tax on the worldwide income of its residents at rates ranging from approximately 0.18 percent on the first $550 of taxable income to 54 percent on income in excess of $40,000. Nonresidents are taxable only on income from sources within Luxembourg, and, except in the case of business income, the Luxembourg tax withheld at source generally satisfies the tax liability. In the case of corporate taxpayers, Luxembourg taxes the

worldwide income of corporations having their central management or seat in Luxembourg, while other corporations are taxed only on income from sources within, or attributable to a fixed place of business within, Luxembourg. The corporate income tax is levied at a 20percent rate on income of $8,000 or less, 30 percent on income between $8,000 and $20,000, and 40 percent on income above $20,000. However, in the case of corporations which are not managed, or do not have their seat, in Luxembourg, and do not have a fixed place of business within Luxembourg, tax is generally limited to the amount withheld at source. The Luxembourg statutory withholding rates which apply in the absence of the convention vary depending upon the nature of the payment; for example, the withholding rates are 15 percent on dividends, 5 percent on interest, 12 percent on industrial royalties, and 10 percent on most other royalties. 

Business profits

Corresponding to the principle of other income tax conventions, article III of this convention provides that an enterprise of one of the contracting countries will not be subject to tax on industrial or commercial profits by the other country unless it carries on business in the other country through a permanent establishment located within that country. However, this provision of the convention, and the related definitions contained in article II, are important in that it is the first time the United States has fundamentally adopted the definition of a "permanent establishment" contained in the draft convention for the avoidance of double taxation approved by the Fiscal Committee of the 

Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), and a substantial part of the language of the OECD draft convention dealing with the rules by which profits attributable to a permanent establishment are to be determined. 

In general, the recitation of factors that do constitute a permanent establishment generally conform to those contained in other U.S. tax conventions. Thus, if the business of an enterprise is wholly or partly carried on in the host country through a place of management, a branch, an office, a factory, a workshop, a mine, a quarry or other place of extraction of natural resources, or a building site, or construction or assembly project which exists for more than 6 months, an enterprise of the other country is deemed to have a "permanent establishment" in the host country. Moreover, the presence in country of a person (other than an independent agent) who has, and habitually exercises, an authority to conclude contracts in the name of his principal, is deemed to constitute a permanent establishment. 

Thus, except for treating "a place of management" as a permanent establishment, and treating construction projects after 6 months as a permanent establishment, the affirmative portion of the definition is consistent with those contained in prior U.S. income tax conventions (for example, the conventions with Austria, West Germany, Italy, Japan, and Norway). Moreover, the definition is consistent with prior definitions in holding that the maintenance of a fixed place of business solely for the purpose of purchasing goods (and the employment of an agent for that purpose) does not constitute a permanent establishment.

However, the definition departs from prior conventions by itemizing several activities which do not constitute a permanent establishment. 

In general, the exceptions have the effect of permitting an enterprise of

one of the contracting countries to economically penetrate the other country to a much greater degree than has heretofore been permitted without incurring an income tax in the host country. For example, under the definition, a Luxembourg enterprise may use U.S. facilities to display goods in the United States, maintain a separate fixed place of business in the United States for the purpose of advertising the goods and, in addition, maintain an inventory of goods from which it may make delivery, without being considered to have a permanent establishment in the United States. Conversely, a U.S. enterprise could conduct similar activities in Luxembourg without being considered to have a permanent establishment in Luxembourg. 

The convention also provides that the maintenance of a fixed place of business in a country "solely for the purpose of advertising, for the supply of information, for scientific research, or for similar activities which have a preparatory or auxiliary character, for the enterprise" will not be deemed to constitute a permanent establishment. Although the term "similar activities" is not defined, it is presumably intended to include, among other things, activities related to servicing a contract for technical services or a "know-how" contract. In general, exclusion of these type activities is based on the premise advanced by some that research, advertising, and related activities, do not, by themselves, produce profits and, therefore, should not, taken alone, be treated as a permanent establishment. Furthermore, some argue that even if profit were attributable to such activities, the amount involved would be negligible and would largely be offset by the administrative expense and inconvenience of collecting a tax. 

The convention also provides that an enterprise will not be considered to have a permanent establishment in a country merely because it carries on business in that country through a broker, general commission agent, or any other agent of an independent status where such persons are acting in the ordinary course of their business. 

Similarly, an enterprise of one of the contracting countries will not be deemed to have a permanent establishment in the other country merely because it controls, or is controlled by, a corporate enterprise of the other state or a corporation which carries on business in the other state. These provisions are consistent with provisions contained in prior U.S. tax conventions.

Article III of the convention also provides that an enterprise of one country having a "permanent establishment" in the other country is to be subject to income tax by that country only on the industrial and commercial profits attributable to the "permanent establishment" in that country. In determining the profits attributable to a permanent establishment, different rules apply depending upon whether the permanent establishment is in Luxembourg or the United States. 

If a Luxembourg enterprise has a permanent establishment in the United States, all industrial and commercial profits from sources within the United States would be deemed attributed to the permanent establishment, whether or not actually attributable to activities conducted by the permanent establishment. Conversely, non-U.S. source income would not be attributed to the permanent establishment even though attributable to activities conducted in the permanent establishment. If a U.S. enterprise has a permanent establishment in Luxembourg, industrial and commercial profits attributable only to the activities of the permanent establishment would be taxable by Luxembourg, without regard to the source of the income. 

---

By adopting these conflicting rules, the convention follows the pattern of prior conventions by limiting the U.S. tax on the income of a permanent establishment in the United States to U.S. source income, while it follows the OECD draft convention with respect to allocation of income to a permanent establishment in Luxembourg. 

In determining industrial or commercial profits, expenses incurred for the purposes of the permanent establishment would be allowable as deductions. However, expenses incurred in connection with the mere purchase of goods or merchandise, and expenses incurred by a permanent establishment in the United States in connection with non- U.S. source income, would not be allowable in calculating taxable profits of the permanent establishment. Moreover, all allocations of income and deductions would be subject to an overriding rule that they be allocated in a manner that would be expected if a permanent establishment were dealing with the enterprise of which it is a permanent establishment on an arm's-length basis. 

The convention, for the first time, incorporates a rule which has been administratively followed by the United States to the effect that if an enterprise of one country has a permanent establishment in the other country at any time during the year, it is deemed to have a permanent establishment in that country for the entire year. 

This provision would require a Luxembourg enterprise with a permanent establishment in the United States to allocate U.S. source industrial or commercial profits to such permanent establishment in the United States even thought the profit may be derived at a time during the taxable year when the permanent establishment was not in existence. 

Shipping and aircraft

The convention provides that income derived from the operation of ships or aircraft registered in either Luxembourg or the United States is to be taxable only in the country in which registered and that income which would otherwise be taxable in the other country will be treated as income from sources within the country of registry 

(arts. V and XVII). 

Dividends

A reciprocal reduction in rate of tax applicable to dividends received. from sources within one country by a resident or corporation of the other country is provided if the dividend recipient does not have a permanent establishment in the source country. 

In the case of U.S. residents and corporations receiving dividends from Luxembourg sources, the Luxembourg rate of tax would be re-duced from the statutory rate of 15 percent to a treaty rate of 72 percent. In the case of dividends paid to Luxembourg residents and corporations from U.S. sources, the U.S. rate of tax would be set at a flat 15 percent, without regard to the amount of income the Luxembourg resident or corporation receives from sources within the United States. Therefore, in addition to reducing the U.S. withholding rate on dividends paid Luxembourg residents from 30 to 15 percent, the convention would also have the effect of limiting U.S. tax on dividend.. income of Luxembourg residents with more than $21,200 of U.S source income (in taxable years beginning after 1964) to 15 percent rather than at the top tax rate bracket (or brackets) at which the

dividend income would otherwise fall on the schedule of graduated rates applicable to income from U.S. sources. 

In reducing the tax allocable to the source country, this convention is consistent with other U.S. tax conventions; however, it is unique in that it ties the tax rate to 50 percent of the statutory rates presently in effect, resulting in the aforementioned 15and 72-percent rates, rather than permitting variations in rate which would be subject generally to a 15-percent ceiling. 

Provision is also made to reduce the source country's rate of tax to 5 percent (rather than the 15or 72-percent rates otherwise applicable) if the recipient of the dividend is a corporation which, together with no more than three other 10-percent or more corporation shareholders, owns at least 50 percent of the voting stock of the payor corporation and not more than 25 percent of the payor's gross income consists of dividends and interest from other than 50-percent-owned subsidiaries. Although this provision differs from the majority of 

U.S. tax conventions by reducing the tax of the source country on cases where the payor is less than 95 percent owned by the recipient, it is substantially identical with provision contained in the U.S. tax convention with Norway and the pending conventions with Japan and Sweden. In general, a lessening of the percentage of ownership required by a corporate shareholder in order to receive the benefits of a reduced withholding rate favors a creditor nation. Therefore, this provision should operate to the benefit of the United States. 

As in the case of interest, the convention provides (in art. X) that dividends paid by Luxembourg corporations (other than Luxembourg holding companies) to persons other than U.S. citizens, residents, or corporations will not be taxed by the United States, and dividends paid by U.S. corporations to persons other than Luxembourg residents and corporations will not be taxed by Luxembourg. In the absence of this provision, the United States might otherwise tax the income under the provisions of sections 861(a)(1)(B) and 861(a)(2)(B) of the 

Internal Revenue Code. 

INTEREST 

The convention provides that interest, other than interest on debts secured by mortgages on real property, received by a resident or corporation of the United States or Luxembourg would, in general, be taxed only by the country of residence of the recipient if the recipient does not have a permanent establishment in the other country (art. 

VIII). Thus, the convention adopts the policy contained in the more recent convention to which the United States is a party by, in general, making the country of residence the exclusive taxing jurisdiction, rather than maintaining split jurisdiction to tax, but limiting the authority of the source country through reduction in withholding rates, as is the case under the earlier conventions. 

INCOME FROM REAL PROPERTY AND MINERAL ROYALTIES 

In general, exclusive jurisdiction to tax income from real property, including gains derived from the sale of real property, mineral royalties, and interest on debts secured by mortgages on real property, is given by the convention to the country in which the property is located, unless, as is the case with all provisions, a country chooses to disregard the convention provision (under art. XVI) but agrees to allow

a credit for taxes paid to the country in which the property is located. 

Moreover, the recipient of the income may elect to be taxed in the source country on the basis of net income from the properties rather than on gross income as would generally be the case for Luxembourg residents and corporations who receive this type of income from sources within the United States but do not engage in trade or business in the United States. This reciprocal provision corresponds to 

Luxembourg law which permits a U.S. resident or corporation receiving income from real property in Luxembourg to pay tax on a net basis. The convention also contains language which clarifies the position the United States has consistently taken with respect to similar provisions in other conventions to the effect that the election. to be taxed on a net income basis applies only to the items of income to which the article (in this case, art. VI) applies. 

Nonmineral royalties

In general, the convention follows the accepted principle of agreements which seek to eliminate double taxation of income by providing that payments for the use of, or for the privilege of using, property or rights are to be taxed only in the country of residence of the recipient, unless the recipient has a permanent establishment in the country in which the property or right is used. 

In this respect, the convention follows prior conventions by specifically providing that income for the privilege of using a copyright, artistic or scientific work, patent, design, plan, secret process or formula, trademark, motion picture film, as well as income from the rental of industrial, commercial, or scientific equipment, is exempt from tax in the source country. This convention adds to the list of items income received for the use of, or for the privilege of using, films or tapes for radio or television broadcasting, and payments for "knowledge, experience, skill, or know-how."

Although income from the use, or for the privilege of using, knowledge, experience, skill, or know-how was not specifically covered in prior conventions, such items of income were treated in the same manner as the items listed if the knowledge, experience, skill, or know-how for which the income was paid was reduced to a form in which it constituted property. This convention excludes such income from tax in the country of use even though the income is paid solely for "knowledge, experience, skill, or know-how" which constitute services, as distinguished from property. In this respect, the convention is to the general advantage of the United States, since U.S. companies presumably supply substantially more services of this type in Luxembourg than Luxembourg corporations furnish in the United States. 

Compensation for personal services

Articles XI, XII, XIII, and XIV follow the usual pattern of income tax conventions in that they- (1) Exclude compensation, including pensions, paid by one country, or a political subdivision thereof, from income tax in the other country if paid (other than to a citizen of that country) for services rendered in the discharge of governmental functions of the payor country; (2) Exclude amounts paid as private pensions and life annuities to residents of one country from income tax in the other country if paid from sources within the payor country;

(3) Provide that compensation for labor or personal services performed in one of the contracting countries by a resident of the other country shall be exempt from tax by the country in which the services are performed if the person performing the services is present in that country for 180 days or less during the taxable year and (a) the compensation does not exceed $3,000, or (b) regardless of the amount of income, the services are performed in one country as an employee of a resident, corporation, or permanent establishment of an enterprise of the other country and the burden of the compensation is borne by such enterprise; (4) Exempt residents of one country from tax in the other country for a maximum of 2 years if such persons are present in the host country for the purpose of teaching at a recognized educational institution in that country; (5) Exempt residents of one country from income tax in the other country on income from outside the host country for employment (or remittances from the country of residence to cover cost of maintenance, education, or training in the host country) if present in the host country for the purpose of study or research as a student, business apprentice, or recipient of a grant, allowance, or award from a charitable organization; and (6) Provide that residents of one country who are present in the other country for not more than 1 year in order to acquire technical, professional, or business experience may exclude up to $5,000 of compensation from tax in the host country (and up to $10,000 if present in the host country under arrangement with the government of that country). 

In addition to these provisions, which are found in substantially the same form in other tax conventions to which the United States is a party, the convention expands the exemption generally applicable to teachers (item (4) above) to include persons who engage in research at an educational institution, whether or not the research is conducted in conjunction with teaching duties; however, this exemption does not apply to the extent the research is carried on for the benefit of anyone other than the educational institution which invited the person to the country. Moreover, as noted in item (4), the exemption applies for the first 2 years' salary regardless of how long the person may stay in the host country. This modification of similar provisions contained in prior conventions avoids retroactive tax on income for the first 2 years if, for example, a nonresident alien temporarily present in the United States for the purpose of teaching elects to remain in the United States for a longer period or to become a U.S. citizen. Provision is also made to exclude residents of one country who are present in the other country as students or apprentices from tax in the country in which located as if they were "residents" of that country. Thus, for example, Luxembourg residents who come to the United States for the purpose of attending school will be taxed as nonresident aliens; that is, only on their U.S.-source income. In the absence of this provision, such persons often attain resident status in the United States and are taxable in the United States on their worldwide income. 

The convention also provides that compensation for labor or personal services performed in one country by a resident of that country shall. be exempt from tax in the other country even though the products of the services are put to use in the other country. Thus, for example, a.

U.S. lawyer who prepares a legal memorandum in the United States for use in Luxembourg will be exempt from Luxembourg tax on the fee he receives for the memorandum. In the absence of this provision, his income would be taxed by both Luxembourg and the United States, since Luxembourg taxes such income on the basis of the place where the product of a personal service is used, as well as on the basis of where the service is performed.

Source of income rules Article XVII contains source of income rules that are more detailed than those set forth in any other treaty to which the United States is a party. In general, these rules adopt the source rules contained in the 

Internal Revenue Code of 1954 (secs. 861 through 863) and, because of the absence of detailed rules under Luxembourg law, should result in more uniform treatment of items of income so as to avoid double taxation. 

Foreign tax credits

The convention provides (in art. XVI) that either the United States or Luxembourg may disregard a provision of the convention and tax any item of income of its citizens (in the case of the United States), residents, and corporations under its revenue laws as if the convention had not come into effect. Thus, if the United States were to tax an item of income without regard to the convention, which it would do in the case of its citizens living in Luxembourg, foreign tax credits would be available to the taxpayer under the provisions of subpart A, part III, of subchapter N of the code (secs. 901 through 

905.) Therefore, except in the case of Luxembourg citizens resident in the United States, the convention does not extend the circumstances under which credit for Luxembourg income tax, taxes on fees of the directors of corporations, and the communal tax on commercial profits would be available to U.S. taxpayers. However, since Luxembourg, by the convention, agrees to allow credit against Luxembourg tax for taxes paid the United States by U.S. citizens resident in 

Luxembourg, the reciprocity required by section 901 (c) (3) of the code is deemed to be satisfied with the result Luxembourg citizens who are resident in the United States will be allowed credit against their 

U.S. tax for creditable taxes paid to Luxembourg.

Under Luxembourg law, residents of Luxembourg and corporations managed in Luxembourg do not receive credit for taxes paid a foreign country. However, under the convention, if Luxembourg chooses to disregard a provision of the convention, Luxembourg is required to allow a tax credit for U.S. tax paid by a Luxembourg resident (including U.S. citizens resident in Luxembourg) or corporation in an amount at least equal to that proportion of the Luxembourg tax otherwise payable as income from within the United States which is taxable by Luxembourg bears to the entire income of the taxpayer which is subject to Luxembourg tax. However, Luxembourg is not required to allow a Luxembourg corporation a credit for taxes paid by a 10-percent-owned, first-tier subsidiary (or a second-tier corporation 50-percent owned by a 10-percent-owned, first-tier subsidiary) from which the Luxembourg corporation receives a taxable dividend. 

Moreover, the convention does not provide for carrybacks or carryforwards of excess foreign tax credits as is the case under the Internal 

Revenue Code.

Nonapplicability to Luxembourg holding companies

The convention does not apply to Luxembourg corporations which are now entitled, or subsequently become entitled, to special tax benefits available to companies which do not engage in an active trade or business, so-called holding companies (art. XV). Under present law, these companies are exempt from Luxembourg income tax on the receipt of income, and their shareholders are exempt from Luxembourg tax on the receipt of dividends from these companies. 

In general, the purpose of an income tax convention is to prevent income from sources within one country which is received by a resident of another country from being taxed twice, first in the country in which the income is derived and a second time in the country which the recipient resides. Consistent with this philosophy, this convention follows the pattern of most other income tax conventions to which the United States is a party, as above described, by providing that interest and nonmineral royalty income is to be completely exempt from tax in the source country, and that dividend income is to be subject to a reduced rate of tax in the source country. Thus, for example, although dividend, interest, or royalty income from sources within the United States which is received by a resident of Luxembourg is exempt from U.S. tax, or subject to U.S. tax at reduced rates, the income is generally subject to full Luxembourg tax when received by a Luxembourg resident. However, if income were received by a Luxembourg holding company whose shareholders reside in a third country, and the provisions of this convention were made applicable to such companies, it would be possible for interest and royalty income to be completely exempt from tax in the United States, in Luxembourg, and also in the country of residence of the corporate shareholder if the third country in which the shareholder resides does not have an income tax. Moreover, the reduced U.S. tax rate on dividends would be the sole tax burden on dividend income.

A hypothetical example may illustrate this point. Under present law, if a resident of the Bahamas invested directly in the United States, he would be subject to U.S. tax at a 30-percent rate on dividends, interest, and royalty income from sources within the United States if his total income from sources within the United States did not exceed $19,000 a year ($21,200 for taxable years beginning after 1964) and would be subject to the graduated rates applicable to U.S. citizens and residents generally if his income exceeded that amount. Since the Bahamas does not have an income tax, the U.S. tax would be the sole tax burden on the U.S. source income. Moreover, under present law, there would be little inducement for a resident of the Bahamas to invest in the United States through a Luxembourg holding company, since payments to the Luxembourg holding company would be subject to a 30 percent U.S. withholding rate, and, if the Luxembourg holding company received 50 percent or more of its gross income for the 3-year period ending with the close of its taxable year preceding the declaration of a dividend from sources within the United States, a portion of the dividends paid by the Luxembourg holding company to its shareholders would also be treated for U.S. tax purposes as income from sources within the United States. In such a case, the 

Bahamian resident would again (theoretically, but perhaps not practically) be subject to U.S. tax at a 30-percent rate or at graduated rates if his U.S. source income exceeded the prescribed $19,000 or

$21,200 amount. However, if the convention were made applicable to Luxembourg holding companies, persons such as the Bahamian resident would be encouraged to make U.S. investments through a 

Luxembourg holding company so as to receive the benefit of the U.S. exemption from tax on interest and royalty income, and the reduction of tax on dividend income, as well as the benefits of article X(1) of the convention which insulates a resident of a third country from U.S. tax on receipt of dividends from Luxembourg corporations by providing that the United States will not tax the dividend or interest income of a resident of a third country which is received from a Luxembourg corporation. Thus, to prevent complete or substantial elimination of tax on U.S. source dividend, interest, and royalty income received by residents of low tax rate third countries, the convention is made inapplicable to Luxembourg holding companies.

In effect, residents of third countries retain their present tax position. 

Since the use of Luxembourg holding companies to invest in the United States has not been widespread (even though the law granting the special tax benefits was first enacted in 1929), denial of the convention benefits to these companies should not adversely affect the U.S. balance-of-payments position. It should be noted, however, that denial of the convention benefits with respect to dividends and interest paid by a Luxembourg holding company to its shareholders, so-called secondary liability, is inconsistent with the treatment afforded shareholders of Netherlands Antilles corporations under similar circumstances in the protocol dated October 23, 1963, modifying and supplementing the extension to the Netherlands Antilles of the income tax convention with the Netherlands.

Unilateral relief from Luxembourg wealth tax and communal taxes on land, commercial profits, and invested capital 

Wealth tax.-Under Luxembourg law, residents of Luxembourg, and corporations having their principal seat of management in Luxembourg, are, in general, subject to a wealth tax imposed at a rate of 0.5 percent of net worth based upon the value of property wherever situated. Nonresidents of Luxembourg, and corporations managed outside of Luxembourg, are similarly taxed on the value of the net worth of their Luxembourg property. Under article XVI (2) (c) of the convention, Luxembourg agrees to exempt real property situated in the United States, debts secured by real property situated in the United States, and all or a portion of the invested capital of a permanent establishment situated in the United States from the determination of the net worth of its residents and corporations. 

Communal tax on land.-Under Luxembourg law, real property located in Luxembourg is subject to tax based on the property's assessed valuation. Although a national tax, the tax is collected by, and for the benefit of, local municipalities. Article XVI (2) (a) of the convention provides that Luxembourg will not extend the scope of the tax so as to tax real property situated in the United States. 

Communal taxes on commercial profits and invested capital. Under 

Luxembourg law, commercial profits of a business enterprise, other than profits allocable to a fixed place of business outside of Luxembourg, are, in general, subject to tax at a flat rate of 4 percent. Moreover, business enterprises are subject, in general, to tax at a flat rate of 0.2 percent on capital, other than that portion allocable to a fixed place of business outside of Luxembourg. Both these taxes 

---

are levied for the benefit of local municipalities who are authorized to adjust the basic tax rates. Article XVI (2) (b) of the convention provides that Luxembourg will exempt from tax profits and invested capital allocable to a permanent establishment located in the United States. To the extent a permanent establishment, as defined in the convention, is broader than the Luxembourg definition of a "fixed place of business," additional profits and capital of business enterprises subject to these taxes will be exempt. 

1ax on fees of directors of Luxembourg corporations Under Luxembourg law, directors of corporations managed in Luxembourg are subject to a special tax on fees paid to them as directors of such corporations. In the case of directors who are nonresidents of Luxembourg, liability for tax, and the amount withheld at source, is fixed at 28 percent of the gross fee. Provision is made in the convention to exclude compensation of this type from the provisions that otherwise apply to income for personal services. 

Thus, Luxembourg retains its jurisdiction to tax such income, while the United States agrees to grant its residents and citizens a credit against U.S. tax otherwise payable for the Luxembourg tax withheld. 

This treatment of taxation of directors' fees, which retains tax jurisdiction in the source country, is consistent with the model convention being prepared by the Fiscal Committee of the OECD. 

Administrative provisions 

In addition to the above-described provisions, the convention contains articles similar to those of other conventions which, in general (1) provide for the exchange of information necessary to carry out the provisions of the convention and to prevent fraud and tax advoidance; (2) establish a procedure whereby a taxpayer may appeal an action of the United States or Luxembourg which he shows results in double taxation in violation of the convention; and (3) requires that neither the United States nor Luxembourg is to impose more burdensome taxes on citizens of the other country than it imposes upon its own residents. Provision is also made to allow the competent authorities of Luxembourg and the United States to prescribe regulations necessary to carry out the provisions of the convention. 

Effective date

The convention is effective for taxable years beginning on or after January 1 of the calendar year in which the convention is ratified and the instruments of ratification are exchanged. The convention is effective for a period of 5 years, and indefinitely thereafter, but may be terminated by either country at the end of the 5-year period, or at any time thereafter provided 6 months' notice of termination is given. 

STATEMENT OF TREASURY DEPARTMENT CONCERNING PROPOSED TAX CONVENTION BETWEEN THE UNITED STATES AND THE GRAND DUCHY OF LUXEMBOURG WITH RESPECT TO TAXES ON INCOME AND PROPERTY PRELIMINARY COMMENT 

The proposed tax convention between the United States and the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg (hereinafter referred to as "Luxembourg") was signed at Washington on December 18, 1962. It had

---

its inception in the desire of both contracting parties to avoid the effects of the double taxation of income, to prevent fiscal evasion, and to promote trade and investment between the two countries. 

Accordingly, negotiations were conducted during October of 1960 which culminated in substantial agreement on the text of a convention. Thereafter an extensive review of the provisions of such convention was carried on by the Office of the Secretary of the Treasury. This review resulted in a request to the Government of 

Luxembourg for its acceptance of certain modifications in the text of the convention. These modifications were accepted by the 

Government of Luxembourg during discussions held between representatives of the two contracting parties in the fall of 1962. 

PROPOSED CONVENTION Article I 

Article I of the convention contains an enumeration of the taxes which are the subject of the convention. On the U.S. side, the convention covers only the Federal income tax, including surtax, while on the Luxembourg side, the treaty covers the income taxes on individuals and corporations, the tax on fees of directors of corporations, and the communal tax on commercial profits, all of which are income taxes, as well as the wealth tax and the communal taxes on invested capital and land, which are property taxes. The convention also applies to taxes which are subsequently imposed in addition to, or in place of, and which are substantially similar to existing taxes. 

However, before the convention can apply to a new tax, the competent authority of the contracting state adopting the new law or amendment must notify the competent authority of the other state of the change or addition. Once such notification has been given, the question of whether the new taxes constitute taxes which are substantially similar to those already covered by the treaty may be raised under the provisions of article XXI (2) of the convention which specifies that the competent authorities of the two countries may communicate with each other for the purpose of giving effect to the provisions of the treaty.

Article II 

Article II of the convention contains definitions of the terms used in the convention, with a provision that in applying the convention any term not defined shall, unless the context otherwise requires, have the meaning which that term has under the revenue laws of the country imposing tax. The terms "U.S. enterprise" and "Luxembourg enterprise" are defined in the treaty (for purposes of, among other things, the exemptions provided for industrial or commercial profits and income derived from the operation of ships and aircraft) to mean. industrial or commercial enterprises or undertakings carried on by residents or corporations (or U.S. citizens in the case of a U.S. enterprise) of either the United States or Luxembourg, as the case may be. 

The requirement that the enterprise or undertaking be carried on in the treaty country, which is found in a number of income tax conventions to which the United States is a party, is not embodied in this definition. The Government of Luxembourg was opposed to the addition of this restrictive provision. As a result, a Luxembourg corporation, the business activities of which are carried on wholly outside Luxembourg and which derives industrial or commercial

profits from sources within the United States, would be exempt under article III of the convention from U.S. tax in respect of such profits if it maintains no permanent establishment in the United States at any time during the taxable year.

As in other treaties to which the United States is party, the "creation or organization test" is used to define a U.S. corporation. The term "Luxembourg corporation", however, is defined as a juridical person, or an entity treated as a juridical person for purposes of Luxembourg tax, if such person or entity has its business management or seat in Luxembourg. Under the laws of Luxembourg a corporation having its business management or seat in Luxembourg is subject to Luxembourg tax in respect of income from all sources (sec. 1 of the Luxembourg corporation tax law). Luxembourg law defines business management as the principal center of management of the business, and the seat is determined by reference to the corporate charter or articles of association or, lacking any mention of seat therein, is considered to be the place where the business management or administration of the corporation is carried on (sec. 15 of the 

Luxembourg tax applicability law). Inasmuch as the tests of "business management" and "creation or organization" are not mutually exclusive, under the tax laws of the United States and Luxembourg, a corporation which was incorporated in the United States and which had its business management in Luxembourg could be subject to tax in both Luxembourg and the United States on all its income. For this reason, it was provided in the convention that the term "Luxembourg corporation" does not include a U.S. corporation.

Subparagraph (f) of paragraph (1) of article II of the convention provides a comprehensive definition of the term "permanent establishment" which is based upon, and, except for certain minor variations, is identical with article 5 of the model tax convention being prepared by the Fiscal Committee of the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), of which both the United States and Luxembourg are member nations. In general it conforms to the definition of a permanent establishment found in a number of income tax conventions concluded by the United States, but does contain certain significant departures from the usual definition. One such departure is that under the OECD model tax convention the conduct of any one of five enumerated groups of activities within a treaty country will not constitute the maintenance of a permanent establishment in such country even if such activities are carried on through a fixed place of business. These permissible activities are covered under article II (1) (f) (iii) (A) through (E) of the convention. Whether only one or more than one of these activities can be engaged in by an enterprise through a fixed place of business without thereby constituting that fixed place of business a permanent establishment of the enterprise has not as yet been fully resolved. The Governments of the United States and Luxembourg are prepared to accept the view that all of the activities enumerated in subparagraph (iii) may be conducted by an enterprise without its being held to have a permanent establishment. However, in the interest of obtaining uniformity among member countries of the OECD, agreement has been reached between the two Governments that, in interpreting this provision, due consideration will be given to the construction adopted by the OECD.

The affirmative portions of the definition of a permanent establishment contained in article II(1)(f)(ii) are generally similar to those found in the majority of our income tax conventions. An exception to this similarity is found in the inclusion of the term "a place of management" as a permenent establishment. The inclusion of this term, as explained in the OECD commentary, is to make clear that there may be cases where a permanent establishment exists even though there is no "office" as such where the effective management of an enterprise is exercised. Thus, a hotel room could constitute a "place of management" if management decisions were rendered at such a place on a continuing basis. On the other hand, it was agreed that the use of a hotel room for a limited period of time by a director or executive official of an enterprise would not consitute a place of management even if certain important management decisions with regard to the conduct of the affairs of the enterprise are made in such hotel room during such period of time. In this connection, there is attached to this memorandum an exchange of letters between officials of the two Governments on the interpretation to be given to the language involved.

The fixed places of business which are to be considered permanent establishments also include "a building site, or construction or assembly project, which exists for more than 6 months." Generally similar provisions are found in the income tax conventions concluded by the United States with Austria and the Federal Republic of Germany and in the protocol to the income tax convention with Japan which was signed on August 14, 1962, and which is awaiting ratification. It is to be noted that article 5(2)(g) of the OECD model convention provides that a permanent establishment includes such site or project existing "for more than 12 months." The 6-month rule contained in the present convention had its inception in the desire of the Government of Luxembourg to retain its statutory rule, which was recently changed from 12 to 6 months, for purposes of the treaty (art. 14 of the Luxembourg law of Apr. 7, 1959). Similar 6-month rules are contained in the tax conventions concluded by Luxembourg with the Federal 

Republic of Germany and France. 

Ünder article II(1)(f)(vi), a corporation of one country is not considered to be a permanent establishment of a corporation of the other country merely because it is a subsidiary of the corporation of the other country or merely because it is the parent corporation of the corporation of the other country. Nor shall a corporation of one treaty country be held to have a permanent establishment in the other country merely because it is controlled by a corporation of a country not a party to this convention although that third-country corporation carries on business in the other treaty country. This means that the mere fact that U.S. corporation, more than 50 percent of the voting stock of which is held by a Luxembourg corporation or which itself holds more than 50 percent of the voting stock of a Luxembourg corporation, shall not be taken into account in determining whether the activities or fixed place of business of the U.S. corporation constitute a permanent establishment of the Luxembourg corporation. In addition, the fact that a U.S. corporation, more than 

50 percent of the voting stock of which is held, for example, by a French corporation or which itself holds more than 50 percent of the voting stock of a French corporation, which French corporation hap-

pens to be doing business in Luxembourg (whether through a permanent establishment or otherwise), will not, in and of itself, constitute grounds for the Luxembourg tax authorities to find that the U.S. corporation has a permanent establishment in Luxembourg. 

This treaty recognition of the principle of regarding different, legal entities as separate and distinct for purposes of granting treaty benefits which depend upon the permanent establishment doctrine certainly constitutes no novel approach to the formulation of U.S. income tax conventions. The principle has rather been expanded to cover a broader scope of intercorporate relationships. 

Article II of the convention also contains certain other definitions of terms used in the convention, such as "competent authority" or "competent authorities." The position adopted in the majority of income tax conventions to which the United States is a party that no express definition of residence be included in the convention and that that concept should be determined by the treaty country where residence is claimed has been adhered to here with respect to residence in the United States but not as to residence in Luxembourg. Unde article II(1) (h) an individual is to be considered a resident of Luxembourg if under the laws of Luxembourg such individual's income both from sources within and from sources without Luxembourg is subject to Luxembourg income tax. Under present Luxembourg law, individuals subject to tax on income from all sources are those domiciled, or having habitual abode, in Luxembourg. An individual is domiciled in Luxembourg for tax purposes if he has a dwelling there under circumstances giving rise to the presumption that he will retain and make use of such dwelling (sec. 13 of the Luxembourg tax applicability law). Habitual abode is established by an individual who remains in Luxembourg for a period exceeding 6 months (sec. 14(1) of the 

Luxembourg tax applicability law). Officials of the Luxembourg 

Government on duty abroad, and the wives and minor children of such officials who generally form a part of his household, are deemed to have their habitual abode in Luxembourg for tax purposes (sec.) 

14(2) of the Luxembourg tax applicability law). It should be reiterated at this point, however, that the United States retains the right to tax its citizens and residents as if the convention had not come into effect under the provisions of article XVI (1) (a) of the convention and that residence in the United States is to be determined under the laws of the United States. Individuals who are both residents of 

Luxembourg and citizens of the United States, for instance, would, therefore, not be entitled for purposes of U.S. tax to receive those benefits of the treaty which accrue to residents of Luxembourg. 

Article II(1) (h) also provides that the individual residents of one country who are present in the other country solely for one of the purposes specified in articles XIII and XIV of the convention, relating to exemptions from the tax of such other country in favor of teachers, professors, research assistants, students, business apprentices, trainees, and recipients of giants and awards, shall not be considered to be residents of the other country merely because they are present there for such purposes.

Article II(2) which contains language found in a majority of the income tax conventions to which the United States is a party provides that, where one treaty country is applying the treaty, any undefined term shall have the meaning which it has under the tax laws

of that country. An exception to this general rule has traditionally been made where no definition of the concept of residence is provided in the treaty. The term "resident" is then to be defined in accordance with the laws of the treaty country where residence is claimed. 

Thus, for purposes of the imposition of tax under the treaty by Luxembourg, a "resident" of the United States, which term is nowhere defined in the present convention, means an individual citizen or alien who is resident in the United States for purposes of the imposition of 

U.S. tax. 

Article II (3) of the convention incorporates as a provision of the treaty a rule prescribed in the Treasury Department regulations under the income tax conventions to which the United States is a party, which rule was recently upheld by the Tax Court of the United States in Jules Samann (36 TC 1011 (1961), aff'd 313 F. 2d 461). This rule deems a resident or corporation of one country, or an enterprise of that country, to have a permanent establishment in the other country if such resident, corporation, or enterprise has a permanent establishment in such other country at any time during the taxable year in which the income is received. 

Article III 

Article III contains the provision relating to the taxation of industrial or commercial profits derived by an enterprise of one country from its business activities in the other country. Under this convention, such profits are exempt from tax in the country where earned if the enterprise maintains no permanent establishment in that country. Where a permanent establishment is maintained in one of the treaty countries, however, tax may be imposed by that country only on so much of the industrial or commercial profits of the enterprise as are attributable to that permanent establishment. This rule, which is consistent with Luxembourg's present system of taxing the business income of foreign enterprises (sec. 49(2) of the Luxembourg income tax law; section 2 of 'the Luxembourg corporation tax law), has been incorporated in other tax conventions concluded by Luxembourg and in article 7 of the model tax convention being prepared by the Fiscal Committee of the OECD. Under this rule, foreign enterprises are not taxed on the basis of the source of their income but rather on the basis of whether the business income is realized through a permanent establishment situated in the taxing country. Thus, a foreign enterprise maintaining a permanent establishment in Luxembourg and deriving income through such establishment from the sale of its product in another foreign country would be subject to Luxembourg tax in respect of such income. On the other hand, under 

U.S. law a foreign corporation engaged in trade or business within the United States is subject to U.S. tax only with respect to its income from sources within the United States under section 882(b) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954, which income can include certain items of income other than those attributable to the trade or business carried on by the corporation in the United States. However, not all of the income which is attributable to that trade or business is necessarily subject to U.S. tax. For example, income derived by a foreign corporation from the purchase of personal property in the United States and its sale without the United States is not considered to be U.S. source income even if that foreign corporation handles such sale through a branch located in the United States. As a result of

these divergent features of the laws of both countries, the Government of the United States considered it necessary to include another sentence in article III(1) of the convention providing that, for purposes of U.S. tax, all the industrial and commercial profits of a Luxembourg enterprise from sources within the United States shall be deemed attributable to the permanent establishment maintained by that enterprise.

Article III of the convention also embodies the usual provisions requiring attribution to a permanent establishment of the industrial or commercial profits which it might be expected to make if it were an independent enterprise dealing on an independent basis with its home office, allowing certain expenses as deductions in determining the profits subject to tax and providing that no profits are to be attributed to a permanent establishment by reason of the mere purchase of goods or merchandise for the enterprise. 

Article IV 

Article IV of the convention contains a provision common to the income tax conventions to which the United States is a party authorizing the allocation of business income between the two treaty countries where an enterprise of one country participates in the management of an enterprise of the other country or where they are commonly controlled. This reallocation is to be made in accordance with the principles of section 482 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954 affecting adjustment of accounts as between related enterprises. 

Article V 

Article V of the convention provides for the exemption from taxation by one country of the income which an enterprise of the other country derives from the operation of ships or aircraft registered in that other country. Where a Luxembourg enterprise, for instance, derives U.S. source income from the operation of ships or aircraft registered in Luxembourg, such income shall be exempt from U.S. tax regardless of whether the enterprise is engaged in trade or business in the United States through a permanent establishment situated therein and regardless of whether the ships or aircraft operated by such enterprise are also owned by it. This article is consistent with the provisions of sections 872(b) (1) and (2) and 883 (1) and (2) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954. For purposes of the exemption granted under article V, however, dividends paid by a Luxembourg corporation which operates ships or aircraft will not be considered to be income from the operation of ships or aircraft. 

Article VI 

Article VI of the convention in general provides that real property income shall be taxable only in the country where the real property in question is situated, subject, of course, to the provisions of article XVI of the convention reserving to each country the right to tax its individual residents and corporations (and citizens in the case of the United States) as if the convention had not come into effect. For this purpose, income from real property includes gains derived from the sale or exchange of such property, interest on debts (other than bonds) secured by mortgages on real property, and royalties in respect of the operation of mines, quarries, and other natural resources. vided, however, that a resident or corporation of one country deriving It is pro-

such income from sources within the other country may elect for any taxable year to be subject to the tax which that other country imposes in respect of that income on a net income basis. Corresponding provisions are found in tax conventions between the United States and other foreign nations.

In the absence of such a provision, a Luxembourg resident or corporation not engaged in trade or business in the United States would be subject to the imposition of U.S. tax at the rate of 30 percent of the gross amount of such real property income or natural resource royalties and would receive no relief for expenses incurred with respect to such income. In such cases, such resident or corporation may elect, under this article, to be taxed as if engaged in trade or business in the United States either under the provisions of section 1 in the case of individuals, at the progressive rates prescribed therein or under those of section 11, in the case of corporations, at normal and surtax rates. However, only those expenses which are allocable to the real property income derived from U.S. sources during the taxable year may be deducted. 

A similar election is included under article III of the protocol to the income tax convention between the United States and Japan which was signed in Tokyo on May 7, 1960.

For purposes of the imposition of Luxembourg tax, no change is brought about by the election provision of article VI. Foreign corporations and nonresident individuals, whether or not carrying on their activities in Luxembourg through a permanent establishment, are by statute allowed deductions against their income from real property situated in Luxembourg to the extent attributable thereto (sec. 

50(1) of the Luxembourg income tax law).

The treatment of interest on debts (other than bonds) secured by mortgages on Luxembourg real property under article VÍ reflects the principle of taxation found in Luxembourg (and in several other European nations) whereby such interest is subject to Luxembourg tax when paid to a foreign corporation or nonresident individual (sec. 49(5) of the Luxembourg income tax law). This principle has also been incorporated into the real property income articles contained in the tax conventions concluded by the United States with Austria, the Federal Republic of Germany, and the Netherlands. 

This type of income is, of course, excluded from the provision of this convention (art. VIII) granting the usual treaty exemption for interest payments.

Article VII Article VII of the convention contains a provision granting re ciprocal exemption in respect of royalties, rentals, and similar payments for the use of, or the privilege of using, copyrights, artistic or scientific works, patents, designs, plans, secret processes or formulas, trademarks, motion picture films, films or tapes for radio or television broadcasting, or other like property or rights where the recipient resident or corporation of one country does not have a permanent establishment in the other country. This article is broader in scope than those found in other income tax conventions to which the United States is a party in that, in addition to the above-described amounts, payments for the use of, or the privilege of using, industrial, commercial or scientific equipment, knowledge, experience, skill, or know-how have been explicitly included within the category of income for which exemption is to be granted. This broader exemption

provision conforms generally to that proposed in article 12 of the model tax convention being prepared by the Fiscal Committee of the OECD. 

In practice, the inclusion of the new language will probably have little effect upon the imposition of U.S. tax as such tax is imposed under other income tax conventions containing narrower provisions. Payments in respect of these items, which are often referred to as "knowhow royalties," have generally been considered by the U.S. tax authorities to be in the nature of royalty income, the source of which depends upon the place where the property or right for which payment is being made are used (Rev. Rul. 55-17, C.B. 1955–1, 388). Where a treaty provision granted exemption in respect of payments for the use of copyrights, patents, etc., and other like property or rights, the exemption applied to such payments as are made in respect of knowhow by reason of the fact that the right to use know-how was considered not to be materially different from the right to use trademarks, secret processes, and formulas, in spite of its generally nonpatentable nature.

Revenue Rule 55-17 has, however, been modified in certain material respects by Revenue Rule 64-56, IRB 1964-8, 9. Nevertheless, to the extent that a payment in respect of know-how is no longer considered as royalty income under U.S. law, the provisions of article VII will continue to grant the treaty exemption in respect of that income. 

Therefore, in such cases, the inclusion of this additional language conveying broader coverage under the royalty article will be significant. 

In no event, however, shall the provisions of this article be construed to grant exemption from U.S. tax on gain derived from the sale or exchange of any of the property or rights enumerated therein. 

For purposes of Luxembourg tax, article VII provides exemption of the items of income covered from Luxembourg tax otherwise imposed. 

In the absence of such a provision, the tax imposed by Luxembourg on nonresident individuals and foreign corporations would be withheld at the source at the rate of 12 percent in the case of industrial royalties and 10 percent in the case of copyright royalties. 

Article VIII 

Article VIII, relating to the taxation of interest income, provides for exemption from the tax otherwise imposed by the country of source on interest on bonds, notes, debentures, securities, or any other form of indebtedness (exclusive of interest on debts, other than bonds, secured by mortgages on real property, which income is treated as real property income under article VI for purposes of the convention) where the recipient resident or corporation of the other country does not maintain a permanent establishment in the source country. In the absence of this provision, U.S. tax in respect of these items of interest income would be imposed at a rate of 30 percent of the gross amount thereof, while Luxembourg tax on most interest items is withheld at the source at the rate of 5 percent of the gross amount thereof. However, even without the convention, no Luxembourg tax is withheld against interest paid by a Luxembourg corporation which qualifies as a holding company under Luxembourg law. 

Article IX 

Article IX of the convention, relating to the taxation of dividends, provides for reciprocal reductions in the rates of tax to be imposed by both contracting states in respect of dividends received from sources 

---

within one state by residents and corporations of the other state if the dividend recipient does not have a permanent establishment in the source country. Where dividends within Luxembourg are received by a U.S. resident or corporation, such dividends will be taxable at a rate of 7.5 percent of the gross amount thereof. No Luxembourg tax is withheld against dividends paid by a Luxembourg corporation which qualifies as a holding company (art. 2 of the decree law of Aug. 17, 1945). Dividends from sources within the United States which are received by Luxembourg residents or corporations will be subject to U.S. tax at a rate not in excess of 15 percent of the gross amount thereof.

In the case of a corporate recipient of dividends from sources within one of the treaty countries, a greater reduction in the statutory rate (to 5 percent) is provided by subparagraph (b) of paragraph (1) of article IX if (i) the recipient corporation does not have a permanent establishment in the country of source; (ii) such corporation owns, during the part of the payer corporation's taxable year which precedes the date of payment of the dividend and during the whole of its prior taxable year, at least 50 percent of the voting stock of the payer corporation either alone or in association with not more than three other corporations of the other country which in turn each own at least 10 percent of the voting stock of that payer corporation; (iii) and, not more than 25 percent of the gross income of the payer corporation (other than a corporation the principal business of which is the making of loans) for such prior taxable year is derived from interest and dividends other than interest and dividends received from its subsidiary corporations. The term "subsidiary corporation," for purposes of the latter restriction, means any corporation, of which at least 50 percent of the total voting power of all classes of stock entitled to vote, or of the total value of all classes of stock, is owned by the payer corporation. Similar reductions in the rate of tax otherwise imposed on dividends are contained in article VI A of the income tax convention, as amended, between the United States and Norway and in article IV of the protocol, signed August 14, 1962, to the income tax convention between the United States and Japan. 

Article X 

Article X provides that dividends and interest paid by a Luxembourg corporation to a person other than a citizen, resident, or corporation of the United States will not be subject to U.S. tax and dividends and interest paid by a U.S. corporation to a person other than a resident or corporation of Luxembourg will not be subject to Luxembourg tax. Corresponding provisions are found in the majority of tax conventions entered into by the United States. This provision exempts dividends and certain items of interest income which are considered to constitute income from sources within the United States (through the application of secs. 861(a)(1)(B) and 861(a)(2) (B) and (C) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954) from U.S. tax if such dividends and interest are paid by a Luxembourg corporation to a nonresident alien individual or a foreign corporation. The fact that the recipient nonresident alien individual or foreign corporation may have a permanent establishment in the United States has no effect upon the application of this exemption. Were this provision not included within the convention, the recipient nonresident alien individual or foreign corporation would be liable to U.S. tax.

The exemption provided under this article applies only to dividends and interest paid by Luxembourg corporations. The liability for 

U.S. tax with respect to dividend or interest income paid by other foreign corporations to Luxembourg residents or corporations is governed by the provisions of either the Internal Revenue Code of 1954 or of other tax conventions concluded by the United States. 

For instance, a dividend paid by a German company to an individual resident of Luxembourg (other than a citizen of th United States) is exempt through the application of article XIV(1) of the income tax convention between the United States and Germany, whereas a dividend paid to such resident by a Swiss corporation is taxable under the revenue laws of the United States since it is not exempt under the present convention nor under article XIV of income tax convention between the United States and Switzerland, which provides exemption from U.S. tax with respect to dividends and interest paid to resident alien individual only if such individual is a resident of Switzerland. non- 

Article XI 

Article XI of the convention contains a provision granting exemption from the tax of one country in respect of wages, salaries, and similar compensation and public pensions, annuities, or similar benefits paid by the other country (or by its political subdivisions) to an individual for services rendered to that other country in the discharge of governmental functions. For purposes of the imposition of U.S. tax, the exemption applies to those items of income paid by Luxembourg, its political subdivisions, or its compulsory social security funds to an individual who is neither a citizen of the United States nor an alien who has been admitted to the United States for permanent residence therein. The concept of admission for permanent residence in the United States has reference to persons who have the status of immigrants in the United States. This provision is based upon the exemption granted by virtue of section 893 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954 in respect of wages, fees, and salaries of employees of foreign governments or international organizations. This statutory exemption, however, does not apply where the foreign employee concerned files the waiver, provided for in section 247(b) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 66 Stat. 163, 218, of all rights, privileges, exemptions, and immunities under any law or any executive order which would otherwise accrue to him because of the acquisition of an occupational status entitling him to a nonimmigrant status. Under that act, each employee of a foreign government must either waive such rights or lose his classification as an immigrant. Exemptions which are granted under treaties concluded by the United States, however, are not abrogated by the filing of such a waiver since the right thus granted does not depend upon a law or Executive order. 

In view of this interpretation of the provisions of the Immigration and Nationality Act, which is contained in section 1.893-c(2) of the income tax regulations, it was deemed necessary by the U.S. Government to include, within the scope of this and other income tax conventions more recently concluded, language providing in substance that such conventions do not result in a vitiation of the effect of waivers filed under section 247(b) of the Immigration and Nationality Act. Among the other income tax conventions which include language preserving the effect of these waivers are those entered into by the

United States with the Federal Republic of Germany, Honduras, Italy, Japan, and Pakistan.

In addition to providing relief similar to that granted under section 

893 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954, article XI exempts from one treaty country's tax pensions, annuities, or similar benefits paid. by the other treaty country for services rendered to that country. 

As an example, a French national who is not also a citizen of the United States and who is employed by the Luxembourg Embassy in Washington is exempt from U.S. tax in respect of the salary which is paid to him by the Government of Luxembourg, and will be exempt from such tax in respect of the pension he later receives by reason of his present employment, unless he was admitted to the United States for permanent residence. These exemptions shall apply regardless of whether the individual is considered to be a resident alien of the 

United States for purposes of U.S. tax.

Paragraph (2) of article XI provides that private pensions and private life annuities from sources within one country which are paid to residents of the other country shall be exempt from tax by the country of source notwithstanding the fact that the recipient resident may have a permanent establishment in the country of source at some time during the taxable year. Similar provisions are contained in numerous income tax conventions concluded by the United States. 

Paragraph (3) of article XI provides that, for purposes of paragraph (2) of article XI, the term "life annuities" means a stated sum payable periodically at stated times during life, or during a specified number of years, under an obligation to make the payment in return for adequate and full consideration.

Article XII 

Article XII of the convention provides under paragraph (1) that compensation for labor or personal services performed in the United: 

States by a resident of Luxembourg shall be exempt from tax by the United States if such resident is temporarily present in the United States for a period or periods not exceeding a total of 180 days during the taxable year and if the compensation is received for labor or personal services performed as an employee of a resident or corporation of Luxembourg, or of a permanent establishment within Luxembourg of a U.S. enterprise, and the burden of such compensation is borne by the resident, corporation, or permanent establishment employing the individual. So long as the individual performs the services for any of the above-specified employers and the burden of his compensation is borne by such employer, the amount of the compensation is immaterial. However, if the services are not performed for any of the specified employers, or the burden of the compensation is not borne by the employer, exemption is granted under this article only if the amount of the compensation does not exceed $3,000. Where the total amount of compensation is more than $3,000, the treaty exemption is lost and the full amount of personal service income from sources within the United States remains taxable as if the convention had not come into effect. Corresponding provisions are found in other income tax conventions to which the United States is a party. The burden of an individual's compensation will not be considered to be borne by a resident, corporation, or permanent establishment employing the individual, where, for example, the compensation is deducted

from the gross income of the employer corporation under article 

III (3) of this convention. 

Compensation for labor or personal services does not include fees of directors of corporations for purposes of the application of this article by reason of the fact that Luxembourg was unwilling to relinquish its taxation in this area. This reservation of the right to tax the fees of directors of corporations is in conformity with the provisions of article 16 of the model convention being prepared by the Fiscal Committee of the OECD. This reservation of the right to tax has reference to a special tax presently imposed by Luxembourg at the rate of 20 percent in respect of the gross amount of fees paid by Luxembourg corporations to resident or nonresident directors of such corporations (the Luxembourg law of Mar. 28, 1934). The payer corporation is required to withhold such tax at the source (the decree law of Mar. 31, 1939). The tax is applied in respect of nonresidents regardless of where the services as a director are performed or put to use. In the case of nonresident directors, tax is withheld at the rate of 28 percent. The additional 8 percent which is retained at the source does not represent withholding of this special tax on fees of directors but rather of income tax. For purposes of the imposition of Luxembourg income tax, only the net amount of the director's fee is includible in the director's gross income. Where a nonresident director is subject to assessment of income tax in Luxembourg, which is only the case if the amount of fees paid during the taxable year exceeds a certain sum of money, credit is allowed for 8 percent of the gross fee which was withheld by the payer corporation. Where assessment is not made, the 28-percent withholding of tax constitutes satisfaction both of the special tax and of income tax in respect of the fee.

Paragraph (2) of article XII provides that Luxembourg shall grant the relief provided under paragraph (1) of this article in the case of residents of the United States performing labor or personal services in Luxembourg. Luxembourg tax, however, is imposed on income derived in respect of services both performed and put to use in Luxembourg (secs. 49 (3) and (4) of the Luxembourg income tax law).

Where the services are performed in Luxembourg, paragraph (2) of this article would apply. This would not, however, be the case if the product of services rendered in the United States were put to use in Luxembourg. If, for example, a lawyer residing in the United States were to prepare a memorandum of law for a client in Luxembourg, such services being entirely performed in the United States, the fee paid by the client in Luxembourg would be subject to Luxembourg tax inasmuch as the lawyer's work product is apparently deemed to have been put to use in Luxembourg. In order to provide relief from 

Luxembourg tax in these cases, paragraph (3) was added to article XII. This provision exempts compensation for labor or personal services (again exclusive of fees of directors of corporations) performed in the United States by a resident of the United States from Luxembourg tax whether or not such services are put to use in Luxembourg.

Under this paragraph, the fee paid to the lawyer in the above example would be exempt from Luxembourg tax.

Paragraph (4) of article XII merely provides that, for purposes of U.S. tax, the rule embodied in paragraph (3) shall apply with respect to compensation for services performed in Luxembourg by a resident of Luxembourg. This additional paragraph provides the desired

reciprocity in the treatment of personal service income under this treaty, although under the revenue laws of the United States a nonresident alien individual is taxable only in respect of compensation he receives for labor or personal services performed in the United States. 

Article XIII 

Article XIII of this convention provides that a resident of one country who, at the invitation of a university, college, school, or other recognized educational institution situated in the other country, is temporarily present in the other country solely for the purpose of teaching, or engaging in research, or for both purposes, at that educational institution shall be exempt from the tax of the other country on his remuneration for such teaching or research. This exemption, however, is granted only for a period not exceeding 2 years from the date of the individual's arrival in the other country. This article conforms, in general, to the so-called "professor and teacher articles" which are found in numerous income tax conventions to which the United States is a party. It is to be noted that, under the provisions of article II(1)(h) of this convention, an individual present in one of the treaty countries solely for the purposes specified in article XIII shall not be considered a resident of that country merely because of his presence there for such purposes. Under this rule a resident of 

Luxembourg temporarily present in the United States at the invitation of a university for the sole purpose of teaching shall not lose the exemption granted under article XIII merely because that temporary presence in the United States exceeds the period of time beyond which, under the Internal Revenue Code of 1954 and regulations, the tax authorities of the United States may otherwise make a determination that residence exists in the United States. 

Under this article, a resident of Luxembourg temporarily present in the United States solely for the purpose of engaging in research at the educational institution which invited him to come to the United States shall not be entitled to exemption from U.S. tax with respect to remuneration paid to him for that portion of the research activities which he carries on for the benefit of any individual, partnership, or corporation other than that educational institution. 

Article XIV 

Under paragraph (1) of article XIV of the convention, a student, business apprentice, or recipient of a grant who is a resident of one of the treaty countries and who is temporarily present in the other country solely in such capacity is granted exemption from the tax of the other country in respect of his remuneration from abroad for employment or remittances from abroad for the purposes of his maintenance, education, or training. The exemption in favor of business apprentices applies only where such individuals are present in the other country for a period not exceeding 1 year.

Paragraph (2) of this article provides that an individual resident of one country who is temporarily present in the other country for a period not exceeding 1 year, as an employee of, or under contract with, an enterprise or charitable organization of his country of residence, solely to acquire technical, professional, or business experience from a person other than that enterprise or organization shall be exempt from tax by the other country with respect to that portion of his remuneration for the prescribed period which does not exceed $5,000 or

its equivalent in Luxembourg currency. The excess of such amount remains subject to tax by the host country. For example, a resident of Luxembourg who is temporarily present in the United States for no more than 1 year as an employee of a Luxembourg corporation solely to acquire business experience from its U.S. parent corporation and who receives $12,000 for that year from the Luxembourg corporation in remuneration for the services he rendered in the United States must include $7,000 in his gross income for purposes of U.S. tax, the first $5,000 being exempt from U.S. tax under the treaty. 

Paragraph (3) of this article provides for exemption from tax in respect of amounts up to $10,000 paid as remuneration where an individual resident of one country is temporarily present in the other country for a period of no more than 1 year solely for the purpose of training, research, or study under arrangements with the government of the other country. As in paragraph (2) of this article, the individual resident of one country must be temporarily present in the other country solely for the specified purposes. In addition, the training, research, or study carried on by the individual must be undertaken as a result of arrangements which he has entered into, or which have been made on his behalf, with the government of the other country. For purposes of U.S. tax, this requirement has reference, but is not limited, to programs established by the U.S. Government for training, research, or study, such as training by the military and Armed Forces, study of U.S. budgetary or financial policies under the auspices of the appropriate agencies of the U.S. Government, and training under the various technical assistance programs offered by the U.S. 

Government to aliens. 

Paragraph (4) of this article provides that an individual who qualifies for exemption under more than one provision of paragraphs (1), (2), and (3) of this article or under either paragraph (1), (2), or (3) of this article and either article XII, relating to personal service compensation, or article XIII, relating to professors and research assistants, shall be entitled to claim the exemption most favorable to him. 

For purposes of the application of article XIV, it should be mentioned that, as under article XIII, an individual present in one of the treaty countries solely for the purposes specified in article XIV shall not be considered a resident of that country merely because of his presence there for such purposes. This rule would permit a student or grantee who is a resident of Luxembourg to enjoy exemption from U.S. tax under paragraph (1) of article XIV notwithstanding the fact that he may be studying at a university in the United States for a degree which requires temporary presence in the United States for 2 years or more if he is temporarily present in the United States solely for the purpose of pursuing his course of study. 

Provisions similar in scope to those found in the present article of this convention were included in the income tax conventions now in force between the United States and Honduras and the United States and Pakistan.

Article XV 

Article XV of the convention contains a provision which has not traditionally been included in income tax conventions to which the United States is a party. A similar provision is, however, found in the protocol of October 23, 1963, which modifies the extension to the Netherlands Antilles of the income tax convention between the United

States and the Netherlands and which is presently awaiting ratification. Article XV provides for the denial of all treaty benefits in respect of income derived by Luxembourg holding companies, income derived from Luxembourg holding companies by their shareholders, and also income derived by other corporations, and by their shareholders, where such corporations receive tax benefits from Luxembourg, which are substantially similar to the special tax benefits presently granted to Luxembourg holding companies, under any law enacted by Luxembourg after the date of signature of the present convention.

At present, the laws of Luxembourg exempt holding companies (companies which hold investments but neither engage in a trade or business nor maintain an office which is open to the public) from all direct taxes otherwise imposed by Luxembourg, and dividends and interest paid by such holding companies are not subject to the withholding of Luxembourg income tax at the source. In addition, the 

Government of Luxembourg is empowered to apply special rules for purposes of the imposition of Luxembourg tax in respect of holding companies, the capital of which amounts to at least 1 billion Luxembourg francs and which have received this capital from a foreign country.

Inasmuch as these laws allow investment income from domestic and foreign sources to be accumulated by these holding companies and distributed to nonresident individuals and foreign corporations without the imposition of Luxembourg tax, it was deemed appropriate not to grant any of the exemptions from, or reductions in the rate of, U.S. tax otherwise available under this convention with respect to such income. A comparable provision is found in the final protocol to the tax convention concluded by the Federal Republic of Germany with Luxembourg.

A further limitation upon the application of the convention has been stipulated in the second and third sentences of article XV through the denial of the benefits of the convention with respect to the income of corporations other than holding companies, such as operating companies, and with respect to income derived from such operating companies by any shareholders thereof, if these corporations are granted substantially similar benefits under any law enacted by 

Luxembourg after the date of signature of the convention. As an example of what is meant by the term "substantially similar benefits,' substantial reduction in the rate of, or exemption from, Luxembourg tax in respect of investment or business income from sources outside Luxembourg would, in general, constitute such benefits. By virtue of the third sentence of article XV, however, the term "substantially similar benefits" does not include tax reduction or exemption granted to any corporation in respect of dividends derived from another corporation, 25 percent or more of the stock of which is owned by the recipient corporation. At present, the laws of Luxembourg contain a provision common to those found in the taxation laws of several other countries, through the application of which dividends paid to a Luxembourg corporation by another Luxembourg corporation are exempt from tax otherwise imposed on the recipient corporation where 25 percent of the stock of the payer corporation is owned by such recipient corporation (sec. 9 of the Luxembourg corporation tax law). 

Other countries having such intercorporate dividend provisions include Austria, the Federal Republic of Germany, the Netherlands, and

Canada. Both the Netherlands and Canada, however, grant this favorable treatment in respect of intercorporate dividends to dividends derived by their domestic corporations from foreign corporations, the required percentage of the stock of which is owned by such Netherlands or Canadian corporations (art. 10 of the Netherland corporation tax law and sec. 28(1) of the Canadian Income Tax Act). The tax treaties concluded by the United States with both the Netherlands and Canada do not contain provisions by which any of the benefits of those treaties are denied to such corporations. In view of the acceptance by various nations of this principle with respect to the taxation of intercorporate dividends where there exists a parentsubsidiary relationship and inasmuch as the Luxembourg Government was insistent upon not being restricted by reason of this treaty in respect of possible legislation in this area which it might wish to enact in the future, it was not deemed necessary by the U.S. Government to cease to apply the provisions of the convention in the event that Luxembourg should extend the intercorporate dividend provisions of its law to cover dividends received from foreign corporations. Should 

Luxembourg, however, grant any benefits in respect of such intercorporate dividends which go beyond exclusion in the hands of the recipient corporation, such benefits would no longer necessarily be covered by the negative definition of the otherwise undefined term "substantially similar benefits."

With respect to the application of this article to corporations other than holding companies, the term "substantially similar benefits" would, of course, include the granting of a total exemption in respect of investment income from foreign sources to corporations engaged in the active conduct of a trade or business while continuing to subject the business income of such corporations to tax at normal rates, as well as the imposition of a tax at nominal or substantially reduced rates. The enactment of tax exemptions or rate reductions in favor of corporations manufacturing goods in Luxembourg and selling them in foreign countries would, however, generally not be sufficient to deprive such corporations of the benefits of the treaty. Generally, however, determinations as to the scope of this term's meaning will be made as cases arise which involve the application of the treaty to corporations benefiting from laws enacted by Luxembourg after the date of signature of the present convention. The term was purposely left undefined in the treaty in order to permit such determinations based on the provisions of subsequent Luxembourg legislation. It is to be noted in this regard that under article I(2), the competent authorities of Luxembourg are to notify the competent authorities of the United States of the adoption of new Luxembourg taxes, or of substantial changes in, or the abolition of, existing taxes covered by the present convention. Articles XIX(2) and XXI (2) of this con vention, in addition, provide avenues for the solution of administrative or interpretative problems arising under the convention and for giving effect to its provisions.

Denial of treaty benefits under article XV will have the effect, for purposes of U.S. tax, of rendering taxpayers who derive income covered by its provisions subject to the provisions of the Internal Revenue Code, or, as the case may be, to the provisions of other tax conventions concluded by the United States, as if this convention had not come into force. For example, if a Luxembourg holding company 

S. Ex. Rept. 10, 88-2-5

derives dividends from a U.S. corporation, such dividend income constituting 50 percent or more of such recipient corporation's gross income in such a manner as to meet the technical requirements of section 861 (a) (2) (B) of the Internal Revenue Code 1954, both the dividends it derives and the dividends it distributes remain subject to the imposition of U.S. tax as if this treaty had never been concluded. 

The reduction in the rate of tax provided in article IX does not apply and the exemption provisions of article X do not come into play with respect to dividends paid by such corporations to nonresident alien individuals and foreign corporations. 

Although this approach adopted for the purpose of excluding socalled "tax haven" income from the scope of the convention is not found in any income tax convention concluded by the United States, other than the Netherlands Antilles Protocol, certain precedent may be found in some of the relief provisions contained in the conventions with the United Kingdom, Ireland, Australia, and Pakistan requiring that the recipient of treaty income be "subject to tax" on such income in the country of residence in order to qualify for exemption from, or reduction in the rate of, the tax of the country of source. Moreover, this new provision is consistent with the spirit of the provisions of section 12 of the Revenue Act of 1962 relating to the taxation of certain "tax-haven" income of controlled foreign corporations to U.S. shareholders.

Article XVI 

Article XVI of the convention has relation to the credit granted under the treaty by one country in respect of the taxes of the other country and to certain exclusions granted by Luxembourg in respect of its trade and property taxes. The "saving clause" common to the income tax conventions concluded by the United States under which the United States reserves the right to tax its citizens, individual residents, or corporations as if the convention had not come into effect is incorporated within this article. The credit against U.S. tax which is granted under paragraph (1)(a) for Luxembourg income taxes on individuals and corporations, for the Luxembourg tax on fees of directors of corporations, and for the Luxembourg communal tax on commercial profits, all of which are "income taxes" under U.S. interpretation of such concept, is to be determined in accordance with provisions of the revenue laws of the United States relating to the allowance of the foreign tax credit as such laws may subsequently be modified or amended. This would presently include sections 901 through 905 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954, as amended by the Revenue Act of 1962. For purposes of the computation of the foreign tax credit limitation contained in section 904, however, the source rules contained in article XVII of this convention may possibly result in a credit limitation which is different from that which would be arrived at in the absence of the treaty. Discussion of such variations will follow under the portion of this memorandum dealing with article XVII. 

Under subparagraph (b) of paragraph (1) of this article, Luxembourg reserves the right to tax its residents (including U.S. citizens) and corporations (including U.S. corporations) having their business management or seat in Luxembourg as if the convention had not come into effect. In addition, Luxembourg agrees to grant as a credit

against such taxes the amount of the income tax of the United States which is imposed upon income from sources within the United States. 

The creditable amount, however, may not exceed that proportion of the Luxembourg taxes which the income from sources within the United States and taxable by Luxembourg bears to the entire income subject to the taxes of Luxembourg. This credit is based upon the provisions of section 904 of the U.S. Internal Revenue Code of 1954 

Nevertheless the U.S. source income must not only be taxable by 

Luxembourg, as is the case of the corresponding credit granted by the United States, but must also be taxed by the United States, since the Government of Luxembourg was insistent upon restricting its credit to cases where the income in respect of which credit is to be granted is taxed by both countries. As a result of this limitation, no credit will be allowed by Luxembourg against its tax imposed in respect of the income of a resident of Luxembourg, who is, for instance, also a citizen of the United States, for U.S. tax imposed in respect of U.S. source income which is not also taxable by Luxembourg. In addition, for example, where a Luxembourg corporation, having no permanent establishment in the United States, receives industrial and commercial profits from sources within the United States, which are exempt from U.S. tax by reason of article III (1) of this convention and also dividends paid by a U.S. corporation which are subject to U.S. tax at the rate of 15 percent by reason of article IX(1) (a) of this convention, both the industrial and commercial profits and the dividends are subject to the imposition of Luxembourg tax. If, however, the portion of the total Luxembourg tax which is attributable to the dividends received happens to be less than the tax which is imposed in the United States under the convention, the excess of such U.S. tax may not be credited against the portion of the total Luxembourg tax which is attributable to the industrial and commercial profits since such profits are not taxable in the United States. 

In spite of the fact that the credit granted by Luxembourg does not go as far as that granted by the United States to provide relief from double taxation, it is agreed, under subparagraph (a) of paragraph (1) of this article, that by virtue of this credit Luxembourg satisfies the similar credit requirement prescribed by section 901(b) (3) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954. This provision applies, however, only with respect to taxes paid or accrued to Luxembourg. The question of whether Luxembourg satisfies the similar credit requirement with respect to taxes paid to other foreign nations will await separate determination based upon the provisions of Luxembourg law and treaties and is in no way governed by the present convention. 

Under its law, Luxembourg presently allows no credit against its tax for taxes paid to foreign countries, the only relief in this respect consisting of the allowance of a deduction of foreign taxes from gross income. Tax conventions are now in force between Luxembourg and 

Belgium, France, and the Federal Republic of Germany.

Subparagraph (c) of paragraph (1) of article XVI provides that the "saving clause" shall not be construed to deny the exemption from U.S. tax granted under article XI(1) to residents of the United States (other than U.S. citizens or persons having immigrant status) in respect of governmental salaries, pensions, and annuities. Nor shall such "saving clause" be construed to deny the benefits of article XX(3) of the convention, relating to equal tax treatment by one country of its residents who are also citizens of the other country.

Paragraph (2) of article XVI provides for certain unilateral relief from Luxembourg communal land tax, communal tax on commercial profits, and invested capital, which is formally referred to in Luxembourg as the "trade tax," and wealth tax in respect of its residents, including citizens of the United States, and of corporations having their business management or seat in Luxembourg, including such of these corporations as may also be U.S. corporations. This relief takes the form of an exclusion of certain items of income derived from, or property situated in, the United States, whereas Luxembourg grants credit for U.S. tax under paragraph (1) of this article against its income taxes on individuals and corporations and its tax on fees of directors of corporations. 

Under subparagraph (a) of paragraph (2), any real property situated in the United States which is owned by a Luxembourg resident or by a corporation having its business management or seat in Luxembourg shall be excluded from the basis upon which communal land tax is imposed. The tax is presently imposed by the communal governments of Luxembourg only in respect of real property situated within its territory (sec. 1 (2) and (3) of the Luxembourg land tax law). 

Under subparagraph (b) of paragraph (2), the profits and capital of a permanent establishment situated in the United States of a Luxembourg resident or of a corporation having its business management or seat in Luxembourg shall be excluded from the basis upon which the communal tax on commercial profits and invested capital (the trade tax) is imposed. Under Luxembourg law, trade tax is presently imposed on the basis of territoriality. Both foreign and domestic commercial enterprises are subject to trade tax only on their commercial profits allocable to, and invested capital appropriated to, a fixed place of business maintained in Luxembourg. Thus, residents of 

Luxembourg and corporations having their business management or seat in Luxembourg are, under the internal law of Luxembourg, presently not subject to Luxembourg tax on "the profits and capital of a permanent establishment situated in the United States," at least to the extent that the treaty concept of a "permanent establishment" and the statutory concept of a "fixed place of business" are not at variance. Moreover, the provisions of article XX(2) of this convention will serve to prevent denial of the exclusion granted under the trade tax law of Luxembourg. In addition, if a Luxembourg corporation is deemed to have a permanent establishment in the United States under article II(1)(f) (iv), the profits which it derives through the activities of such an agent will be excluded from the basis upon which trade tax is imposed, even if the statutory requirements for exclusion are not met.

Under subparagraph (c) of paragraph (2), in the case of Luxembourg residents or of corporations having their business management or seat in Luxembourg, certain items of property are excluded from the basis upon which Luxembourg wealth tax is imposed. Luxembourg wealth tax applies in respect of the total property of such residents and domestic corporations (sec. 1(2) of the Luxembourg wealth tax law). Nonresident individuals and foreign corporations are, however, taxable only with respect to their domestic property (sec. 2(2) of the Luxembourg wealth tax law). The exclusions under this subparagraph of the convention are generally based upon the principles under which the income arising from such items of property is treated under this convention. Since income from real property,

including interest on debts (other than bonds) secured by mortgages on real property is taxable in the United States if the real property is located in the United States under article VI, such items of property are, under subdivisions (i) and (ii) excluded from the base on which the wealth tax is computed. Likewise, since industrial or commercial profits attributable to a permanent establishment in the United States remain taxable in the United States under article III(1), subdivision (iii) of this subparagraph provides an exclusion for invested capital of a permanent establishment situated in the United States (and other than a maritime shipping or air transport undertaking). Finally, an exclusion is provided for the invested capital of a maritime shipping or air transport undertaking. This exclusion is directly connected to the treatment of income derived from the operation of ships and aircraft under article V. The income which is exempt from U.S. tax under the provisions of that article, or which is not from sources within the United States, is not included within the numerator of the fraction limiting the amount which is to be excluded under subdivision (iv).

In determining the rate of its wealth tax, however, Luxembourg reserves the right under subparagraph (c) to take into account all items excluded from the tax base pursuant to such subparagraph. 

The provisions of paragraph (2) of article XVI are based in principle upon those embodied in article 22 of the model tax convention being prepared by the Fiscal Committee of the OECD, relating to the taxation of capital. Similar provisions are contained in article 19 of the tax convention between the Federal Republic of Germany and Luxembourg and in article 20 of the tax convention between France and Luxembourg. The reservation by Luxembourg of the right to take into account all property items excluded under the treaty for the purpose of arriving at the proper rate at which to apply the wealth tax is found in article 20(4) of its treaty with the Federal Republic of Germany.

Paragraph (3) of article XVI provides certain additional unilateral relief from Luxembourg wealth tax, communal tax on invested capital (the portion of the trade tax applicable to capital), and communal tax on land in the case of citizens, residents, or corporations of the United States. In principle, this provision affects the imposition of these 

Luxembourg property taxes on nonresident individuals and foreign corporations. Under this paragraph, exemption from the enumerated taxes is granted in respect of certain property items unless Luxembourg is entitled under other provisions of the convention to tax the income derived from such property. The relief here is explicity based upon the rules prescribed in the treaty for taxing the income derived from the property items involved. As a result, U.S. citizens who are residents of Luxembourg and U.S. corporations having their business. management or seat in Luxembourg are not entitled to any relief under this paragraph since Luxembourgh may tax their entire income under the "saving clause" of paragraph (1) of this article. This provision has its counterpart in Luxembourg's treaties with France and the Federal Republic of Germany and is based, in principle, upon article 14 of the OECD model treaty. 

The property items excludable under this provision include (1) real property and all accessories appertaining thereto; (2) debts secured by mortgages on real property; and (3) any property used by commercial or industrial enterprises, including maritime shipping or air

transport enterprises. As to the items falling under (1) and (2) above, where the real property involved is situated in Luxembourg, Luxembourg will not grant exemption. Where the real property is located in the United States, however, Luxembourg may impose tax only in respect of U.S. citizens who are residents of Luxembourg and U.S. corporations which have their business management or seat in Luxembourg. This provision is, of course, based directly upon article VI of the convention. 

As to the items of property covered under (3) above, the allowance of exemption by Luxembourg from its trade tax on invested capital and its wealth tax will depend upon a number of considerations. 

Luxembourg tax under the treaty on the income arising from the property used by a U.S. industrial or commercial enterprise is dependent upon the existence, or lack of existence, within Luxembourg of a permanent establishment maintained by that enterprise except where it is a maritime shipping or air transport enterprise, since article V contains no such requirement. Property used in the ordinary trade or business of a Luxembourg permanent establishment would remain subject to tax in Luxembourg by reason of the fact that Luxembourg is entitled under article III(1) of the convention to tax the industrial or commercial profits derived from such property. Where a patent is used in Luxembourg by a U.S. corporation maintaining a permanent establishment in Luxembourg, such patent remains taxable inasmuch as the royalty income in respect of such patent is subject to Luxembourg income tax under article VII. If, however, the patent is used in Luxembourg by a U.S. corporation which does not have a permanent establishment in Luxembourg, it shall be exempt from these Luxembourg property taxes. For purposes of the Luxembourg trade tax on invested capital, this exemption is of little importance since that tax is imposed only with respect to the portion of the property appropriated to the use of a fixed place of business situated in 

Luxembourg (sec. 12(4) of the Luxembourg trade tax law). The

Luxembourg wealth tax, however, is imposed on the domestic property, as defined in the Luxembourg tax evaluation law, of nonresident individuals and foreign corporations (sec. 2(2) of the Luxembourg wealth tax law). Domestic property includes industrial patents and copyrights registered in Luxembourg and utilized in Luxembourg or elsewhere for business purposes, regardless of the existence of a fixed place of business in Luxembourg (sec. 77(4) of the Luxembourg tax evaluation law). Therefore, under the wealth tax statute, a U.S. corporation maintaining no permanent establishment in Luxembourg but owning a patent which is registered in Luxembourg would be liable to wealth tax with respect to such patent (sec. 7(2) of the 

Luxembourg wealth tax law). The provisions of clause (c) of paragraph (3) of article XVI limit Luxembourg's statutory right to tax that corporation with respect to the patent in question (to the extent that they are considered by Luxembourg to be used by a U.S. commercial or industrial enterprise).

Precedent for the unilateral relief granted under paragraphs (2) and (3) of article XVI by Luxembourg in respect of taxes other than income taxes is found in the tax convention concluded by the United States with the Netherlands. It is, of course, to be noted that, under subparagraph (a) of paragraph (1) of this article, the United States in no way obligates itself to grant its foreign tax credit in respect of any of these Luxembourg property taxes.

Article XVII 

Article XVII of the convention contains the source rules to be used by both treaty countries in applying the convention where the source of income is a factor to be taken into consideration under a particular provision of the convention. These treaty source rules are applicable only for purposes of the convention. The treaty provision to which these source rules may most frequently be applied is article XVI (1), under which credit for the tax of one country is granted against the tax of the other country. Such rules are also to be used in applying article 

IX of the convention, relating to the taxation of dividends. In addition, the amount excludable from Luxembourg wealth tax under article XVI (2) (c) (iv) is to be determined with reference to one of these source rules. Mention should, however, be made at this point of the provisions of paragraph (2) of article XX of the convention and of its effect upon the application of the treaty source rules. That paragraph provides that the provisions of the treaty shall not be construed to restrict in any manner any exclusion, exemption, deduction, credit, or other allowance now or hereafter accorded either by the taxation laws of one of the treaty countries or by any other agreement between the treaty countries. As a result, for purposes of the imposition of U.S. tax with respect to a Luxembourg corporation or a nonresident alien individual who is a resident of Luxembourg, these source rules may not be used as a basis for the imposition of tax where no tax would be imposed by reason of the source rules contained in the Internal Revenue Code of 1954. As an example, paragraph (h) of article XVII treats dividends paid by a corporation of one country as being income from sources within that country. 

Under section 861 (a) (2) (A) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954, however, certain dividends even if paid by a domestic corporation are considered to be from sources without the United States. Where a Luxembourg corporation receives such dividends from a U.S. corporation, they are to be excluded from gross income for purposes of U.S. tax notwithstanding the combined provisions of articles IC and XVII(h) inasmuch as under section 882 (b) gross income of any foreign corporation includes only income from sources within the United States. Nor may the treaty source rules affect the computation of the amount allowable as a credit against U.S. tax under the foreign tax credit provisions of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954 where the use of such source rules would result in less favorable treatment to a particular taxpayer than that resulting from the application of the source rules of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954. 

Some examples of the applicability of article XX(2) with regard to the foreign tax credit follow under the discussion of the various treaty source rules.

This article contains provisions fixing the source of most of the items of income in respect of which exemption from, or reduction in the rate of, the tax of both countries has been granted under the convention. These rules tend to follow those found in sections 861 through 863 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954, with the exception of certain minor variations which were introduced in order to bring the source rules into conformity with the corresponding substantive provisions of the convention.

Under paragraph (a), industrial or commercial profits attributable to a permanent establishment which an enterprise of one country has in the other country shall be treated as income from sources within

that other country. This provision is in conformity with the substantive rule, contained in article III(1) of the convention giving the country where the permanent establishment is maintained the right to tax such profits. For example, for purposes of the computation of the per-country limitation under section 904(1) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954, the industrial or commercial profits arising from the sale in France of personal property purchased in the United States which are attributable to the Luxembourg branch of a U.S. corporate taxpayer may be included in the numerator of the limiting fraction as income from sources within Luxembourg even though such profits would constitute income from sources within France under section 

862(a)(6) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954. 

Paragraphs (b) and (c) of article XVII contain what are essentially the source rules embodied in sections 861(a)(6), 862(a)(6), and 

863 (b) (2) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954. The rule contained in paragraph (a) discussed above, however, takes precedence over these two rules where a conflict exists. Where, for instance, the gains, profits, or income of a U.S. corporation from the purchase within the 

United States and the sale through a Luxembourg branch within France of personal property are not considered to constitute industrial or commercial profits, they may not be treated as income from sources within Luxembourg for purposes of the foreign tax credit. Through the operation of article XX(2), however, income from the purchase within the United States and sale within Luxembourg of personal property by that U.S. corporation may be considered to be income from sources within Luxembourg, if the taxpayer so desires, even if Luxembourg determines that such income does not consist of industrial or commercial profits attributable to the corporation's Luxembourg branch.

Paragraph (d) of this article provides that income which is exempt from the tax of one country under article V, relating to the exemption of income from the operation of ships or aircraft, shall be treated as income from sources within the other country. This provision was introduced into the convention for the primary purpose of providing a rule of source to be used by Luxembourg in applying the provisions of article XVI(2)(c)(iv), which is discussed above, relating to exclusion from the basis upon which Luxembourg wealth tax is imposed in respect of the invested capital of maritime shipping or air transport undertakings. For purposes of the computation of the U.S. foreign tax credit, the source rules of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954 would continue to apply where the taxpayer chooses to have them made applicable by reason of provisions of article XX(2). Thus, the income of a U.S. shipping corporation which is derived from the operation of ships registered in the United States and which is exempt from Luxembourg tax under article V is not necessarily to be considered as income from sources within the United States for purposes of the computation of the foreign tax credit limitation contained in section 904 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954, since the provisions of the convention shall not be construed to restrict in any manner any credit now or hereafter accorded by the United States. 

Paragraph (e) of article XVII provides in substance that income which is considered to constitute real property income under article VI of the convention shall be treated as income from sources within the country in which the real property, mines, quarries, or other natural resources are situated. As a corollary thereto, paragraph

(g) provides for a similar determination of source with respect to interest income dealt with in article VIII of the convention. As a result, interest on a debt (other than a bond) secured by a mortgage on real property situated in the United States may be treated as U.S. source income whether or not such interest is paid by a U.S. resident, corporation, or enterprise. Interest on a bond which is so secured, however, constitutes U.S. source income only if the payer is a 

U.S. resident, corporation, or enterprise. These rules are mainly applicable for purposes of the computation of the credit to be granted by Luxembourg under article XVI(1)(b). For purposes of the U.S. 

foreign tax credit, the taxpayer may choose to apply either these source rules or those contained in the Internal Revenue Code of 1954. 

The source rule contained in section 861(a)(1) (B), relating to certain interest paid by U.S. residents and corporations which is not deemed to be income from sources in the United States, shall continue to apply for purposes of the imposition of U.S. tax in respect of any nonresident alien individual or foreign corporation by virtue of article XX(2). 

On the other hand, if Luxembourg were to impose its tax on interest. paid by one U.S. corporation to another U.S. corporation with respect to a note secured by a mortgage on Luxembourg real property, such interest income may be treated as Luxembourg source income for purposes of the application of section 904 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954 despite the provisions of section 861 (a) (1). 

Paragraph (f) of this article provides that income considered to be royalty income under article VII of the convention shall be treated as income from sources within the country where the property, rights, knowledge, experience, skill, or know-how, in respect of which the payments are made, may be used. If any of the items of income so covered would be considered to constitute compensation for personal services for purposes of U.S. tax in the absence of the convention, the taxpayer may treat such income as being either from sources within the country where the services are performed or from sources within the country where the services are used. 

Paragraph (h) provides that dividends paid by a corporation of one of the countries shall be treated as income from sources within that country. The practical effects of this source rule have been mentioned above under the discussion relating to the application of articles IX and X of the convention. The source rule contained in section 

861(a)(2) (A), relating to certain dividends paid by U.S. corporations which do not constitute U.S. source income, shall, by reason of article 

XX(2), continue to apply for purposes of the imposition of U.S. tax on a nonresident alien individual or foreign corporation. Either the statutory or the treaty rule would, however, be available for purposes of the computation of the U.S. foreign tax credit.

Paragraph (i) contains a provision which treats compensation for labor or personal services, including compensation or remuneration from the practice of liberal professions or from public entertainment but not including fees of directors of corporations, as income from sources within the country where the labor or personal services are performed. Under this provision, Luxembourg in substance adopts the U.S. rule of source with regard to such compensation (secs. 861 (a) (3) and 862(a)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954), at least to the extent that directors' fees are not involved. This rule is in conformity with the provisions of article XII of the convention relating to re-

Paragraph (i) contains a special source rule with respect to directors' fees. The formulation of this rule was necessitated by the reluctance of the Luxembourg Government to give up its tax where the corporation paying the fees is a corporation having its business management or seat in Luxembourg. The paragraph treats directors' fees paid by a corporation of one country as constituting income from sources. within that country. As a result, a fee paid by a Luxembourg corporation to a U.S. citizen, who is a director of that corporation, in respect of services performed at a directors' meeting held in the United States, may be treated as Luxembourg source income for purposes of the computation of the U.S. taxpayer's foreign tax credit, since Luxembourg was insistent upon retaining its right to tax these fees under the convention. In addition, where a U.S. citizen, who is a resident of Luxembourg and a director of a U.S. corporation, receives a fee from such corporation for his participation in a directors' meeting held in Luxembourg, the Luxembourg foreign tax credit provided under article XVI(1)(b) in respect of U.S. tax imposed on the fee will apply, since the fee constitutes U.S. source income under this treaty source rule.

Source rules similar to those contained in article XVII are found in the income tax conventions in force between the United States and Austria, Honduras, and Japan.

Article XVIII 

Article XVIII of the convention establishes a system of reciprocal cooperation between the competent authorities of the two countries. 

By virtue of paragraph (1) thereof, conditions are specified under which information may be exchanged to facilitate administration of the treaty or to prevent fraud and the avoidance of tax in cases involving taxes to which the convention relates. The information to be exchanged is that which would be available under the taxation laws of the country to which the request for information is directed if the tax of the requesting country were its own tax. Such information shall not be disclosed by the requesting nation to any persons other than those concerned with the assessment and collection of the taxes. which are the subject of this convention. Certain income tax conventions concluded by the United States specifically include courts as "those concerned with the collection and assessment" of taxes. 

Specific mention was not deemed necessary here inasmuch as agreement was reached between the two Governments during the treaty negotiations that such information may be disclosed to a court. No information is to be exchanged which would disclose any trade, business, industria, or professional secret, or any trade process. 

Paragraph (2) of this article provides that each country may assist the other in the collection of taxes imposed by the such other country to the extent necessary to insure that any exemption or reduced rate of tax granted under the convention by the other country shall not be enjoyed by persons not entitled to such benefits.

Paragraph (3) stipulates that the exchange of information and collection provisions of this article shall not be construed to impose upon either country the obligation to carry out administrative measures at variance with its regulations and practice or which would be contrary to its sovereignty, security, or public policy or to supply

Article XIX 

Article XIX contains certain provisions authorizing the competent authorities of both countries to cooperate with each other in order to make the convention a workable and meaningful document. Paragraph (1) contains the usual treaty provision regarding action to be taken in respect of claims lodged by taxpayers. Under this provision, where a U.S. citizen, resident, or corporation shows proof that the action of the Luxembourg tax authorities has already resulted, or will in all likelihood result, in double taxation contrary to the provisions of this convention, such citizen, resident, or corporation may present his case to the U.S. competent authority, who, if he deems the claim to be worthy of consideration, shall attempt to come to an agreement with the competent authority of Luxembourg with a view to avoidance of such double taxation.

Paragraph (2) of this article provides that the competent authorities of the two States shall endeavor to settle by mutual agreement as quickly as possible any difficulties or doubts as to the interpretation or application of the convention and also any difficulties or doubts in respect of the relation of this convention to conventions concluded by either the United States or Luxembourg with third countries. 

If, for example, an item of income, which is considered by the taxation authorities of the Federal Republic of Germany to constitute German source industrial or commercial profits of a U.S. corporate enterprise having a permanent establishment in the Federal Republic, is also considered by the Luxembourg tax authorities to constitute industrial or commercial profits attributable to another permanent establishment which the same enterprise maintains in Luxembourg, the U.S. competent authority shall endeavor to reach a mutual agreement both with the Luxembourg competent authority and, under article XVII of the income tax convention between the United States and the Federal Republic of Germany, with the German competent authority to settle this case. The fact that the U.S. corporate enterprise has no standing under the tax convention between Luxembourg and the Federal Republic renders this provision of particular value by providing a remedy for what could possibly result in the U.S. 

Government being obligated to give credit for both Luxembourg and German taxes on the same income over a period of years under the provisions of section 904 (d) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954. 

Article XX 

Paragraph (1) of article XX provides that this convention shall not be construed to restrict in any manner the right of diplomatic or consular officers to other or additional exemptions now enjoyed or which may hereafter be granted to such officers. U.S. income tax treaties have generally included such a provision for the preservation,

of any such rights. This provision will be largely important in the future since no consular convention at present exists between the 

United States and Luxembourg.

Paragraph (2) of this article, which has been discussed at some length above, provides in substance that the U.S. tax imposed by reason of this treaty may not be less favorable to a domestic taxpayer, a nonresident alien or a foreign corporation, than that which would be imposed under the Internal Revenue Code of 1954 or under any other agreement between the United States and Luxembourg. The same restriction on the use of this convention also applies for purposes of the imposition of Luxembourg tax. The term "any other agreement" includes the agreement effected by exchange of notes signed at Luxembourg on March 10 and 13, 1952, which entered into force on March 13, 1952, providing relief from taxation by Luxembourg of expenditures in Luxembourg by the United States Government for equipment, facilities, and services for the common defense effort, including expenditures for any U.S. foreign aid program (3 UST 4001; TIAS 

2538). This agreement affords relief in respect of (1) Luxembourg duties and taxes payable on the importation of articles, or of materials or components used in the production of articles purchased by means of such expenditures, (2) Luxembourg turnover tax on certain sales by and to Luxembourg dealers of articles purchased by means of such expenditures, and (3) export and transport taxes imposed by Luxembourg.

The term "any other agreement" would also include the "Agreement Between the Parties to the North Atlantic Treaty Regarding the

Status of Their Forces," a multilateral treaty signed at London on June 19, 1951, which entered into force on August 23, 1953. Article 

X of this treaty has references to the taxation of the military personnel and civilian employees of the armed forces of both countries. 

Paragraph (3) of this article provides that citizens of one of the treaty countries shall not, while resident in the other country, be subject therein to other or more burdensome taxes than are citizens of that other country who reside in that other country. This constitutes the traditional nondiscrimination provision and is the only one in the treaty which applies to taxes imposed by the States of the United States, the District of Columbia, and the local municipalities of the United States. The term "citizen," when used in this paragraph includes all juridical persons, partnerships, and associations created or organized under the laws in force in both countries. This nondiscrimination provision has no application to the imposition of tax by either country with respect to individual citizens, or corporations, of the other country who are not residents of the taxing country.

(sec. 882(a) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954). 

Article XXI 

Article XXI provides that the competent authorities of both treaty countries may prescribe regulations necessary to carry the convention into effect within the respective countries and that they may communicate with each other directly for the purpose of giving effect to the treaty provisions.

Article XXII 

Article XXII provides for the ratification of the conventions and for the exchange of instruments of ratification. The effective date of the convention is also provided in this article. The convention shall have effect for taxable years beginning on or after the first day of January of the calendar year in which the exchange of instruments of ratification takes place. Further provision is made that the convention shall continue effective for a period of 5 years, beginning with the calendar year in which the exchange of instruments of ratification takes place and indefinitely after that 5-year period, but that it may be terminated by either treaty country at the end of the 5-year period or at any time thereafter, provided that at least 6 months' prior notice of termination has been given by one country to the other, in which event the convention would cease to be effective for taxable years beginning on or after the first day of January next following the expiration of the 6-month period.

IV. NETHERLANDS ANTILLES PROTOCOL 1. PURPOSE 

The purpose of the protocol with the Netherlands is to increase the U.S. tax rate on dividends, interest, and royalties received from U.S. sources by Netherlands Antilles investment companies. 

2. BackgroUND 

The basic income tax convention between the United States and the Netherlands was approved by the Senate on June 17, 1948, and the instruments of ratification were exchanged on December 1, 1948. 

Subsequently, on July 29, 1955, the Senate gave its advice and consent to a supplementary protocol extending the application of the basic convention to the Netherlands Antilles. The pending protocol, which modifies and supplements the 1955 protocol, was signed at The Hague on October 23, 1963, and received by the Senate on December 3,.1963. Upon entry into force, it would provide for changes in the 

U.S. tax rate on dividends, interest, and royalties received from U.S. sources by Netherlands Antilles investment companies owned by persons who are not residents of the Netherlands or the Netherlands Antilles. At the present time the Antilles do not impose any income tax on outgoing royalties or on dividends derived from sources therein to nonresidents thereof.

Pursuant to the provisions of sections 871 and 881 of the Internal Revenue Code, the United States normally taxes U.S. source gross income from dividends, interest, and royalties paid to nonresident aliens and to foreign corporations at a minimum withholding rate of 30 percent. Under our income tax convention with the Netherlands, however, interest and royalties received from U.S. sources by a resident or corporation of the Netherlands are exempt from U.S. taxation while dividends are taxed at a reduced rate of 15 percent. 

By reason of extending the applicability of the Netherlands convention to the Netherlands Antilles in 1955, the same tax benefits are now enjoyed by residents and corporations of the Netherlands Antilles. 

---

As a result of this favorable tax atmosphere, individuals residing in countries which do not have income tax conventions with the United States have been forming investment companies in the Netherlands Antilles to hold their U.S. investments and to collect dividends, interest, and royalties from U.S. sources on their behalf. The extent to which our tax treaty with the Netherlands has been used by residents of third countries to invest in the United States was not intended when coverage under the tax convention was extended to the Netherlands Antilles in 1955. Accordingly, it was decided to negotiate another protocol in order to rectify the situation. 

3. PROVISIONS OF PROTOCOL 

The pending protocol contains two substantive articles, the effects of which are to increase the U.S. tax rate on dividends, interest, and royalties paid to Netherlands Antilles investment companies. Under its terms, beginning in 1967 the full 30-percent statutory tax rate on dividends and interest would be applicable for these companies. 

However, in the case of investment companies incorporated in the Netherlands Antilles after May 14, 1963, the statutory rate on dividends and interest will become generally applicable in the first year following the year in which the protocol takes effect. The protocol also provides for an increase to 30 percent in the U.S. tax rate on royalties paid to Netherlands Antilles investment companies. This rate would be generally applicable for existing companies as well as new companies as soon as the protocol comes into force. It should be pointed out that this 30-percent tax on dividends, interest, and royalties is the same rate which is now imposed on these types of income when paid to investment companies incorporated in a country which does not have a tax treaty with the United States. 

4. EFFECTIVE DATE 

In general, the protocol would be effective with respect to royalty payments made on or after January 1 of the year following the year in which the exchange of instruments of ratification takes place, and at the same time for dividend, interest, and royalty payments made to Netherlands Antilles corporations organized after May 14, 1963. 

Special provisions are made to cover the gradual increase in the 30percent U.S. tax rate on dividends and interest.

MEMORANDUM OF JOINT COMMITTEE ON INTERNAL REVENUE TAXATION TAX CONVENTION WITH THE NETHERLANDS 

The protocol dated October 23, 1963, would modify the tax convention with the Netherlands dated April 29, 1948, as supplemented by a protocol signed at Washington on June 15, 1955, which extended most of the provisions in the 1948 convention to the Netherlands Antilles. 

Nonapplicability (in part) to Netherlands Antilles holding companies

In general, the major proposition involved in this convention is essentially the same as that presented in the provision of the pending

Luxembourg convention (art. XV) which would deny the benefits of that convention to so-called Luxembourg holding companies. In essence, it is the question of the extent to which residents of countries other than the contracting countries should be allowed to use the provisions of a tax convention to reduce or eliminate U.S. and/or overall income tax on dividend, interest, and royalty income derived from sources within the United States. 

The context of the situations in which this matter arises under the tax convention with the Netherlands, as it applies to the Netherlands Antilles, involves the interrelationship of four factors. First, the fact that the present convention (in arts. VII, VIII, and IX) provides, in general, (a) for a reduction of U.S tax on U.S. source dividends payable to Netherlands Antilles corporations to 15 percent (5 percent in the case of 95-percent-owned subsidiaries) and (b) an exclusion from U.S. tax on U.S. source interest and nonmineral royalties. Second, the fact that under a special tax law in the Netherlands Antilles (arts. 13, 14, and 14(a) of the Netherlands Antilles National Ordinance on Profit Tax of 1940), the Netherlands Antilles limits its tax to 3 percent on dividend, interest, and royalty income of so-called Netherlands Antilles holding companies, in lieu of the 30-percent rate otherwise applicable (2.4-percent rate on approximately the first $50,000 of income in lieu of the 24-percent rate otherwise applicable). 

Third, the fact that the present convention (art. XII) provides that the United States will not tax dividends and interest paid by a Netherlands Antilles corporation to residents of third countries. Fourth, the fact that individuals resident outside the United States and the Netherlands Antilles may not pay income tax to the country in which they are resident on U.S. source income either because the country of their residence may not impose an income tax or the individuals do not report U.S. source income in that country. 

The effect these factors may have on the U.S. and overall tax on 

U.S. source dividend, interest, and royalty income may be illustrated by assuming that an individual who is neither a citizen nor resident of the United States or the Netherlands Antilles invests in the United States through the use of a Netherlands Antilles corporation. On this basis, dividend income received by the Netherlands Antilles corporation from sources within the United States would be subject to a 15-percent U.S. tax (5 percent in the case of dividends received from 

95-percent-owned U.S. subsidiaries), while interest and royalty income received by the corporation from U.S. sources would be exempt from U.S. tax (point 1). The Netherlands Antilles corporation would be subject to a 3-percent Netherlands Antilles tax on its income (point 2). There would be no United States or Netherlands Antilles tax on the nonresident alien on the receipt of a dividend from the Netherlands Antilles corporation (point 3), and the dividend would be tax exempt in the country of the recipient's residence (point 4). In the case of U.S. source dividend income, the overall rate of tax would be approximately 17.55 percent (15-percent United States tax and 2.55 percent Netherlands

STATEMENT OF TREASURY DEPARTMENT WITH RESPECT TO THE PROTOCOL DATED OCTOBER 23, 1963, AMENDING THE UNITED STATES-NETHERLANDS INCOME TAX CONVENTION 

On October 23, 1963, a protocol entered into by the Government of the United States and the Government of the Kingdom of the Netherlands was signed at the Hague. This protocol modifies and supplements the United States-Netherlands Income Tax Convention signed on April 29, 1948, which was supplemented by a protocol signed on June 15, 1955, and extended to the Netherlands Antilles by exchanges of notes dated June 24 and August 7, 1952, September 15 and November 4 and 10, 1955.

This latest protocol is limited in application to the Netherlands Antilles, which consist of six islands of the Kingdom of the Netherlands, located in the Caribbean near the coast of South America, having a total population of approximately 200,000. In recent years the 

Netherlands Antilles have become a popular base for investment and holding companies (often called "Curacao corporations" after the capital city). The income which these companies derive from the 

United States is subject to reduce rates of U.S. tax under the United 

States-Netherlands Income Tax Convention as it applies to the Netherlands Antilles and is also taxed at only nominal rates by the Netherlands Antilles. Thus, use of Curacao corporations as "tax haven" corporations has become quite popular among residents of other countries. This protocol, which is a product of extended negotiations beginning in March 1962, between delegations from the Netherlands Antilles and the United States, withdraws some of the U.S. tax benefits which have proved to be incentives to formation of these companies.

The protocol was ratified by the Government of the Kingdom of the Netherlands, with the approval of the Government of the Netherlands Antilles, on December 20, 1963. It was transmitted by President Johnson to the Senate for advice and consent to its ratification by the Government of the United States on November 29, 1963, and was referred to the Committee on Foreign Relations on December 

3, 1963.

A summarization and explanation of the protocol follows.

ARTICLE I Summary of provisions Paragraph (1) of article I of the protocol provides that article VII, article VIII, and article IX of the convention, which reduce the statutory rate of tax imposed by the United States or the Netherlands. 

Antilles, as the case may be, on investment income derived from sources within the country and received by residents and corporations of the other country, shall be inapplicable to income derived from sources within the United States by any investment company, holding company, or other entity entitled to any of the special tax benefits (consisting principally of substantial reduction in rates of income tax) available under article 13, article 14, or article 14A of the Netherlands Antilles ordinance on profit tax of 1940 as in effect on September 1, 1963, or to substantially similar tax benefits granted under any law of the Netherlands Antilles enacted after such date. The effect of paragraph (1) is to make generally applicable, on the effective dates set forth in article III of the protocol, the U.S. statutory tax rate of 30 percent on dividends, interest, and royalties derived from sources within the United States whenever such income is subject to reduced tax rates under the aforementioned provisions or under any substantially similar law of the Netherlands Antilles enacted after September 1, 1963. 

Paragraph (2) of article I of the protocol provides several exceptions to the general rule contained in paragraph (1). The effect of paragraph (2) is that articles VII, VIII, and IX of the convention will continue to be applicable, despite the availability of "special tax benefits" under Antillean law, to entities described therein. 

Under subparagraph (a) of paragraph (2), articles VII, VIII, and 

IX of the convention continue to apply to dividends, interest and royalties paid by a United States corporation to an Antillean corporation owning at least 25 percent of its 'stock. This exception is not operable, however, if the U.S. corporation derives 60 percent or more of its income from specified passive investments. 

Under subparagraph (b) of paragraph (2), articles VII, VIII, and 

IX of the convention remain applicable to dividends, interest and royalties from United States sources to Antillean holding companies, the stock of which is wholly owned by (1) individuals residing in the 

Netherlands Antilles; (2) individuals residing in the Netherlands; or (3) by one or more corporations of the Netherlands. 

Article VII, VIII, and IX of the convention Sections 871 and 881 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954 provide generally for a 30-percent U.S. tax rate on dividends, interest and royalties derived from U.S. sources by nonresident alien individuals and foreign corporations if they are not engaged in trade or business within the United States. However, this statutory tax rate applicable to the specified types of investment income is normally reduced substantially in the bilateral tax treaties to which the United States is a party in consideration for a reciprocal reduction rate by the other country which is a party to the treaty. The reduction in the statutory tax rate of the country which is the source of the investment income is made directly available to residents and corporations of the other country so long as the income recipient is not engaged in trade or business in the source country through a permanent establishment. 

The treaty tax rates on dividends, interest, and royalties in the 1948

Tax Convention between the Netherlands and the United States are found in articles VII, VIII, and IX. When the convention was extended by protocol to the Netherlands Antilles in 1955 at the request of the Netherlands, articles VII, VIII, and IX became applicable to income from U.S. sources paid to a resident or corporation of the 

Netherlands Antilles. 

Article VII of the convention reduces the U.S. tax rate on U.S.

source dividends to 15 percent. (If the dividend recipient is a corporation owning at least 95 percent of the stock of the U.S. corporation paying the dividend and certain other conditions are met, the U.S. tax rate is reduced to 5 percent.) Article VIII grants a complete exemption from U.S. tax on interest from U.S. sources (other than interest attributable to real property located in the United States) unless the income recipient is a corporation owning more than 50 percent of the stock of a U.S. corporation paying the interest. Article

Articles VII, VIII, and IX of the convention grant similar reductions and exemptions in Netherlands and Netherlands Antilles tax to residents and corporations of the United States. 

Articles 13, 14, and 14A of Antillean corporate tax law 

Income taxation in the Netherlands Antilles of the earnings and profits of an Antillean corporation is imposed by the national ordinance on profit tax of 1940 as amended. Article 15 of this law provides generally for a corporate tax rate of 24 percent on net income up to 100,000 florin (approximately $53,050) and a tax rate of 30 percent on net income in excess of 100,000 florin. However, a special reduced tax rate of only one-tenth the rate imposed by article 15 (or a maximum of 3 percent) is provided by articles 13, 14, and 14A for organizations described in these articles. 

Article 13 grants the special tax rate for the dividend income received by an Antillean corporation from a foreign corporation in which it owns at least one-tenth of the paidup capital if the foreign corporation is subject to income taxation in its country of incorporation. 

Article 14 grants the special tax rate for all of the net income of an Antillean corporation engaged "exclusively or almost exclusively" in the business of investing in stocks and securities.

Article 14A grants the special tax rate for all of the net income of an Antillean corporation engaged "exclusively or almost exclusively" in the business of licensing or leasing various types of property or property rights.

Reasons for article I of protocol 

The combination of reduced treaty tax rates and special Antillean tax rates for investment companies and holding companies results in a maximum effective tax burden on investment income derived by an 

Antillean corporation from U.S. sources of 17.55 percent in the case. of dividends and only 3 percent in the case of interest and royalties. 

In addition, nonresident shareholders in Antillean corporations are. not subject to Antillean taxation on dividends from those corporations. 

Therefore, the taxes at the corporate level can be the only taxes imposed on U.S. source income where the nonresident shareholder fails to report his dividend income from the Antillean corporation to the tax authorities of his country of residence, is not liable for

income tax in his country of residence, or arranges his holdings so that dividends are paid into another company or trust which he controls in a tax-free jurisdiction.

This favorable tax atmosphere for investment in the United States through an Antillean corporation by residents of third countries has been widely publicized and commented upon by writers in tax periodicals (e.g., Weissman, "Tax Advantages of the Netherlands 

Antilles for Motion Picture Companies", 32 Southern California Law 

Review 391 (1959); Joseph & Koppel, "Curacao Organizations, Taxes The Tax Magazine," p. 485 (1961); Lowe, "Curacao Investment Companies: Some Shoals in a Tax Haven," 16 Tax Law 

Review 177 (1961)). The result has been the formation of an everincreasing number of non-resident-owned investment and holding companies in the Netherlands Antilles.

When the applicability of the United States-Netherlands Tax Convention was extended to the Netherlands Antilles in 1955, there were no more than a handful of Antillean investment and holding companies, most of these being subsidiaries of Netherlands operating companies. 

The Netherlands Antilles Government has advised the Treasury that by January 1963 the number of such companies formed on behalf of nonresident shareholders had increased to 986. Not all of these companies were active on that date and many of those which were active held investments in countries other than the United States. 

Nevertheless, it seems clear that the reduced treaty tax rates available under articles VII, VIII, and IX of the convention have been a major inducement to the formation of a substantial number of nonresidentowned investment and holding companies in the Netherlands Antilles. 

In the past, U.S. tax treaties have not limited the source-country reductions in statutory tax rates on dividends, interest and royalties paid to corporations of the destination country to those cases where the shareholders of a corporation of the destination country also are residents of that country. On the other hand, when residents of third countries are lured into holding their U.S. investments in a company incorporated in a treaty country through artificial reduction by that country of its own tax rates on the income, the primary purpose of a tax treaty is subverted. Tax treaties are governmental agreements designed to prevent double (and therefore excessive) taxation of an investor's foreign-source income by resolving conflicting jurisdictional claims to tax which arise because one country is the source of the income and the other is the residence of the recipient. 

Exemptions and reductions in source taxation provided for by treaty are reciprocal revenue adjustments between governments designed to implement a tax credit for foreign taxes paid. They are not intended to reduce the tax burden of the investor, as it is assumed that the tax waived by the source country will be imposed by the destination country.

Exemptions and reductions in U.S. tax on United States source income paid to non-resident-owned Antillean investment and holding companies are not justified as long as this income is taxed at low rates (not exceeding 3 percent at present) in the Netherlands Antilles. It is therefore deemed necessary to restore the U.S. statutory rate of tax on dividends, interest, and royalties derived from U.S. sources by Antillean investment and holding companies. Article I of the protocol accomplishes this without denying treaty tax benefits to Antillean resi-

dents or to those Antillan corporations which are wholly owned by Antillean residents or by residents or corporations of the Netherlands. 

In recommending ratification of article I of the protocol, the Treasury continues to recognize the desirability of encouraging foreign portfolio investment in the United States during our present imbalance of international payments. To this end, care has been taken not to remove from the treaty those provisions (such as the exemption of Netherlands Antilles corporations from the so-called "secondary tax" on dividends and interest paid by them to persons other than U.S. persons) which have long been an intended benefit of the U.S. treaty program. The changes made by article I have also been "phased in" on a gradual basis, as explained below. However, the Treasury Department does not feel that a combination of treaty tax reductions intended as revenue adjustments and the special tax laws of another country is a satisfactory vehicle through which to attract foreign investment to the United States.

V. SUPPLEMENTARY CONVENTION WITH SWEDEN 1. PURPOSE 

The purpose of this convention is to modify and supplement the basic convention and accompanying protocol between the United States and Sweden which was approved by the Senate on August 2, 1939, and which entered into force on January 1, 1940. According to the Department of State, it is designed to effect certain modifica-tions in the 1939 convention in order to reflect more recent developments in tax legislation of the two countries and in their more recentlynegotiated income tax conventions.

2. PROVISIONS OF CONVENTION The pending Supplementary Income Tax Convention With Sweden would result in five basic changes in the 1939 convention and its accompanying protocol.

First. Paragraph (a) of article I provides for an increase from 10 to 15 percent in the tax on dividends received from sources within one country by a resident, corporation, or other entity of the other country, provided the recipient does not have a permanent establishment in the source country. At the same time, the supplementary convention would reduce to 5 percent the withholding tax on dividends flowing from a corporation in one country to a parent corporation in the other. Paragraph (a) also provides that Sweden will exempt its corporations from tax on dividends from U.S. corporations to the same extent that they would be exempt if they were received from Swedish companies.

Second. Paragraph (b) of article I provides, on a reciprocal basis, for the exemption from tax in the source State of interest on bonds, notes, etc., received from such State by a resident, corporation, or other entity of the other State not having a permanent establishment in the source State.

Third. Paragraph (c) of article I extends the circumstances under which students, teachers, apprentices, and research workers from one country may be temporarily present in the other country without being taxable in the host country. 

Fourth. Paragraph (d) of article I gives more effective relief from double taxation in the case of Swedish residents who derive U.S. source income by providing for an exclusion from tax in Sweden in the case of U.S. source income which is exempt from Swedish tax under the convention. Paragraph (d) also provides for a credit against 

Swedish tax payable by Swedish residents and corporations in the case of U.S. source income which is taxed in the United States under the convention and which is not exempt from Swedish tax. 

Fifth. Article II replaces the present definition of "permanent establishment" with the one contained in the draft convention for the avoidance of double taxation approved by the Fiscal Committee of the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, and proposed by it for use in the income tax treaties of its member nations.

3. EFFECTIVE DATE 

The pending convention is to be effective for taxable years beginning on or after January 1, 1963, except in the case of the provision relating to the taxation of dividends which would be effective for taxable years beginning after January 1 of the year in which instruments of ratification are exchanged. 

MEMORANDUM OF JOINT COMMITTEE ON INTERNAL REVENUE TAXATION INCOME TAX CONVENTION WITH SWEDEN The supplementary convention dated October 22, 1963. would modify and supplement the convention between the United States and Sweden signed at Washington on March 23, 1939.

Business profits 

Under the present convention, a business enterprise of one country is taxable on its industrial and commercial profits in the other country only if it maintains a permanent establishment in the other country. 

For this purpose, the term "permanent establishment" is presently defined as a branch, mine, oil well, plantation, factory, workshop, warehouse, office, agency, or other fixed place of business. The pending supplementary convention would replace this definition with the definition contained in the draft convention for the avoidance of double taxation approved by the Fiscal Committee of the Oragnization for 

Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD). As noted in the explanation of the pending Luxembourg convention, which, in general, also adopts the OECD definition, the new definition is most significant in that it itemizes a series of activities which could not be treated as constituting a "permanent establishment." The result is that it permits an enterprise of one country to economically penetrate the other country to a greater degree than has heretofore been provided in the tax conventions to which the United States is a party without incurring tax in the host country. As in the convention with Luxembourg, an enterprise of one country maintaining a permanent establishment in the other country would be subject to tax in the host country in respect of its industrial and commercial profits only on such profits which are "allocable to" the permanent establishment. Dividends

Under the present convention, U.S. citizens, residents, and corporations receiving dividends from Swedish corporations are subject to a 

10-percent Swedish tax, withheld at source, in lieu of the 30-percent 

Swedish tax otherwise applicable. Similarly, United States tax on dividends paid to Swedish residents and corporations by U.S. corporations is limited under the present convention to 10 percent of the gross dividend, in lieu of the minimum U.S. 30-percent rate otherwise applicable. The pending supplementary convention would increase the tax in the source country from 10 to 15 percent and thus bring the source country rate in line with that provided in most other income tax conventions to which the United States is a party. However, provision is made to reduce the source country's rate of tax to 5 percent (rather than the 15-percent rate otherwise applicable) if the recipient of the dividend is a corporation which, together with no more than three other 10 percent or more corporate shareholders, owns at least 50 percent of the voting stock of the payer corporation, and not more than 25 percent of the payer's gross income consists of dividends and interest from other than 50-percent-owned subsidiaries. 

This provision is similar to that contained in the United States tax convention with Norway and in the pending conventions with Luxembourg and Japan.

The pending convention also provides that dividends received by Swedish corporations from United States corporations would, in general, be exempt from corporate tax in Sweden. Interest

Under the present convention, interest on bonds, debentures, and other forms of indebtedness received from sources within one of the contracting countries by a resident or corporation or other entity of the other country is subject to tax in the source country at the nor-

mal withholding rate applicable in the source country on interest income payable to nonresident aliens. Thus, at the present time, the United States withholds tax at a 30-percent rate on United States source interest paid to Swedish residents and corporations. However, since Sweden exempts Swedish source interest payable to nonresident aliens from tax in Sweden, United States citizens, residents and corporations are, at present, exempt from tax in Sweden on their Swedish. source interest income.

The pending supplementary convention would provide for the exclusion of interest income from tax in the source country on a reciprocal basis. In this respect, the pending convention adopts the policy contained in the more recent income tax conventions to which the United States is a party by, in general, making the country of residence the exclusive taxing jurisdiction for interest income. 

Compensation for personal services

Under the present convention, students and business apprentices from one country who are present in the other country to study or to acquire business experience are exempt from tax in the host country on remittances received from their home country if the remittance is used for their maintenance or studies. The pending supplementary convention extends the circumstances under which persons from one country may be temporarily present in the other country for an education related purpose without being taxable in the host country. 

In general, the additional exemptions correspond to provisions contained in the more recent income tax conventions to which the United States is a party and may be summarized as follows: Residents of one country would be exempt from tax in the other country for a period of 2 years on compensation received for teaching or conducting research (other than for research carried on for profit) at an educational institution situated in the other country; an exclusion from tax would be provided in the host country for compensation for employment remitted from the other country to a student or business apprentice without regard to the use made of the remittances by the recipient; recipients of grants, allowances, or awards from religious, charitable scientific, or educational organizations for the primary purpose of study or research would be exempt from tax in the host country on the same basis as remittances to students and business apprentices; and remuneration received by a resident of one of the contracting countries temporarily present in the other country solely for the purpose of training, research, or study would be exempt from tax in the host country if (a) he is present in the host country under an arrangement with the government of that country, (b) the total amount of the remuneration does not exceed $10,000, and (c) the remuneration is directly related to the training, research or study undertaken. 

Foreign tax credits

Under the present convention, as in other income tax conventions to which the United States is a party, the contracting countries each retain the right to tax the income of their citizens, residents, and corporations as though the convention had not come into effect. However, to relieve double taxation of income in these cases, the conventions generally provide for the allowance of a tax credit in the country of the taxpayer's residence for income taxes paid the other country.

Since the statutory law of Sweden does not provide for crediting United States tax paid by Swedish residents or corporations against Swedish tax liability, the present convention requires Sweden to grant its residents and corporations a foreign tax credit for U.S. tax paid on U.S. source income which would otherwise be exempt from tax in Sweden under the convention. Thus, in effect, a credit for taxes paid directly by the taxpayer, on a per-country basis, is allowed with respect to income exempt from tax in Sweden under the convention. However, if the income is taxable both in the United States and Sweden, United States tax is allowable as a credit against Swedish tax only in the case of dividend income; moreover, the credit is limited to 5 percent of the gross dividend, although the actual U.S. tax paid is 10 percent of the gross dividend. Under the present convention, no relief from double taxation is provided in other cases where the same income is taxable both in the United States and Sweden (for example, in the case of Swedish corporations which derive industrial and commercial profits in the United States through a permanent establishment located in the United States or U.S.source interest income).

In the case of Swedish residents or corporations deriving U.S.-source income, the pending supplementary convention would provide more effective relief from double taxation in two ways: First, income which under the convention is exempt from tax in Sweden (for example, U.S.-source rent, mineral royalties, wages paid by the United States to Swedish residents, and certain pensions), would be exempt from tax in Sweden. However, the income would be taken into account in computing the Swedish resident's or corporation's effective Swedish tax rate. This method of treatment, the so-called exclusion method, has been recognized by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) as an appro priate method for the relief from double taxation of income and has been adopted by the United States in its tax conventions with Germany and Switzerland.

Second, in the case of income which under the convention is taxable both in the United States and Sweden (for example, U.S.-source industrial or commercial profits and dividends), the pending supplementary convention would adopt the more generally utilized tax credit method for the relief from double taxation and would require that a credit be allowed against Swedish tax for U.S. tax paid by 

Swedish residents and corporations on their U.S.-source income. The credit would, of course, be limited to that proportion of the Swedish tax otherwise payable which the U.S.-source income which is taxable under the convention in both the United States and Sweden bears to the taxpayer's entire income subject to tax in Sweden. 

The pending supplementary convention would also provide that the United States would allow a tax credit for Swedish taxes paid by its citizens, residents, or corporations in accordance with the internal revenue laws of the United States in effect for the taxable year in which the dividend is received, rather than in accordance with the U.S. statutory foreign tax credit provisions in effect in 1939 when the present convention was ratified. 

Effective date

In general, the pending supplementary convention would be effective for taxable years beginning on or after January 1, 1963. However,

the provision relating to the taxation of dividends in the source country at a 15-percent rate (5 percent in the case of certain closely held corporations), in lieu of the present 10-percent rate, would be effective for taxable years beginning after January 1 of the year in which instruments of ratification are exchanged. 

STATEMENT OF THE TREASURY DEPARTMENT WITH RESPECT TO THE SUPPLEMENTARY INCOME TAX CONVENTION OF OCTOBER 22, 1963, BETWEEN THE UNITED STATES AND SWEDEN 

A supplementary convention, signea October 22, 1963, modifies the income tax convention and protocol between the United States and Sweden signed March 23, 1939. The changes would, for the most part, add to the convention provisions similar to those included in other income tax conventions to which the United States is a party. 

ARTICLE I Article I of the supplementary convention contains replacements for the following articles of the existing tax treaty: Articles VII, VIII, XII, and XIV(b). 

Dividends-New article VII 

Article VII of the existing convention provides that dividends shall be taxable in the contracting State where the shareholder is resident, or if the shareholder is a corporation or other entity, where such corporation or other entity is created or organized. However, the State of source reserves the right to deduct at the source taxes at a rate not in excess of 10 percent of the dividends. The article in the existing convention also provides for its unilateral termination by either contracting State subject to certain time limitations. In the event of such unilateral termination, provision is made for the automatic termination of related provisions of the treaty. 

The new article VII provides that dividends received from sources within one of the contracting States by a resident, corporation, or other entity of the other State not having a permanent establishment in the State of source shall be subject to tax in the State of source at a rate not in excess of 15 percent of such dividends. Furthermore, the rate shall not exceed 5 percent when dividends are paid to a corporation that meets certain additional conditions: (1) At least 50 percent of the paying corporation's voting stock must be owned during the part of the taxable year preceding the payment of the dividend and during the whole of the prior taxable year by the recipient corporation, either alone or in association with not more than three other corporations of the same contracting State, and at least 10 percent of said voting stock must be owned by each such corporation; and (2) not more than 25 percent of the gross income of the paying corporation other than a corporation the principal business of which is the making of loans for the said prior taxable year may be derived from interest and dividends other than interest and dividends received from a corporation in which the paying corporation owns at least 

50 percent of the voting stock.

Paragraph (2) of the new article VII is a unilateral provision stating that a Swedish corporation or other entity deriving dividends from a U.S. corporation or other entity shall be exempt from Swedish tax with respect to such dividends provided such dividends would be

exempt from tax under the laws of Sweden if the paying and recipient corporations or other entities were Swedish. Under Swedish law intercorporate dividends are, generally speaking, exempt in order to avoid double taxation at the corporate level, subject to certain limitations. It is to be noted that dividends paid by a Swedish corporation to nonresidents in third countries are subject to a 30-percent coupon tax.

The proposed new article VII embodies the 15-5 formula incorporated in a number of income tax conventions to which the United States is a party. At least 13 of the 21 income tax conventions in force contain the 15-5 formula, with slight differences in detail, such as the holding period during which at least 50 percent of the stock must be owned by the recipient corporation, and the definition of a subsidiary corporation as one in which the paying corporation has at least 50-percent stock ownership. In this connection, it should be noted that the ownership requirement is "at least 50 percent" and not "more than 50 percent." This change is in recognition of the common practice to establish internationally owned companies with ownership shared equally between U.S. interests and foreign interests. 

The effect of the new article VII, as compared with the one being superseded, will be to increase the withholding tax rate on portfolio dividends from 10 to 15 percent and to reduce the rate on direct investment dividends from 10 to 5 percent. These rates compare with statutory rates of 30 percent applicable in the United States under sections 871 and 881 of the Internal Revenue Code and in Sweden under its coupon tax, which is a substitute for the liability of nonresidents of Sweden for all national taxes based on an annual assessment. 99 

Interest-New article VIII 

Article VIII of the existing convention, dealing with interest, did not change the tax imposed under the Internal Revenue Code, but the new interest article provides, on a reciprocal basis, for exemption of interest derived from sources within the United States by Swedish residents, corporations, or other entities, not having a permanent establishment in the United States. Such an exemption is in line with the treatment accorded interest income under almost half of the income tax conventions to which the United States is a party. Of the 21 conventions in force, 10 contain a reciprocal exemption on interest income. The effect of the provision is to establish reciprocity in the tax treatment of interest. In the case of the United States, it will result in a reduction of tax liability from 30 percent to 0. In the case of Sweden, it preserves more or less its existing policy which is not to tax interest income paid to nonresidents, unless such income is connected with a business undertaking which the nonresident recipient is carrying on in Sweden.

Students, apprentices, and teachers-New article IX Article XII of the existing convention related solely to the tax treatment of students and business apprentices, but the substitute article extends the scope of the convention to cover teachers, professors, and research workers. Paragraph 1 of the new article XII embodies the provisions of the existing convention to the effect that students and business apprentices of one of the contracting states residing in the other state exclusively for the purpose of study or

acquiring business experience shall not be taxable in the latter state in respect to remittances received for their maintenance or studies from within the former state. In addition, paragraph 1 enlarges the scope of the exemption to include a recipient of a grant allowance or award granted by a religious, charitable, scientific, or educational organization for the primary purpose of study or research and to provide that the exemption covers amounts paid from abroad as remuneration for employment. While article XII of the existing convention refers to "students and business apprentices from one of the contracting states ** * residing in the other contracting state," paragraph 1 of the new article XII provides that "A resident of one of the contracting states *** temporarily present in the other contracting state * *" shall receive the prescribed benefits. The change in terminology conforms to the normal language utilized in the more recent income tax conventions concluded by the United States. Paragraph 2 of the new article XII provides another type of exemption for residents of one of the contracting states who are temporarily present in the other contracting state as students, trainees, or research workers under arrangement with the government of such other state. This exemption covers remuneration, whether or not remitted from abroad, for services directly related to such training, research, or study (including any emoluments or remunerations from the person's employer abroad), if the amount of such remunerations does not exceed $10,000. If the remuneration does exceed $10,000 for the period the person is temporarily present in the United States, the exemption provided by paragraph 2 does not apply. Persons temporarily present in the United States under arrangements with the United States or its agencies would not include those who may be in the United States under a general cultural exchange agreement between the United States and a foreign country, but would include those here under a specific program, sponsored or supported by the U.S. Government, such as, but not limited to, technical assistance trainees, military and armed forces trainees, and foreign central bank employees studying budgetary and financial policies. 

Paragraph 3 of the new article XII provides the usual 2-year tax exemption on remuneration derived by Swedish teachers and professors temporarily present in the United States to teach at a university, college, school, or other recognized educational institution in this country. Research work is also included within the scope of paragraph 3, so that a person may qualify for the exemption by being engaged in teaching or in research work, or both. Certain qualifications are imposed under paragraph 3 in order for the individual to be entitled to the exemption. The teaching or research must be performed at the educational institution which extended the invitation, and in the case of research it must be research of a kind that is not carried on for the benefit of any person using or disseminating the results of such research for purposes of profit. Similar language is to be found in the pertinent provision in the Luxembourg convention, except that the educational institution is, itself, not included within the category of persons "using or disseminating the results of such research, for purposes of profit" in determining whether or not the exemption applies. Nevertheless, the language of the new article XII is to be interpreted as if it contained the language incorporated in the applicable provision of the Luxembourg convention.

Paragraph 4 of the new article XII provides that any individual who qualifies for more than one of the exemptions provided under the first three paragraphs of the new article XII is entitled to claim that one most favorable to him. This provides a solution for those cases where a particular individual may fit within the scope of more than one of the exemption provisions contained in the proposed article XII. 

The new article XII continues the development of income tax convention provisions dealing with students, business apprentices, teachers, professors, and research workers from one country temporarily present in the other, to provide a fuller, freer exchange of such persons between the countries. The provisions contained in the new article 

XII will also be found in the Luxembourg convention and to varying extents in all of the income tax conventions entered into by the 

United States.

As for the effect of these provisions on the respective countries' statutory provisions relating to the tax liability of aliens temporarily present within their borders, it should be noted that both countries have somewhat similar tests of residence for tax purposes. For the 

United States the test of residence is set forth in section 1.872-2 of the regulations. Generally speaking, the test is a dual one involving both (1) physical presence and (2) intent. Under Swedish law, it depends upon where the person makes his "real dwelling and home" or whether he makes a "permanent sojourn" in Sweden; again intent is a factor. Although no specific period of time is specified under either country's laws as determinative of resident status, in practice under both countries' laws the length of time of stay is considered an important factor in determining residence. (In Sweden it appears that permanent residence is normally regarded as commencing after a stay of 6 months.) As no specific period of time is specified in the student-business apprentice provisions, it is intended that a liberal construction be given in keeping with the general intent of such provisions to facilitate cultural exchanges and in recognition of the usual periods of time required to complete any formal program of education or training under normal circumstances. It should be noted that the status of such persons present in the United States would be that of nonresident aliens; however, whether or not they would be considered as being engaged in trade or business would be determined under the regular statutory tests.

Swedish tax credit--New article XIV(b) 

Paragraph (d) of article I of the proposed supplementary convention provides a new Swedish credit provision, replacing existing article XIV(b). Under article XIV (b) of the existing treaty, Sweden is not obliged to allow a full credit for U.S. tax imposed on Swedish taxpayers, Sweden is permitted to include in the basis upon which it determines the graduated tax on income and property of its residents or corporations or other entities, all items of income or property which are subject to such tax in Sweden, notwithstanding any other provision of the convention. However, once the liability is so determined, Sweden deducts from the tax that portion of the liability which the taxpayer's income and property exempt from Swedish taxation under the provisions of the convention bears to his entire income and property. With respect to dividends, Sweden is obliged under the existing treaty to give a credit of 5 percent to offset the tax deducted

at the source in the United States. Since U.S. withholding is at the rate of 10 percent (and in the case of U.S. citizens resident in Sweden, the U.S. tax may be much higher) the credit granted by Sweden for 

U.S. tax does not provide complete elimination of the double taxation. 

The new article XIV (b) requires Sweden to grant a broader foreign tax credit. Under subparagraph (1), Sweden excludes from its tax base those items of income and property which, under the convention, are exempt from taxation in Sweden. However, it may take such items of income and property into account in the determination of the graduated rate of Swedish tax imposed on its residents, corporations, or other entities. Under subparagraph (2), full credit will be allowed against Swedish tax for United States tax imposed on those items of income from sources within the United States which are not exempt from Swedish tax. The credit, cannot, however, exceed that proportion of the total Swedish taxes which such income bears to the entire income subject to tax by Sweden. Since Sweden, under its domestic tax laws, has no provision for the allowance of a credit for foreign taxes (although foreign taxes may be deducted in computing income taxable under that law), the credit available for U.S. taxes will be determined solely under the provisions of the convention.. 

Thus, whether Sweden satisfies the similar credit requirement of code section 901(b) (3) will be determined by reference to article XIV(b). 

With respect to this article, the Swedish tax authorities, in a memorandum accompanying a letter of March 20, 1963, to the U.S. Treasury 

Department, have stated in part as follows:

The proposed subparagraph (b)(2) of article XIV is in tended to grant to residents of Sweden a credit for United States tax imposed under the Convention on income from sources in the United States, e.g. for non-United States citizens residing in Sweden the limited tax of the United States on dividends paid by United States corporations or the nonresident aliens tax on alimony payments-for United States citizens resident in Sweden the full income tax on income from United States sources.

In view of the position set forth in the excerpt, Sweden, under the new article XIV (b) will be considered to satisfy fully the similar credit requirement with respect to the United States source income of United States citizens resident in Sweden. The fact that Sweden may take into account items of income and property exempt from its tax by virtue of the convention in determining the graduated rate of tax to apply to other income and property is not a rejection of the similar credit requirement. It may be noted in this connection that the 

Fiscal Committee of the Organization for Economic Cooperation and 

Development (OECD) has adopted this "exemption with progression" technique as one of the acceptable means to be used in income tax conventions for the prevention of double taxation. 

ARTICLE II Article II of the supplementary convention modifies the protocol that accompanied, and was signed at the same time as the existing convention and forms an integral part of it. The following changes are made in the protocol: A substitution is provided for subparagraph (a) of paragraph 1, containing the definition of permanent establish-

ment, a modification is introduced in paragraph 6 insofar as it relates to the foreign tax credit given by the United States, a new paragraph 7 is added relating to nondiscrimination of citizens of either contracting states when they are resident in the other contracting state, and a new paragraph 13 is added relating to the definition of "competent authority."

Permanent establishment definition The permanent establishment definition included in the new subparagraph (a) of paragraph 1 of the protocol is the one provided in the model income tax convention prepared by the Fiscal Committee of the Organization of Economic Cooperation and Development 

(OECD) and proposed by that organization to be adopted in future income tax treaty negotiations by its member nations, which include both the United States and Sweden. One of the compelling reasons for inclusion of the OECD definition, in addition to the facts that it reflects the latest thinking of the nations that have been most active in the income tax treaty area and incorporates modern refinements on older definitions, is that through its use by OECD member nations and through continued consideration of it by a permanent body such as the Fiscal Committee of the OECD it may be possible to evolve a general application of uniform interperations of its terms. Thus, although the Fiscal Committee's commentary on the article indicates some problems to be resolved in interpreting its provisions, it is believed on the whole to represent an improvement on the permanent establishment definitions to be found in existing income tax conventions to which the United States is a party. 

Most of the provisions embodied in the new permanent establishment definition are in use in other income tax conventions to which the United States is a party. As under the other income tax conventions concluded by the United States (including the original Swedish convention), for purposes of determining whether there is to be imposition of U.S. tax, the concept of permanent establishment is substituted for the statutory concept of being engaged in trade or business, thus requiring more stable or permanent business connections with the United States before a foreign enterprise is subject to U.S. tax with respect to its income from U.S. sources. 

The list of what constitutes a permanent establishment includes places of business that are for the most part familiar to U.S. treaties, but certain new terms are incorporated, specifically, "a building site, or construction or assembly project, which exists for more than 12 months," and "a place of management." This language is also included in the income tax convention with Luxembourg signed in December 1962, and the language with respect to a building project is included in the supplementary protocol with Japan signed in August 1962. Although "a building site, or construction, or assembly project which exists for more than 12 months" may seem fairly clear in meaning, there may arise the question of whether or not the units, where there is more than one, in a building, construction, or assembly operation are to be considered individually or as a group. A project could consist of more than one unit provided they were sufficiently integrated to constitute one operation, even when they are being constructed at different locations. Thus, a chain of gasoline stations at spaced intervals along a new superhighway or a number of units in a defined housing project could constitute one construction project.

As for the "more than 12 month" test, it is to be noted from the OECD commentary that tax liability can be assessed before a year has expired if the project is likely to exist for more than 12 months, and that the computation of the time may take into account portions of different taxable years.

The language "place of management" is a new term, as the commentary to the OECD permanent establishment provision indicates. 

The object of including this term, as explained in the OECD commentary, is to make clear that there may be cases where a permanent establishment exists even though there is no "office" as such where the effective management of an enterprise is exercised. Thus, a hotel room could constitute a "place of management" if management decisions were rendered at such a place on a continuing basis. This term is also used in the convention with Luxembourg and for further discussion of its significance, the technical memorandum on that convention should be consulted.

The provisions of the definition relating to activities of agents, to the carrying on of a business through a broker or a general commission agent or any other agent of an independent status, and to having a subsidiary corporation within the other contracting state are all concepts already embodied in existing income tax conventions to which the United States is a party.

The principal new language contained in the OECD permanent establishment definition consists of an enumeration of five types of activity the conduct of any one of which within a treaty country will not constitute a permanent establishment therein, even if such activities are carried on through a fixed place of business. Whether only one or more than one of these activities can be engaged in by an enterprise through a fixed place of business without thereby constituting that fixed place of business a permanent establishment of the enterprise has not as yet been fully resolved. The Governments of the 

United States and of Sweden are prepared to accept the view that all of the activities enumerated may be conducted by an enterprise without its being held to have a permanent establishment. However, in the interest of obtaining uniformity among member countries of OECD, the interpretation given this provision will be made with due consideration to the construction adopted by the OECD. 

Application of U.S. foreign tax credit

Paragraph (b) of article II of the proposed supplementary convention modifies paragraph 6 of the existing protocol to the convention by deleting the second and third sentences thereof, and substituting in lieu thereof a sentence to the effect that in the application of the provisions of the convention the benefits of the internal revenue laws. of the United States relating to credits for foreign taxes shall be accorded. The effect of this modification is to "unfreeze" the foreign tax credit granted by the United States under the existing convention. 

At present the credit is tied to section 131 of the Revenue Act of 1938 by virtue of the third sentence of paragraph 6 of the protocol. Under the new language the foreign tax credit is not tied to the statutory foreign tax credit in effect on any particular date, but the basic commitment under article XIV to allow a credit for Swedish taxes remains unaffected.

The second sentence of paragraph 6 of the existing protocol also provides that the foreign tax credit granted by either country should

not apply to dividends and interest income, except as provided in article XIV (b) of the existing convention. This provision affected the imposition of only the Swedish tax since by reason of paragraph 9 of the protocol, the United States was bound to grant its statutory credit in respect to such items. The new articles VII, VIII, and 

XIV (b) of the convention require the deletion of this sentence. 

The third sentence in paragraph 6 of the existing protocol states that the foreign tax credit would not extend to U.S. excess profits taxes or to the surtax imposed on personal holding companies. Such a provision is no longer necessary because the United States does not now impose an excess profits tax and the foreign tax credit is not available against personal holding company tax under section 901 (a) of the 

Internal Revenue Code. 

Nondiscrimination provision Paragraph (c) of article II of the supplementary convention replaces paragraph 7 of the protocol to the existing convention. The original paragraph contains the usual treaty provision, specifying that a citizen of one of the countries while residing in the other country shell not be subjected to other or higher taxes than are imposed upon the citizens of the latter country. The new paragraph 7 in general restates this rule, adding for the sake of clarity, that it refers to those citizens of the latter country "residing in its territory," and that the term "citizen" includes all legal persons, partnerships, and associations created or organized under the laws of force in the respective contracting states, and the term "taxes" means taxes of every type and description, whether Federal, State, or municipal.

Competent authority definition Paragraph (d) of article II of the supplementary convention pro vides a new paragraph 13 for the original protocol which adds to the definition of the "competent authority" the words "or his authorized representative" after "the Secretary of the Treasury" and after "the 

Minister of Finance." These changes are intended to conform the language of the convention to the term found in numerous places in the Internal Revenue Code and defined in section 7701(a) (12). The use of the word "representative" instead of "delegate" was made at the request of the Swedish Government, which indicated there was no word in Swedish equivalent to "delegate." Thus, "representative" should be read as being synonymous with "delegate" on the U.S. side.

VI. JAPANESE PROTOCOLS 1. PURPOSE 

The purpose of the two Japanese protocols is to modify and supplement the basic tax convention betwen the United States and Japan which was signed on April 16, 1954, and approved by the Senate on February 25, 1955. They are designed to bring that convention into greater conformity with other income tax conventions to which the United States is a party. 

2. SUMMARY OF PROVISIONS 

The 1960 protocol contains eight articles which deal with such matters as the tax treatment of interest received by the Export- Import Banks of the United States and Japan; the taxation of certain rental income; the tax status of Government employees; and the source rule with respect to interest. 

The 1962 protocol contains nine articles. Among other things, it modifies in some respects the definitions in the 1954 convention, particularly with respect to the definitions of "permanent establishment," "Japanese corporation or other entity," and "industrial and commercial profits"; it reduces from 30 to 15 percent the rate at which Japanese residents are presently taxable on dividends received

from U.S. corporations; it reduces the source country's maximum rate of tax on interest and nonmineral royalties from 15 to 10 percent; and it revises the so-called 180-day rule which determines whether a corporate employee of one country is exempt from tax in the other country. 

3. EFFECTIVE DATE 

According to the terms of the protocols, they will enter into force on the date of the exchange of instruments of ratification, with varying provisions regarding the applicability of specified amendments. 

MEMORANDUM OF JOINT COMMITTEE ON INTERNAL REVENUE TAXATION INCOME TAX CONVENTION WITH JAPAN 

The protocols between the United States and Japan signed on May 7, 1960, and August 14, 1962, have the general purpose of making the income tax convention with Japan conform more closely with other income tax conventions to which the United States is a party. 

They also remove inequities and resolve technical problems which have arisen under the present convention. The 1954 convention, as supplemented by a protocol signed at Tokyo on March 23, 1957,is presently in force. The 1957 protocol would terminate when the 1960 protocol becomes effective. 

Business profits

Under the present convention, the industrial or commercial profits of an enterprise of one country are taxable in the other country only if the enterprise has a permanent establishment in the other country. 

The term "permanent establishment," as presently defined, means, among other things, "an office, factory, workshop, branch, warehouse, or other fixed place of business." Under this definition, controversy has arisen between the United States and Japanese authorities with respect to the taxation of enterprises of one country which engage in the construction business in the other country. The United States has taken the position that construction-related activities do not, by themselves, constitute the creation of a "permanent establishment," while the Japanese have taken a contrary position. The 1962 protocol resolves the issue by holding that an enterprise of one country is deemed to have a permanent establishment in the other country if it has a "construction, installation, or assembly project" which lasts for more than 12 months in the other country, or if an enterprise carries on supervisory activities in the other country in connection with such a project for a period of more than 12 months. Conversely, construction activities will not, by themselves, constitute the maintenance of a permanent establishment if the resulting activity in the other country lasts 12 months or less. This method of treatment is consistent with that adopted in the tax conventions the United States has with Austria and West Germany; moreover, it adopts the approach incorporated in the draft convention approved by the Fiscal Committee of the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD). The proposed U.S. tax convention with Luxembourg also provides similar treatment for construction projects, but adopts a 6-month period, rather than a 12-month period, as a basis for distinction.

63 Although construction activities in a host country may result, under the proposed definition, in the taxation of industrial or commercial profits in that country, it will not result in the denial of reduced tax in the host country on dividend, interest, and royalty income from sources within the host country if such income is not attributable to the construction activity and the recipient does not otherwise have a permanent establishment in the source country. Dividends

Under the present convention, which does not modify U.S. statutory law in this respect, Japanese residents (other than U.S. citizens) are currently subject to U.S. tax on dividend income from sources within the United States at a 30-percent rate (which is withheld at source) if their U.S. source income does not exceed $19,000 a year ($21,200 for taxable years beginning in 1965) and are subject to tax on U.S. source income at the graduated rates applicable to individuals generally if their U.S. source income exceeds $19,000 a year ($21,200 for taxable years beginning in 1965). Similarly, Japanese corporations which are not engaged in trade or business within the United States are taxable at a flat 30-percent rate on their U.S. source dividend income. Conversely, U.S. citizens (other than those resident in 

Japan), U.S. residents, and U.S. corporations and other entities which do not maintain a permanent establishment in Japan are exempt from Japanese tax on dividends received from Japanese corporations. Moreover, U.S. shareholders of Japanese corporations are allowed a credit aganist their U.S. tax, on a gross-up basis, as if the Japanese withheld tax at a 25-percent rate. Thus, for example, if a U.S. resident receives a $100 dividend from a Japanese corporation, the U.S. taxpayer is required to include $125 in gross income, but is allowed a $25 credit against his tentative U.S. tax, subject, of course, to the overall and per-country limitations of section 904 of the Internal Revenue Code. In addition, if the recipient is a U.S. corporation, it is entitled to a credit for a pro rata share of the Japanese income, war profits, and excess profits tax paid by a 10-percent-owned payor corporation (or 50-percent-owned second tier subsidiary) under the provisions of section 902 of the Internal Revenue Code. 

The 1962 protocol would terminate the provisions of the present convention relating to the foreign tax credit provisions and would adopt the approach for relief of double taxation on dividend income contained in most income tax conventions to which the United States. is a party. This would be accomplished by limiting the tax of the source country on dividends paid to residents, corporations, and other entities of the other country to a maximum rate of 15 percent, regard-. less of the amount of the recipient's income, if the recipient does not have a permanent establishment in the source country. This approach would have the effect of reducing U.S. tax payable by Japanese residents on dividend income from U.S. sources from 30 percent (or higher rates if the U.S. source income of an individual recipient exceeded $19,000 in taxable year 1964) to 15 percent. Conversely, it would generally increase the Japanese tax payable by U.S. shareholders of Japanese corporations from 0 to 15 percent and would increase the overall U.S. tax payable by U.S. shareholders of Japanese corporations by denying them a tax credit against their tentative. 

U.S. tax for the 25 percent Japanese tax they are presently deemed to have paid.

Besides conforming this convention to the type of reciprocal formula embodied in most other conventions, this provision should help the U.S. balance-of-payments position by encouraging Japanese investments in the United States and by making U.S. investments in 

Japan less attractive. Moreover, in light of the present development of the Japanese economy, it is questionable if it is necessary for the United States to continue tax incentives for its citizens to invest in 

Japan. 

The 1962 protocol also provides for a further reduction of tax in the country of the payor to a rate not to exceed 10 percent if the dividend is paid to a corporate shareholder of the other country who, together with no more than three other 10-percent-or-more corporate shareholders, owns at least 50 percent of the total voting power of all classes of stock, entitled to vote, or 50 percent of the total value of all classes of stock, of the distributing corporation. This provision would not apply, however, if more than 25 percent of the gross income of the distributing corporation consists of interest and dividends from lessthan-50-percent-owned subsidiary corporations. This additional reduction of tax by the source country on intercorporate dividends is consistent with the policy adopted by the United States in its convention with Norway and in the proposed conventions with Luxembourg and Sweden. The 1962 protocol differs from these conventions by extending the reduced rate to cases where 50 percent of the value of the stock of the distributing corporation is held by not more than four 10percent-or-more corporate shareholders (one of whom is the recipient) in addition to cases where such persons own 50 percent of the voting stock of the distributing corporation. However, by extending the scope of the provision, the protocol more closely approximates the 

Internal Revenue Code treatment of intercorporate dividends which allows an 85-percent dividends received deduction to corporate shareholders without regard to the degree of their stockownership in the distributing corporation. Interest

The present convention provides, in general, that interest paid from sources within one country to a resident, corporation, or other entity of the other country not maintaining a permanent establishment in the first country is to be taxed in the source country at a rate not to exceed 15 percent. The 1962 protocol reduces the maximum source-country rate to 10 percent. However, in the case of Japanese-source interest, the present 5-percent Japanese statutory withholding rate will continue to apply until March 31, 1965, at which time the rate would increase to the proposed 10-percent treaty rate. These rates compare with the 30-percent United States and 20-percent Japanese (after 

March 31, 1965) withholding rates that would be applicable in the absence of the convention.

The 1960 protocol provides that interest received by the Bank of Japan, and the Export-Import Bank of Japan, from sources within the United States, and interest received by the Federal Reserve banks of the United States, and the Export-Import Bank of Washington, from sources within Japan, are to be completely exempt from tax in the source country. This provision expands the exemption provided by the 1957 protocol (which would be superseded) so as to include Jual

the Bank of Japan and the Federal Reserve banks of the United States. In general, the additional exemptions provided by the 1960 protocol correspond to the provisions of section 895 of the Internal Revenue Code which exempt foreign central banks of issue from U.S. tax on U.S. source interest income. Moreover, complete exemption of interest income from tax in a source country is granted in many tax conventions to which the United States is a party. 

Income from real property and mineral royalties Under the present convention, a resident, or corporation or other entity of one of the contracting countries who receives mineral royalties, or income from real property, from sources within the other country may elect to be taxed in the source country on a net income basis. The 1960 protocol makes it clear that the election to be taxed on a net basis applies only to income from real property and royalties. in respect of the operation of mines, quarries, or other natural resources. 

Nonmineral royalties

Under the present convention, royalty income received from sources within one of the contracting countries by a resident, corporation, or other entity of the other country not maintaining a permanent establishment in the first country is taxable in the source country at a rate not to exceed 15 percent. The 1962 protocol provides that the source country rate of tax shall be at a rate not to exceed 10 percent. 

Since U.S. individuals and corporations receive substantially moreroyalty income from Japanese sources than Japanese persons receive from the United States, the proposed reduction in source country tax should benefit the United States to the greater extent. The protocol also redefines the term "royalties" to bring it more in line with the more specific definitions contained in the more recent conventions to which the United States is a party. The substance of the definition is, however, not changed. 

Compensation for personal services

The 1960 and 1962 protocols make two basic changes in the tax treatment of income from personal services. 

First, under the present convention, wages, salaries, or similar compensation paid by a government of one of the contracting countries. to one of its citizens for services rendered as an employee of the government in the discharge of a governmental function, are, in general, exempt from tax by the other country. The 1960 protocol expands the application of this provision to provide (1) that government pension and annuity payments received by citizens of the payor country will be exempt from tax in the other country if the pension or annuity is paid with respect to services rendered as an employee of the payor government in discharge of its governmental functions and (2) that pensions or annuities paid to residents of one country by the government of the other country are to be taxed only in the country of the recipient's residence to the extent the pension or annuity is allocable to services the remuneration for which was exempt from tax by the payor country. Thus, for example, U.S.. citizens resident in Japan (other than those admitted to Japan on a permanent residence basis) will be exempt from Japanese tax on their U.S. Government annuities and Japanese nationals resident in 

Japan will be exempt from U.S. tax on pensions received from the

United States on account of services rendered outside the United States as employees of the U.S. Government. 

Second, under the present convention, a resident of one of the contracting countries is exempt from tax in the other contracting country on compensation received for labor or personal services performed in the other country of (1) the resident is present in the other country for not more than 180 days during a taxable year and (2) the compensation is received for services performed as an officer or employee of a corporation of the country in which the recipient of the income is resident. Exemption is also provided if a resident of one country is present in the other country for a period not exceeding 90 days in a taxable year if the compensation received does not exceed $3,000. 

The 1962 protocol does not change the 90-day $3,000 rule; however, it makes the 180-day rule inapplicable if (1) the officer or employee who performs the services for which the compensation is received owns 25 percent or more of the voting stock of the corporation, or 25 percent of the value of all classes of stock of the corporation, and (2) 50 percent or more of the income of the corporation from sources within the other country is derived from furnishing the labor or personal services of one of more such 25-percent shareholders, In applying the 25-percent stockownership test, a person is considered to own stock held, directly or indirectly, by his brothers, sisters, spouse, ancestors, and descendants. The 50-percent test is based on net income from sources within the other country, plus gross compensation paid 25-percent shareholders for furnishing labor or personal services. 

The protocol also provides, in effect, that if a corporation receives income for furnishing the labor or personal services of a 25-percent shareholder, and 50 percent or more of the corporation's net income (plus gross compensation paid 25-percent shareholders) consists of such income, the corporation will be taxable on such income in the country where the services are performed, whether or not it maintains a permanent establishment in that country. There would, of course, be no tax at the corporate level if such amounts are paid out as compensation to 25-percent shareholders or if the amounts received would have been exempt under the 90-day, $3,000 rule if distributed to the shareholder who performs the service. 

The special rule limiting application of the 180-day exemption provision for corporate officers and employees may apply to persons of any profession who perform personal services for a corporation in which they owned 25 percent of the stock. However, the provision will have its greatest impact in the case of persons engaged in the entertainment business. In general, the change will not affect the overall tax liability of U.S. persons affected, but merely shifts the tax from the country of residence to the country in which the services are performed. Thus, for example, if a U.S. citizen performs personal services in Japan as an employee of a domestic corporation in which he owns 100 percent of the stock, the compensation he receives from his corporation (providing it exceeds $3,000) will be taxable in Japan, regardless of how long he is present in Japan during the year. However, the Japanese tax would be allowable as a credit against the shareholder's tentative U.S. tax. Similar results would also follow if the compensation remained at the corporate level in that the U.S. corporation would be taxable on the personal services income as income from sources within Japan, even if the corporation does not have a permanent establishment in Japan (since personal service income would be

excluded from the term "industrial or commercial profits") and the Japanese tax would be allowed as a credit against U.S. tax otherwise payable by the corporation.

Althouth there is some question whether the constructive ownership rules adopted are appropriate in that they provide attribution between possibly estranged persons (for example, brothers or sisters), there is little practical likelihood that the shareholders will in practice be estranged since at least 50 percent of the adjusted net income of the corporation must be derived from labor or personal services of one or more major shareholders.

Source of income rules The present convention establishes rules for determining the source of income for convention purposes. These rules generally parallel the U.S. statutory rules contained in sections 861 through 863 of the 

Internal Revenue Code. The 1960 and 1962 protocols modify the rules relating to interest and personal service income. 

In the case of interest, the present convention provides that interest paid by an enterprise of one of the contracting countries is to be treated as income from sources within the country of which the payor is an enterprise, unless the payor has a permanent establishment in the other country. If the payor has a permanent establishment in the other country, the convention source rule is no longer applicable and the source of interest income is determined under the appropriate laws of the United States and Japan. Since the laws of the countries may differ, so that both may treat the same income as income from sources within their respective countries, the present convention does not satisfactorily eliminate double taxation of income in this instance. 

For example, if a U.S. corporation receives interest from a Japanese corporation which maintains a permanent establishment in the United States, the interest paid by the Japanese debtor may be treated by the Japanese as income from sources within Japan (on the basis that the payor is a Japanese corporation) and may be treated by the United States as income from sources within the United States (if, for example, the payor is engaged in trade or business within the United States and derived 20 percent or more of its gross income from sources within the United States over the 3-year period ending with the close of the taxable year of the payor preceding the payment of the interest (sec. 861(a)(1)(B) of the 

Internal Revenue Code of 1954)). Under these circumstances, although the payor may withhold Japanese tax on the payment of the interest on the basis that the interest is Japanese source income, the United States may deny the recipient of the income a credit for the Japanese tax against his U.S. tax (under the provisions of sec. 

904 of the Internal Revenue Code) on the grounds that the recipient has no income from sources within Japan. 

The 1960 protocol modifies the basic rule to provide that interest paid by an enterprise of one of the contracting states is to be treated as income from sources within the payor's country, whether or not the payor maintains a permanent establishment in the other country. 

However, this basic rule is subject to two exceptions, if the indebtedness is incurred other than in connection with the purchase of ships or aircraft. First, if the payor maintains a permanent establishment in the other country and interest is paid on indebtedness incurred for the use of the permanent establishment in the other country, or on

banking deposits made with the permanent establishment in the other country, the interest is deemed to be from sources within the country where the permanent establishment is located. Second, if the interest is paid on indebtedness incurred for the use of a permanent establishment of one of the enterprises located in a third country, or on banking deposits made with a permanent establishment of an enterprise of one of the contracting countries located in a third country, the income is to be treated as income from sources within the third country. These exceptions have the effect of treating such permanent establishments as separate corporations for source of income purposes and require the treaty country in which the payor is incorporated to exclude interest paid by a permanent establishment outside of that country from tax. Although this rule has not previously been adopted in income tax conventions to which the United States is a party, the result is consistent with the basic approach adopted in other conventions which exempt all interest from tax in the source country if the recipient does not have a permanent establishment in that country.

The 1962 protocol amends the source rule relating to the source of income of compensation for labor or personal services by providing that income derived by a corporation or other entity for furnishing labor or personal services is to be treated as income from sources within the country where the labor or services are rendered. This change correlates with the proposed change in the taxation of personal service income so as to permit the source country to tax a nonresident foreign corporation on the service income attributable to a 25-percent shareholder if the income remains at the corporate level. The 1962 protocol also conforms the source rule relating to income from royalties to the new definition contained in article VII(2) of the protocol. 

Foreign tax credits

As previously noted, under the present convention U.S. citizens, residents, and corporations and other entities are allowed a tax credit against U.S. tax in an amount equal to 25 percent of their dividend income received from Japanese corporations, on a gross-up basis, even though no Japanese tax is in fact paid on the distribution of a dividend by a Japanese corporation. The 1962 protocol would eliminate this provision. Moreover, as a result of the changes made by the 1960 and 1962 protocols, the foreign tax credit provision of the convention would be amended so as to be consistent with corresponding provisions in other income tax conventions to which the United States is a party. 

Thus, the United States and Japan could tax their residents, corporations, and other entities (and citizens in the case of the United States) as if the convention had not come into effect if they allow such persons a foreign tax credit for the amount of tax paid to the other country (or any political subdivision or local government of the other country). 

In general, the statutory foreign tax credit provisions of the United States and Japan are similar, although Japan limits allowance of indirect credits to taxes paid by first tier corporations, rather than first and second tier foreign corporations as allowed by the United States, and Japanese law does not provide for a carryover or carryback of excess credits. The 1960 protocol also provides that the 

U.S. foreign tax credit would be determined in accordance with U.S. law applicable to the taxable year in question, rather than in accord-

ance with the Internal Revenue Code in effect on January 1, 1954, as is required under the present convention. 

Effective date

In general, the modifications made by the 1960 and 1962 protocols would become effective with respect to payments made on or after January 1 of the year immediately following the year in which the exchange of instruments of ratification takes place. However, the change in the definition of a "permanent establishment," so as to include construction projects of more than 12 months' duration, would be applicable only with respect to projects begun after such January 1. 

Moreover, the present rules would continue to apply to dividend payments made during the first 2-year period beginning on January 1 of the year immediately following the year in which the exchange of instruments of ratification takes place if a dividend is paid by a Japanese corporation to a U.S. shareholder (other than a U.S. corporate shareholder owning 10 percent or more of the stock of the payor corporation) with respect to stock held by the shareholder prior to such 

January 1. However, in the third year, Japan would withhold tax at a 7.5-percent rate, rather than at a zero rate, and the United States would allow a credit, on a gross-up basis, of 12% percent of the dividend. rather than 25 percent of the dividend. Thus, the new rules which provide for Japanese tax at a 15-percent rate, and for the elimination of the present 25-percent foreign tax credit for U.S. tax purposes, would apply only with respect to payments made after the 

3-year transitional period.

STATEMENT OF TREASURY DEPARTMENT WITH RESPECT TO PROTOCOLS DATED MAY 7, 1960, AND August 14, 1962, AMENDING THE INCOME TAX CONVENTION BETWEEN THE UNITED STATES AND JAPAN 

Protocols were signed at Tokyo on May 7, 1960, and August 14, 1962, respectively, modifying and supplenting the existing Convention for the Avoidance of Double Taxation and the Prevention of Fiscal

Evasion with Respect to Taxes on Income signed at Washington on April 16, 1954, as supplemented by the protocol signed in Tokyo on March 23, 1957. The proposed protocols had their inception in the desire of the contracting parties to make certain changes in the convention in the light of their experience with it in recent years. The modifications embodied in these protocols would bring the present. convention into greater conformity with other income tax conventions to which the United States is a party. The 1960 protocol contains eight articles; the 1962 protocol contains nine articles. They cover a broad range of subjects, including the taxation of business and personal services income, dividends, interest, rents and royalties, as well as defining the source of items of income and specifying rules for the application of tax credits. A summary and explanation of the proto cols follows. 

PROTOCOL, 1960 Article I 

This article amends the definition of "competent authorities" in paragraph (1)(h) of article II of the convention to mean, in the case of the United States, "the Secretary of the Treasury or his authorized representative," in lieu of the former designation of the Commissioner of Internal Revenue, as authorized by the Secretary of the Treasury.

This purely technical revision is intended to conform the language of the convention to the terminology of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954, as well as the more recent U.S. tax treaties. 

Article II 

This provision amends article VI of the convention, which relates to the taxation of interest payments.

Paragraph (1) of this amendment is superseded by a new paragraph (1) contained in the 1962 protocol; the provision will be discussed in connection with the latter protocol. 

Paragraph (2) reaffirms and expands an exception to the foregoing rule incorporated by the 1957 protocol (which will terminate when the changes proposed in this article come into effect). Under subparagraph (a), the total exemption from U.S. income tax granted to interest received from sources within the United States by the Export-Import Bank of Japan is preserved and extended to similar interest received by the Bank of Japan, which performs the functions of a central bank in Japan. Under subparagraph (b), the total exemption from income tax accorded by Japan to interest received by the Export-Import Bank of Washington from sources within Japan is maintained and expanded to cover similar receipts by the Federal Reserve banks of the 

United States.

Paragraph (3) separates the definition of interest from the body of the operative provision contained in paragraph (1) of this article, and makes no substantive change.

Article III Article III of the protocol amends article VIII of the convention by adding the words "with respect to such income" immediately before the words, "for any taxable year". This is a minor clarifying amendment which the United States sought in a provision of the convention which gives relief to foreigners investing in U.S. real estate, as well as 

U.S. investors in Japan. Sections 871 and 881 of the U.S. Internal 

Revenue Code impose a tax of 30 percent of the gross amount of such income from U.S. sources if the foreign recipient is not engaged in trade or business in the United States. No deduction for expenses incurred in obtaining the income is provided under the code. This can result in hardship if the net income derived from the U.S. property is substantially less than the gross (as it would be in many cases, where deductible operating expenses, depreciation or depletion are incurred). 

Article VIII of the convention permits foreign investors an election to be taxed on their rental and royalty income "on a net basis as if such resident or corporation or other entity had a permanent establishment in such other State during such taxable year". Similar provisions are . contained in other tax treaties to which the United States is a party. 

It has been suggested that the present language of article VIII of the convention might permit a foreign investor electing under that article to be taxed on a net basis in the United States not only on rental or royalty income but also on all other income from U.S. sources. For example, a foreign corporation may claim an intercorporate dividend-received deduction on dividends from U.S. sources under section 243 of the Internal Revenue Code on the ground that such a deduction is available to foreign corporations engaged in business in the United States through permanent establishments.

The amendment embodied in the protocol makes it clear that the net basis election extends only to the rental and royalty income referred to in the article.

Article IV 

This provision expands the income tax exemptions granted to Government employees under article X of the convention. 

Article X of the present convention provides that wages, salaries, and similar compensation paid by one of the contracting states to its own citizens (other than persons who are also citizens of the other contracting state or have been admitted to the other state for permanent residence) shall be exempt from tax in the other state, if the services for which the compensation is paid are not rendered in connection with a trade or business carried on for profit by the government involved. Under the new subparagraph (1), as added by the protocol, this exemption is confirmed and extended to pension payments. Thus, not only wages, salaries, and similar remuneration paid by (or paid out of funds created by) the United States but also pension and annuity payments made by the U.S. Government to its citizens residing in Japan (other than as permanent residents thereof) will be exempted from Japanese income tax. Conversely, individual 

Japanese nationals who may have retired as employees of their government and reside (other than on a permanent basis) in the United States will be exempt from U.S. tax on pensions or annuities paid to them by the Government of Japan, or out of funds to which that Government contributes, with respect to services rendered as employees of the Japanese Government in the discharge of governmental functions, thus expanding the exemption presently granted by the United States with respect to salaries, wages, and similar compensation paid by the Government of Japan to such employees. 

The protocol clarifies the requirement that all payments covered by paragraph (1) of article X of the Convention must be made to the recipients with respect to services rendered as employees of the paying government and in the discharge of a governmental function, in order to qualify for this exemption. Moreover, a new subparagraph (c) of paragraph (1) reiterates the limitation previously set forth in paragraph (2), providing that paragraph (1) shall not apply to pensions or annuities, as well as salaries, wages or similar compensation, paid with respect to services rendered in connection with any trade or business conducted by the government of either contracting state for purposes of profit.

There is added to article X a new paragraph (2), which states that pension or annuity payments by one contracting state (out of the respective funds referred to in paragraph (1)) to an individual who is a resident of the other contracting state shall be exempt from tax by the payer state to the extent that such payments are allocable to services performed for the payer state, the remuneration for which was itself exempt from tax by the latter state. This exemption applies regardless of whether the pension or annuity payments are attributable to employer or employee contributions or accretions thereto. 

For example, if the U.S. Government employs a resident of Japan to perform services anywhere outside the United States and that employee is not a U.S. citizen, compensation received by that employee for the performance of his services is exempt from United States tax because it is earned by a nonresident alien and is derived from sources

without the United States under section 862(a)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954. Consequently, any pension or annuity payments received by such a Japanese resident after his retirement, with respect to his former employment by the U.S. Government, will also be exempt from U.S. income tax under this new paragraph. The provision is reciprocal, of course, so that any U.S. resident receiving a similar pension or annuity from the Japanese Government with respect to services performed outside Japan will be exempt from Japanese tax thereon.

Article V 

This article revises and extends the source rules affecting interest payments under article XIII (b) of the convention. 

The present convention states the general rule that interest paid by one of the contracting states (including local governments thereof) or by an enterprise of one of the contracting states, shall be treated as income from sources within such state. In the case of an enterprise, however, the rule is applicable only if the enterprise does not have a permanent establishment in the other contracting state; and the convention does not specify the source of the interest income if such a permanent establishment is maintained. In this situation, the laws of each of the contracting states apply, and the possibility of conflict arises. For example, if Japan considers the source of the income to be Japanese, it will tax; at the same time, if the United 

States considers the income to be from sources within the United 

States, it may deny a credit for the Japanese tax.

To deal with this situation, the protocol adds a new subparagraph (i) to article XIII(b) of the convention; this provides that interest paid by an enterprise of one contracting state on indebtedness incurred for the use of (or banking deposits made with) a permanent establishment maintained by that enterprise in the other contracting state in connection with the conduct of a trade or business of that permanent enterprise shall be treated as income from sources in the contracting state where such permanent establsihment is situated. 

For example, assume that X, a U.S. bank, operates foreign branch Y in Japan as a permenent establishment within the meaning of the convention. If A, a Japanese national residing in Japan, maintains a time deposit account in Y and receives interest payments from Y with respect to his account, such payments would be deemed to have their source in Japan, and would be exempt from U.S. income tax withholding.

The amendment of article XIII(b) effected by the protocol extends this source rule to situations where an enterprise of one of the contracting states maintains a permanent establishment in a third country. Interest paid by such enterprise on indebtedness incurred for the use of that establishment, or banking deposits which the establishment receives, in the conduct of a trade or business is deemed to be derived from sources outside both contracting states. For example, assume that U.S. bank X operates its branch Ž as a permanent establishment in Hong Kong, and that Japanese manufacturer L maintains permanent establishment M, a manufacturing branch, in Hong Kong. In the event that M received interest payments on time deposits maintained with Z, such payments would be deemed to have their source outside the United States, and hence would not be subject to withholding for U.S. income tax. On the other hand,

any interest paid by M to Z with respect to indebtedness incurred for its own use-while it would be subject to U.S. tax as income of 

X-would be deemed to have its source in Hong Kong. Thus, it would not be subject to Japanese withholding tax, and the available U.S. tax credit would be computed on the same basis. 

These exceptions do not apply, however, in the case of interest payments on indebtedness incurred in connection with the purchase of ships or aircraft. Under the general interest source rule, such payments are always deemed to have their source in the state of paying enterprise, whether or not that enterprise has a permanent establishment in the other contracting state or in a third state. For example, assume that U.S. aircraft manufacturer P sells an airplane to Japanese corporation R, an airline which derives 20 percent or more of its gross income from sources within the United States. P helps R to finance this purchase by taking back a series of notes. In the absence of any treaty provision governing the source of such payments, Japan would withhold its tax from interest payments on such notes, on the ground. that such income was derived from sources within Japan. R's interest payments to P would be deemed to have its source within the United States for U.S. income tax purposes, however, under section. 

861 of the Internal Revenue Code; and P might accordingly be denied a credit for the Japanese tax withheld. Under the new general rule of amended article XIII (b), as applied to R's aircraft purchase, by article V of this protocol, the source of interest payments to P is assigned to Japan, and P will be entitled to a foreign tax credit. Applying to interest payable in connection with the purchase of ships and aircraft a different source rule from that applied to indebtedness of other business enterprises mirrors other rules which are a standard feature of U.S. tax conventions. Under article V of the Japanese convention as in other conventions-an air or shipping enterprise of one contracting state is exempted from tax in the other contracting state, whether or not a permanent establishment is maintained there. 

Article VI 

This article modifies article XIV, paragraph (a) of the existing convention to provide that where the United States allows a credit for Japanese income tax, the amount of credit shall be determined in accordance with the revenue laws of the United States. The original provision required the determination of the credit under section 131 of the Internal Revenue Code as it was in effect on January 1, 1954. 

This freeze of the credit provisions as of a particular date might be interpreted as barring the application to taxpayers deriving incomefrom Japan of subsequent modifications in our statutory tax credit. rules, even where such modifications did not impinge on the principle of the credit. The change proposed in article VI of the protocol retains the existing commitment to that principle, while providing flexibility as to technical formulation of the credit rules. 

Article XIV of the convention would also be amended by deleting a reference to the treatment of interest income where payment is attributable to a permanent establishment maintained in Japan by a U.S. enterprise; tax credit will now be provided for through application of the source rules of article XIII (b) of the convention, as amended. 

by article V of this protocol.

Article VII 

This article provides that the supplementary protocol signed in 1957 shall terminate when the provisions of article VI of the convention, as modified by article II of this protocol, come into effect. 

Article VIII 

This provision contains standard procedural rules for effectuating the protocol. Paragraph (1) states an intention to obtain ratification and an exchange of instruments of ratification as soon as possible. 

Paragraph (2) provides that the protocol shall enter into force on the date that such an exchange of instruments of ratification takes place, and shall be applicable to income or profits derived during taxable years beginning on or after the 1st day of January of the calendar year in which such exchange takes place. The third paragraph provides for the continuation in force of the protocol as long as the underlying convention remains effective. 

THE 1962 PROTOCOL Article I 

This provision makes three changes in the definitions of terms set forth in the first paragraph of article II of the convention. 

1. Definition of permanent establishment.-Under the convention, as under all of the U.S. tax conventions, an enterprise of one of the contracting states is taxable by the other contracting state on commercial or industrial profits only if it maintains a permanent establishment in that other state. In general, if a permanent establishment is maintained in the other state, that state may tax all of the enterprise's income derived from sources in that state. Paragraph (1)(c) of article II of the convention contains the definition of "permanent establishment".

In the past the Japanese have asserted tax against American enterprises on the ground that a permanent establishment exists whenever the company carries on a construction, installation or assembly project in Japan, or carries on supervisory activities in connection with such a project. The result of this position would be to impose tax on all of the income of the affected enterprise from sources within Japan.

The amendment embodied in article I of the protocol would clarify this issue by specifying that an enterprise of one of the contracting states will be deemed to have a permanent establishment in the other contracting state if it carries out a construction, installation, or assembly project in the other state for a period in excess of 12 months, or carries on supervisory activities in connection with such a project for more than 12 months. If the project or activities last 12 months or less, there would be no permanent establishment. The recognition. of this special category of permanent establishment corresponds to provisions in the conventions with Austria and West Germany, and is in accordance with the approach incorporated in the draft convention approved by the Fiscal Committee of the Organization for Eco nomic Cooperation and Development (OECD). A similar definition, with a 6-month time limit, appears in the proposed convention with Luxembourg. Furthermore, the existence of this type of permanent establishment is accorded limited significance. Under amendments made by articles III, IV, and V of the protocol, discussed below, unrelated dividend, interest, or royalty income of the enterprise main-

taining the establishment would not become subject to tax by reason. of its existence. These amendments, taken together, thus tend to restrict the rights formerly claimed by Japan under the convention with respect to construction activities, since they provide clear exemption where the activities last a year or less and they also protect the rights of U.S. corporations to reduced rates of tax under the convention on income unrelated to specific projects or supervisory activities which they may undertake in Japan. In most instances, the greatest benefit will accrue to corporations earning substantial royalties in Japan, which will no longer be deterred from undertaking long-term projects in Japan because of a fear that they might forfeit the convention's reduced tax rates on unrelated royalty earnings.

On the other hand, U.S. tax jurisdiction will actually be broadened in this area, since the United States has not interpreted the conduct of such activities alone as constituting the maintenance of a permanent establishment in the United States under the treaty. The net result will be a uniform treatment of these special activities, for income tax purposes, by the two contracting states. 

2. Definition of "Japanese corporation or other entity".-Article I 

of the protocol substantially revises this definition, appearing in paragraph (1)(f) of article II of the convention, to clarify it and aline it more closely with the Japanese tax law defining those entities subject to income tax in Japan on their worldwide income. The original definition did not encompass certain entities which, although not considered juridical persons as such, are taxed as though they were juridical persons created or organized in Japan. Specific reference to partnerships is omitted, on the other hand, in order to exclude from the scope of the definition those Japanese partnerships which are not treated as juridical persons for purposes of Japanese tax. A similar provision exists in the convention with Federal Republic of Germany for the purpose of defining a "German company" under that treaty. 

3. Definition of "industrial or commercial profits".-Paragraph (1)(i) of article II of the convention is divided into two subdivisions. 

These repeat the substance of the existing subparagraph, but add one new feature. The convention presently excludes personal services income from the definition of industrial or commercial profits. The protocol refines this exclusion into two categories: (a) Compensation received by an individual for his own labor or personal services, and (b) income received by a corporation or other entity of one of the contracting states from sources within the other state for furnishing the labor or personal services of an individual who would not qualify for an exemption with respect to his remuneration under the new rules incorporated in article IX of the convention by article VI of this protocol. Income derived by a corporation or entity from the furnishing of other services would be considered commercial and industrial profits.

This refinement is intended to prevent individuals who earn large amounts of income for performing personal services of brief duration, such as entertainers, from avoiding the taxes imposed on their remuneration by forming a corporation to employ them. So long as it maintained no permanent establishment in the contracting state in which the services are to be performed, the corporation would be exempt from tax there, despite the fact that its earnings are in effect

remuneration for the services of its stockholders. The situations at which this change is directed are discussed below in the explanation of article VI of the protocol. 

Article II 

This provision amends article III of the convention to make it clear that if an enterprise of one state is deemed to have in the other state a permanent establishment under the special rule set forth in paragraph (1) of article I of the protocol (relating to construction, installation, and assembly projects and related activities), the general rule (applied to enterprises with other types of permanent establishments) that the latter state may impose its tax upon the entire income of the enterprise from sources within such state is inapplicable to interest, dividends, and royalties which are unrelated to the activities giving rise to the permanent establishment. Thus, interest, dividends, and royalties received by the enterprise from sources within such other state will continue to be eligible for the reduced rates provided under the convention with respect to recipients who do not maintain a permanent establishment in the payer state, provided that the interest, dividend, or royalty is not attributable to the trade, business, or assets of the special permanent establishment or to a transaction which is of a kind normally engaged in by such permanent establishment. For example, if a U.S. corporation undertakes a construction project in Japan which lasts for more than 12 months, the profits arising from such activities would be subject to tax in Japan, because of its duration. But if the U.S. corporation were to purchase stocks or bonds in Japanese corporations from its home office while its project was in process, or were it to license a patent for use in Japan during that interval, the resulting dividend or royalty income would continue to be eligible for the reduced rates provided in the convention. 

If, however, the U.S. corporation maintained a Japanese bank account to provide funds for the construction project and temporarily invested funds from that account in Japanese short-term indebtedness, the resulting interest would be subject to the full rate of Japanese tax, since it would be attributable to assets connected with the construction project.

Article III 

This provision replaces paragraph (1) of article VI of the convention (which was not affected by the 1960 protocol) with a new provision concerning the taxation of interest income. 

Prior to the effective date of this amendment, interest received from sources within one state by a resident, corporation or other entity of the other state not having a permanent establishment in the former state is subject to tax in the source state at a maximum rate of 15 percent. At the time this protocol was under consideration, the normal tax withholding rate in Japan of 20 percent with respect to interest income of nonresident individuals and foreign corporations not having businesses in Japan had been reduced to 10 percent (Japanese special measures law art. 3(1), (2), as in effect through March 31, 1963). Since that rate was less than the maximum fixed in the convention, the United States negotiated a reduction of the maximum treaty rate to 10 percent, seeking greater conformity to most of our other conventions, under which interest income is exempt from tax in the state of source. Under a 1963 amendment to the Japanese tax special measures law (law No. 65 of 1963), article

3(1), (2) was amended to reduce the Japanese withholding rate to 5 percent until March 31, 1965. Upon expiration of this provisional measure, the withholding rate would revert to 20 percent. The effect of the rate change incorporated by article III of this protocol is to permit qualified U.S. payees to receive the benefit of the 5-percent withholding rate through March 31, 1965, and to be subject to the 10-percent ceiling under the convention thereafter. 

As previously indicated, the reduced rate of tax will be applicable to certain interest received by an enterprise of one contracting state, even though it is considered to have a permanent establishment in the other state by reason of construction or similar activities described in paragraph (1) of article I of this protocol. 

Article IV 

This article adds a new article VIA to the convention, providing for reductions in the rate of each country's tax on dividends, in conformity with the relief generally granted under other income tax conventions to which the United States is a party. The original convention made no reduction in the 30-percent minimum rate of U.S. tax on dividends paid to a Japanese resident, corporation, or other entity. Japan, on the other hand, was bound by the original convention (art. XIV(b)(ii)) not to impose any tax on dividends received by a U.S. resident, corporation or other entity not having a permanent establishment in Japan, other than the tax imposed on the income or profits of the corporation out of which such dividends were paid. Under this provision, U.S. recipients received dividends free of the Japanese withholding tax otherwise imposed at the rate of 20 percent. In addition, the United States agreed to grant a "deemed" foreign tax credit against its tax for 25 percent of the amount of a dividend received by a U.S. resident, corporation or other entity from a Japanese corporation, provided that an amount equal to such credit was included in the taxpayer's gross income. 

Subject to certain transitional rules discussed in connection with article IX of this protocol, article VIA of the convention provides, in conformity with similar provisions of other conventions concluded by the United States, that dividends paid by a corporation of one state to a resident, corporation or other entity of the other state are generally to be taxed at a rate not in excess of 15 percent if the recipient has no permanent establishment in the former state (or if the recipient has therein only a special permanent establishment of the type described in paragraph (1) of article I of the protocol and the dividend is unrelated thereto). Moreover, if (a) the dividend recipient is a corporation which owned more than 50 percent of the stock of the paying corporation, either alone or in association with not more than three other corporations of the same state, each owning at least 10 percent of the stock of the paying corporation, for the 12-month period immediately preceding payment of the dividends, and (b) not more than 25 percent of the gross income of the paying corporation (if not a bank or other such lending institution) was derived from interest and dividends received from persons other than subsidiary corporations, the rate of tax to be imposed by the source state may not exceed 10 percent. A similar provision is contained in the income tax convention, as amended, concluded between the United States and Norway, and in the proposed convention with Luxembourg.

​For purposes of this provision, the term "subsidiary corporation” is defined to mean any corporation or other entity of which more than 50 percent of the stock is owned by the payer corporation. The 

50-percent ownership test is met, wherever it appears in this article, by ownership of the specified percentages of either the total voting power of all classes of stock entitled to vote or the otal value of all classes of stock. The use of a value yardstick as an alternative to a voting test to determine the availability of the reduced rate is an innovation in our tax conventions, but follows criteria established in several provisions of the Internal Revenue Code, as, for example, in determining constructive ownership of stock under section 

318(a)(2) (Č) or applying the stock ownership test for personal holding company purposes under section 542 (a) or for defining a foreign investment company under section 1246(b). 

Article V 

This provision substitutes a new article VII for the same article of the present convention, which deals with the taxation of royalty income.

A final paragraph in this article provides that in the event of any substantial change in the taxes of either contracting state the competent authorities of both states may consult with each other to consider whether such change makes it appropriate to amend the provisions of this article.

Article VII of the existing convention limits to 15 percent the maximum rate of tax which can be imposed by one contracting state on royalties and other similar amounts received from sources within such state, as consideration for the right to use certain properties, by a resident or corporation or other entity of the other contracting state not having a permanent establishment in the former state. The 15 percent rate is the present Japanese statutory rate applied against outgoing royalties.

The first paragraph of article VII, as amended by this protocol, reduces the maximum rate to 10 percent if the recipient does not maintain a permanent establishment in the payer's state (or if it maintains only the special permanent establishment of the type described in paragraph (1) of article I of this protocol and the royalties are not attributable to the trade, business, or assets of that establishment or to a transaction in which that establishment normally engages).

The second paragraph defines the term "royalties" as used in this article. The specific enumeration of rights covered is expanded and includes models, plans, good will, trade brands, films or tapes for radio or television broadcasting and similar rights (as well as property). 

This is in accord with the definitions in our more recent conventions. 

Article VI 

This provision makes a significant change in article IX of the convention by adding a new paragraph (2).

Paragraph (1), which remains intact, provides that an individual resident of one of the contracting states who receives compensation for labor or personal services (including the practice of the liberal professions) performed in the other state in any taxable year during which he is temporarily present in such other state for a period not exceeding a total of 180 days, is fully exempt from the tax of such

other state if his compensation is received for labor or personal services performed as an officer or employee of a resident or corporation or other entity of his own state of residence. Furthermore, if such an individual is present in the other state for a period or periods not exceeding a total of 90 days and his compensation does not exceed $3,000 (or an equivalent sum in yen as computed at the official exchange rate), such income is similarly exempt, no matter who the employer may be.

Under the new paragraph (2) of article IX, if (a) compensation is received for labor or personal services performed by an individual resident of one state in the other state as an officer or employee of a corporation or other entity of the former state, (b) such individual owns, directly or indirectly (taking into account any interest owned directly or indirectly by a brother, sister, spouse, ancestor, or descendant of that individual), at least 25 percent of the total voting power of all classes of stock entitled to vote or of the total value of all classes of stock of such corporation or other entity, and (c) 50 percent or more of the income of such corporation or other entity (computed before deductions for compensation to such 25 percent stockholders) for the taxable year from sources within the state in which the services were performed is derived from the furnishing of such services by one or more individuals with such ownership interests, then such individual will not quality for the exemption provided with respect to personal service compensation under the 180-day rule. Such an individual may still qualify under the 90-day rule for an exemption with respect to compensation of $3,000 or less, however, provided that the corporate income (computed without deduction for his compensation) which is properly allocable to his labor or personal services for such aggregate 

90-day period or periods is $3,000 or less.

The effect of this amendment, together with the parallel amendment of article III of the convention discussed above, is to restrict the exemption privileges of controlling stockholders in personal service corporations and of the corporations themselves in the same manner. For example, where a Japanese corporation owned in accordance with the rules described in article IX and not having a permanent establishment in the United States derives 50 percent or more of its U.S. source income from furnishing the labor or personal services of one or more 25-percent shareholders described in that article, the U.S. source income of the corporation or entity will not qualify as industrial and commercial profits under article III and will be subject to U.S. income tax at corporate rates as if the convention had not come into effect. Similarly, the individual will be subject to tax on compensation received from the corporation for services performed in the United States. If any income of such corporation is allocable to the labor or personal services of a 25-percent shareholder in an amount not exceeding $3,000 for a period or periods of 90 days or less during the taxable year, however, such corporate income will continue to be classified as "industrial and commercial profits" and will be exempted from taxation at the corporate level under article III of the convention, where the enterprise does not maintain a permanent establishment in the United States. Similarly, the individual may continue to claim exemption under the 90-day, $3,000 limitation of article IX.

Article VII 

This provision amends the source rules contained in article XIII of the convention in two respects. 

First, paragraph (1) substitutes a new paragraph (f) for the existing provision concerning personal service income. The existing provision provided that compensation for labor or personal services shall be treated as income from sources within the country within which the services are rendered; this rule is expanded to provide that if a corporation or other entity furnishes such services, the state within which the furnished services are rendered shall be treated as the source of the income derived by the corporation or other entity. This change is in accord with the definition of industrial and commercial profits and the treatment of income received for personal services furnished by corporate employees, as set forth in parallel amendments to articles I and VI of the convention, respectively. 

The second paragraph of this article, amending paragraph XIII(g) of the convention, conforms the source rules governing royalty income to the substantive changes made by this protocol in the definition of royalty income. The existing convention declared that royalties for using, or for the right to use, in one of the contracting states property enumerated in the existing article VII of the convention as a source of royalty payments, shall be treated as income from sources within such state. The amended provision expands the kinds of payments treated in the same manner as royalty payments (to include "rentals and other similar amounts") and refers to the additional types of property set forth in amended article VII, which is discussed above. 

A special problem arose during the negotiation of the 1962 protocol with regard to payments in respect of technical services. Under article XIII(f) of the convention, the source of income derived from performing or furnishing personal services is the place where the services are performed. Under Japanese law, regulations, and circulars, however, technical services income is treated as royalty or rental income and therefore taxed where the income is received from an individual or corporation carrying on a business activity in Japan (income tax law, art. I(3)(vi)). The treaty source rule provides that royalty income is to have its source in the state where the items in respect of which payment is made are used or may be used. In the course of the discussions, it developed that, under the definition of royalties contained in article V of the protocol and under the source rule of article VII thereof, Japan would treat technical service payments as royalties when the product of such services is used in Japan. In view of the fact that such a rule of construction would go beyond the avowed intent of the contracting parties, it was decided that paragraph(1) of article VII should be made part of the protocol and that the two countries should enter into an exchange of notes, acknowledging an understanding concerning uniform interpretation of the convention provisions. These notes, and accompanying memorandum, were submitted to the Senate in conjunction with this protocol. 

Under the exchange of notes, where a U.S. resident, corporation, or other entity renders to a Japanese resident, corporation, or other entity technical services consisting of preparing or developing designs, models, plans, drawings, or other like property or by performing engineering, research, testing, experimental, or other like services, the

payments made in respect of these services are not to be considered as royalties if those payments cover costs and a normal profit for the services rendered and if they are not contingent upon, or coterminous with, the use of, or right to use, the product of such services or the profits or sales derived from the use of the product of such services in Japan. The same rule would apply on a reciprocal basis to payments made to a resident, corporation, or other entity of Japan for services rendered to residents, corporations, or other entities of the United States. If the payments are contingent upon, or coterminous with, the use of a design produced as a result of the technical services rendered in the United States or upon the profits or sales derived. from the use thereof or where they are contingent on the right to use such design, they would be considered royalties, since they are similar to payments under a licensing arrangement.

According to this understanding, if a U.S. corporation renders technical services to a Japanese concern and those services produce income of the type to be classified as personal service income, the fact that such services are rendered in connection with, or to implement, a contract or arrangement providing for the payment of what is considered to be a royalty, has no bearing in determining the source of such income. For example, assume that a U.S. corporation licenses a patent to its Japanese affiliate and receives payment therefor based upon the profits earned by the Japanese affiliate from the sale of equipment produced under the patent. In addition, as a part of the same licensing contract, the U.S. corporation renders technical assistance to the Japanese concern by delivering designs developed in the United States relating to technical improvements in the method of manufacturing the patented equipment. In this case, the payments in respect of such technical assistance would be considered to be income from sources within the United States so long as they are not also based upon the sale profits derived from the use of the patent, or otherwise contingent upon, or conterminous with, the use of, or the right to use, the product of such services or the profits or sales derived from the use of the product of such services in Japan. 

A further issue raised during the discussions involved the allocation of profits in a case where an enterprise of one contracting state manufactures goods in the other state, but makes its plans, creates designs, and conducts similar preliminary or auxiliary activities outside the state where the manufacturing activities take place. In such event, under the protocol as interpreted by the understanding between the contracting states, none of the income of the enterprise arising from the manufacturing activities would be allocated to the state where the preliminary or auxiliary activities are conducted merely by reason of that fact. The expenses incurred in such preliminary or auxiliary activities would, however, be allowed as a deduction from the manufacturing income for purposes of determining the tax of the state where the manufacturing activities take place, to the extent connected with such income. Such deduction would also be allowed where a separate corporation of the other state furnishes technical services connected with the manufacturing income on an arm's-length basis. 

In that event, the source of the separate corporation's income for

performing such services would be determined under the rules discussed above.

For example, if a U.S. corporation engages in the manufacture of certain machinery through its branch in Japan, but has the specifications to be used in such manufacturing prepared in the United States at its domestic laboratories, none of the manufacturing income would be allocated to the United States for purposes of the tax of either contracting state, but the expenses incurred in preparing the specifications would constitute an allowable deduction from the manufacturing income for purposes of Japanese tax. Where a U.S. parent corporation renders such a service to its Japanese subsidiary which is manufacturing the said machinery, the fee paid by the subsidiary to its parent will continue to be an allowable deduction in computing Japanese tax, but will normally not constitute income from sources within Japan unless such fee is to be characterized as a royalty under the other provisions of the notes exchanged. 

Article VIII This article amends article XIV of the convention. The resulting changes affect the foreign tax credits to be granted by the two contracting states.

Under the existing convention the United States is required to grant to a citizen, resident, corporation or other entity of the United States, receiving a dividend from a Japanese corporation, a credit (for the underlying Japanese corporate tax on the profits distributed) equal to 25 percent of the amount of the dividend distribution, if the recipient grosses up his income by the 25-percent figure. This provision has been eliminated by this protocol, thus bringing this provision I into alinement with other conventions to which the United States is a party. The provision for the granting of tax credits by the United 

States, as previously discussed, was recast by the 1960 protocol; in this protocol, the provision granting credits against Japanese tax for United States tax is rephrased to base the extent of, and method of calculating, such credit on the appropriate provisions of Japanese law. Under articles 10-3 and 26-7 of the Japanese juridical persons" tax law and under articles 15-9 and 36-2 of the Japanese income tax law, credit is allowed against Japanese tax for taxes paid to foreign I countries in much the same manner as is provided under sections 901 through 905 of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954. In general, a. 

Japanese corporation receiving a dividend from its foreign subsidiary may take credit for the foreign taxes imposed on the foreign subsidiary. 

Both per-country and overall limitations are provided for in Japanese law.

Provision is also made within this article of the protocol to assure that income taxes imposed by political subdivisions, including local governments, of one of the contracting states will be taken into consideration in determining the credit allowed by the other state. 

As has been provided in other conventions to which the United States is a party, the protocol states that Japan satisfies the similar credit requirement of section 901(b)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954 by virtue of the credit Japan grants under the protocol so as to permit alien residents of the United States to credit any taxes paid. to Japan against their U.S. taxes.
