Legislative Background 

1. General Home Rule Issue 

In recent years members of the General Court have been disturbed by complaints of local officials and others that the School Building Assistance

-3 Commission has been "unreasonable" in certain cases in exercising its broad statutory power to approve or disapprove local school building projects for which state aid is being sought. Such views have been expressed openly or privately by local authorities and by citizens with respect to projects in such communities as Ashland, Melrose, Millville, Swampscott and West Boylston

Some local criticisms have motivated heated "letters to the editor". 

As a result, legislative proposals have been filed calling for legislative investigation of the practices of the Commission, or for modification of Commission decisions. Thus, H.2371 of 1958 (filed by Senator

In support of this opposition opponents of the Millville bill declared that it was not a proper legislative function to have the Senate and House become a Board of Appeals as to decisions on specific school building assistance decisions reached by an agency of the executive department.

Commission Policy and Practice 

The procedures of the School Building Assistance Commission in pro cessing and approving local applications for state aid to school constructia projects are summarized as follows in the Council report: "The School Building Assistance Act stipulates local procedures in applying for state grants-in-aid of the construction, enlargement and original equipping and furnishing of a county agricultural school or of a regional, consolidated or municipal public schoolhouse. "Such application must be filed in prescribed form before any construction is undertaken, accompanied by information, drawings, plans, estimates of cost and proposals for defraying this cost. Upon receipt of the foregoing, if the Commission decides that the proposed construction would be in the best local interest with respect to site, type of construction, sufficiency of accommodation, and otherwise, it must then estimate construction approved costs and compute the state construction grant using the formulae set out in Section 9 of the act ... "The final construction grant, based on the final approved cost of the construction, is paid to the localities in equal annual installments over the life of the school building bonds ... "If the Commission disapproves the plans, cost, site or other aspects of a proposed school building, it must so notify the local government with its reasons for that action. "Initially, approvals or disapprovals of school planning projects are made by the Administrator, but his decisions are subject to the approval of a majority of the commissioners present and voting. However, in all matters requiring the written approval of the Commission, including the approval of projects, a favorable majority vote of the total Commission is necessary (Section 2). It is the usual practice to permit local authorities aggrieved by decisions of the Administrator to appeal to the Commission.

"By statute the Commission must pass upon the 'adequacy' and 'sufficiency' of proposed school buildings for which state aid is sought, and determine whether the site and construction are in the local interest ... 

"The Commission has not established any uniform minimum standards because of the two following considerations as to which there will be strongly clashing points of view: (a) the possibility that a state-prescribed minimum standard would come to be accepted as a maximum beyond which school projects ought not to go; and (b) the fear of educators and others that uniform minimum school planning and construction standards are impossible without uniform minimum educational programming which promotes centralized state controls at the expense of local home rule .... "When plans and specifications of a school building are submitted for approval, the Commission staff reviews the educational requirements for their adequacy in terms of current educational norms and their compliance with the educational specifications approved by the local school committee .... "Architectural plans are examined to determine their adequacy in terms of the above educational considerations ... (and) for violations of ... (state) ... building code provisions ... .... "Generally speaking, the Administrator and Commission will not approve plans which (a) do not meet local educational requirements; (b) fail to comply with state building safety regulations; or (c) contain wasteful or extravagant features." (Council report, pp. 76-78).

Local Criticisms of Commission 

During its school building study the Research Bureau staff considered local complaints against the School Building Assistance Law and Commission which were voiced to it, many of them on an "off the record basis" without documentation. They indicated an undercurrent of local discontent over the state control aspects of the School Building Assistance Law and fear of state "invasions" of municipal independence in the public school field.

The principal local complaint appears to lie against the broad statutory authority of the Commission to determine what are "adequate" and "satisfactory" local school building facilities. Particular hostility is exhibited by local officials against vesting in the School Building Assist-

ance Commission, rather than in the local community and its government, the right to determine what school building facilities are "in the best interests of the city, town, region or county". (School Building Assistance Act, ss. 6 and 8).

In the eyes of many local officials, this statutory language violates local autonomy by giving too much power to the School Building Assistance Commission to pass upon the educational features to be built into proposed new buildings, thus passing rather permanently upon the educational program of the community itself. The statute provides no uniform definition of what is meant by "adequate" and "satisfactory" facilities, and does not require the Commission to establish such uniform definitions. Hence, the Commission is free to use different rules for different communities, and require applicant cities and towns to follow its opinion as to "adequate" and "satisfactory" facilities. On this general score, the Council report states that "The Commission staff is also said to give consideration to what it thinks the individual community can afford in the way of an educational program, thereby really intervening, rightly or wrongly, in a basic financial aspect of municipal home rule." (Council report, p. 78).

Critics of School Building Assistance Law point out that the absence of any statutory provision permitting aggrieved local governments to appeal decisions of the Commission has the effect of making the Commission the final judge in disputes over the "adequacy" and "sufficiency" of proposed local school buildings. The only possible redress available to an aggrieved town is an appeal to the General Court, by filing a bill to settle the dispute by statutory means. 

Local officials complain that the present School Building Assistance Law leaves them without reasonable bargaining power in negotiations

with the Commission.

Local building committees believe they must accept the suggestions of the Commission staff relative to educational specifications and building features, or run the risk of Commission disapproval of their applications for state aid. To paraphrase one local official: "We are told that we can build the school without state aid if we so desire. But we are not allowed to reduce our state income tax payments to offset the portion of that tax collection going to finance the School Building Assistance Program. Since we must pay the income tax in full, and our property taxes are high, we have no real choice but to do business with the state entirely on its terms."

The School Building Assistance Law makes possible two extremes of action by the Commission. On the one hand it can act to prevent costly or wasteful decisions by local officials in school projects; on the other hand, as critics contend, it can be wielded to force inclusion of features of school buildings, or site selections, which local officials think wasteful and unnecessary. In either case, the dispute is in large degree a matter of opinion and standards.

There is often a tendency among local authorities to blame the School Building Assistance Commission for site areas and locations and for construction "frills" which have aroused local resentment or criticism. 

In some of these cases the available information suggests that the Commission had simply approved what local school departments had requested, and that "frills" reflected the decisions of local authorities and pressure groups.

To an extent the Commission serves as a "whipping boy" for local citizens who dislike the aesthetic features or design of a new school. 

The Commission's close collaboration with local school officials has also tended to draw fire from critics of the local educational program. This is particularly likely to happen if the Commission is believed to side with one local faction against another.

Review of Commission Decisions

Some authorities have therefore suggested that the School Building Assistance Law should be amended to allow aggrieved cities and towns to appeal from a decision of the Commission to some higher review body, such as the State Board of Education,or the Governor and Council, or an impartial arbitration panel. 

follows: 

Arguments pro and con attach to each of these alternatives, as (1) An appeal to the Governor and Council is particularly favored by those who believe that "unreasonable" administrative agency decisions should be subject to correction by the Chief Executive and his Council who are elected by the people. It is opposed however, by those persons who feel that the Governor and Council might render "political" decisions. (2) Review by the State Board of Education would, in the opinion of some, provide a less "political" method; but it is opposed by others who consider the State Board of Education biased in favor of one local faction, the educators. (3) Arbitration, favored by still another school of thought, involves the problem of selecting panels which are reasonably unbiased in a matter of intense interest to almost every segment of society. Proponents of an appeal or arbitration procedure argue that such a procedure is especially necessary where the statutory power of the School Building Assistance Commission is so broad and so vaguely defined. 

They point out that in many communities the state contribution is less than the local financial burden, and should not have absolute control. They believe that, in any event, local governments are entitled to some statutory "checks and balances" which will afford reasonable protection to their educational and financial independence.

Opponents believe that an appeal or arbitration procedure will encourage local authorities to resist the reasonable efforts of the Commission to prevent waste in local school construction, and will result in numerous appeals which will bog down the construction of schools. They point out that almost every school project has vigorous opposition from some local group, and that with an appeals procedure available, local political pressures might increase the number and frequency of state-local quarrels. They fear that an appeals procedure would reduce the Commission to an intermediary board and that the appeal board would be called on to make the real decision in too many cases. Finally, opponents believe that there has been considerable exaggeration of the number and severity of state-local disputes under the School Building Assistance Act; they doubt that sufficient evidence has been produced by local officials to justify institution of an appeal or arbitration procedure. 

Alternative Connecticut Practice 

An alternative to the above appeals provision to protect local home rule is suggested by:a related Connecticut statute (Conn. Public Act 

No. 593 of 1957) reproduced in Appendix E of the Council report. The Connecticut School Building Assistance Law greatly restricts the "disapproval powers" of the state school building assistance agency which in this case is the State Board of Education. The State Board may disapprove a project only for violations of the state building code. 

The statute stipulates that no school building project shall be undertaken in a town except according to a plan approved by the town board of education and the town building committee. Final authority as to educational specifications of new buildings, and non-educational features, is thus clearly vested in the local school committee and local school building agency. (Sec. 10).

To avoid the possibility of local extravagance at state expense, the Connecticut law uses the number of pupils to be housed as the principal control in making state grants. The state commitment does not increase or decrease according to the cost of the school, since that commitment is governed almost entirely by the number of pupils to be housed. The language used in the act limits controversies to questions of health and safety. If the powers of the Massachusetts School Building Assistance 

Commission were to be similarly limited, it would be necessary to make a major change in the state aid formula. At present, state grants range

from 20% to 50% on approved construction costs for county and municipal schools and from 35% to 65% in the case of regional school projects (Mass. 

Act, s.9); the specific percentage for an individual county, regional or municipal school project is determined by a complex formula involving ratios of state to local equalized assessed valuation, and of state to local pupils in net average school membership. This percentage is guaranteed to the county, regional school district or municipality regardless of how costly or economical the proposed school may be, so long as the School Building Assistance Commission approves the project. 

Hence, the more the community spends, the more subsidy it receives from the state. Beyond question this Massachusetts approach has had an "inflationary" cost effect on some local school building decisions. If the Connecticut system were applied under Massachusetts law, state control would be restricted to public health and building safety aspects of local school construction; however, the omission of the second feature of the 

Connecticut system establishing the maximum amounts of subsidization per pupil served, might stimulate extravagant local school construction in Massachusetts such as cannot now occur in Connecticut. 

2. Tighter State Cost Controls Present Massachusetts Practice 

The Massachusetts School Building Assistance Act provides that - ... "For the purpose of determining the estimated approved costs of school construction, the ... (School Building Assistance) 

Commission shall, from time to time, by regulation, establish maximum unit costs of construction." (Section 8).

Beyond this brief reference to the cost estimating process, the statute is silent as to the uses of any maximum unit costs of construction and the Commission has declined to determine them. (See lengthy argument

in Commission letter reprinted in Council report, pp. 79-80). Hence, there are no statutory or administrative controls in effect to prohibit, for example, state subsidy payments toward building oversized auditoriums which are intended as town meeting halls, or for non-educational facilities which are included primarily to serve other community purposes, or for "luxury" items such as swimming pools. The present policy of the 

Commission is to approve state aid payments on the total cost of the project including all such facilities. On this score the Council report states that "No effort is made to differentiate between those communities which provide reasonable school facilities and those which clearly have been extravagant ... several states have experimented in this direction as indicated by the following text relative to California, Connecticut, Delaware and Washington. "These states have established maximum unit cost controls or other restrictions designed to prevent the wasteful use of state grants-in-aid to local school districts for school construction." (Council report p. 136). 

Controls in California, Delaware and Washington

The methods used in three other states of California, Delaware and Washington to control costs are quoted from the Council report (pp. 136-138): "California. The Education Code of the State of California seeks to prevent extravagance in new school construction by applying both space controls on a square footage basis, and maximum cost controls, also on the square foot basis. "The purpose of the space limitation is to prevent like school districts from trying to outbuild each other. Thus, elementary schools (grades, kindergarten through six) may not exceed 55 square feet per pupil, including classroom, administration, corridors, multi-purpose rooms and the like. Space in elementary schools for seventh and eighth grade pupils may not exceed 75 square feet per pupil. And the limitation on high school space is 92 square feet per pupil. The State Department is empowered to permit variations from these requirements in the case of smaller schools. "A square foot cost limitation is established by the California Department of Finance in accordance with the type of facility to be built. The square foot unit cost does not include either off-site

or on-site development, or the purchase of sites: sites are purchased separately, and an allowance is made for site development. 

The Square foot costs are prescribed on a geographical basis, and must not exceed the cost of comparable new construction by nonstate-aided school districts in the same geographical area. The school building's budget must come within the maximum allowable. "The square foot maximum unit cost of construction promulgated by the State Department of Finance is not a flat figure for the entire building. The spaces in the building have been divided into 21 types, each with its distinctive per square foot cost. 

And the maximum allowable cost for each of the 21 types of spaces varies from one of the seven geographical areas of the State to the next ... "Delaware. The statutes governing state aid to local school construction in Delaware prohibit the payment of such aid toward any classroom which costs more than $30,000. The law also bars state aid if the school project includes a swimming pool, or if the architect's fee exceeds 5 per cent of the construction cost. 

"Washington. The State Board of Education in Washington is required to determine the prevailing square foot ceiling cost of school building construction. The State will make payments in aid up to that ceiling, and any expense above the ceiling figure must be borne by the school district. The State will not contribute toward the cost of land, site development, administration buildings, auditoria, garages, cafeterias in secondary schools, libraries in elementary schools of less than six classrooms, multipurpose rooms in schools of less than six classrooms, stadia, grandstands, balconies, swimming pools, fireplaces, movable equipment, architect's fees in excess of 6 per cent, or inter-communications systems." 

3. Maze of Building Regulations 

Confusion in Building Codes and Regulations On the subject of the impact of building codes and regulations, and building inspection, in Massachusetts, the Council report declares that "In preparing the detailed architectural and engineering plans of a new school building, the architect must bear in mind a maze of regulations relative to plumbing, fire prevention, fire safety, structural safety, health, zoning and other matters which have been issued by various state and local authorities. Some of these regulations are spelled out in the statutes, notably, chapter 143 of the General Laws. Some are promulgated by municipal ordinance or by-law. And others are regulations made by state and local agencies under statutory grants of regulatory power.

"Enforcement responsibilities are divided in a similar fashion. Locally made regulations are generally enforced by local inspection officers. Some statutory provisions are enforced by both state and local inspectors. There are statutory requirements and regulations formulated by state boards which call for action by state inspection officers. "Educators, architects and state officials indicated to Research Bureau staff that a uniform state-wide building code would make life easier for the architect, engineer and contractor and would help reduce construction costs of schools and other buildings. Unless the contractors and subcontractors who bid on local school construction are familiar with the codes applicable in the particular city or town, there is a likelihood that unnecessary contingencies may be included in their bids. They do not have the time to study codes in all locations where work is being proposed, and many of the codes give the particular local authorities a wide discretion which affects construction costs. Architects who have studied local codes state that they often find them contrary to state requirements. 

Correction of this situation is time-consuming, especially when public hearings and other legal procedures may be necessary to eliminate the differences." (Council report, pp. 80-81).

Health Regulations 

In addition to these heating, ventilation, water, sewer and plumbing regulations, school buildings must comply with other health specifications laid down by local boards of health and by the State Depart ment of Public Health. 

The Public Health Council in the State Department of Public Health is directed by law to promulgate a uniform sanitary code containing rules and regulations of a general as well as a specific nature to protect and improve the public health of the Commonwealth (G.L., c.111, s.5). Initial responsibility for enforcement of the uniform sanitary code lies with the local board of health; but statutory provision is made for enforcement by the State Department of Public Health if action is not forthcoming. The 

Public Health Council is further empowered to make regulations establishing minimum standards of fitness for human habitation in buildings, which become effective in a city or town if accepted by the city council or town meeting. Local boards of health may also make such "habitation" rules. (G.L., c.111, ss.128C-128F).

The State Department of Public Health has general authority in matters relating to water supply and sewerage disposal systems; and municipal plans for such systems and for drainage are subject to its approval. 

Local boards of health may also make regulations to protect the local water supply from contamination (G.L., c.111, ss.5, 17 and 160). The State 

Department of Public Health is further authorized to make such examinations of school buildings as in the opinion of the department the protection of the health of the pupils may require (G.L., c.143, s.42). It must also advise the government concerning the location and other sanitary condition of any public institution (G.L., c.111, s.5). Administration of the Depart-

ment's specifications relative to the drainage, water supply and sewerage aspects of school building projects is carried out by its Division of 

Sanitary Engineering. 

Zoning Regulations 

The Zoning Enabling Act authorizes cities and towns to regulate and restrict the height, number of stories, set-back, and size of buildings and structures, on a uniform basis for each class or kind of buildings, structures or land throughout the individual zoning district. Among other things, local zoning codes must facilitate the adequate provision of transportation, water, sewerage, schools, parks and other public requirements (G.L., c.40A, s.3).

Enforcement of local zoning ordinances and by-laws is vested in municipal building inspectors or "the officer or board having supervision of the construction of buildings or the power of enforcing the municipal building laws". In towns not having a building inspector, the selectmen act as the enforcement authority. Local officers responsible for issuing building permits may not issue permits for construction which violate the zoning ordinance or by-law (G.L., c.40A, s.12).

Because public school building projects have not been exempted from all local zoning requirements, it is frequently necessary for the school building committee to go through special formalities with the local board. Such a variance is invariably granted, since the community has already expressed its will with respect to the school. 

Questions for Legislative Consideration

The foregoing text suggests a number of questions with respect to Massachusetts building codes and regulations, and inspection practices, as applied to school buildings, which the General Court may wish to consider.

(1) Should the present temporary State Board of Schoolhouse Struc tural Standards be replaced by a permanent state agency with exclusive authority to make all building and health regulations governing the construction of public school buildings? This function might be assigned to a permanent 

Board of Schoolhouse Structural Standards located in the Department of Public Safety, as at present, or in the Department of Education. Or it might be assigned to the State Board of Education, or to a permanent School Build ing Assistance Commission. 

(2) Should the jurisdiction of the above permanent agency extend only to building safety and health regulation for continued operation of existing school buildings? This would make possible uniform standards of school building safety applying across the board to all public schoolhouses, thus avoiding policy conflicts in the making of building safety regulations for schools. (3) Should the jurisdiction of the central schoolhouse structural as well? standards agency extend to enforcement aspects That is, should the agency's standards be enforced by a single set of building inspectors employed by that agency to specialize in schoolhouse inspections?. Or should the inspection task continue with the Division of Inspections of the Department of Public Safety? In either case, should such inspections, now made in some cases by state inspectors and in others by local government inspectors, become exclusively a state function? Centralization of schoolhouse inspection responsibilities, if feasible and desirable, would go far to meet the criticisms of architects and contractors relative to jurisdictional differences and lack of uniform standards. (4) Should the Zoning Enabling Act (G.L., c.40A) be amended fully to exclude public school buildings from the application of local zoning codes?

Answering these questions is difficult and will in some cases require a "Hoover Commission" type of study of the organization, powers and duties of the School Building Assistance Commission, the State Board of Schoolhouse Structural Standards and the State Board of Education. Some authorities favor abolition of both the temporary School Building Assistance Commission and the temporary State Board of Schoolhouse Structural Standards, and assigning their functions to a Division of School Buildings in the Department of Education, under the control of the Board of Education, That Division would then administer the School Building Assistance Act, make rules and regulations relative thereto and to school building health and safety (subject to the approval of the State Board of Education and the Commissioner of Public Safety) and provide local and regional technical advice on school construction and renovation. 

4. Qualifications of Clerks of the Works 

On the subject of local Clerks-of-the-Works on school building projects, the Council report states that "The clerk-of-the-works, variously known as construction supervisor, resident inspector, resident engineer, and the like, plays a most important role in the protection of the public interest in the construction of a schoolhouse or other public building ... "The clerk-of-the-works must possess technical knowledge of and experience with building materials and processes, and should be thoroughly cognizant of the practices of the various trades involved in the project. He should be able to interpret drawings and specifications correctly. While he must not interfere with a contractor's work program, he should establish in his mind the sequence and proper progress of operations under the different trades. "He must examine materials and workmanship, verify lines and levels, measure work departures from the contract, and promptly report all irregularities in the performance of the contract to the architect." (Council report, pp. 96-97).

Concerning the clerk-of-the-works role, the Governor's Committee

to Study School Construction Costs observed in its 1955 report that - "This continuous inspection is especially important for the pouring of concrete foundations, walls and floors, the replacement of reinforcing steel, the laying of brick and block, and all other processes where improper workmanship and materials can be concealed in such manner as to prevent subsequent detection." (Council report, p.97).

The losses likely to be suffered if a public building project proceeds without a qualified clerk-of-the-works have been described by the 

Special Commission on the Structure of the State Government as follows: 

"In such circumstances a contractor working under a lump sum contract is inevitably tempted to cut many corners during the course of construction; further, he is invited to take his chances as low bidder with knowledge that, in the absence of adequate supervision, he can probably add to the contract's profit from change-order requests approved while the project is going along. Sometimes the designer too is not required to give the project the same supervisory time and attention demanded for the same fee in the case of private projects on which a competent clerk-of-the-works is employed and the supervision is exacting. "It is impossible to estimate precisely the damage done ... by...inspection deficiencies. The price of poor construction and non-adherence to plans and specifications is later paid for in excessive maintenance budgets over the course of years. This is an undisputed assumption of construction engineering. In addition, of course, when corners are cut, the cost of the project does not truly reflect the value received..." (House, No.2575 of 1953, p.26).

5. Increasing Scope of Non-competitive Sub-bids

Bidding Procedure on School Building Projects 

Massachusetts bidding procedures for school construction contracts have been summarized by the Council report as follows: "To solicit bids of general contractors and sub-bids of sub-contractors for construction of a school project, the school building agency must advertise for such bids and subbids. For this purpose it must provide every person applying therefor with a copy of the plans and specifications of the project, and furnish him on request with a copy of a general bid form or sub-bid form which is in accord with the format and content also prescribed by the State Bidding Law (G.L., c.149, ss.44E, 44F, and 44G). "The proposals submitted by each general bidder must include specifications and if deemed necessary by the school building agency plans detailing all labor and materials to be furnished. The specifications must list separately the following 15 sub-trade items, if they are estimated to cost over $1,000: roofing and flashing; metal windows; waterproofing, damp-proofing and calking; miscellaneous and ornamental iron; lathing and plastering; accoustical tile; marble; tile and terrazzo; resilient floors; glass and glazing; painting; plumb-

ing; heating; ventilating and air-conditioning; electrical work; and elevators. Listing may also be required of other classes of work if the awarding authority deems it necessary or convenient to receive sub-bids (G.L., c.149, s.44C). "The general contractor's bid must present the proposed work according to 'Item 1' and 'Item 2'. The former Item 1 must cover the work of the general contractor, including the amount necessary to pay for each sub-trade service of $1,000 or less. Item 2 must list for each of the 15 sub-trades to cost over $1,000, the subbids proposed by the general bidder, giving the name of each subbidder and the amount of the sub-bid. A general bidder who customarily does any Item 2 work may also bid on such work; but his sub-bid may be rejected if he cannot prove that he is qualified to do the work called for by the specifications of the sub-trade. (G.L., c.149, ss. 44F and 44J). "Sealed bids of sub-bidders must be filed with the school building agency four days before the opening of general bids, and opened two days before the general bids are opened. After scrutiny at the time a list of the sub-bids which were not rejected by the school building agency must then be mailed to every person on record who received a set of plans and specifications. To this list there must be attached a memorandum of the general contractors expressly precluded from using certain sub-bids. "From this list of sub-bids, the general bidder must select for each of the 15 sub-trades the lowest responsible and eligible sub-bidder against whose standing and ability he makes no objection.; and his selections must be indicated in the general bid, under Item 2. With the agreement of the school building agency, the general contractor may substitute for any sub-bidder another sub-bidder acceptable to the general contractor as to standing and ability. "Every general bid, and every sub-bid under Item 2, must be accompanied by a bid deposit of 5 per cent of the value of the work to be performed, but not less than $100 nor more than $50,000. 

These deposits are returned immediately to bidders whose bids have been rejected; and successful bidders receive back their bid deposits after they have executed the required contracts and posted the prescribed bonds. "The school building agency must award the general contract for the project within thirty days. It may reject any or all general bids in the public interest; and any sub-bid if the sub-bidder is not deemed competent. Similarly, the agency may reject a sub-bid if only one such sub-bid has been made and it is deemed to be unreasonable. (G.L., c.149, s.44D). However, the courts have ruled that this right to reject bids is not the source of any option to disobey the statutory mandate in sections 44A to 441 to award contracts to the lowest responsible and eligible bidders." (Council report, pp. 90-92).

6. Permanency of Local Building Committees Importance of Experienced Local Building Committees 

In most towns the responsibility for new school construction authorized by vote of the town rests with a temporary committee composed of citizen volunteers. Their success usually depends on the quality, training and experience of committee members.

Some critics of this system contend that the towns do not make a great enough effort to find individuals with previous building committee service. Thus, they say, a committee is appointed, works for two or three

years on a project, probably making some costly mistakes and gaining practical experience, and then is dissolved upon termination of its assignment. 

Later when a new project is under way, a new "green" committee may be organized which will repeat old and costly errors. 

The Council report states that some towns seek continuity of experience in the planning and construction of schools and other buildings through the establishment of permanent building committees. Thus, Brookline adopted its present by-law to that effect in 1945. Concord voted in a 1947 town meeting to establish a "General Building Committee" of five members appointed by the selectmen for staggered two-year terms. 

Similarly, a 1957

Lexington town meeting voted to create a "Standing School Building Committee" with substantially the same duties as in Concord; and Wayland is also reported to have established a permanent school building committee. Arguments

Against Permanent Local Building Committees 

The pros and cons of such permanent committees are summarized thus in the Council's report: "Opponents of permanent building committees and permanent school building committees in towns contend that they are likely to become inflexible in their approach to school building problems, and that the large amount of money at the committee's disposal might prove too great a temptation to 'patronage politicians'. Other critics point to the heavy workload of the usual temporary building committee and express doubt that a committee of unpaid citizens meeting only at night can handle more than one project at a time, assuming it is willing to do so. "Some legal authorities have expressed the informal view that inasmuch as there is no general statute expressly authorizing the establishment of permanent town building committees, the creation of such a committee requires a special act. It is argued that while towns have legislative authority to organize any kind of study committee, permanent administrative boards and committees can be set up only when clear statutory permission is granted. Thus, generally speaking, permanent school building study committees are lawful; but administrative bodies such as permanent school construction committees are not. This argument is at times expanded to include

the assertion that school construction and other public building construction are intermittent functions of the town government, for which a special continuing administrative board cannot properly be created in the absence of a permissive law." (Council report, pp. 65-66).

Possible Legislative Action

2. Authorize towns similarly to provide for the appointment or election of a town school building commission, with functions as set forth above, with respect to school buildings alone. 

3. Empower town meetings to authorize such commissions to enter into contracts for particular building projects determined by vote of the town.
