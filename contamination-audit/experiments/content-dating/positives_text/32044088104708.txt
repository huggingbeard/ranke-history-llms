BUILDING THE POST-WAR WORLD 

The fundamental problem with which we are confronted in these tragic days is the oldest of all human problemsthe problem which promptly presented itself to the family of Adam, and which Cain and Abel so notoriously failed to solve. How can human beings living together and moved by rival impulses, needs, and ambitions, avoid destroying one another? How can their relations be converted from hostility to peace and cooperation?

But while the problem is as old as man the conditions under which it has to be solved have in recent years been profoundly changed. 

I suspect that posterity will look back upon our time and find its technological advances to be as epoch-making as we now find the fall of the Roman Empire, the advent of 

Christianity, the age of discovery, the industrial revolution, and the French 

Revolution. I am not sure that the technological changes of the early twentieth century will not mark a more important turning point in human history than any of the great events which I have just recited. For they touch every quarter of the globe, every class in society, and every department of life. 

I do not, of course, mean that technological progress began in the twentieth century, but that in that century-in our day-it has reached a crucial point at which two basic ideas which have hitherto been accepted as assumptions in human affairs are becoming obsolete. 

The first of these is the idea of distance, and the second is the idea of scarcity. 

For better or for worse, we live in a world of compactness and abundance.

Our old problem of living together is now the problem of how we can live together compactly and distribute our abundance. 

I need not recite to you the technological changes which have annihilated distance. They are the results of many scientific discoveries and engineering inventions. But the fact is that distance has been so reduced that it no longer operates either as an obstacle or as a 2

remedy. When I speak of distance, I 

, mean, of course, practical distance, that is, time taken to act at a distance. The distance from New York to Chicago is still 725 miles, but if you ask the travel agent how far Chicago is from New 

York he will answer, "Four hours," meaning that you can get your body or your merchandise or your letter there in that time by air. But you can get your written message there in an hour by telegraph, and you can get your voice there in a fraction of a second by telephone. You can do business there in "no time"-that is, the distance is reduced approximately to zero. Hitler's voice, if you should want to hear it, can be heard here as quickly as in the rear of the coliseum where he is ranting. 

The result of all this is that mankind need no longer suffer, nor can they any longer profit, by distance. It is important to note that distance lends enchantment. It used to be one of the most effective ways of solving the difficulties of human relations. Oddly enough when men first began to predict this disappearance of distance they thought it would bring peace and understanding, forgetting that quarrels and misunderstandings, like epidemics, are effects of proximity. In a world without distance men need not lack company. But congestion is more disturbing than solitude.

The long and short of it is that if in the future men want to be let alone, human relations must be organized. 

Mere space will no longer do the job. 

There is no longer such a thing as isolation. Isolationism is not a policy, it is a failure to recognize the facts. The question, "Shall we or shall we not be isolated?" has no more meaning than the question, "Shall we or shall we not be attracted to the earth's surface by the force of gravitation?" or "Shall we or shall we not have parents?"

A con- siderable fraction of our vocabulary becomes obsolete along with isolation. 

There is no such thing any more as a "hermit nation." There is no such thing as a "localized war." There is no such 3

thing as "intervention." For interventionism assumes that in order to be embroiled in world affairs you have to go out of your way. Now, and from now on, you can stand still where you are, and look the other way, or stick your head in the sand, but world affairs will reach out to you wherever you are.

The second outstanding fact about the present state of the world is the disappearance of scarcity. It was once assumed that the supply of material goods was so limited that only a few people could enjoy abundance, which was called luxury, while another few must perish of hunger, cold, disease, floods or earthquakes, with the great mass of mankind obliged to suffer from some intermediate degree of privation. This is still the actual condition of mankind: the difference is that it is no longer accepted as necessary. When natural resources are exhausted or are inaccessible we do not fold our hands and learn to get on without them; we invent ways of creating them. If people live in an arid region we irrigate. If people lack food, clothing, medicine or the conveniences of life, we carry these to them where they are, or move the people to some other area. We are beginning to think of a world economy in which the aggregate needs of mankind are amply met by their aggregate productive capacity.

An inkling of this idea has spread abroad to the remotest corners of the globe, and down to the nethermost levels of poverty and ignorance. It is impossible to keep such news from spreading. It means that in the future unfortunate men are not going to accept their lot with resignation. They are not going to accept a compensation in heaven for what they lack on earth.

They are not going to accept spiritual goods as a substitute for material goods. 

They are not going to be satisfied even by the persuasion that goods are distributed according to merit-so that if they have nothing, it is because they deserve nothing. They are convinced that there is enough to go around, and that if they receive nothing it is because others are

keeping something from them, or because those who rule human affairs are not fit to rule. They ask to be so organized and led that the fruits of labor, management and invention shall raise the standard of living high for men everywhere. They refuse to accept that deadly struggle for existence in which the survival of some implies the extinction of the rest; they are willing to accept only a struggle for eminence in which even those who lose gain by the success of those who win. This, I take it, is what is meant by saying that this is a "people's war"; or that a "social revolution" is taking place simultaneously with a world war. would not belittle the continuing demand for national self-determination.

This will be a determining condition of whatever settlement may come at the conclusion of the present war. But that which is new in our thinking, and will make the next peace, if there is to be peace, different from any peace which has preceded it, consists of these two ideas: first, that the world has grown so small that it must be organized as a whole; second, that human resourcefulness has grown so great that when the world is organized all men can be raised to a level hitherto enjoyed only by the privileged minority. 

Given these new conditions and premises, there are two plans inscribed on the banners of the opposing contestants in the present war. Both plans have been plainly indicated, and in large measure already practised. The Axis plan is conquest and a rule of force by one or more master races. Since the world has grown so small, they argue, there can no longer be small independent nations, but only provinces dependent in varying degrees on great nations. If there is to be permanent peace there can be only one such dominant nation, and all others must "collaborate" or be reduced to servitude. This one dominant nation will then take care of its collaborators and its slaves-it will develop the technical arts, and distribute their fruits. 

But in so doing it will be governed by

its own superior judgment. Other societies will receive only what the dominant nation thinks is good for them, and only on the condition of their renouncing any will of their own.

Our plan is the extension to the world at large of the basic principles of what we call "democracy." Since the world has grown so small, we argue, world affairs must henceforth be regulated, as we have hitherto regulated our domestic affairs. Applied to world affairs, democracy means that the aggregate of mankind shall be so organized as to create for each human society the maximum opportunity of growth in accordance with the dictates of its own genius and aspiration. It does not prescribe how any society shall live except that it shall contribute by self-restraint and by cooperation to the lives of other societies. Precisely how restrictive this condition must be nobody knows. The democratic purpose for mankind means pressing steadily in a certain direction, boldly and cautiously-going forward but learning as one goes, claiming and conceding freedom, but renouncing and withdrawing it, if necessary, when “live” violates "let live."

It is the purpose of the United Nations, or of those among their leaders who are actuated by a constructive moral purpose, to introduce this system throughout the world, as it is introduced by each democracy among its own people, namely, by a mixture of persuasion and force; by persuasion if possible, by force if necessary; always recognizing that nothing can be imposed for long by force which has not already been generally adopted by persuasion.

Let those who object to imposing a single pattern of life on all mankind bear in mind that it is the essence of our proposal that there shall be the minimum of imposition. It will not be imposed by one nation on others, but by all nations acting collectively upon themselves severally. It will be self-imposed. And when and if such a system is imposed the evidence of its

success will be the degree to which the several members of the family of mankind are left in effective control of their own destinies. A world so ordered will be an orderly world, but its order will be distinguished by the rich variety of its cultures and by its creation of multiple spheres of independence. 

The Axis powers and the United Na tions have each a plan which takes account of the new conditions created by advancing technology. The difference between their plan and ours is a moral and not a technological difference. Mr.

Elmer Davis is quoted as saying before the Foreign Policy Association Forum in New York: 

"If the world attains a sane international organization the men who deserve most of the credit will not be statesmen or philosophers or moralists, but the Wright brothers and their successors, who made the world so unsafe that at last everybody had to get together, whether they liked it or not." 99 If we take the Wright brothers as a symbol of the advancing technology which has destroyed distance and forced mankind to choose some organization of world affairs-well and good. But there are at least two ways in which men can "get together", the Axis way and our way. With the difference between these two ways the Wright brothers have nothing to do. If the world attains an insane international organization the

Wright brothers will deserve most of the discredit, for they will have created the means by which one nation can hold the rest in subjection.

For the difference, and for our choice of a future world, the credit goes not to the Wright brothers, but to Isaiah, to 

Jesus, to Socrates, to Justinian, to John

Locke, to the founders of modern democracy, to the long line of teachers and statesmen who have given us the vision of a world-wide peace founded on justice, and have implanted in us a belief in the rights of men to the utmost development of their latent capacities and the maximum control of their own

destinies. That is our idea of a "sane international organization", and the conditions that must be satisfied before we of the United Nations are willing to speak of peace.

There are two schools of thought regarding the best strategy for the organization of peace the "all-at-once" school, and the "creeper-up" school. 

By the "all-at-once" or instantaneous school of thought I mean those who believe that a world-wide convention should be called either now or immediately after the cessation of hostilities; that this convention should settle then and there the main questions now at issue, and at the same time adopt a constitution providing for some sort of improved League of Nations or other world-wide organization, having its terms of membership, and functions fully defined; and that the settlement of any further issues that might arise be entrusted to this organization. 

By the "creeper-up", progressivist, or gradualist, school of thought I mean those who believe that we should make a series of arrangements dealing with immediate and specific problems and reserve the creation of an over-all, unified international organization for a later day.

Both of these plans have merit and neither can be ignored. I should like to outline briefly a course of action which will take account of them both, and which is consistent with human nature and with circumstances. This course of action might be described as a directed gradualism. Those who advocate "creeping-up" must assume a goal toward which we shall creep; otherwise we may for all we know be creeping backwards instead of forwards. Those, on the other hand, who advocate an allcomprehensive world organization embracing the political, economic, juridical, social, demographic, and cultural relations of all of the many and diverse societies of mankind forget that this result can be attained only by a series of steps, patiently negotiated by the interested parties, and with each step condi-

tioned by the steps which have gone before. 

The fact is that we are subject here to precisely the same illusion that has misled us heretofore into supposing that there was a sharp line of demarcation between pre-war and war, and between war and post-war. Similarly we are inclined to suppose that there will be a time when our international problems will be finally solved, and we can turn our thoughts to other matters. But after post-war there will be post-post-war and so on ad infinitum; and we shall then learn that there is no dividing line between the two. The army of mankind engaged in the task of peace can never be demobilized or even rest on its arms. 

If we look back to the pre-war period and forward to the post-war period we see a continuous process, marked at every stage by the advent of new conditions, by partial failures and successes, by the formation of habits and by the accumulation of experience. The essential materials of peace like those of war are human beings; and human institutions as well as human beings grow, and are not manufactured. The most we can hope for is that this growth will move in a certain broad direction.

This does not mean that it should be left to the control of unconscious forces. 

I am assuming that it will be a rational process in the proper human sense of that expression. It will be a fitting of means to ends, combined with a realization that when we realize the end it is never, because of the means which we have used, precisely what we have con- Iceived in advance. Means have effects other than those for the sake of which we have selected them; and when these effects have occurred we find that they must be taken account of when we take the next step. This spells flexibility; a mixture of premeditation and ad hoc decisions, or of foresight and opportunism.

It comes, I think, to this. When we embark upon an undertaking of vast complexity and which is to occupy mankind for the remainder of their history

we must look ahead and frame ideas of what we want to attain. But the remoter stages of the undertaking must be conceived in terms of broad principles and concrete alternatives. We must say to ourselves: "This is the kind of thing in general that we want to bring about. These are the different situations in which we may find ourselves, and these are the different things which we will do according to which of these situations arises." When the actual situation confronts us we want to have thought about it before, so that it will not catch us unprepared; but since we cannot have predicted in advance precisely what that actual situation will be, we must not have committed ourselves to any one detailed line of action. 

The first steps after our enemies lay down their arms will be to replace what has been destroyed, enable the peoples of the world to return to non-military occupations, and resuscitate the organs which enable society to function without the drugs and unnatural controls of war. The system has got to be made to run, and to work, again. 

But we are resolved that it shall not be the same system. We are thinking of a system which shall be better. We are going to rebuild, but the new house is going to be fire-proof and more commodious and comfortable. So if we are wise we shall during the transition not seize on the first materials and design that happen to be handy, but select what would be convertible into that better structure which will come later. We should not be too hasty to adopt a complete architectural drawing or builder's blue print, for we shall know better as time goes on both precisely what the tenants want, who they are going to be, what they are prepared to pay. But we can build foundations, or we can build a modest structure of a type that can be remodelled. Meanwhile we shall need to have many architects and builders working on plans, and we shall need to keep our ulterior ambitions firmly in mind. In short, resourcefulness and direction. 10

Our ulterior ambition may, I think, be subsumed under two major ideas; security and collaboration. We want a world so organized as to release the energies of men from the necessity of resisting enemies, or from the temptation of attack first lest one give the enemy the advantage of the offense. 

When all of the energies of men are so absorbed we call it war; but what we have hitherto been satisfied to call peace has differed only in degree. 

Beyond security, we want collaboration. Security means that men can do a thing better if they can be sure that they will not be interrupted: collaboration means that men can do more and better things by working together than by working alone. Security requires collaboration, collaboration is based on security; but there is a shift of emphasis. 

Collaboration opens upon an indefinite vista of change and of constructive possibilities. In proportion as men view one another fundamentally as partners rather than as rivals, they will be enabled to realize the optimum possibilities of life, pooling their resources, combining their efforts, and sharing their goods. War, post-war and impending war, are a sort of self-mutilation and selfbinding, with which man has defeated and all but destroyed himself. Peace in the sense in which we use the term, is the opportunity of man to make full use of his latent powers, and become that which, if his life has any meaning, he is meant to be. 

To this goal, whatever be the words one uses to describe it, we are, I hope, irrevocably committed from pre-war to war, through war to post-war, and through post-war to all that comes after. 

The pursuit of this goal is the great adventure of human history. It is and will continue to be attended with all the doubts and difficulties that beset any human effort but on the grand scale. It requires cool skill, calm fortitude, stubborn persistence, and also that ardent devotion by which the moral will is raised to its highest levels of force and daring. 11

What YOU Can Do: 

THE NEXT STEP 

To give the other United Nations a dependable forecast of the part that America is prepared to play in post-war international affairs, the will of the American people must be expressed through the United States 

Senate. There is pending in the Senate now a Resolution (the Ball-Burton- 

Hatch-Hill Bill) which would give the other 

United Nations such adequate assurance.

Text of Ball-Burton-Hatch-Hill Bill, S. R. 114: 

"Resolved, That the Senate advises that the United

States take the initiative in calling meetings of representatives of the United Nations with specific and limited authority: (1) To assist in co-ordinating and fully utilizing the military and economic resources of all member nations in the prosecution of the war against the Axis. (2) To establish temporary administrations for Axiscontrolled areas of the world as these are occupied by United Nations forces, until such time as permanent governments can be established. (3) To administer relief and assistance in economic rehabilitation in territories of member nations needing such aid and in Axis territory occupied by United 

Nations forces. (4) To establish procedures and machinery for peaceful settlement of disputes and disagreements between nations. (5) To provide for the assembly and maintenance of a United Nations military force and to suppress by immediate use of such force any future attempt at military aggression by any nation. "That the Senate further advises that any establishment of such United Nations organization provide machinery for its modification, for the delegation of additional specific and limited functions to such organization, and for admission of other nations to membership, and that member nations should commit themselves to seek no territorial aggrandizement."
