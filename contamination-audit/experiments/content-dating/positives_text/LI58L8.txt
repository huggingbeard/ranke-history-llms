CHAPTER I INTRODUCTION 

As the United States faces ever greater responsibilities in its foreign relations, there seems to be no more important problem than that of improving. relations between the President and Congress in matters of foreign policy. Because of its sheer power superiority the United States finds itself today bearing a major burden of world leadership which demands positive and consistent. United States foreign policy. The United States has not always been able, however, to act either positively or consistently- often because of bad relations between the executive and legislative branches of our government. It is the aim of this report, therefore, to examine what means have, on occasion, been used to bridge the gap between these two branches and to seek ways of developing better cooperation in the future.

Before any detailed study can be made of this problem, however, it is essential to set forth briefly three basic requirements of a modern United States foreign policy which necessarily set the limits within which this report must be written. 

1. Agreement Within the Government on Broad Objectives 

Action - both positive and sustained - which is required of the United States today by the world problems confronting it is scarcely possible without agreement within the government concerning the basic objectives of foreign policy. Nor is this agreement likely without real cooperation between the legislative - 1 -

2and executive branches. The President alone cannot control our foreign relations because both the Constitution and historical usage have given Congress certain powers which materially limit the President's freedom. Today both the Senate and the House are being consulted regularly because of their constant involvement in such matters as foreign aid appropriations, the reciprocal trade program, participation in the United Nations, admission of displaced persons, etc. Nor can Congress alone control United 

States foreign policy since the President, with his power to recognize other nations, to negotiate executive agreements, and to dispatch executive agents all without reference to Congress has sufficient authority to be quite independent of the legislative branch in many respects. Thus, both the President and 

Congress must work together frankly and regularly. This kind of cooperation cannot be produced solely by inventing new governmental "gadgets." It will come primarily through the recognition on both sides of the need for united action to help solve the tremendous problems that face us, Once the will to cooperate is present, however, improvements in governmental machinery may help to make the most of that spirit of cooperation. 

2. Promotion of Broad Public Support for Foreign Policy 

Today's United States foreign policy requires the broadest public understanding and support possible since our government must constantly call on John Doe for votes, dollars, and manpower. 

Thus any study of executive-legislative relations with regard to foreign policy must at the same time be aware of the impact of

3public opinion on these relations. Since our constitutional system, unlike cabinet government, makes both the President and Congress more responsible to the public than to each other, it is obvious that any effort to improve relations between these two branches must provide for the careful coordination of their relations with the public and their relations with each other. The 

President, for example, should not release information to the public which will unnecessarily offend Congress. Furthermore 

Congress must be respected as an important channel of information both to and from the public. 

3. Cooperation with the United Nations

Finally, our foreign policy must have as its highest objective full and positive cooperation with the United Nations. 

This cooperation in turn requires the fullest collaboration between the executive and legislative branches of our government. 

As the United States has actively participated in the work of the United Nations and various specialized agencies associated with the United Nations, the President has had to depend more and more on Congress to implement that participation with funds and legislation. This experience has already demonstrated that only with the best working relationship between Congress and the President can we make the most of our activities in the United Nations the best hope we have for world peace.

5Theory of Executive Predominance 

A doctrine of executive predominance may be supported by the following facts: The President has the exclusive power to negotiate treaties and has the final word on their ratification; he nominates ambassadors and other public officers; and he receives foreign ambassadors. He has nearly unlimited discretion in the recognition of new governments and states; an undefined authority to send personal agents abroad; a somewhat vague power to enter into executive agreements with other governments without the approval of the Senate; practically complete authority to initiate foreign policy; and great authority stemming from the development of his war powers during the Civil War and two World Wars. 

The members of the Convention of 1787 were familiar with contemporary European governments, especially that of Great Britain, in which the Chief Executive controlled foreign relations. Although the treaty-making power was first given to the Senate by the Convention, it was finally placed in the section dealing with the Executive power after the office of President had been added. The restrictions placed upon the President's exercise of that function in the making of treaties developed largely from the fear of the Southern States of a treaty which might close the Mississippi, of the New England States of interference with their fishing rights, and of the small states in general who felt that a requirement of the concurrence of two thirds of the Senate was necessary to protect their interests. The Theory of Legislative Predominance Article 1, Section 8, Paragraph 18 of the Constitution gives 

Congress the power: "to make all laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into execution the foregoing powers vested by this Constitution in the Government of the United States or in any department or officer thereof." 

In addition, Congress has the power to regulate foreign commerce, to establish uniform rules of naturalization, to declare war, and to raise and support an army. The Senate plays an especially important role in the establishment of foreign policy because of its part in the treaty procedure. The House, in its turn, plays an important role since it controls the origin of appropriations bills. No other important nation in fact has assigned to the legislative branch such power over foreign relations and "no legislative assembly in the world rivals the Senate... in its influence in the 11 international sphere."

Another reason for including the Senate in the treaty-making procedure may have been that it was at that time composed of representatives chosen directly by state legislatures.

Theory of a "Fourth Branch" of the Government 

It

This The view has been expressed in the Federalist that the foreign relations power constitutes a "fourth branch" of government. distinction is emphasized by the fact that the Chief Executive exercises greater political power relative to Congress in foreign affairs than in domestic affairs. Without the power of personally initiating and guiding legislation without firm party discipline and without the power to dissolve Congress, the President has less power than the British Prime Minister over domestic affairs. In foreign affairs, however, the role is perhaps reversed, for the President is assured of a fixed term of office and he is not "responsible" to Congress to the extent that the Prime Minister is to Parliament. The President, to a large degree, may control the foreign affairs of the United States without reference to Congress. 

It appears, moreover, that in giving "executive power" to the President, the "Fathers" had the foreign relations power chiefly in mind,16 although they protected this power from abuse by giving

Although Hamilton later shifted to the defense of the wholly executive nature of the foreign relations power, he wrote in the 75th Federalist Paper that the power to conduct foreign relations "seems ... to form a distinct department, and to belong, properly, neither to the legislative nor the executive. The qualities elsewhere detailed as indispensable in the management of foreign negotiations point out the Executive as the most fit agent in these transactions, and the operation of treaties as laws pleads strongly for the participation of the whole or portion of the legislative body in the office of making them."

The Fathers were familiar with the works of Locke and Montesquieu, both of whom indicate that they regarded the control of foreign relations as a distinct function and department of government. Locke used the term "federative" in distinguishing the foreign relations power from the executive and legislative powers, but he noted that the executive and federative powers were "always almost united." Montesquieu used the term "executive" in describing the foreign relations power, but makes it clear that he is concerned with a separate department of government dealing with powers of war and peace and the sending and receiving of ambassadors. 

In Great Britain the foreign relations power, at least in theory, has been exercised by the Crown in council largely independent of both party politics and parliamentary responsibility.

24CHAPTER III THE HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT OF THE EXECUTIVE-LEGISLATIVE RELATIONSHIP IN THE CONDUCT OF FOREIGN RELATIONS 

For the purposes of this study the history of the foreign relations of the United States has been divided into three periods. 

In the first period from 1789 to the end of John Quincy Adams' term of office in 1829, foreign affairs played a vital part in the affairs of the U. S., and domestic politics generally turned on foreign policy issues.

Presidents were chosen in large measure 1 because of their competence and experience in international affairs. 

Four Presidents out of the six in this period were former Secretaries of State, and, generally speaking, all of them displayed strong leadership in the exercise of the foreign relations power. It has become axiomatic that concentrated authority has tended to develop in the United States when foreign problems have come to the fore. 

In the second period, extending from 1829 to 1898, the problems confronting the government were mainly domestic. Congress generally assumed governmental leadership with the exception of the Civil War experience. Presidential Initiative 

Presidential initiative in the formulation of United States foreign policy was quickly established. The neutrality proclamation of 1793, and the doctrine of no "entangling alliances" attest to Washington's leadership. The Treaty of Versailles 

No case study better demonstrates the misfortunes that can beset our system of a separation of powers, if operated in an intense partisan spirit, than the defeat of the Treaty of Versailles. Neither side made any great use of institutional machinery to promote bi-partisan cooperation, but an objective observer cannot say that it was the absence of machinery alone that defeated the treaty. It was even more the stubborn animosity on both sides that paralyzed whatever machinery might then have been used.

A brief summary of the story indicates many important lessons for our generation. There seems to be little doubt that President 

Wilson was as responsible as anyone on either side for ending the uneasy political truce that had existed during World War I when he issued his plea on October 25, 1918, to the voters of the country to return & Democratic--not simply a pro-League--majority to Congress in the fall elections. World Court 

The case of the Permanent Court of International Justice is another example of the difficulty of establishing an effective working relationship between the President and the Senate even when the latter may be controlled by the President's party. In spite of the fact that Republican President Warren Harding, a former member of the Senate Committee on Foreign Relations and an opponent of the League, advocated United States' membership in the Court in February, 1923, the senior Republican member of the Committee, Chairman Lodge, and Senators Borah, Brandegee, Johnson, and Moses--all strong anti-League men--took the lead in demanding among other things that the Court be entirely separated from the League and that it never issue an advisory opinion contrary to United States interests. Borah and others made it plain that they favored such reservations as the best way of defeating the plan and were instrumental, though they could not defeat it altogether, in getting the Committee to lay the issue aside until the following session of Congress.

Reciprocal Trade Agreements 

A brief study of the events leading up to the passage of the 

Reciprocal Trade Act of 1934 emphasizes the importance of Congress in the economic aspects of foreign relations and that means other than the treaty-making process can be utilized to make important international agreements. The Act itself illustrates some of the principal features of United States foreign policy today. The trade agreements which the President has been authorized to enter into are cooperative rather than negative in character. The trade policy envisaged by the Act is a positive one requiring executive implementation of a broad policy prescribed by law. The program in many respects gave advanced notice of the swing of United States policy away from economic isolation to economic cooperation through the United Nations and its affiliated agencies. The Act gives the 

President authority to enter into negotiations with other governments and to offer concessions in the form of limited tariff rate reductions in exchange for similar concessions. The agreement may then be concluded without further reference to Congress. By 1944 twenty-seven such agreements had been concluded under the leadership of Secretary Hull.

The methods used to Secure enactment of an economic policy, so far-removed from the preceding high-tariff era, are important to note. 

In the first place, the diverse economic interests in the United States, rendering trade agreements under the two-thirds rule a

difficult undertaking, made the executive agreements procedure seem preferable. The difficulty of establishing a policy of international economic cooperation by treaty is magnified by the fact that under the Presidential form of government, the opposition is not constantly prepared to take over governing responsibility on short notice as is generally the case under parliamentary government. Consequently, the party out of power in the United States has little responsibility for the conduct of government, and it is difficult for opposition leaders to refrain from opposing everything endorsed by the party in power. 

Secondly, the provisions of the Act, which have required renewal every three years, served as a warning to the State Department that it must establish a good working relationship with Congress, The State Department originally wanted the act to specify no time limit and, when the House insisted on a three-year limit, Department officials were reported ready to drop the whole program. Congressman 

McCormack, however, is reputed to have advised the State Department negotiators to compromise, and to elicit the continuing interest and support of Congress by a vigorous information program. Until 1948 the Act was renewed successfully every three years, and each renewal was preceded by strong representations by the Executive Branch, including the Vice-President, the Secretary, and the Under Secretary of State.

95CHAPTER IV CONCLUSIONS 

General One hundred sixty years of United States experience in foreign relations indicate that, as world problems have grown more complex and more curcial in their impact on our government, even greater pressures have surged against and occasionally overflowed the barriers that separate the President from the Congress. The lessons of this period seem to point to the need, not for any radical change in our constitutional system which has served us so well but rather for a further development of those informal methods of executivelegislative collaboration which have been so useful in the past, In accomplishing this objective, however, it is important not to let the letter of our constitutional system defeat our ability to deal positively and courageously with world problems which challenge the very spirit and existence of that system. After all, there is little to fear as long as the growing strength of the government is balanced by a continued development of the strength of the people. To these ends the following conclusions attempt to sum up the lessons of the past in order to improve foreign policy of the future. 

A. THE PRESIDENT 

Experience has demonstrated that few problems have been so important or so difficult for a President as that of winning the good will of Congress, while retaining his policy initiative as Chief 

Executive. Even strong Presidents like Woodrow Wilson and Franklin

96Roosevelt have discovered that on particular issues, like the League of Nations and neutrality, they have had to trim their sails according to the legislative wind. The discussion that follows seems to demonstrate that the President must have Congress constantly in mind in three major areas of his activities that influence our foreign policy-relations with the public, relations with Congress, and relations with other states.

President's Public Relations As They Affect Congress 

When Woodrow Wilson went "over the heads" of Congressmen to go directly to the people in the League issue, he chose to ignore the fact that under our Constitution it does little good for the President to insist that he has a "mandate" from the people since Congress may also say that it too is following the people's will. 

Far more effective, therefore, was the attitude of Franklin Roosevelt and Cordell Hull who consulted so intimately with Congressional leaders of both parties during the discussions which shaped the United Nations that the public relations program on that issue was inextricably linked with the Congressional relations program. When 

Congress is not consulted in advance as in the case of the ill-fated Roosevelt "Quarantine Speech" in Chicago in 1937, it is apt to react unfavorably, especially because it feels a responsibility to know everything its constituents know--and perhaps more and earlier. 

Efforts to build a fire under Congress and force it to more against its will have, as in the case of Wilson's appeal to the people, almost invariably failed. A review of the effect of the President's public

97relations on his relations with Congress suggests the following observations: (1) Presidents have found it increasingly necessary to coordinate their public relations with their Congressional relations. (2) This can best be done by frequently consulting Congressional leaders, on major issues, as to the information to be given the public and also by seeking Congress's collaboration as a twoway channel to the public.

President's Direct Relations with Congress 

In their direct relations with Congress Presidents have used several channels to implement their foreign policy programs, Much of the time they have acted in the role of Party Leader. From Thomas 

Jefferson to Harry Truman, Chief Executives have tried to harness the machinery of party politics to pull through Congress such measures as the ratification of the Louisiana Purchase and the more recent extension of the reciprocal trade agreements program. Party discipline on foreign policy issues has not, however, seemed as strong in the United States as in other governments. On occasion, when elections have been near, party affiliations have exerted an important influence as in the League fight. On most other occasions, however, as in the debates on the World Court, World War II neutrality legislation, and the United Nations Charter, party lines have tended to blur. 

Especially in urging the approval of treaties in the Senate, Presidents have been forced to look outside their party ranks for support since seldom has any party been certain of the necessary two-thirds majority.

98Partisan politics, however, is not as black as many people have painted it. Party machinery plays a vital role in democratic government by assisting the voters to make political decisions. As part of that process, parties stimulate, educate, and represent the electorate. In placing issues and candidates before the public, parties provide the machinery for reviewing and criticizing the work of governments, for changing governments peacefully, and for providing an opposition that can run the government when the party-inpower has fallen from favor. And as parties act to blend local issues into larger issues, they also help to formulate a broad national point of view. Foreign policy like domestic policy, requires subjection to the glare of party debate and public criticism. 

But there is a darker side. Foreign policy runs the risk of not being successful unless the parties can reach common agreement, after full discussion, on basic, fundamental foreign policy objectives. 

The United States cannot present a united front to the world without such agreement. Party politics, nevertheless, still has the important function of reviewing those basic objectives from time to time and of debating at all times lesser objectives and methods. In the past, however, there have been occasions when narrowly partisan politics have so split the public that the result has been paralysis rather than positive action in matters of foreign policy. 

Because of the inadequacies of party machinery, therefore, especially in relation to treaties, various Presidents have experimented with a bi-partisan or non-partisan approach to foreign policy. Perhaps the first was Washington who tried sincerely but unsuccessfully to use the whole Senate as an advisor on the negotiation

99of treaties.

Wilson made a point of calling in various Republican Senators when he was urging the Senate to approve the Treaty of Versailles, but like most of the Presidents before and after him, he never made any serious or systematic attempt to build a truly bipartisan foreign policy. 

In February, 1942, however, President Roosevelt authorized the formation in the State Department of the Advisory Committee on Postwar Foreign Policy with both Democratic and Republican Congressmen as members. This bi-partisan group met regularly to discuss the formulation of the United Nations Charter and is generally credited with having greatly facilitated final approval of that Charter by Congress.

Few observers of government, however, have been willing to accept the principle of bi-partisan foreign policy as the whole answer without qualification, Hamilton Fish Armstrong, Editor of Foreign Affairs, has written, "(The People) have a right...to ask their leaders to rule out partisan considerations in trying to reach agreement on basic national objectives. These admittedly are not easy to define in general terms, but are not hard to recognize in practice... On lesser issues the operation of the party system normally requires that there be debate both as to objectives and as to methods. Any device promising to facilitate understanding between the responsible representatives of the two parties should be given a trial. But it is the spirit in which the party leaders determine to cooperate rather than the precise form of the mechanism available which will determine the outcome."1

Also attached to the Executive Office of the President is the Bureau of the Budget which has exerted significant influence on the Chief Executive's relations with Congress on foreign policy. (2) There have been indications that, were these planning functions removed from the Bureau, Congress might look with more confidence on the Bureau's budget studies.

President's Relations with Foreign Nations as They Affect Congress 

As is the case in their relations with the public and directly with Congress, Presidents have also had to consider the climate of Congressional opinion when they have dealt with other nations, especially in connection with such matters as the appointment of official representatives abroad and in negotiation of agreements with those nations. In accordance with the Constitution every President since Washington has been obliged to ask the Senate for its "advice and consent" concerning the appointment of "ambassadors, other public ministers, and consuls. In order to render the consent of the Senate quite certain, Presidents have Frequently asked Senators to serve as ambassadors as was the case when Senator William King was selected to be our Minister to France, or to act as delegates to important conferences as when Harding asked Senators Lodge and Underwood to go to the Washington Disarmament Conference and as Franklin Roosevelt asked

104Senators Vandenberg and Connally to go to San Francisco. 

On the positive side this device has served to give Senators first-hand experience concerning important foreign policy proglems, to win general Senatorial good-will for the President's program, and at the same time to bring to bear on the formulation of executive policy Senatorial knowledge and experience. 

On the other hand the same device has resulted in frustration of public policy when Congressional members of delegations have disagreed with the fundamental objectives of the Chief Executive as in the case of Senator Chaves at the 1946 ILO Conference in Mexico City or when Congressmen are selected who do not have the support of the major number of their legislative associates as in the case of Representatives Javits and Fulton who represented the United States at the Havana ITO Conference. 

Furthermore the need for political freedom and the pressure of official business make it difficult for legislators to devote much of their time to this kind of activity except on the most important issues.

Since Washington began the practice by sending Gouverneur Morris to England as his personal representative in 1789, other Presidents have employed similar agents such as Roosevelt's reliance on Harry Hopkins as a roving ambassador before and during World War II. B. THE DEPARTMENT OF STATE 

Since the Secretary of State is the President's principal assistant on matters of foreign policy, it is inevitable that the Department of State play a major role in shaping the Chief Executive's relations with Congress. As foreign policy has required increased participation by Congress, the Department has had to devote more and more attention to both the problem of coordinating its Congressional relations with its public relations and also of coordinating all important direct contacts between its own personnel and Congress. 

Department of State's Public Relations and Congress 

As the Department of State has been forced to go to the "grass roots" to arouse John Doe to take a more active and intelligent interest in United States foreign policy, it has become increasingly aware that this effort must be carefully coordinated with its legislative relations program. Since the Department's public relations have been aimed primarily at exerting favorable public pressure on Congress, it is only natural that Congress has taken an active interest in these efforts. Since Secretary of State Daniel Webster

108employed "special propaganda agents" to promote his treaty of 1842 with England and since Secretary Seward spent hundreds of dollars to create support for the acquisition of Alaska or "Seward's Ice Box," Secretaries of State generally have been concerned with this problem. Perhaps no greater efforts have been made to coordinate the handling of the reins of Congressional and public relations than during the incumbency of Secretary of State Cordell Hull. Having served in the Senate himself, he was particularly careful, especially in connection with the reciprocal trade program and the United Nations, to keep Congress informed fully and usually well in advance of the general public.

The close working relationship that existed for some time between Assistant Secretary of State Dean Acheson, who was responsible for the Department's relations with Congress, and Assistant Secretary Archibald MacLeish, responsible for public relations, resulted in a very effective linking together of these two important activities. 

When this kind of coordination was lacking at an earlier time, however, as in the case of Franklin Roosevelt's Chicago "Quarantine Speech," the Administration won little support from Congress. Nor did the legislators like the fact that much of the history of our relations with Japan in the 1931-41 decade was revealed for the first time in State Department public releases and in a nation-wide broadcast. This brief review of the problem seems to point to two primary conclusions: (1) As the Department of State has acted to evoke more intelligent and active support from the public it has become increasingly aware of the need to consult Congress concerning both the substance of what is

109to be told the public and how it is to be told, since Congressmen are also vitally concerned with opinion "back home." (2) At the same time it would seem reasonable for the Department to think of Congress as a vital information channel both to and from the people.

The State Department's Direct Relations with Congress 

A review of recent United States foreign relations reveals the many factors that have served to increase the extent and complexity of the Department's direct relations with Congress-the increased need for implementing foreign policy with legislation and money, as in the case of ERP; the swelling flood of foreign policy problems; and the growing need to build a broader foundation for our foreign policy by developing closer relations with Congress as well as the general public. Even with the decrease in the use of formal treaties, the 

Chief Executive still feels that it is wise to consult with Congress specifically the Senate Foreign Relations Committee--before deciding whether to use a treaty or executive agreement, as in the case of UNRRA. As the State Department has had to bear a major part of the burden of these increasing relations with Congress, it has had to devote more and more attention to all phases of the problem--the decision as to who within the Department is to deal with Congress, the kinds of issues to be discussed, the degree of secrecy that must be maintained in those discussions, the actual organization of Departmental Relations with Congress, and the problem of timing those relations.

110Who Is To Represent The Department Before Congress? 

The increasing attention paid by the Department of State to the question of who is to represent it before Congress emphasizes the fact that this is one of the most vital jobs in the Department. 

Since 1790, when the Department consisted of a total of eight persons and Mr. Jefferson could handle all necessary relations with Congress himself, later Secretaries of State have increasingly had to lean on various assistants to help them in their dealings with Congress. 

First the Secretaries used various aid es on an ad hoc basis calling on officers whose specialized knowledge qualified them to talk to Congress on particular problems as they arose; more recently, because of the growing volume of foreign policy problems that call for Congressional attention, the task of maintaining liaison with Congress has assumed the proportions of a full time job.

What and When to Tell Congress 

The twin questions of what to tell Congress and when to tell it have been increasingly answered by the State Department, "as much and as soon as possible." Congress resents being told too little and too late as seems to have been the case with the aid program for Greece and Turkey; it wants time to think, to hold hearings, and to get reactions from "home." The objection often heard on the executive side that there is always danger of an advance "legislative veto" which will thwart executive leadership may be countered by the consideration that it is often better to learn of legislative objections early rather than late in order to attempt to meet or alter them. Dean Acheson took special pains to keep Congress informed from the birth of such important projects as the Warm Springs and Bretton Woods Conferences. (5) Much time and effort can be saved by developing good relations between the Department's liaison staff and the staffs of the various Congressional Committees. If Congressmen's advisers have confidence in the Department, their advice will reflect that confidence. (6) Various devices which have been successful in winning Congressional cooperation in the past should be improved and used to the fullest extent possible. Miscellaneous Problems That Affect Liaison 

In order to comprehend fully the problem of bridging the abyss that separates the legislative from the executive branch, one must also take a good look at the foundations on either side. 

On the executive side within the Department of State there are factional disputes and general conditions of morale which, though beyond the strict limits of this report, must be mentioned since they inevitably complicate the problem of maintaining good relations with Congress. For example, no one can work in the Department without

120soon becoming aware of the division between the Foreign Service and the domestic civil service staff that became so evident when the Foreign Service tended to establish independent relations with a Congressional "constituency" of its own in order to get a very favorable Foreign Service Act in 1946. The disputes which have frequently arisen between economic and geographic divisions of the Department, have also resulted in the presentation of conflicting views to Congress. Finally such matters as the general post-war "shakedown," the forced absorption of war agencies, the increasing volume of emergency problems, indiscriminate Congressional criticism, unfavorable governmental salary policies--all of these have damaged the morale of the Department and increased the difficulty of enforcing discipline down the line within the Department, thus encouraging "leaks" to Congress and the press. The liaison function cannot be greatly improved without at the same time going to the roots of these ancillary problems.

Similarly there are factors on the legislative side which complicate the problem of liaison. The struggle over the passage of ERP legislation has demonstrated the difficulties the executive branch faces in dealing with the many committees that had jurisdiction over that legislation--the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, the House Foreign Affairs Committee, the Senate and House Appropria tions Committees, the House Special "Herter Committee," and finally the Joint Committee on Foreign Economic Cooperation. The growing interdependence of these various committees was emphasized by Senator Vandenberg's recent dramatic plea to the Senate Appropriations

Should the committees concerned with foreign affairs decide in the future to hold joint hearings and perhaps joint conferences in order to formulate an over-all Congressional foreign policy, somewhat along the lines of the present joint meetings of the appropriation and revenue committees to recommend a legislative budget, such a plan might well serve both branches a great deal of time and effort and would certainly help to simplify the liaison problem. INTER-DEPARTMENTAL COORDINATION AS IT AFFECTS LEGISLATIVE- CONGRESSIONAL RELATIONS IN MATTERS OF FOREIGN POLICY 

In addition to the President's and the State Department's relations with Congress on matters of foreign policy there is also the problem of coordinating the views of all executive departments and agencies, including those of the State Department, in order to present to Congress an integrated and consistent program. 

In the past, the lack of this kind of coordination has resulted in considerable damage to executive-legislative relations. At the time of the conferences leading to the establishment of the Food

123and Agriculture Organization, open conflicts between the State and Agriculture Departments greatly embarrassed the Administration in its efforts to win Congressional support for the proposed body. 

Similar failures to integrate policy within the executive branch on international issues such as the Morgenthau-Hull differences over our reparations policy in Germany, the Wallace-Jones dispute over economic warfare, and the Wallace-Byrnes differences which finally ended in the resignation of Henry Wallace as Secretary of Commerce-all of these have made it more difficult for the Chief Executive to win Congressional approval of a foreign policy on which his own lieutenants could not agree.

A related problem is that of bringing more into line with the President's own foreign policy, the views of the so-called independent regulatory commissions. Many of these, such as the Civil 

Aeronautics Board, the Tariff Commission, the Atomic Energy 

Commission, the Export-Import Bank, and the Maritime Commission, have an important influence on our foreign policy and yet remain, with the encouragement of Congress, relatively independent of the President, who nevertheless is primarily responsible for the execution of that foreign policy. An example of the kind of difficulty that can arise because of this lack of integration is the dispute that arose between the Department of State and the Maritime Commission because of the latter's unwillingness after World War II to make available certain surplus vessels to the United Kingdom. 

Because of the State Department's efforts to get the Commission to agree to the Department's point of view, it incurred severe

Congressional criticism during its 1948 appropriation hearings. 

Congress has also tended to curtail the freedom of the President, in the negotiation of reciprocal trade agreements by building up the authority of the independent Tariff Commission in this field. 

On occasion, however, differing lines of policy on the executive side have serged and blended so well that they have taught us lessons that can be used to improve future operations. One example of relatively effective coordination is the system which has been developed over the years to formulate our reciprocal trade policies. 

In this case the major decisions have been made by a seven-agency Interdepartmental Committee on Trade Agreements which has included the Tariff Commission. The major burden of Congressional relations on reciprocal trade has been carried by the State Department. In spite of the good work of this inter-departmental committee, however, Congress has recently acted to give more power to the Tariff Commission, which, as noted above, is quite independent of the President. 

Another example is the State-Army-Navy-Air Coordinating Committee, (SANA CC, formerly SWNCC), created in December, 1944, to formulate broad foreign policy objectives, especially in connection with post-war occupation operations. Although this committee has served well in certain instances it has not developed as a true over-all executive foreign policy board nor has it replaced the usual separate departmental contacts with Congress with any joint cooperative link.

In addition to these specialized inter-departmental committees, the Bureau of the Budget, through its Legislative Reference Division, is responsible for seeing that all bills proposed by any executive department or agency be examined in their relation to the President's over-all policies, and, if necessary, circulated for comment to other interested agencies. This is frequently done through one of the thirty-odd interdepartmental committees that deal with foreign policy questions, many of which the Bureau has been instrumental in organizing. With all of this inter-departmental coordination, however, the lines of communication to Congress generally still run independently through separate departments. 

This brief survey of the problem of interdepartmental coordination as it affects the executive branch's relations with Congress leads to certain conclusions: (1) Interdepartmental committees have been an important factor in improving the President's relations with Congress to the extent that they have helped to formulate an integrated and consistent

126foreign policy program. The inter-agency wrangles that have plagued executive-legislative relations in the past need to be checked and attempts made to approach the "collective responsibility" of the British cabinet before the Executive will win true support from Congress or the country. There seems to be a real need for some top-level policy formulating group, such as a foreign policy cabinet to set the major objectives for these various interdepartmental committees, settle their jurisdictional disputes, and serve as a general court of appeal. (2) In spite of the good work done by these interdepartmental committees to coordinate policy within the executive branch, there seems to have been less success in coordinating contacts with Congress. Yet it is at this point of contact with Congress that progress such as reciprocal trade and ERP have faced their greatest challenges. Thus, it is at this point that it would seem wise to develop the greatest teamwork, when common foreign policy problems are involved, rather than to let the State Department or the Army or the Tariff Commission bear the major part of the burden of pleading particular cases to Congress. (3) In order to attain this greater coordination of relations with Congress it would seem advisable to develop the closest liaison between the various interdepartmental committees and the joint executive-legislative foreign policy body that has already been mentioned. A two-way flow of information and recommendations both to and from the over-all legislative-executive body would undoubtedly create a stronger link than now exists between these

127various interdepartmental committees and Congress. When desirable, Congressmen might also be invited to meet with these committees and to participate in their discussions. (4) Past experience has also demonstrated that it is difficult for the President to develop a coordinated foreign policy program as long as various independent agencies such as the Maritime and Tariff Commissions, which play an important role in our foreign relations, remain relatively independent of his authority. This same experience indicates that the President's relations with Congress on matters of foreign policy would probably be clarified if the long-range general policy functions of these commissions were under the President's jurisdiction while, at the same time, the quasi-judicial functions, which result in decisions of individual rather than general application, remain as independent as at present. INTERNATIONAL ORGANIZATION AS IT AFFECTS EXECUTIVE-LEGISLATIVE RELATIONS 

Although international organization is only one of many foreign policy problems that are being tossed daily into the hopper of legislative-executive relations, it is a problem that has tested and will continue to test the effectiveness of those relations. The success of the United Nations depends to large measure on the capacity of all nations to work together with fundamental unity of purpose. 

That unit in turn demands more from individual nations than temporizing and equivocation; it requires positive, decisive, and courageous action. No country can work effectively with the United 

Nations if divisive influences within that country tend to paralyze its efforts to cooperate internationally. Thus the United States

128cannot afford to let the constitutional division between its legislative and executive branches defeat another and more fundamental constitutional mandate--"to...provide for the common defense, promote the general welfare, and secure the blessings of liberty..."which today cannot be achieved without positive international cooperation. 

Only too frequently in the past, slips between the executive cup an the legislative lip have rendered United States policy toward international organizations weak and equivocal. The long failure of the 

Department of State and Congress to come to some agreement that would allow this government to join the World Health Organization defeated for some time an important part of the Administration's program which most informed groups, including professional medical organizations, had strongly endorsed. Congress's recent failure to pass legislation which would make available a $65,000,000 loan to the United Nations for the construction of its headquarters has not only caused painful embarrassment to our own United Nations delegation but has also had damaging effects on the general morale of the United Nations. The strong movement for the revision of the United 

Nations Charter which developed in Congress this year has also demonstrated a certain lack of mutual understanding between the legislative and executive branches. All of these cases have made it more difficult for the United States to play a strong, positive and consistent role in the United Nations. 

On the other hand, effective legislative-executive cooperation with regard to international organization has been achieved on

129certain occasions in the past and undoubtedly will be in the future. 

No better example of such cooperation can be cited than the intimate collaboration between Secretary of State Hull and various legislators such as Senators Connelly and Vandenberg in the preparatory discussions which led to the Dumbarton Oaks Conference. And once the United 

Nations was established the very effective work done by Congressmen Vandenberg, Connelly, Eaton, and Bloom as delegates to the General Assembly was evidence of the potential capacity of our government for unified action with regard to international organization. 

It has been argued by some, however, that coordination within the United States, as it deals with international organizations, should not be carried too far. There are "technical aspects" of international relations, it is said, such as narcotic drug regulation, postal arrangements, etc., that are essentially "cooperative" and that must be kept as separate as possible from the "political aspects" which tend to be "oppositional." This line of argument would leave the "technical" aspects free to develop the greatest cooperation possible with all other countries regardless of our political attitude towards them. The difficulty, however, is that technical matters have political consequences and can therefore scarcely be separated from the political aspects of international relations. Since "political" questions have to do with the basic struggle for survival, technical matters have always tended by the very nature of things to be subordinated to the political aspects of foreign policy. Moreover unless there is coordination within the technical field itself no positive action may be possible since

130one decision in one technical area may tend to negate another decision in another area. In these times of unstable power relations therefore it seems inevitable that there be considerable coordination of all aspects of United States foreign relations. 

This discussion leads to certain final conclusions concerning the need for improved executive-legislative relations in matters of foreign policy that in a way sum up all the previous conclusions that have already been discussed. (1) While it is clear that the most disturbing factor in executive-legislative relations is not the problem of organization but rather of substance--not how but what we are going to do in foreign policy--nevertheless we must still strive to make organization serve policy to the greatest degree possible. (2) The importance of facing the outside world with clarity and unity of purpose makes it essential first of all that the executive branch within our federal government exert ever greater efforts to coordinate the views of its own members, the various departments and agencies, in order to be able to present an integrated and consistent program to Congress, to the people, and to the world. (3) This program will not receive a fair and friendly hearing in Congress, however, unless every effort is made to bridge the gap which now exists between the President and Congress, in fact to associate Congress as much as possible with important phases of policy formulation and administration both by asking advice from, and by giving information to Congress. Men who work side by side tend to think side by side.

131- (4) Finally, a further development of the trend, now apparent on the legislative side, to pull together conflicting lines of authority by means of joint committees and other devices will perhaps facilitate the process of executive-legislative liaison and will thereby help to develop a unified and consistent United States foreign policy.

132CHAPTER V RECOMMENDATIONS GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS 

1. 

The President and Congress should recognize the single most important fact in United States foreign policy today--that the overwhelming resources of the United States have thrust upon this country a great responsibility for international leadership. 

2. In order to assume that role of leadership, the United States must be so organized that it can act positively and consistently. Only in this way will the United States win the confidence of other nations. 

3. To this end of positive international leadership, it is imperative that the divisive aspects of the separation of powers between the legislative and executive branches be eliminated by: (a) promoting a recognition within those branches of a common responsibility to facilitate United States world leadership, and (b) instituting machinery that, once the two branches develop a will to cooperate, may help them to do so more easily than at present.

History suggests that the United States has been unable to act either positively or consistently for any length of time when the President and Congress have not cooperated with each other. This report does not for a moment recommend any fundamental change in our Constitutional separation of powers; it merely suggests that, within our present framework, the separation of powers be prevented from causing any

133recurrence of the paralysis of United States action in foreign affairs that has occurred not infrequently in the past.

The Presidency 

Since the President must depend on both the Congress and a. the general public for support of his foreign policy, he must de all he can to coordinate his relations with the people and Congress.

(1) The objectives of this Committee would be to exchange legislative and executive views on fundamental issues of foreign policy in order to reach a general informal "sense of the meeting" on these problems. To this extent the

134- (2) Since such joint legislative-executive cooperation is most vital at times when the Chief Executive's party does not control a majority in Congress and since treaties can seldom be passed by a single party in the Senate, it is recommended that the Committee be established on a bi-partisan basis. Party organization can be useful in bringing to foreign policy discussions the views of important sectors of the public and in creating popular support for foreign policy programs once they are adopted, but party politics should not be allowed to defeat the fundamental objectives of our foreign policy. It is these fundamental objectives which must be agreed to by all major sectors of the public and which will be the primary concern of the Committee. 

That Committee, therefore, should be bi-partisan in composition. While its membership should remain flexible subject to the President's discretion, it is suggested that the core of such a body probably would be the Secretary of State and the Chairmen and ranking minority members of the
