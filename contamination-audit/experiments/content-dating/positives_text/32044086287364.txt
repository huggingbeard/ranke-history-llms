CAPTA APTAIN ROBERT MACFEELEY is listed number 41 on this radiant, redundant roll of honor. He graduated from West

Point with the class of 1850; served in the so-called Yakima 

Indian war, and, for the period immediately following and extending until the Civil war, he was in charge of the commissary department at Fort Yamhill, Oregon. Rockey P. Jefferson Davis Sent Him on the Survey for a Railroad from the Mississippi to the Pacific; to Europe to Study Military Systems; New York, Pennslyvania & Ohio Railroad Wanted Him; He Was a Great Organizer, but Never Was Quite Ready for a Decisive Battle.

GE EORGE B. MCCLELLAN is listed 42 on this peculiarly predestined, picked poll, and his name thereon, strangely, cynically enough, is partly due to the choice of Secretary of War Davis, who, only a few short years later, was to head as president the Confederate States of America, seeking to secede from the mother country, the United States of America. Jefferson 

Davis (during his term under President Franklin Pierce, (1853-7), as secretary of war, had a clear vision of the need of a railroad joining the Pacific coast area with the rest of the country. 

So Davis organized two exploring and surveying parties, one under Geo. B. McClellan and the other in charge of Isaac I. 

Stevens, then just appointed first governor of Washington Territory. Their official reports made up a great book of about 500 pages, with 70 splendid illustrations, many maps, etc., pub-

lished in 1850 at the expense of the United States government, entitled "Executive Document No. 56, 39th Congress, 1st Ses sion"; its title page reading: "Reports of Explorations and Surveys to Ascertain the Most Practicable and Economical Route for a Railroad from the Mississippi River to the Pacific Ocean, Made under the Direction of the Secretary of War, in 1853-5," etc.

Heads of these exploring and surveying expeditions had authority from the United States interior department "to hold councils and make treaties with the Indian tribes." 

The Captain McClellan-Governor Stevens report was favorable. Among many things, it said: "The Columbia River pass is not only undoubtedly practicable, but it is decidedly favorable. The only tunnel required will be to effect the passage around Cape Horn mountain; this will not exceed 700 feet." 

Anyway, railroad lines have for years operated there on both sides of the Columbia river.

McClellan's great-grandfather fought in the Revolution; reached the rank of brigadier general. Several members of the McClellan family were in the Civil war, mostly with the Union forces; but Henry B., a cousin, was on the staff of the famous Confederate cavalry leader, J. E. B. ("Jeb") Stuart, and wrote a biography of that noted character who was one of the daring, darling spirits of the lost cause.

Geo. B. McClellan stood second in the 1846 West Point graduating class; was brevetted first lieutenant at Contreras, Mexican war, and captain at Chapultepec; returned to West Point and for three years was assistant instructor in military affairs. 

In 1851 was assistant engineer for the construction of Fort Delaware; the next year went with Capt. B. B. Marcy in 1848, in an expedition to explore the Red river in Arkansas; then was chief engineer on the staff of General Persifor F. Smith, engaged in river and harbor work in Texas. After that (1853), he had joint charge of the Pacific railroad survey noted above. 

Jefferson Davis, secretary of war, was so highly pleased with McClellan's work that he directed him to continue his study and report later; then Davis sent him to report on Samana bay, Santo Domingo, as a proposed naval station.

In 1855, the armed forces of the United States being increased, McClellan was made a captain in one of the new regiments of cavalry-but he never reached his command, for in April, that year, Secretary Davis detailed him as a member of a board of officers to study European military systems, especially cavalry practices. Among other things, they studied the siege of Sebastopol. McClellan made excellent reports; among other things, he recommended the Hungarian saddle for American

THE great list grows and grows. Frederick West Lander is accorded number 43 on this singularly select scroll. He was civil engineer on the staff of General-Governor Isaac I. Stevens during the survey of the Pacific railroad route, 1853. In 1854 

Lander headed a party of exploration to report on the feasibility of a projected railroad from Puget Sound to the Mississippi river. The four years following he was chief engineer and superintendent of the overland wagon road-the "Old Oregon Trail." He opened what was known as the "Lander cut-off," a shorter route between the summit of the Rockies and Fort Hall. 

In 1858 his party of 70 was attacked by Piute Indians, who were repulsed. He made a report on a proposed railroad from the Mississippi to Salt Lake, with branches to San Francisco and Puget Sound; led or participated in five continental surveys; received high praise for his energy and ability from the 

U. S. secretary of the interior.

In the Civil war, Lander was charged with a secret confidential mission to Governor Houston of Texas; later he was on 

General McClellan's staff. In 1861, was made brigadier general of volunteers, with Gen. C. P. Stone's division on the upper 

Potomac; was seriously wounded at Edwards' Ferry, but reorganized his brigade into a division, which he commanded, and January 5, 1862, successfully defended the town of Hancock, Md., against a superior force. February 4, 1862, still suffering from his wounds, he led a brilliant charge at Blooming Gap, for which he was given special commendation by the secretary of war. March 2, 1862, while preparing to move his division into the Shenandoah valley, to join General Banks, he died suddenly in the division camp on the Cacopon river, Va. Gen.

McClellan announced his death in a special order. Lander was the author of many patriotic poems. In October, 1860, he married Jean Margaret Davenport, famous English actress, who had made her first visit to America in 1838.

FREMONT, THE PATHFINDER 

Great Lover, Faithfully Loved, Led a Singularly Checkered and Stormy Career. JOHN 

OHN CHARLES FREMONT, one of the strange characters of

American history, is number 45 on this exclusive list of notables. Born January 21, 1813, died July 13, 1890, explorer, politician, soldier, candidate for president on the Republican ticket in 1856; his mother, wife of an old man, ran away with his father and they were never married. Birthplace, Charleston, C. C., where the boy, precocious, handsome, daring, enlisted the sympathy and support of Joel R. Poinsett, Jacksonian political leader, secretary of the U. S. treasury 1837-41. *Poinsett secured young Fremont work in the federal service, including employment with surveying and exploring parties, and United States 

Senator Thomas H. Benton of Missouri, long a leader in that branch of congress and an enthusiast on the importance of the Oregon Country, met the youth, invited him to his home, and gave him substantial help. 

In that home, the young fellow met Jessie, 16 year old daughter of Senator Benton, beautiful, talented, ambitious. The natural event followed-it was a case of love at first sight; the greatest thing that ever happened to Fremont, for she helped him ably, faithfully, to the last day of his turbulent career. Senator Benton tried to break off the contacts. The young people were married secretly. The senator was furious; ordered Fremont from his door. "Whither thou goest I will go,” quoted from Ruth of old the strong willed daughter, jewel above all precious things of her father's heart. After his fury was spent, Benton became

sponsor for and protector of his son-in-law, a strong undergirding. 

So Fremont led the government exploring expedition of 1843 to Oregon, behind the Applegate covered wagon train. When he had returned, Jessie, talented wife, corrected and edited his reports and, presto, he was in the world's eyes a great explorer-"the pathfinder."

Fremont went from Oregon to California, had many troubles, and all but ran everything in the southern part of that great domain during part of its transition period from Spanish-Mexican to American rule. California, broken into the Union mostly by Oregon men who discovered the precious metal and were first in the gold rush, commencing in 1848-9, elected Fremont one of her United States senators, for the short term, September 9, 1850, to March 4, 1851. He was nominated by the Republican convention of 1856, meeting at Philadelphia, for president of the United States. 

When the Civil war began, President Lincoln appointed Fremont major general in charge of the West, headquarters St. 

Louis, Mo., where he arrived July 25, 1861. Later Lincoln removed him, but, in March, 1862, appointed him to command the mountain district of West Virginia. In May, 1862, he was outgeneraled by Stonewall Jackson. Then he was placed under the command of General John Pope, but soon asked to be relieved.

Fremont had grand ambitions to organize and build railroads and open mines; did at one time control vastly valuable 

California mining properties. He was appointed governor of the Territory of Arizona, 1878-83. In after years, when his fortunes were low, Jessie Benton-Fremont, his true and faithful wife, helped support them with her able writings. In his last years, Fremont was restored to his army rank, with retirement pay. *Robert Poinsett, cousin of Joel, came early to Oregon; was long a leading citizen of Hubbard, near Salem, the capital; died there at a great age August 31, 1931.

Halleck in San Francisco married the granddaughter of Alexander Hamilton. Captain Warner, Sherman wrote, secured a leave of absence, in the fall of 1848, and secured a contract from Captain Sutter for locating and surveying the town of Sacramento, at $16 a day; Sutter paying the wages of assistants. 

Later Warner, Ord and Sherman making up the surveying party, camped there. Sherman wrote: "I was cook, Ord cleaned up the dishes, and Warner looked after the horses; but Ord was deposed as scullion because he would only wipe the tin plates with a tuft of grass, according to the custom of the country, whereas Warner insisted on having them washed after each meal with hot water. Warner was in consequence promoted to scullion, and Ord became the hostler. We drew our rations in kind from the commissary at San Francisco, who sent them up to us (at Sacramento) by boat; and we were thus enabled to dispense a generous hospitality to many a poor devil who otherwise would have had nothing to eat."

Afterward, Sherman, with two civilians, surveyed the townsite of Benicia, for Col. J. D. Stevenson. That new boom town, at the head of San Francisco bay, about 30 miles northeast of

SHERMAN'S LEAVE TO MARRY 

General Persifor F. Smith, then military commander for the 

West, had promised Sherman that he would send him to New 

York and Washington with dispatches as soon as he (Smith) had returned from a tour of Oregon. General Smith prolonged his stay in Oregon, but sent the dispatches, and, January 2, 1850, the steamer Oregon appeared at Monterey, whither they had gone, from San Francisco, for a short visit, when Sherman, Ord, A. J. Smith and others—"some under orders and the others on leave," boarded her, on the first leg of a journey via 

Panama to New York; fare $600 each. Sherman and Ord "put up at Delmonico's, on Bowling Green," meaning on their arrival in New York City. 

Sherman secured a leave of absence for six months; May 

1, 1850, married Ellen Ewing, daughter of Thomas Ewing, then secretary of the interior, an office lately created and at first called the home department. Sherman in that year saw the three intellectual giants, Webster, Clay and Calhoun, singing their swan songs, in the United States senate; and mixing medicine that led to the Civil war.

Sherman went to New Orleans in September, 1852, was on military duty there when he was offered a position and partnership in a bank proposed to be established in San Francisco. 

He secured a leave of absence for six months, to go and investigate the proposition-and Capt. J. F. Reynolds was named to perform his duties while absent-the man who brought old Chief John to the reservation, and then had charge of Fort Umpqua, Oregon; the man largely responsible for vic tory at Gettysburg. 

Sherman reached San Francisco Oct. 15, that year, and, deciding to accept the proposition, made a trip east and was back and in charge toward the end of 1853. 

The bank in which Sherman was interested did a good business; it stood up when other banks around it failed. But conditions changed and it was not paying in 1857, and early in 1858 the partnership was dissolved and it discontinued business. Sherman went to New York, employed by the same people, in banking there; but a financial panic came, and he went home to Ohio. He was strapped. But he had done nothing dishonorable. However, upon their insistence, he had made investments in California for some of his army friends, including Braxton Bragg, who became the famous Confederate general, and a few of the investments proved sour. While Sherman had not become responsible, he held himself so, as a man of honor, and finally reimbursed them all. But this kept him broke or poor for a long time.

SHERMAN AND DONA MARIA YGNACIA BONIFACIO 

Lloyd Lewis, Sherman's biographer, in his book, "Sherman, Fighting Prophet," wrote, page 78: "The diversions of the Sunday night balls that Spanish families gave in Monterey gave Sherman only temporary re-

lief from gloom. Years later, there would flourish a legend that 

Sherman had been deeply in love with a leading belle, Dona 

Maria Ygnacia Bonifacio. "Gertrude Atherton, the novelist, visiting Dona Maria in the latter's old age, understood from younger people of the region that: "I 'Sherman, when a young lieutenant, had loved her, and together they had planted a rose tree in her garden. He had loved her and ridden away but she had remained faithful to his memory and never married . . . She told me that she had been so victimized by tourists, who snipped the branches from her rose tree and even wrenched the keys off the piano, that she had put a lock on the gate and admitted no one but her friends. To her confidants Dona Maria declared that although she had known Sherman they had never been engaged and that he had never jilted her'."

Rider's California, page 301, paid some attention to this matter, while describing Monterey, Cal., in these words: "Where the new First National bank now stands . . . is the site of the former home of Senorita Bonifacio, in whose garden grew the famous 'Sherman rose.' According to romantic but purely fictitious story, General Sherman gave this rose to the seniorita, promising that when it bloomed he would come and claim her for his bride. The house and the rose bush were both removed to the 'mesa,' (high terrace or table land), near the house of Gouverneur Morriss, and the former is now occupied by Percy Gray, the well known water color artist."

Whatever the truth, many Californians are agreed upon belief in some strong emotional bond between the then young lieutenant and the beautiful Monterey maiden. 

Don Wiggins, well known resident of Salem, Oregon, who takes interest in things historic and is an authority on guns, of which he has a great collection, ancient, less aged, and new, spent a little time at old Monterey in 1904, and there saw Dona Maria Ygnacia Bonifacio and the famous rose tree, and brought away and treasurers a photograph of the two-the woman and the tree; the then old woman and the old and very large tree; for a rose tree.

Mr. Wiggins keeps an Associated Press clipping which appeared in American and other newspapers under the date line, Monterey, Cal., Jan. 6. That was in 1916. It reads: "The 'Senorita of the Sherman rose cottage' is gone. She died at the age of 86, firm in the belief that the romantic soldier who came into her life for a brief time many years ago and

promised to return, is still alive. During the Mexican war General William T. Sherman, then a captain, visited this section, and met Senorita Maria Ynacia Bonifacio. He planted in her garden a rose tree, since grown to huge proportions, and promised to return to wed her, it is said." 

The attentive reader knows Sherman was not yet a captain, and that he more than visited the section around Monterey; that he was a young lieutenant, and was located there many months, on duty; performing numerous and various tasks, and that later, for several years, he was a prominent early day resident of California, a leading banker in San Francisco. Also, the aforesaid reader knows the Associated Press reporter at Monterey in 1916 made a rather bad out at spelling the name of the deceased senorita.

Mr. Wiggins said the tree was of the cloth of gold variety of rose, and that both its size near the ground and its spread of limbs were enormous—almost beyond belief, for one used to roses on small bushes. 

General Sherman died February 14, 1891. The only explanation of the statement in the Associated Press dispatch that the senorita never learned the news of his death is that she was old then, and that the good Spanish people surrounding her were careful to keep the news from her. Oh, anything is possible, if one wishes to keep alive the tradition; the romance. 

Sherman was engaged to Ellen Ewing, daughter of his foster father, Thomas Ewing, when he was sent to California. He carried with him a lock of Ellen's hair. The two had grown up together, in the Ewing family; hardly knew when they ceased to be as sister and brother and became lovers.

It is far from likely that redheaded young Sherman thought of deserting Ellen. Their love letters were constant and tender during the period. They awaited their marriage only until Sherman could support her in the style she was used to, in her well to do and distinguished father's home.

Ellen was religious. At one time, she told "Cump" that he should become a preacher as a way out of the poverty of military life in the lower brackets. "Cump" was short for Tecumseh, Sheridan's middle name. He was always "Cump" as a young man, among his familiars.

One of their sons became a Roman Catholic priest, and taught at Santa Clara college, not far from Monterey and San Francisco.

 MONTEREY INTERESTING CITY 

Don Wiggins found Monterey a city of great historic interest, as he had expected. A woman died there about the time of his visit who had lived in Monterey when it was the capital for the California of three governments-Spain, up to 1821; Mexico, up to 1848; the United States after that.

Don saw the restaurant of Jules Simoneau, in which that sympathetic Frenchman supplied food to Robert Louis Stevenson when he was hungry; where he took him to his own bed and room when he was sick and homeless. The room is pointed out yet.

Monterey, mecca for artists, writers and antiquarians, kept the inquiring mind and wearied body of Don busy days on end; but he had much diversion, so many things of world interest are to see there.

The light from the ship Natalia on which Napoleon escaped from Elba, foundered in Monterey bay in 1835, is there, also many recovered pieces of the woodwork of the vessel. The light is in a shop that is the property of the state of California. 

The room was California's first American theater, fashioned in the end of the biggest old time saloon of the time and place. 

The custom house stands in Monterey that served three governments, Spanish, Mexican, American.

Fort Halleck is there yet, named for the great American scholar and general of the Civil war, who laid it out. It is the fort where Ord and Sherman commanded.

Commodore Sloat of the American navy took charge of California there in the name of the United States government July 7th, 1846. He was thought by some cautious persons to be a little early, but that is now about forgotten, and forgiven. 

Now they have built a Sloat monument there, its foundation containing stones from all of California's counties, and distinguished with historic names. That of Peter H. Burnett, first supreme judge of Oregon and first governor of California, is on one of them. The monument was dedicated June 14, 1910; 

California Bear Flag day, and American Flag day.

OREGON CALIFORNIA'S MOTHER-IN-LAW 

By the way, Oregon, mother of all the states draining into the Pacific ocean, is also the mother-in-law of California. It comes about in this way:

Peter H. Burnett, a member of the first elected Oregon provisional government legislature (1844) and from 1845 supreme judge under that government, until 1848, when he joined the

California gold rush, took with him printed copies of the laws of that first American government west of the Rockies. Burnett had been largely responsible for framing and making those laws. So, when laws were needed for California, whose gold 

Oregon men had discovered, and who were the first gold rushers into California-and who made up the leading elements in forming the state of California, and in "breaking" California into the Union, full fledged, with no territorial waiting or training period-Burnett had the first laws for California ready made and tried in the mother state. Furnishing California her first laws, it is submitted that Oregon is the mother-in-law of California.

TO Hugh Boyle Ewing is apporpirately given number 48 on this honor roll. Born October 31, 1826, at Lancaster, Ohio, son of Thomas Ewing, he studied at West Point, but did not graduate. Instead, following the arrival of the news of gold discoredy there in 1848, he went the next year to California, by way of New Orleans and Texas. Ewing traveled over a great part of California, and must have met there W. T. Sherman, his foster brother, and, May 1, 1850, to become his brother-in-law. 

Ewing was one of the men of an expedition of 1849 in the high Sierras, sent by his father, who was secretary of the interior, to rescue stranded immigrants from the deep snow. He returned by way of Panama in 1852, bearer of dispatches to Washington. 

At the opening of the Civil war, Ewing was given the rank of brigadier general, inspecting Ohio volunteers; was brevetted major general in 1865; commanded a brigade at Antietam and at Vicksburg; had charge of a division at Chickamauga, which formed the vanguard of Sherman's army; which, after a desperate struggle, captured Missionary Ridge. After the war, Ewing was made minister to Holland. He wrote several books, one of them "A Tale of Early California."

July 25, 1870, President Grant gave to General Sheridan a letter addressed to our country's representatives abroad, asking that they aid in securing provisions that would allow him to accompany the German armies in the war of that nation with France.

General Sheridan took with him as his aide General Forsyth; they were absent over a year; sailed from New York July 27; were in Liverpool Aug. 6; left London for Brussels Aug. 9; the same evening were off for Germany and the seat of war. The night before the battle of Gravelotte, near Metz, fought Aug. 18, 1870, they were cared for in Bismarck's quarters; to be up at 

4 in the morning, to see the battle, in which 400,000 fighting men participated. They witnessed the gigantic show from vantage points provided by Von Moltke, on suggestions of King William the First of Prussia, the movements and their objects being explained by the great general, Von Moltke. Generals Sheridan and Forsyth followed the operations of the German armies from Gravelotte to the siege of Paris, which was an operation of starvation, to save great bloodshed. After that Sheridan and his aide visited Turkey, Greece, Italy, England, Ireland, Scotland and other lands. In his book, "Personal Memoirs," Sheridan reported that they "saw no new military principles;" that what

they witnessed was done according to the same general rules with which they were already familiar. 

The average reader knows Sheridan was one of the three generals present at Appomattox to receive Lee's sword-the others Grant and Ord. He and Forsyth were at the world famous little weaver's cottage when Bismarck and Emperor Louis Napoleon arranged the preliminary terms of peace a peace that cost France Alsace and Lorraine, and a huge money indemnity. cans, with great gallantry, Boggs overtook it, Sept. 14, 1855, and recovered it. The next few years, Boggs was on a furlough, commander of the mail steamer Illinois, of the California Steamship company.

At the outbreak of the Civil war, Boggs was given command of the steamer Varunna, unit of Farragut's fleet below New Orleans. His was the first vessel to force its way past the batteries; did great damage to the Confederate gunboats and auxiliaries. 

At dawn April 25, 1862, the Varunna was attacked by two powerful rams, one of them the Stonewall Jackson. Boggs was able to beach the Varunna, practically destroying her two adversaries, firing till their guns actually sank below the surface of the river. Boggs, promoted to captain, was given command of the Sacramento of the blockading squadron off Cape Fear. 

Overwork obliging a return to shore duties, he was assigned to superintending and fitting out, in New York, vessels for the navy. In 1866, resuming sea duty, commander of the steamer 

Connecticut, a special cruise to the West Indies caused an international incident. He demanded surrender of the Confederate ironclad Albemarle in the harbor of Havana, an act resented by the Spanish government. Promoted to rear admiral, 1870, Boggs retired in 1872.

Stockton supported Andrew Jackson; was an intimate friend. 

In 1830 he was promoted to master-commandant and in 1838 captain-returned to active service; the latter year went to the 

Mediterranean on the Ohio, squadron flagship. In 1840 Stockton, on furlough, took part in the presidential election, speaking in most New Jersey counties for William Henry Harrison. In 

1841 he refused an offer of President Tyler to appoint him navy secretary. He assisted in the construction of the Princeton, named for his native and home town; because her first commander, 1834-5; was in command during an excursion down the Potomac when one of her guns burst, killing among others Abel P. Upsuhr, secretary of state, and Thomas W. Gilmer, secretary of the navy. A court of inquiry exonerated him. He was appointed by the president to convey to the Texan government the resolution of the American government for annexation. 

War with Mexico now (October, 1845,) being imminent, Stockton was ordered to proceed to the Pacific in the Congress and reinforce the American squadron. 

July 15, 1846, he arrived at Monterey, Cal., the war having already begun. On the 23d he relieved Commodore J. D. Sloat, and on the same day issued a dashing proclamation to Californians, now considered by some historians a curious, fantastic document. He assumed command of the land operations and enrolled the Bear Flag battalion of John C. Fremont as volunteers of the American army and proceeded to conquer southern California. He took possession of Santa Barbara; sailed

for San Pedro, where he arrived August 6. A week later he combined the forces of the army and navy, entered Los Angeles, raised the American flag. August 17 he issued a proclamation declaring California a territory of the United States and proceeded to organize a civil and military government. As for himself, he was declared governor and commander in chief. He placed the Mexican coast south of San Diego under blockade and planned for himself an expedition inland from Acapulco to the City of Mexico, but was forced to abandon this because the Mexicans recaptured Los Angeles. Early in January, 1847, the combined forces of Stockton and General W. S. Kearney (next on this roll of honor), after fighting the battles of San Gabriel and the Mesa, repossessed Los Angeles and ended the war on California soil. Soon Stockton was superseded. He went overland, arriving in Washington during October. May 

28, 1850, he resigned from the navy. He was elected to the 

U. S. senate on the Democratic ticket from New Jersey; served from March 4, 1851, to Jan. 10, 1853; introduced a bill for abolishing flogging in the navy and urged adequate harbor defenses, making speeches on both subjects. From 1853 to his death, Stockton was president of the Delaware and Raritan Canal company. He espoused the American party and was considered as a candidate for president in 1856. He was a delegate to the peace conference held in Washington early in 1861. 

While Stockton did not participate in the Civil war, his colorful, useful career in the years preceding it entitle his name to a place on this roll. He died October 7, 1866. The day of his birth was Aug. 20, 1795.

He graduated at the head of his class from West Point in 1853, and went to the corps of engineers, a brevet 2nd lieutenant; but was retained a year at the academy as assistant instructor; thence to river and harbor improvements and seacoast fortifications; on the Pacific coast until 1861.

He supervised the main part of the work of construction of Alcatraz federal prison in San Francisco bay near the entrance to the Golden Gate. One may find in the 1858-9 report of John B.

Floyd, secretary of war, references to the construction of the "fortifications on Alcatraces Island, San Francisco bay, California, in charge of Lieutenant F. E. Prime till the 31st of December, 1857; since then of Lt. J. B. McPherson." In the 1859-60 report of Secretary of War Floyd, further progress is reported in detail on the work of constructing the "fort at Alcatrazes Island, in charge of Lt. James B. McPherson." A year later, in his report of 1860-61, the secretary, Floyd, made extended mention of that project. He said: "The fort at Alcatraz Island, San Francisco harbor, California, in charge of Lt. James B. 

McPherson" had been "occupied by troops since last December," (1860), that it was "in a very efficient condition, already provided with a greater part of the armament and in readiness to receive the balance whenever it may be furnished," saying in particular: "A considerable number of barbette guns can yet be added at comparatively small cost." It appears that Alcatraz, on its twelve and a half acre island, was first intended to be a fort; then became a federal prison, afterward a military prison. It must have become a federal prison soon, for the period covered by Secretary of War Floyd's reports was when the fierce and fiery old Chief John and his dull son, Adam, of the Rogue River Indian tribes were being received and confined there, not long after the close of the Rogue River war, May 28th, 1856, they having been sent thither from Fort Hoskins on the 

Coast reservation of Oregon by Captain F. T. Dent, brother-inlaw of Captain U. S. Grant, the latter then out of the service, he trusted permanently.

The Civil war found Lieutenant McPherson at San Francisco; ordered to fort work in Boston, and, May 14, 1861, when the army was enlarged, he was made a captain in the new 19th Infantry (which he declined), and, Aug. 6, reached the same grade in his own corps. When General Halleck (who of course had known him in California), assumed command in Missouri, he took McPherson with him as an aide-de-camp, soon advanced to colonel. He was first assistant in the engineering department of Missouri. But when Grant (who also had known 

McPherson in California) opened his Tennessee campaign in

February, 1862, he promoted him to brigadier general of volunteers and after occupation of the section made him military superintendent of railways in the district of West Tennessee. 

General Sherman, who, too, had known him in California, said: "McPherson was . ... one of the most useful staff officers in the whole army, riding night and day. I think he knew more of the lay of the country than any officer of the army." October 2, (1862) Rosecrans, at Corinth, was heavily attacked by Van Dorn; the situation seemed critical, and Grant, at Jackson, Tenn., made efforts to reinforce him. McPherson, then at headquarters in Jackson, was directed to select four regiments stationed along the railway between there and Corinth and report with them to Rosecrans. He moved by rail to within 10 miles of Corinth, detrained, and marched the rest of the way, too late to assist in the repulse of Van Dorn, but in time to lead the pursuit. For that action, McPherson was made major general of volunteers and assigned to command the 2nd division, Department of Tennessee, in January, 1863, and, the army having been reinforced, it was reorganized, and McPherson received command of the 17th Army Corps. His corps was in the entire Vicksburg campaign, and, after the surrender, (July 4, 1863,) he remained in command of the district of Vicksburg until the following 

March, participating in Sherman's raid to Meridian. Aug. 1, 1863, McPherson became a brigadier general in the regular army. March 18, 1864, Grant went east to assume direction of all the armies; Sherman succeeded him in command of the

Military Division of the Mississippi, and began preparations for the Atlanta campaign. McPherson took over Sherman's

Army of the Tennessee, March 26, 1864; fought at the head of the army for the entire campaign, up to the fortifications of Atlanta. 

At Kenesaw Mountain the soldiers named McPherson's division "the whipsaw of the army." By July 22, 1864, the Army of the Cumberland and the Ohio were well established at Atlanta. The Army of the Tennessee under the direction of Mc- 

Pherson was ordered to connect with the Army of the Ohio on

its right and extend its line to the south. A Confederate turning movement against the left and rear developed. McPherson was at Sherman's headquarters, receiving his advices, and at once started to join his troops. Passing, with a single orderly, through a wood road which had been previously reconnoitered and found clear, he suddenly encountered hostile skirmishers who had penetrated his 17th and 18th corps, and was killed, July 

22, 1864. His death was one of the heaviest individual losses ever suffered by the Union forces. He was brave, loyal, without a spirit of self seeking. Grant coupled Sherman and Mc- 

Pherson as "the men to whom, above all others, I feel myself indebted for whatever I have had of success." Sherman was equally emphatic in his praise of McPherson.

At Adobe Walls, northwest Texas, Nov. 25, with about 400 men and two pieces of artillery, he attacked a force of Indians 3000 to 5000 strong, but after two days of hard fighting was compelled to withdraw. Junta, Colo., May 23, 1868. Generals Sherman and Fremont left testimonials of the greatness of the unlettered Kit Carson as man and soldier. "Simple as a child, brave as a lion," said

2Number 120 on this peculiarly precious and printable presentation is very properly reserved for Benjamin Louis Eulalie de Bonneville. His father was a learned philosophical radical, editor and pamphleteer, an intimate friend of Lafayette, Condorcet and Thomas Paine; his mother educated and talented.

Paine was at one time a dweller in their home, and godfather for one of the sons, who was named for him. When Paine left 

Paris for America, fall, 1802, it was agreed that the Bonnevilles should follow him. Madame Bonneville and children arrived in August of the next year, but Bonneville, who had incurred the wrath of Bonaparte, was kept under close surveillance and could not leave France until after the Corsican's downfall. Mrs. 

Bonneville gave her children careful training. Benjamin Eulalie entered West Point on his 17th birthday, April 14, 1813; graduated Dec. 11, 1815. Soon he was in a force constructing a military road through Mississippi. His contact with the frontier, which was to grip him the greater part of his life, began with his assignment to Fort Smith, Ark., in 1821. After four years at this post and at Fort Gibson, Okla., he was detached (1825) to serve as aide to the nation's guest, Lafayette; on completion of this internationally acclaimed visit he accompanied the marquis to France. Became a captain that year. Back to Fort

Gibson, 1826, he remained four years. The fur trade had long fascinated him. He now resolved to reap some of the rewards it offered in fortune, fame, adventure. In the fall of 1830 he took an eight months' leave in New York; where he interested capitalists, including Alfred Seton, one time Astorian, in a project for a thorough exploitation of the fur country. "Astor, too, though the project involved certain competition with the American Fur company, was friendly." (Quoted words from the Dictionary.)

Provided with funds, Bonneville asked for a two years' leave for exploration and gathering information. Granted, on May 

1, 1832, with 110 men, he left Fort Osage, Mo., for Green River; spent more than three years in the mountains, sending detachments of trappers and hunters in every direction, but the result was negative. He could not successfully compete with men who knew the field. Early 1835, he gave up the struggle and with a part of his force started for the settlements; reached Independence, Mo., Aug. 22 and learned that he had been dropped from the army May 21, 1834, for overstaying his leave. Hastening to report to Astor, he then went to Washington, beginning a campaign for reinstatement. Against protests of many fellow officers, President Jackson, April 22, '36, restored him to his captaincy. He was sent to Fort Gibson, but, finding the social atmosphere uncongenial, was detached to serve at other posts. 

Made a major in 1845, he served with distinction in the Mexican war; won a lieutenant colonelcy for gallantry at Contreras and 

Churubusco. He was retired Sept. 9, '61, for disability owing to sickness and exposure in the line of duty, but immediately became active again in various services, till the war's close. 

March 16, '65, brevetted brigadier general for long and faithful services. 

Washington Irving, writer of popular books, and Chittenden, historian, disagree as to the value of Bonneville's work. But the historian allows him certain merits: that he was a skillful commander; lost not a single life in any case where the men were under his personal control: showed an exceptional humaneness in dealing with Indians.

Alfred Seton, who assisted Bonneville in securing the money necessary for his great and profitless enterprise, was one of the ablest and most thrifty of the Astor party of 1810-14. Seton became vice president of the Sun Insurance company, New York.

Presumably John Jacob Astor helped him to the position and in other ways. The lower Astor fort on the Willamette river, Oregon, started early 1813, was built by Seton and John Reedand this writer believes the Dorion Woman was with them. 

That fort was called the Henry House; should have been named the Seton-Reed House. (The Astors called their forts or posts houses.) The site is unmarked; a slothful crime of omission; a disgrace to America. It is about a mile and a half up river from the Champoeg State Park; the exact spot known now to only a few.

David Glasgow was the second of five children of George Farragut, and the third member of the family to enter the U. S. navy, being preceded by his father and elder brother. It happened that the father of Commodore David Porter was cared for in his last illness by the

Farraguts and died in their home. Out of gratitude, Porter, commanding the New Orleans naval station, adopted young David Farragut; promised him a place in the navy. Born near Knoxville, Tenn., July 5, 1801, at the age of nine and a half years, David was appointed a midshipman by Paul Hamilton, secretary of the navy. In 1811 Porter, on the Essex, took David with him in active sea service. "In the war of 1812, when the Essex made her memorable cruise in the Pacific ocean, the very young midshipman gave a good account of himself in the various duties that fell to him. 

He was actually made prize master of one of the Essex's prizes, the Alexander Barclay. 'This was an important event in my life,' he wrote in his journal, 'and when it was decided that I was to take the vessel to Valpairaso, I felt no little pride in finding myself in command at 12 years of age.' The cruise of the 

Essex came to an end on March 28, 1814, in the harbor of Valparaiso, in her engagement with the Phebe and Cherub, the longest and most bloody sea fight of Farragut's career. During the fight he performed the duties of captain's aid, quarter gunner and powder boy, to the entire satisfaction of Porter, who commanded him, and would have recommended him for promotion had it not been for his extreme youth. A prisoner on parole till exchanged in November, 1814, Farragut was then ordered to the brig Spark, fitting for sea at New York, but before she was ready to sail the war ended. During 1815-20 he was in the Mediterranean, mostly on the Independence, Washington and Franklin, ships of the line that flew the pennant of the squadron's commander. Farragut served as the aide of 

Commodores Brainbridge and Chauncey and of Captain Gal lagher; saw historic places, mingled in society, and Charles 

Folsom, American consul at Tunis, took an interest in the youth, and under his direction he studied French, Italian, English literature and mathematics. Young Farragut spoke French, Italian, Spanish and Arabic. In 1826, while living in New Haven, he attended lectures of professors at Yale college and later of those at the Smithsonian Institution; he was the chief historian of the old navy. At 21, Farragut was acting lieutenant of the brig 

Spark; in '22 cruised on the John Adams; was the two following years with Porter suppressing piracy in the West Indies; first ordered to the Greyhound, his brother her commander, then was executive officer of the Sea Gull, flagship, then on the Ferret-his first command of a naval vessel. He learned tactics from the wily pirates, lessons that were to be useful to his country in the Civil war. One of Farragut's first commands was on the Brandywine, which in 1825 returned Lafayette (and Bonneville) to France, after their historic tour of the country which Lafayette had helped make free. He (Farragut) commanded

the Natchez at Charleston in 1833, to support the federal government in the dispute with the nullifiers. His last duty in the lieutenant's grade was in 1838, on the Erie, to protect American interests in the trouble between Mexico and France; in '42 was on the ship of the line Delaware at the Brazil station; transferred to the Saratogo to help in the Mexican war. OF PRIMITIVE LIVING CONDITIONS . . . At the end of four years he returned east by way of the Isthmus of Panama." (Quoted words (not capital letters) from Dictionary.) 

Farragut spent the winter of 1860-61 at Norfolk on waiting orders. He was then in his 60th year; in the navy more than 

49 years. April 17, 1861, the Virginia convention passed its order of secession, and it became necessary for Farragut, living in a Virginia city, to choose between state and nation. Next evening he left for the North. The navy department ordered 

Commander D. D. Porter to visit Farragut and sound him out. 

The Union cause required a leader big and brave enough to open the Mississippi. December 21 official Washington chose

Farragut as the man for the task; ordered him to undertake the capture of New Orleans; January 9 he was appointed to command the West Gulf blockading squadron. February 3, '62, he sailed from Hampton Roads in the steam sloop Hartford, his flagship. Farragut, 18 days later, was at Ship Island, naval rendezvous, about 100 miles northeast of the Mississippi's mouth. In two more months his fleet, 17 vessels and a mortar flotilla, was ready. Fort Jackson was on the west side, Fort St. 

Philip on the east side; the Confederate flotilla above the forts. 

The battle began April 18 with the bombardment of Fort

Jackson. After several days and nights of fighting, Farragut decided to run by the forts before they were reduced-contrary to the orders of the naval department and the advice of some of his ablest officers. Before daylight on the 24th (April), the 17 ships started, under a terrific fire. All but three passed the forts; next engaged the Confederate flotilla, destroyed 11 of its vessels and the ram Manassas. The Union fleet lost 184 men. Te day after the battle it reached New Orleans, which was taken without bloodshed. April 28 the forts surrendered to the mortar flotilla. The moral effect was exceedingly great. 

It made Farragut the leading figure of the navy, to his death. 

July 11, 1862, President Lincoln asked congress for a vote of the nation's thanks to Farragut and his men. Farragut was made a rear admiral. The West Davis flotilla was captured in 

June; followed those of Memphis, Galveston, Corpus Christi and

Sabine Pass which surrendered to Farragut's ships. By Decem-

ber the Union fleet held the whole gulf. In March, '63, he attacked the batteries of Port Hudson; two of his vessels and his flagship and a gunboat passed them. August 1, '63, Farragut went to New York, on the Hartford. He and his flagship, which had been struck 240 times by shot and shell, were the objects of vast interest in that metropolis. 

In 1864 he left New York for Mobile bay, into which he sailed with his fleet. "Torpedoes ahead!" warned his commanding officers. "Damn the torpedoes!" Farragut answered, and took the lead with his flagship, the Hartford. Fort Morgan on the east and Fort Gaines on the west surrendered. (Torpedoes would now be called mines.) President Lincoln approved a bill creating the office of vice admiral, passed especially to fit the case of Farragut. The people of New York City make up a $50,000 purse for Farragut when he was formally received there, December 12, 1864; the idea being that he would buy a house and live in the nation's metropolis, which he did. In January, '65, Farragut with his fleet made the James river safe for Union vessels and loyal commerce.

2Edward Davis Townsend is given the even number 150 in this choice and charmed circle of immortals. He (Townsend) was responsible for many measures taken in 1861 for the defense of Washington and for the organization and discipline of the newly raised troops. 

On Scott's retirement, Nov. 1, 1861, he became senior assistant to the adjutant general's department. Number 177, given to Rodney McCamley Price, is slightly irregular in that he did not participate in the Civil war-was a "copperhead"—and the excuse is the interesting part of his career in California, whose new gold helped to save the Union; besides the strange Nemesis, or Artemis, that pathetically pursued him to the end. Born in Sussex county, N. J., 1816, he studied law; married Matilda Trenchard, daughter of a naval captain, sister of Stephen Decatur Trenchard, famous admiral.

Price became a naval purser, 1840; was on the new Missouri, one of the first steam warships to cross the Atlantic; was received by Queen Victoria and Wellington; learned Spanish and helped Washington Irving to copy the Columbus records in the Spanish archives. He (Price) played an important part in the 

American acquisition of California. Early in 1846, after what he described as a confidential interview with President Polk and Secretary of the Navy Bancroft, he was ordered to the Cyane in Scott's Pacific squadron. He claimed that he gave the irresolute commodore the necessary prodding to occupy Monterey. He related that, being rowed to the flagship at midnight, on July 6, he strongly urged Commodore Sloat to occupy California, before Sir George Seymour's British squadron should do so. He said the commodore, still in his nightshirt, paced the cabin for an hour, and, finally, exclaimed: "Mr. Price, you have convinced me. I will hesitate no longer!" In the morning Price read the proclamation of annexation from the Monterey custom house. He was appointed alcalde, and was said to be the first American to exercise judical authority in California. He was later sent with dispatches to Scott and thence to Polk, who asked him to draw up a report on California. While he was

in Washington, news of the gold discovery came, and he was sent back to San Francisco with added fiscal powers as naval agent to facilitate the transfer of funds and prevent gold shipments to England. He made heavy profits in real estate, furnished money for San Francisco's first wharf, sat in its first municipal council; was a man of true "California spirit." In 

1849, he was relieved from duty as naval agent. He settled his accounts and advanced $75,000 to his successors for government use, but when traveling on the Alabama river the steamer was burned, and he lost all his receipts and vouchers. This created an embarrassing tangle, resulted in litigation that stretched over 40 years; caused imprisonment just before his death.

Price was a Democratic politician; was in 1850 elected to the 

New Jersey house of representatives; was a booster, spoke, worked for the Paterson silk industry; was defeated for reelection, but in 1853 was elected governor; January, '54, began his three year term. In spite of his youth made an excellent record for constructive legislation; fostered educational reform; appointed judges of both parties, etc. Not being eligible for reelection, he returned to private life. 

He refused to take sides; remained a "copperhead" throughout the Civil war. He started the successful ferry from Weehawken, N. J., to 42nd street, New York City, later acquired by the West Shore railroad. Litigation over the missing naval papers clouded his last years.

McCann, after peace came, was on duty at the Naval Academy. In 1876, sent to command the Lackawanna, North Pacific squadron; became commodore, 1887. Commodore Schley spoke highly of him. 

2Charles Stewart McCauley is number 180 on this near perfect peculiar poll of patriotism. Warranted a midshipman January, 1809, he served in the war of 1812; in several engagements; became acting lieutenant September, 1813; was thereafter in the Mediterranean on the Constellation and United States; became captain, 1839. In the '46-9 period was commandant of Washington navy yard. "From 1850 to 1853, he was commander in charge of the Pacific squadron, whose cruising grounds at that time extended from the west coast of the two 

Americas to the 180th meridian," says the Dictionary.

July 25, 1860, McCauley was made commandant of the Norfolk navy yard, a position which in the following year entailed much responsibility. After the secession of Virginia, when the yard was threatened by the Confederates, he ordered the guns spiked and the ships scuttled, and abandoned it without making a defense. In 1862 he returned to duty with the rank of captain, and in 1867 was promoted to commodore. A select committee of the senate inquiring into his conduct at Norfolk navy yard reported that he was highly censurable. He fell into a melancholy that hastened his death, May 21, 1869. He felt that his honor had been unjustly wounded and his reputation tarnished. 

3Nathaniel Lyon has number 181 on this innately inimitable grouping of immortals. He was a member of the 1841 West 

Point class. "He wrote, 1844, that the 'sending of the troops to the Texas frontier bears the earmarks of madness and folly.'

Nevertheless, two years later, he was doing his bit at Vera Cruz, Cerro Gordo, City of Mexico. For several years he was on frontier duty in California. Between 1854 and 1861 he was stationed in 'bleeding Kansas.' (Quoted words from the Dictionary.) A brother of Lyon came west, likely led by news from the soldier brother in California. The former erected a warehouse at Independence, Oregon, in the beginning days of that town. 

A son of his, while a druggist in California, started Lyon's tooth powder, which made fortunes; now has a worldwide sale. 

Mrs. Boise, deceased, Salem, Oregon, wife of the famous pioneer Judge and Supreme Court Justice R. P. Boise of Oregon, was a cousin of General Lyon.

Lyon was assigned, Feb. 6, 1861, to the St. Louis arsenal; was made brigadier general, given supreme command. (He had questioned the zeal of Gen. W. S. Harney (for whom the 

Oregon Harney county was named), and threatened to throw the arsenal ordnance officer into the Mississippi if he weakened toward the Southerners.) Lyon seized Camp Jackson; armed volunteers. He had a hot interview with Confederate General 

Sterling Price and Claiborne Jackson, governor of Missouri. "This means war," said Lyon, closing the interview. He sent 

Col. Franz Sigel with a small force into southwest Missouri.

He himself, with 2000 regulars, pushed up the Missouri; took 

Jefferson City June 15; captured Bonnville two days later. State forces retreated to southwest Missouri. Lyon turned in pursuit, reaching Springfield July 13. He decided, Aug. 9, to attack the main forces of the enemy; camped 10 miles south of Wilson creek. Confederate forces and state troops numbered over 

10,000; Lyon's regulars and volunteers 5400. Lyon sent Sigel with 1200 men to attack, next morning, the enemy's extreme right wing, while he, with 4200 troops, fell upon the rear of their

left. Initially succeeding, he was later surprised and routed; thus a probable Union victory was turned into defeat. After about five hours of able fighting, Lyon was killed at the head of his troops. He was immediately a national hero and martyr. 

His work had done much to hold Missouri for the Union. 

Sigel was a "48" German, plus. That is, he should be counted among the great number of men who, in 1848, fled from Germany, self banished for the safety of their lives, because they had a desire for a free government of the people. 

That was the 1848 purge. Many of them found homes in Missouri. Sigel soon headed the 3d Missouri Infantry, then the 2nd

Missouri brigade; became a brigadier then a major general. 

His Union victory in the battle of Pea Ridge, leading the second division of Fremont's army, settled the state of Missouri for the Union. Who of the older generation has not heard the apothegm, "I fit mit Sigel," attributed to Civil war veterans? The 

Steusloffs of Salem, Oregon, leading packers, came from fathers who were "48" Germans. Who knows how many more ""'48" men and women Germany has now than dare make their voices heard? Time will tell. After the war, General Sigel worked, wrote and lectured for the principles in favor of which he had sacrificed and fought.

PAULDING, GRAND OLD MAN 

His Father Was the Seventh Child of John Paulding, Captor of Major 

Andre; $1500 and Congressional Medal for Gallantry in the Battle of Lake Champlain; Ericsson Said: "Without Pauling's Support, the

Monitor Would Not Have Been Built." (Thus a Vast and Timely Gain for the Union Would Not Have Come to Fruitage.) 

WE COME to number 182 on our silver and gold and diamond screen. It is accorded to Hiram Paulding. His father was the seventh child of John Paulding, celebrated captor of Major Andre in the Revolution. He became a midshipman, 1811; in 1813 was ordered to Lake Erie, then to the Lake Champlain squadron. In recognition of his gallant service in the battle of Lake Champlain as acting lieutenant on the Ticonderoga, he received $1500 prize money and a sword from congress; and $1500 in those days was far from chicken feed. "He was made a lieutenant in 1816, and spent the next three years on the 

Macedonian of the Pacific squadron," says the Dictionary. "While on duty again in the Pacific, on the United States, he carried Admiral Hull's dispatches from Callao to General Bolivar's headquarters in the Andes, a journey of 1500 miles on horseback. He volunteered the following year (1825) for a long cruise in the South Seas as 1st lieutenant on the Dolphin, pursuing mutineers. He wrote two books about that Dolphin

cruise. Paulding was on the Vincennes, 1844-7, on her voyage to China; was, the next four years, in charge of the Washington navy yard, before, prior to 1855, operating in the Caribbean. In 

December, 1857, he seized 'General' William Walker and 350 fellow filibusters at Greytown, Nicaragua; sent 150 of his men to take 350 filibusters, and ordered them home. Nicaragua gave 

Paulding a jeweled sword." (Quoted words from the Dictionary.)

In the Civil war, though above the regulation age of active command, Paulding was in charge of the bureau of detail, selecting suitable officers for wartime activities. He led the expedition which, April 21, '61, evacuated the Norfolk navy yard; was on the board of construction of the new type of ironclads; took much of the responsibility for the fashioning of the Monitor and the New Ironsides. John Ericsson, in whose brain was conceived the Monitor, said: "Without Paulding's support, the Monitor would not have been built." That nation-saving new style fighting ship was built at the New York navy yard, on the board of which sat Paulding, remaining through 1865. During the draft riots he looked to the saving of lives and the protection of property in and about New York City. On his death Paulding was senior on the retired list; he was the last survivor in the battle of Champlain; date of his death, Oct. 2, 1878.

MORE AND MORE The List of 182 Is Not Complete; the Number with Searching Grew and 

Grew and with More Searching Would Still Grow and Grow.

THE 

HE writer does not claim the list is complete with the names of 182 men on this imperishable roll of honor. When, not many months ago, he started with 39 men who learned in the Oregon country the lessons that served them in the Civil war to aid in saving the Union for America and democracy for the world-lessons gained while low ranking officers fighting and handling Indians here, in the '40s-'50s; lessons on how to lead victorious regiments, brigades, divisions, corps, armies in the '60s, that number seemed wonderful, remarkable, distinctive. 

But, taking in California, 12 names were quickly added, and then it became evident that still more men were entitled to places on the distinguished, outstanding honor roll. It appeared that the original 39 might be doubled, then multiplied by three, then four. But the list grew and grew. The original number is multiplied by four, with yet 26 illustrious names added-182 in all.

But it does not follow that all who ought to have numbers on the shining silver screen are there. If we counted Indian fighters west of the Missouri river-of the original pioneer westernmost West-the number would go far above the 200 mark; including of course, only those who, when the Civil war came, aided in winning it for the Union cause. When one in the search was reached who fought here and then went with the Confederate side, his name was not eligible for the exclusive company. But, by no means insignificant, their number was comparatively small. For some unaccountable reason, a considerable number of West Point men who as low ranking officers fought in the Indian wars of eastern Washington went with the 

Confederate forces. Some names follow: Major R. S. Garnett, who fought Indians in the Puget Sound country and then in the Yakima section. He was killed while fighting in the Confederate army. Archibald Gracie was another. He led the party which, in May, 1855, conducted the peace commissioners from The Dalles to the famous council grounds at the site of 

Walla Walla-where Governor Stevens of Washington and his companions escaped massacre through the intervention of 

Chief Lawyer; the first Chief Lawyer, son of Chief Twisted Hair who kept the Lewis and Clark horses the winter of 1805-6: where (at the council grounds) one of the greatest American Indian demonstrations of savage grandeur ever witnessed was gaudily enacted. Then there was Major George Rains; also John B.

Magruder, who fought on the Confederate side at Big Bethel, first battle of the Civil war. And Captain John Henry Winder, who was in charge of the notorious Confederate prisons. And no doubt several more. Even Wm. Wing Loring, brevet colonel, U. S. Army, who left Fort Leavenworth for Oregon May 10, 1849, with the renowned rifle regiment; 500 enlisted men, 31 officers, some women and children, 160 wagons, teamsters, guides, train agents, 2000 mules and horses, and subsistence, to make the 

Old Oregon Trail safer; strengthened Fort Laramie; established

Cantonment Loring near old Fort Hall. Even Loring went with the Confederacy. So did John B. Hood, who became one of the foremost Confederate generals. 

August 5, 1855, 2nd Lieut. P. H. Sheridan relieved Lieutenant 

Hood near Klamath lake, Oregon: Hood went south to a new station, Sheridan north to Washington, then back to Oregon.

They were, a little over six years later, to lead armies, in grips against and for the nation born of the Revolution: the nation of, by, for the people. One more, briefly: Sheridan tells in his 

Memoirs, volume 1, page 121, of being ordered east in 1861, from Fort Yamhill, where, though only a 2nd lieutenant, he had charge: he having been instructed to turn over the post to Captain James J. Archer. Sheridan wrote: "But I had been notified

that he (Archer) intended to go South, and his conduct was such after reaching the post that I would not turn over the command to him for fear that he might commit some rebellious act. 

News came that he had tendered his resignation. . . . On July 

17 (1861) he took his departure, but I continued to command till 

Sept. 1, when Capt. Philip A. Owen came and gave me my release." Most readers know what happened to Sheridan, one of the three generals who at Appomattox received Lee's sword -the other two Ord and Grant, both of them also having fought and handled Indians in the Oregon Country. Well, what of 

Archer? Briefly, here is his record: • • 

Born in Maryland. Was a lawyer; went to the Mexican war; brevetted major for gallantry in battle; got a vote of thanks and a sword from the Maryland legislature. In '61 he organized "Archer's Brigade," in the Army of North Virginia; was in the battles of Seven Days, Cedar Mountain, 2nd Manassas, Antietam, Fredericksburg, Chancellorsville, Gettysburg-was with Stonewall Jackson in the flanking movement at Chancellorsville, .. was in the part of the rebel army that began the three day battle of Gettysburg opposing the positions where 

Reynolds and Doubleday on the Union side were in command. (He must have known Reynolds in Oregon, when he (Reynolds) brought old Chief John from Port Orford overland to Fort Yamhill in 1856). Then, says the record: "Archer was captured by a flank movement with many prisoners; was a prisoner for over a year; was then sent southward and exchanged, but died soon after his release." (He died Oct. 4, 1864.)
