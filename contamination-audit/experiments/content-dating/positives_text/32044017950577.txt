Mystical Reflections 

ON 

THE AMPERSAND 

TO EXIST as a corruption descriptive of a contraction seems an ignoble destiny. To be called "ampersand" not because of descent from one of the great languages of antiquity, but for the shameful reason that for centuries snuffling little Britishers refused to enunciate properly the tag at the end of their alphabets is not the best basis for a life of dignity. But to the philologist, as well as to the merely curious, the circumstances are interesting, for in that word he sees language developing through inadvertence, and, if he is a man of proper breadth, is better pleased than at the prospect of those normal derivations in which exist a relationship between meaning and employment. Droning their hornbooks in unison or alone these speech-lazy boys and girls of the past gradually allowed their "X, Y, Z, &," their “ex, wye,

zed, and per se and," to become "ex, wye, zed, ampersand," creating from four monosyllables, without thought or design, a lovely, liquid word that not only sounds as if it meant something, but that belongs to the hierarchy of poetic sounds with Samarcand and Avalon.

The apocryphal, extra-alphabetical character designated by this word stands in the mind of the child as the goal attained, the end of endeavor, the very symbol of completeness, or, at least it so stood in the days when children learned the alphabet by rote. It was not until they reached later years that they perceived their triumphant “ampersand" to be but a fresh beginning, a doorway opening into the complexities of knowledge, an introduction to all the thoughts and concepts that may follow the great connective, that is, to all those thoughts and concepts that may be put together from the innumerable permutations and combinations of the twenty-six alphabetical characters. 

Broken-backed, cramped as though with pain, homely, mysterious, difficult to form, the

& or contracted "et" and its concomitant the &c retain their dignity and large importance even though the prim lady teachers of our world forbid us to use them in writing or printing almost as soon as we learn their names and meaning. The Society for the Restoration of the Ampersand to Common Writing and

Printing must first realize the two chief causes of this inhibition, that is, the desire of teachers to make things harder, and, more reasonably, the recognition by teachers and pupils alike of the extreme ugliness of most renderings of the character. The first cause must be overcome by passive resistance; the second by direct action. A dozen or so symbols on a printed page resembling eccentric dancers in contorted pose offering the world an empty platter stand out as so many spots on a fair cloth, painful to the sensitive eye. It is necessary, therefore, for the 

Society to redesign the character, going back, perhaps, to some particularly fluid drawing of it in a mediaeval manuscript, or, in print, to one of the happier executions of the sixteenth-cen-

tury Frenchmen. Then we shall have a convenient, unobtrusive, and even decorative character productive of enormous economy to the buyers of printing, a definite and universally understood symbol for one of the commonest and most important of the words of daily use. 

Then the ancient character will be raised from its present low estate, no longer serving only as the link between Messrs. Weber & Fields, or between Jones & the mysterious Co.

When did the use of the ampersand in writing begin? It was not known, I believe, to the 

Romans in the form in which we have it now, that is, as a character developed from a ligature of the letters "e" and "t." But in an eighthcentury manuscript at Lucca it appears in full development, devoid of the slight horizontal stroke that even in some modern forms stands for the “t” and suggests the history of the character. In a twelfth-century manuscript that I have in hand, the ampersand betrays a memory of the word for which it stands by the presence in it of a rudimentary "t." In certain

other early manuscripts the symbol for the contraction is completely arbitrary, a shorthand character bearing no resemblance to either of the letters it replaces. These arbitrary symbols are beyond our present interest. They stand for "et," but they are not the ampersand. 

Through what influences, geographical or cultural, certain printers were moved to use these, and certain others the ampersand, is a question that would lead us into deep waters when our intention is only to wade. Let us avoid this issue and look for a moment at some early specimens of the true ampersand, the vestigial ligature of "e" and "t."

That is because italic is closer than roman to the natural writing hand, and the character, after all, is a scribe's, and not a printer's, device for brevity. It remains for the

designer of the new form of ampersand to reconcile this symbol of cursive writing to employment with the stiff and upright roman. 

In writing by hand the ampersand is a short cut too often neglected because of those remembered school-marm admonitions, but none of the canonical characters of the alphabet gives the same opportunity for self-expression by the penman who thinks and feels with his pen as this which he so generally ignores. One writer likes to form certain letters, another prefers a different group, but except for the oily and smooth-flowing O, or the quirky Q, or the figure 8 with its convolutions, no character in its formation gives greater satisfaction to the sensitive pen than that which comes from the circumscribed and disciplined whirl of the ampersand, resulting in an intricate and pleasing design, in the creation of a little work of art upon the page instead of the commonplace "and." Furthermore in its very design it fulfills graphically the function of the conjunction itself; up and backward, down and backward,

it picks up and ties in with what has gone before; up and forward, it joins what it has picked up with what is to come, connecting thought with thought in an articulated expression. At once, by this employment of a symbol for a word, the printed page assumes an interest previously lacking, takes on a touch of color, even of fancy.

The word "ampersand," if not the character, bulks larger in the American world than anywhere else. In the deep Adirondacks stands a mountain, 3300 feet in height, we are told, which some imaginative person long ago gave the name "Ampersand" from its fancied resemblance to the character. One need not be completely convinced of the likeness to accept willingly for this noble work of Nature a name that bears to such degree the accent of grandeur. Climbing Ampersand one hot and muggy August day in the wake of two stripling girls came near to having for me the significance of finality that clings to the name. It did in a sense, because the mountains I have

climbed since that day are, virtually, none; that is, to say, one half a mountain. But I have already suggested that this interpretation of the symbolism of the ampersand is childish and superficial. Even though that was my last as well as my first mountain, the summit attained was a fresh beginning for me, an introduction to the earth's immensity and its rich and careless beauty, a connective between elements known by hearsay and elements experienced. ... But perhaps these reflections should come to an end here and now lest some unkind critic say I have long since finished with the discussion of the & and am simply rambling along with the &c.
