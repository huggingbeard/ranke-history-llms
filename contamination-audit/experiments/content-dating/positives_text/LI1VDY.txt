CORN Corn is grown in many areas because it is adapted to a wide range of environments. 

The main producers are the temperate regions that have a rainfall greater than 

8-10 inches in the summer. gated land to some extent. 

It is also produced on irri- In the United States, corn ranks first in acreage and is heavily concentrated in the Corn Belt, from the Dakotas, Nebraska and Kansas on the West to Ohio on the East. From this region, in average years, is produced almost half of the world's supply of corn. The principle type of corn grown there is dent, which accounts for more than 90 percent of our production. 

Flint is the other main type grown. Yellow and white varieties of both types are grown, with the yellow variety predominating. 

Widespread use of hybrid seed in the production of corn in our Corn Belt, which started around 1933, can be expected to continue. Large further increases there, above the present use of hybrids are doubtful, however, because in many localities it has reached almost 100 percent. Corn producers in the South did not start to use hybrid seed as soon, but the trend there is rapidly moving upward. Extension of hybrids in foreign countries could increase substantially the yields there, too. 

Corn, as a feed grain, is marketed primarily through livestock and livestock products. As such, corn makes up approxim-tely 60 percent of the total grain and by products fed to livestock, but only about one-fourth of the total feed, including forages. This method of disposal has made the surplus feed-grain producing areas of the Midwest the center of the livestock industry, particularly hogs and beef, which can be processed and shipped to major consuming areas more economically than feed grains. However, shipments of grain from the Midwest to the Northeast has enabled this area to produce large quantities of livestock products in the high consumption area of the East as well. The North Central States produces about 85 percent of the corn going into commercial channels. 

The normal flow is A typical example would show the Illinois farmer selling more than 40 percent of his corn, the Iowa farmer 25 percent, Pennsylvania 15 percent and Georgia 10 percent. from the Midwest to the East, South and to the West. 

But because each region is a producer as well as a consumer of feed grains, the movement is not uniform This movement varies from year to year and in an occasional year may reverse due to drought conditions. or continuous.

The marketings of corn are heaviest during November- 

January, after the harvest in the Corn Belt. Prices received are seasonally low during November and December, then normally advance during the rest of the season reaching a high in August as a result of the seasonal marketing pattern, the cost of storage, and loss of grain in storage as basic elements contributing to this phenomenon.

A little less than one-third of the corn sold by farmers is purchased by processors for making food and industrial products. The wet processing industry converts corn into starch, sugar, and sirup, whereas, the dry processors produce cornmeal, hominy grits, flour and prepared cereals. The distillers use corn in producing alcohol, distilled spirits, and other alcohol products. Usually, a little more than half of the corn sold is bought for livestock feed and includes that going directly to livestock producers and also that purchased by feed manufacturers. Thus, including the corn fed on farms, about 90 percent of the total corn produced is fed to livestock. 

Exports of corn averaged 190 million bushels or about one-half of the total tonnage of feed grains exported. 

They accounted for a little more than one-tenth of the total sales, and about 3 percent of the total production of corn in the 1948-52 period. 

Imports of corn have been negligible except during the drought years of 1934 and 1936.
