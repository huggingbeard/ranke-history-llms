Definition and Scope of System 

The major components of the City's criminal justice system are generally defined to include the Police Department, the Department of Correction, the Office of Probation, the District Attorneys, and the courts. Many other Citysupported departments are also involved with aspects of criminal justice, including the Transit Authority Police, the Housing Authority Police, the Department of Investigation, the Corporation Counsel (juvenile matters), the Legal Aid 

Society, the Youth Services Agency, the Addiction Services 

Agency, and the Criminal Justice Coordinating Council.

According to the Twentieth Century Fund report: 

These separate but interrelated institutions together form or ought to form -- a system. Each institution performs a separate task or set of tasks, but success in performing any one task depends on the effectiveness with which all the other tasks are performed...For the system to control crime properly, each part of it must be working well, and all the parts must be working in coordination with one another.*

The need for effective integration and coordination of these various agencies is made manifest by the massive size of the City's criminal justice system. As of 1970-71, New 

York City employed approximately 50,000 persons in criminal justice work: more than 39,000 in the various police departments and agencies, more than 3,400 in the courts, 3,500 in corrections agencies, over 1,700 in the probation department, and nearly 1,000 in the prosecutorial (District Attorneys') *Ibid., P. 35.

Judicial Screening Panel 

Under the State Constitution* the Mayor is responsible for appointing all judges of the Family and Criminal Courts throughout the City, and for filling vacancies in the Civil Court. The last several Mayors have used an informal screening panel to review qualifications and abilities of prospective appointees. Members of the twenty-five-person screening panel are appointed by the Mayor and the Presiding Justices of the Appellate Division, First and Second Departments. 

The panel's determinations are advisory only, with the final decision made by the Mayor.

The Commission believes that the quality of judicial appointments is the cornerstone of an effective system for the administration of justice. It further finds that instilling confidence in a nonpartisan, professional judiciary is a 

*Article 6, Sections 13, 15, and 21.

21. 

The matter of paramount importance to the public welfare. 

Commission believes that the principle of professional review of the legal abilities, character, and professional experience of candidates for judgeships should be formalized by law. This can be accomplished by requiring that all prospective appointees be approved by a screening panel and that the Mayor make his appointments from those approved. 

The concept of commissions and panels to provide preliminary review of judicial candidates has been gaining favor nationally during the past thirty-five years. Attorneys and bar associations throughout the country have concluded that the panel system results in a higher caliber of judicial appointments. A 1972 survey found that this type of system has been adopted by law for the selection of judges in one or more of the courts of eighteen states. Approval by nominating commissions in such states is not merely advisory; it is a mandated prerequisite for judicial eligibility. The 

American Bar Association has recommended the creation of a similar commission for the appointment of Federal judges. 

At the time of the State Constitutional Convention in 

1967 former Mayor John V. Lindsay voiced the hope that his advisory committee on the judiciary would be given "legal status" in the State Constitution, and that approval would be made a mandatory prerequisite for appointments. A nominating commission for all State courts was also recommended at the time of the 1967 Constitutional Convention by the New 

York County Bar Association.

The concept was supported by the New York City Bar Association in a 1967 report which argued that: "The State/

Commission..

Recently, Governor Hugh Carey established a series of judicial nominating committees to screen prospective candidates for judicial posts to be filled by the Governor. 

Pursuant to his Executive Order of February 21, 1975, the 

Governor announced that nominating committees would review the qualifications of all candidates and submit the names of well-qualified candidates to the Governor. The order further stated that the Governor would in turn make his appointments only from the names so submitted. In a release accompanying the executive order, Governor Carey said:

For the first time in the history of this State a Governor has established a system for the merit selection of judges. It is in fulfillment of my pledge that I will appoint as judges only those persons who are, by virtue of their learning, experience, character and temperament, well qualified to assume the duties of judicial office.

This merit selection plan represents a major step toward the goal of taking partisan politics out of the system by which judges are selected. I am confident that the committees established by this order will recommend to me only the most qualified persons.

The Charter Commission fully endorses the intent of Governor Carey's executive order and advocates that a similar approach be formalized by law for judical appointments by the Mayor to the Family, Criminal, and Civil Courts.

THE POSITION OF COORDINATOR FOR CRIMINAL JUSTICE SHALL BE CREATED UNDER THE MAYOR, WITH RESPONSIBILITIES FOR OVERALL PLANNING, BUDGET INTEGRATION, AND COORDINATION OF THE CITY'S CRIMINAL JUSTICE SYSTEM. 

The administration of criminal justice in New York City suffers from the fact that there is little overall coordination or integration of the efforts of disparate agencies involved in crime-related programs. In a word, the system is fragmented. There is an acute need for comprehensive planning that provides for the rational allocation of resources among the various units, unified record keeping, and improved operational coordination.

a. b. c.

THE BOARD SHALL BE EMPOWERED TO INVESTIGATE GRIEV- ANCES OF INDIVIDUAL INMATES AND EMPLOYEES OF THE DEPARTMENT OF CORRECTION AND TO HEAR AND DETERMINE APPEALS OF ANY INMATE AGGRIEVED BY AN ACTION OF THE DEPARTMENT. 

THE BOARD SHALL HAVE SUBPOENA POWERS IN FURTHERANCE OF ITS DUTIES. 

The Board's jurisdiction under the Charter extends only to evaluation of the Department of Correction's performance; the making of recommendations; the visitation and inspection of departmental facilities and records; and the preparation of studies and reports relating to long-range capital planning, program planning, and closer cooperation of custodial, probation, and parole agencies of government (Charter, Section 626b).

Authority to Promulgate Rules and Regulations: The 

Commission proposes that the Board be empowered to promulgate rules and regulations establishing minimum standards for the care, custody, correction, treatment, supervision, and discipline of all persons confined in institutions or facilities within the jurisdiction of the Department of Correction.

This proposal reflects a logical extension of the Board's present advisory oversight efforts into binding

30. policy declarations.

The power to issue rules and regulations would enhance the developing role of the Board as an active, visible overseer of the correctional system. 

Without this power, the Board is able to function only as a goodgovernment appendage with minimal capacity to insure that its recommendations are translated into action.

The reform was specifically recommended by the Board of 

Correction and is modeled on Section 48 (6) of the State Correction Law. 

Investigation of Grievances: 

The Commission proposes that the Board be authorized to establish procedures for the effective investigation of grievances, complaints, or requests for assistance by inmates of institutions under the Department's jurisdiction, or by employees of the Department. The 

Board should also be able to institute investigations on its own motion if it reasonably believes that an appropriate subject for investigation exists.

The Board is not now formally empowered to handle and investigate complaints and grievances of individual inmates. 

As a result, investigations into individual grievances are conducted on a haphazard basis. This proposal would insure that the Board has authority to serve in an ombudsman capacity for both employees and inmates. Giving the Board "broad powers to... investigate complaints" was a specific recommendation of the report of the State Senate Committee on Crime and Correction following the 1970 riots in the Tombs.

31. 

The Charter Commission believes that it is important to formalize this ombudsman role and to enunciate not only the power but also the responsibility to carry out this mandate. 

To put it simply, the Board cannot perform its critical overview functions without authority to investigate inmate grievances, which go to the heart of the Board's substantive work.

Subpoena Power: The Commission proposes that for the purpose of ascertaining facts in connection with any study, investigation, evaluation, or other activity in furtherance of its duties, the Board be empowered to compel the attendance of witnesses; to administer oaths and to examine such persons as may be necessary; and to compel the production of books, records, documents, or papers. The Board should be empowered to bring suit in an appropriate court to enforce these powers.

Further, the Board should be able to apply to an appropriate court to obtain an order directing compliance with any rule or regulation promulgated by the Board. This would strengthen the Board's enforcement power to compel adherence to minimum standards promulgated for the institutions. The proposal is derived from the State Correction Law, Section 50(4).

Giving the Board power to enforce subpoenas is necessary; otherwise it would properly be within the authority of the Corporation Counsel to enforce a subpoena. The suggested

32. language guarantees that the Board will be able to enforce its subpoenas, notwithstanding hostility from any particular administration.

Whenever one body is charged with responsibility for evaluating the performance of another, relationships must at times be of an adversary nature. Such is the case with respect to the relationship between the Board and the Department of Correction. Consequently, the Board's access to essential information must be insured. For that reason, the 

Charter Commission believes subpoena power is essential in furtherance of the Board's objectives. Under the Charter, the Board now has the right to inspect all books, records, and documents of the Department but not to compel the production of witnesses and documents. 

The Board of Correction has proposed, in addition, that the power of subpoena be extended to any three members of the Board (as well as the full Board), to accommodate those members who are most active and effective. The Commission rejects this proposal on the ground that it constitutes an unprecedented extension of the subpoena power which could lead to abuse of this important right.

Reports or recommendations of the Board are currently submitted to the Commissioner of Correction and the Mayor. 

The Charter Commission believes, however, that wider dissemination of the Board's studies and reports would enhance its oversight efforts, strengthen its "independence," and result in greater public awareness of the Board's work. The 

Commission proposes, therefore, that the Board's reports also be submitted to the City Council for review. 

This was a specific recommendation of the report of the 

New York State Senate Committee on Crime and Correction in 1970. It is analogous to the reporting relationship between the New York State Commission of Correction and the State Legislature.

A requirement of reporting to the public and to its elective representatives is consistent with a strong, independent body of interested citizens with power of visitation and inspection of institutions.

In addition, Section 626b3 (c) of the Charter currently limits the scope of the Board's studies and reports to "methods of promoting closer cooperation of custodial, probation and parole agencies of government." This authorization should be extended to the criminal court system as well.

DISSENT OF VICE CHAIRMAN EDWARD N. COSTIKYAN 

1 The trouble with the criminal justice report is that it attempts to deal with problems far greater in scope than the Charter is capable of dealing with. It seeks to add to the melange of governmental entities with a finger in the criminal justice pie a new Charter-created coordinating device. It fails to recognize the third law of politics, that one cannot coordinate what one cannot control. Here the governmental entities to be coordinated include three within the power of the City, five independent District Attorney offices, one State-controlled agency (the courts), and one basically private agency (the legal defense system). coordination of these elements is, in my view, little more than a pious hope.

Charter-mandated 

I agree with Recommendations Nos. 1 and 2. I suggest, however, that Recommendation No. 2 could be accomplished simply by deleting from the Charter any provision for a correction system, thus forcing the State to act. 

I believe that Recommendations Nos. 3 and 4 are unlikely to produce any significant results for the reasons I have explained. In addition, I shy away from the creation of "boards," which are notably ineffective in governmental process and tend to slow down rather than accelerate the processes of government. 

As to Recommendations Nos. 5, 7, 8, 9, and 10, it seems to me that a strengthened Board of Correction, even with the 40.

41. power to subpoena and an executive director, is a Band-Aid on a tremendous disease, which is the whole penal system. 

As to Recommendation No. 6, I think we have required so many reports and recommendations to be made by City agencies that another report will get lost in the shuffle.

As to Recommendation No. 11, we already have an advisory judicial screening panel without the consent of the State 

Legislature. And I hope I don't sound cynical if I suggest that nonpartisan screening panels have not in fact taken "partisan politics out of the system by which judges are selected." Screening panels have eliminated some of the unfit, but politics continues to play a substantial role. 

I do not object in principle to formalizing the Mayor's 

Committee on the Judiciary, but I have some problem in ascertaining what it is that we are proposing. If all we are to propose is a screening panel with advisory powers, with the ultimate power of appointment remaining in the hands of the Mayor, we already have that. 

The desire of the Commission to "do something" about criminal justice is laudable, as was its desire to do something about City economy. But laudable desires do not a 

Charter make.
