The Czechoslovak Cabinet as an Indicator of Political Developments 

The purpose of this paper is to show how the structure and composition of the Czechoslovak Cabinet after World War II reflected and reflect the various phases of political trends and events in Czechoslovakia. 

Under her pre-war Constitution of February 29,1920 Czechoslovakia's Government (Cabinet) as her highest political organ was headed by a Prime Minister, one of the other members of the cabinet acting as his deputy if the Prime Minister was prevented from carrying out his office.× 

When on his way to the liberated territory of Czechoslovakia, Dr. Benes arrived in Moscow with some of the members of his London Cabinet in March 1945, the Moscow Politburo, with the enthusiastic support of the exiled Communist politicians who had spent the war in the Soviet Union, dictated to the President of Czechoslovakia not only the composition of his first cabinet on Czechoslovak soil but compelled him at the same time to change its structure.

By a constitutional decree signed by him, Dr. Sramek and five other members of the London Cabinet, in Moscow on April 2,1945 Dr. Benes had to change the Constitution. A Government Presidium was set up, consisting of the Prime Minister and his five deputies. The deputies were to be appointed and dismissed by the President of the Ropublio. The Premier and his deputies were to be the chairmen of the six political parties of the "National Front" in the Czech lands and in Slovakia. This Government Presidium wa s expected to act as a kind of inner cabinet and political directorate the Government and the National Front. of The constitutional decree also stipulated that sixteen ministries listed in its section 2 would head the State administration. Section 3 provided that in the Ministries of Foreign Affairs, National Defense and Foreign Trade there would be State Secretaries as members of the Cabinet. In case that the Minister was a Czech the State Secretary (of the respective ministry) had to be a Slovak and vice versa. This governmental setup was the result of the likewise Moscow enforced platform of the first Czechoslovak post-war Government. This platform had been prepared under the instructions and with the approval of the Kremlin by the Czechoslovak 6ommunist exiles in Moscow and was presented to Dr. 

Benes as a fait accompli. It was accepted by him and the other non-Communist politicians under duress and proclaimed after the formation of the new Cabinet in Kosice on April 4, 1945 (the so-called "Kosice Program").

Significantly the Government spokesman was not the stooge Zdenek Fierlinger who was the first Social-Democratic (!?) Prime Minister but his Czech Com-munist deputy Klement Gottwald.

This was the situation Dr. Benes had to face when the nationalization and confiscation decrees were submitted to him by the Cabinet in October 1945.

The negotiations which led to the setting up of this new cabinet were extremely difficult and protractei. The Communists, bolstered up by their election victory, insisted on retaining practically all the key ministries On the other hand offices had to be found for the representatives of the other political parties. Although the number of 16 ministries in reduced and partly depopulated post-war Czechoslovakia had already been considered excessive, two new ministries had to be created, not for administrative purposes but to provide jobs for ministers. They were the Ministry of Public Buildings and the Ministry of Unification of Laws. It is obvious that these were only minor central departments. The respective ministerial posts were filled by a member of the People´s (Catholic ) Party and a member of the Slovak Democratic Party. By allocating these less important positions to two of their partners (or rather "rivals") in the Cabinet, the Communists succeeded in weakening the non-Communist component of the governmental machinery. 

The new Cabinet had 26 members (2 ministers added, 1 State Secretary dropped). Two of the ministers (Masaryk and Svoboda) were again "nonpolitical", of the remaining 24 political members 9 were Communists and 2 of the Social Democrats were again stooges of the Communist Party (Fierlinger, and at least to some degree Lau sman), Of the balance of 13 cabinet seats the National Socialists, the People´s (Catholic) Party and the Slovak Democratic Party had each four, while the thirteenth was held by the staunch anti-Communist Social Democrat Vaclav Majer. 

In spite of the Communist victory in the elections of May 1946 the position of the non-Communists had somewhat improved. Dr. Zenkl, the chairman of the National Socialist Party, who had finally joined the Cabinet, was an energetic leader of his group. It now happened quite often that the two major Czech anti-Communist parties, the anti-Communist Slovak Democrats, and Vaclav Majer succeeded in preventing the adoption of Communist sponsored measures although the Communists could always also rely on General Svoboda, who was still formally a non-political "expert", to come to their aid. However, even together with Fierlinger and Lau sman the Communists could thus muster only 12 votes.

A personal change at the Ministry of Public Buildings occurred soon after the formation of the new (third) Cabinet when Dr. Alois Vosahlik was killed in an automobile accident. The People's Party designated Jaromir Kopecky in his stead. This appointment did not alter the political picture.

In October 1947, three months after Moscow had forbidden Czechoslovakia to join the discussions on the Marshall Plan and had made it quite clear that the country was her domain, a crisis was provoked by the Communists in the Slovak Board of Commissioners. Democratic Party.
