The dispute between the Carriers and the Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, referred to your Board for decision, is only part of a broader dispute over wages and working conditions between the Carriers and many organizations representing their employees. While I shall not attempt to review decades of collective bargaining, I believe that it would be helpful to your Board to know something of the parties' handling of this matter in comparatively recent settlements and the considerations that entered into those settlements.

Post War Lead Wage Settlement Agreements 

One of the most important differences between the railroad industry and other industries whose employees are organized along the lines of the so-called industrial union lies in the fact that under craft unionism in the railroad industry, no one organization represents a majority of railroad employees. 

As a result, the Carriers have seldom been able to bring all of the splinter groups together at one time for a single settlement of such matters of common concern as a general wage movement. Commonly, an initial or "lead" settlement is made with one or more organizations. By common acceptation, this "lead" settlement sets a floor for all further bargaining with the hold-out organizations, and commonly the Carriers voluntarily offer the terms of the lead settlement to all other organizations, at least insofar as its terms are generally applicable. The process of concluding settlements with the remaining organizations is typically long, laborious and hazardous in that the remaining organizations seek to gain advantages over the organization or organizations which have first settled, thus imperiling the lead settlement itself. Typically in this phase of the dispute, the hold-out organizations will contend that they have not been treated fairly in prior settlements, and will thus open the question of intra-industry wage comparisons. 

However, at this

-3- time I shall confine my testimony very largely to a discussion of the lead wage settlement agreements in the post-war period, and to the conditions and considerations which entered into the making of those agreements. 

During the war-time period and thereafter in the 1946 and 1947 movements, the Carriers were unable to make any voluntary lead wage settlement agreements, and emergency boards, and in one case an arbitration board, were brought in to assist in making, or to make, the initial settlement. However, in every general wage movement since that of 1947 the Carriers have succeeded in making the initial settlement on a voluntary basis that is, without the intervention of emergency or arbitration boards, whose role in this period was thus confined to determining whether the initial voluntary settlement should be applied to other organizations.

SUMMARY OF EVENTS BETWEEN 1956 AGREEMENTS AND THIS PROCEEDING 

By the time the last of the 1956 group of agreements were executed in the summer of 1957, the economic picture had vastly changed from that which prevailed in 1956 when the lead settle- . ment was negotiated. A recessionary trend was in progress and as is usually the case, the decline of the nation's railroads was more precipitous and calamitous than that of the economy as a whole. Net railway operating income which reached a near record high of $121 million in October, 1956, just prior to the initial or lead settlement, dropped to, $9 million in February 1958, while large numbers of Class I railroads were in the red, and segments of the industry faced prospects of bankruptcy. Meanwhile, the Consumers' Price Index, which apparently had waited for the Carriers to commit themselves to a wage escalator agreement, began a steady and sustained rise in total disregard of all other downward trends in the economy. By November 1, 1957 the first anniversary of the lead settlement, 84 per hour had been added under the cost-of-living escalation clause, and a total of 27.5¢ per hour had been added to the carriers' labor cost at an early stage of the recession as a result of the 1956 series of agreements. By November 1, 1959 the end of the moratorium period, cost-of-living increases totaled 16¢ per hour, and total general wage increases under these term agreements amounted to 

42.5 per hour.

In the squeeze which developed between dwindling revenues and unexpectedly increasing labor costs, the only course for sur-

vival was drastic cut-backs in improvement programs coupled with whatever reductions in payroll could be achieved with safety. 

Widespread unemployment in the railroad industry resulted and at one point the carriers considered approaching the organizations for relief from the 1956 series of agreements since far different and happier circumstances had been contemplated at the time of their execution. However, our efforts were diverted as the organizations had joined forces in Washington in an effort to improve benefits available under the Railroad Retirement Act and the Railroad Unemployment Insurance Act, despite the fact that these statutes as existing were extraordinarily generous, and in the case of railroad retirement provided far greater annuities than social security applicable to most industrial workers. The organizations' efforts in this direction, which were in the main part successful, persuaded us that the employees were totally unconcerned about the continued solvency of the railroad industry and any efforts on our part to seek relief during the moratorium period would be of no avail. In February, 1959, after considerable deliberation, the Carriers requested the operating crafts to join with the Carriers in asking the President of the United States to appoint a commission to conduct a public investigation of the rules problem. The Carriers hoped that in view of the serious condition of the industry, and the inequitable operation of the rules involved, the organizations would approach the problem with a long-term view and with a statesmanlike attitude. 

The Carriers' hopes were disappointed. The organizations

publicly stated that there was nothing in the way of featherbedding in railroad operating rules or at least nothing to speak of. They publicly claimed that operating employees were actually underpaid, and within three weeks of the Carriers' request, the Engineers served the wage notice that is before your Board. CURRENT DEMANDS PENDING ON INDIVIDUAL RAILROADS 

While it has become the custom in railroad labor relations for unions to file notices national in scope, relative to general wage increases, the organizations continue to incessantly serve a myriad number of demands on individual railroad properties covering a wide range of subjects. In fact, in recent years, system notices have increased in number and are at least as much, and probably more, of a problem to the carriers than national demands. During my explanation of recent wage agreements I mentioned the importance and effect of these local requests on the 

1956 national agreements. 

These system requests present serious cost considerations to the railroads involved, as I will later demonstrate. But in addition, history has taught us that once one railroad yields to a particular request, the issue is then carried to another line and in chain fashion, what originated as a local demand becomes a national cost problem, oftentimes of major proportions. 

Every Section 6 notice served on a property is a potential strike threat, and the number, variety, and quality of such notices are limited only by the determinations of their authors. As a result, requests for practically every type of conceivable benefit or concession have been, and are being served. I will later discuss the subject matter of typical representative system 

Section 6 notices, in detail. 

However, before doing so, I would like to demonstrate how these system demands become national problems for the entire industry.

- 

Economically, some railroads are stronger than others, and thus a strike threat to the weaker presents a greater hazard to their financial structure than would a similar threat to a more prosperous neighbor. The brotherhoods are well aware of this phenomena and use it to their advantage in the following manner. following manner.

First, a brotherhood arrives at a policy decision to seek a costly concession or benefit from the railroads in general, but because of pending national demands, which tend to delay handling of any new nationwide notices, the weapon of system demands is decided upon as the method to follow. Next, a carrier, or perhaps two or three carriers, more susceptible to economic coercion, are selected and individual Section 6 notices are served on the se properties only. 

Intimidation and coercion by way of threatened work stoppage with inevitable loss of business to competitors and financial disaster, result in the carrier or carriers succumbing. The organization, having established a precedent, then shift the battlefield to the next most likely victim, until what was only a system demand when initiated, becomes a cost reality to every railroad in the country.

There are other considerations which also make these system Section 6 notices most troublesome. Other witnesses have mentioned the constant state of rivalry, jealousy, enmity, and the bitter struggle for representation which presently pertains among the various organizations. 

A system demand served by one craft inevitably results in other organizations presenting notices soon after

similar in nature or even more extravagant in order to maintain face with its constituents, and more important, those it hope s to enlist as constituents from its rivals. Since the rank and file membership are well aware of what progress these system Section 6 notices attain, and often determine their allegiance by results achieved, the organizations consider them of prime importance. It is not uncommon for the result to be a contest between organizations, each striving to out distance the other in initiating, progressing, and eventually compelling the carriers to adopt extravagant, costly and harmful system demands, Thus, when I cite and discuss samples of system demands now pending, you can be assured that in many cases a competing organization contemplates serving similar demands or even something more drastic.

The third series of requests reproduced in the exhibit on pages 17 and 18B relate to the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy Railroad Company. As you will note, the first notice was served on September 23, 1954. The first demand requests a five-day work week not only for yard service personnel but also for local freight, suburban, transfer, mine run and work train service enginemen.

On the second page of the summary of these local demands, page 6B of the exhibit, I have listed a BofLE notice of 

April 9, 1957, served on the Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul and

Pacific Railroad Company. The complete notice is reproduced on pages 193-23B of the exhibit. This notice is not a request for a complete new agreement but would materially change many of the moré important rules as they are now written. 

The more important revisions, from a cost standpoint, involve Rules 3(b), 14, 16, 19(a), 25, 43 and new rules 1 and 2.

The Milwaukee is one of Chicago's larger suburban service carriers. As now written, Rule 3(b), the short-turn-around passenger service rule, provides for overtime payment for all service`rule, time actually on duty'or held for duty, in excess of eight hours within nine consecutive hours; and also for all time in excess of nine hours computed continuously from reporting time to

final release. The requested new rule would eliminate the eight-hours-within-nine provision and substitute a straight eight hour overtime requirement, which, considering the magnitude of the Milwaukee's short-turn-around service, would involve considerable added labor costs and additional earnings for locomotive engineers engaged in that type of service. 

The notice also contemplates changing the present initial terminal delay rules in two major respects. As now written, 1 the time period for computing both types of delay is determined by time leaving or arriving at the terminal. Revised Rule 14 would change this determinant to the time when the train passes switching or yard limits, whichever is last in the case of initial terminal delay, and whichever is first for final terminal delay. The result of such a revision would be to materially increase these arbitrary payments. Likewise the notice seeks to change assessment time from 1 hour 15 minutes in through freight service and 1 hour in passenger service to 15 minutes in the case of initial terminal delay. This simply means that under existing rules an engineer in through freight service can be on duty 1 hour and 15 minutes before initial terminal delay time begins to accrue. 

Under the revision such penalty time would start to accrue after only 

15 minutes on duty.

Other requested schedule changes all involving added costs include held away from terminal amendments, road and yard earnings quarantees, arbitraries for changing engines, making out reports, turning trains between terminals and a demand for an engineer on all diesel locomotives run or towed.

Gentlemen, the above is an infinitesimal sample of what the carriers are confronted with day after day on the local level, in addition to national wage requests of the type you are presently considering.

In conclusion, it is my opinion that local demands of the type I have just discussed involve as serious cost consequences to the carriers as do the national demands with which we are now confronted. 

This means that an industry with net income of just over 500 million dollars in 1959 is faced with pending existing employee demands estimated at a potential cost of approximately 2 billion dollars per year. 

Gentlemen, this 2 billion dollar figure can only become a reality if every pending employee demand would be granted.

In this respect the estimate is as unrealistic, as are the majority of the demands which produce that figure. However, I am being most realistic in stating that railroad labor. costs, which in 1959 absorbed 52.8% of every revenue dollar, should not and cannot be allowed to undermine and destroy. the vitality of an enterprise which presently supplies a more-than-average livelihood for over 800,000 Americans and many others in closely allied industries.
