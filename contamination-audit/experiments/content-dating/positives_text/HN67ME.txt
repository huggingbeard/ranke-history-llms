THE Story OF THE CANARY BIRD 

YING off the northwest coast of Africa are two small groups of islands, the Canary Islands, and the Islands of Madeira. These islands, and the Azores, are the native home of the wild Canary (Serinus Canarius). 

This bird is a- little green finch, resembling closely in color the tame variety known as the green Canary. 

It was introduced into Europe early in the 16th Century. A ship bound for Leghorn in Italy was wrecked off the Isle of Elba, and the little wild canaries which were liberated by this disaster were so diligently sought for by the bird catchers of that time that not one can now be found on the Island. 

The Canary has shown such adaptability to cage life that today its sweet song is enjoyed all over the world.

The breeder has brought about many changes from the original green finch to all the forms and varieties which exist today. The wild bird is only about four inches long, but some varieties are now almost double that size.

In France some years ago there were twenty-seven distinct varieties and types of Canary birds. 

In Germany there is the famous Hartz Mountain singing bird. There is the peculiar Dutch bird with his 3

feathers all turned the wrong way, there is the weak and consumptive looking Belgian and Scotch Fancy, and the beautiful English birds from the Manchester Coppy, a great crested bird, down to the bird that is called the

London Fancy, unfortunately now nearly extinct, a very beautiful bird, usually of an exquisite orange with black marking on the head and tail.

In England (where they are perhaps the greatest fanciers in the world) the bird is bred for its feather or its beauty of plumage and form, and there are hundreds of societies organized for the purpose of perfecting the standard of the particular bird that fanciers admire. 

There are shows all over the United Kingdom, two of the largest have been held at the Crystal Palace,

London, where Professional judges decided upon the quality of the birds, the prizes being very considerable. 

The majority of English fanciers are working men, who have this fancy as a hobby. 

Birds frequently sell for high prices, and at one exhibition £100 was refused for a Canary. 

In Germany the Canary has been bred exclusively for its song, and the beauty of the bird is not of so much importance. St. Andreasburg and Leipzig have the reputation of producing the very choicest singers. There are probably more birds raised in St. Andreasburg than in any other place, and here originated (a great many years ago) the St. Andreasburg Roller. But today there are as good rollers raised in Leipzig and other cities as there are in St. Andreasburg, and as a consequence, the birds are now known as "German Rollers."

It is estimated that over two million Canaries are bred annually in Germany.

Special attention is given to the cultivation and perfecting of the Canary's song, to the elimination of harsh notes and chirps, and to the production of the deep alto, bells, rolls, and trills, for which the "Rollers" are famous. 

These birds are carefully trained. Not only are the young birds trained by being put in a place where they can listen to superior singers, but certain instruments are used in piping to them the music they imitate. 

The most famous strain of the Hartz Mountain birds is what is called the "Trute strain." Some years ago a poor miner named Trute kept as a hobby quite a large number of these Hartz Mountain birds. There was a ready sale for the better singers, and this miner in his spare time trained his birds, and finally succeeded in producing a number of songsters which in a German competition swept everything before them. The “Trute strain" soon became the fancy, but while the miner never was willing to impart his secret of teaching his birds, he sold a great number at high prices. It is from them that the "Trute strain" is developed today, and the very finest singers come from the Hartz Mountains.

HIS book is written for the purpose of giving sugges- To tions to the amateur, not the professional, as to the care and breeding of Canary birds, and in the hope that by these hints some birds may possibly be saved from the fate that often overtakes them through the carelessness, over-affection or over-indulgence of their owners. CAGE

In size the cage should be medium or larger. Most prefer the medium size (about ten inches in diameter) because it is more easily cleaned and fits into the average room better. The cage may be round or square, brass or enamelled but preferably should have a zinc or other rust resisting tray and bottom. The draw tray should work smoothly and easily, and a fine mesh wire or glass guard should extend from the bottom up to a height of two or three inches around the sides of the cage. 

When introducing a bird to a new cage, see that the cage is placed away from the light and out of sight of anything that might excite the bird, for an hour or two, until he is accustomed to his new home. 

WHERE TO PLACE THE CAGE 

A bird should not be hung in a draught and while windows are bright and cheerful, they are likely to be draughty 11

and, in warm sunshine, the bird is likely to die from the heat. Direct sunshine will certainly blanch and dry the bird's feathers. Hang the cage near the window but preferably off to one side a little where the bird will not be in the direct sunshine and away from the draughts that almost invariably occur around the frame and sills, and around the window itself, particularly at the joint if it is a double hung window.

Do not hang the cage near the ceiling of a room. The best place is where there is a fair amount of light (but not direct sunshine) where draughts do not reach the bird, and in a room where the temperature is fairly even, day and night.

You may, if you wish, cover the cage with a piece of thin linen or muslin at night although this is not necessary usually if the room does not cool off rapidly to freezing temperatures. Also if the bird is kept in a room frequently lighted at night, which we do not recommend, it is best to cover his cage to reduce as much as possible the inevitable interruption to his sleep.

REMOVING THE BOTTOM OF THE CAGE 

A great many cages are made with the entire bottom removable. This is convenient when giving the bird a bath and cleaning the cage floor, adding new gravel, etc.

However, lift the top off with your hands at the bottom of the cage sides, rather than grasping the cage top by the ring, for in the latter case, the bird is usually frightened by your hand at the top and flies out the bottom. When both hands are at the bottom, however, the bird stays at the top, and hence does not escape. 12

THE CARE of a Cage 

When the cage becomes soiled, make a warm soapsuds, and wash it with a very soft cloth, drying it with an equally soft cloth, or place the cage where it can dry in the air. Never use any cleaning powder, and never scrape the cage. After being polished by the manufacturer, brass cages are covered with a hard lacquer. Little bits of verdigris would then be liable to form on the brass, and these are poisonous; also, the cage will become dull and lose its brightness. If yours is an enamelled cage, the enamel can be retouched with any good quick drying enamel if it becomes chipped. Do not place the bird back in the cage until the enamel dries. 

CATS 

A cat is a natural enemy of the canary and many other of the smaller song birds. In addition they frequently carry disease on the fur and paws. If a cat is kept in the home, be sure to hang the cage where it cannot be knocked down by the cat leaping upon it, or where it is in any way within reach of the cage. 13

SONG 

If your purchase is a German Roller, the very best results in the way of song will be procured if he is hung in a dim light, and away from where he can be annoyed by the noise and possible shrieks of other Canaries. These little creatures' vocal organs are most delicate, and soon become worn and destroyed if not properly cared for. If you want your bird to sing during the evening, cover his cage up with a shawl and keep him as quiet as you can for four or five hours in the afternoon.

It is not wise to have a bird sing too much, because if he does, his voice will soon wear, and it is possible you may have difficulty in bringing him back into the full sweetness of his song again. 

Never approach the place where your bird is with any sudden or unusual motion. He is easily frightened, and sometimes highly nervous birds are thrown into a condition where they lose their song entirely by sudden fright. 

Approach the cage slowly, raise your hand slowly and talk to the bird gently, and you will find that he will soon become accustomed to your presence. Then when you are reading or when you are sewing, you can bring the cage beside you at the table and pay no attention to him at all, and in a short while you will find that he will commence to sing by your side.

If you feed him a little bit of hemp seed on the end of your finger, he will probably come and take it. It is a strange thing, but birds like to feed from the mouth, and after a bird has taken a hemp seed from your finger, if you will place one between your lips and he takes it from you, you can rest assured that you have his confidence and probably always will have it. 19

CARE AND FEEDING 

One should adopt a program of frequent cage cleaning and we recommend that this be done daily for best results. If a generous layer of bird gravel, say 14" thick is kept on the cage floor and the perches are arranged directly over and under each other in the cage so that they will not be fouled by the bird's droppings it works out satisfactorily to clean the cage every two or three days. 

Whenever the perches are dirty they should be well scrubbed with hot water containing a little French's Bird 

Cage Wash. See that they are thoroughly dry. It is a good plan to have an extra set of perches which can be placed in the cage while the freshly washed set is drying. 

The drinking water should be changed at least once every day and every now and then the drinking cup should be thoroughly scalded. The water should be of the temperature that comes from the faucet but the chill should be taken off in the coldest part of the winter in the northern part of the United States. 

Fresh seed should be placed in the seed cup once a day.

The amount must be determined by the appetite of the bird. Endeavor to give just a little more than the bird without wasting will eat in one day-usually this is about one teaspoonful. A mixture of seeds is necessary for best results and you should be very particular to use nothing but the best and cleanest. Seed that has been wet or stored in a damp place very often becomes musty and occasionally a poisonous mustiness develops. Therefore it is best to store your seed in a glass jar provided with a tight lid, such as a Mason Jar where it will be kept dry and protected from mice and insect pests and is easily examined for mustiness. 20

Should the bird leave part of his seed in his cup at feeding time next day pour what remains from one hand to the other, blowing off the chaff which remains in the cup from the seeds which he has eaten and place the sound seeds left back in the cup. Some birds will not readily eat some kinds of seeds. This is especially noticeable when changed from one mixture to another which contains seeds with which the bird is not familiar. If you are annoyed by your bird's scattering seed on the floor of the cage, reduce the amount of seed which you have been giving him, and if the bottom of the cage is clean (as it should be) leave the seed there. Do not give him any fresh seed the following day. You can soon break him of the habit this way.

FEEDING GREEN FOODS 

One of the best green foods for birds is a little piece of sweet apple placed between the bars of the cage; chickweed and watercress are also good. Many bird owners feed lettuce which is satisfactory but must be examined carefully and any frozen parts removed. The long stalks of the rattail or plantain weed that usually grow by the wayside are very much relished and good for birds. You can get these and keep them several days by cutting off the ends and standing them in water. Before giving them to the bird cut off the ends that have stood in the water. 

Dandelions, both the flowers and the very tender green leaves are not only pleasing but very beneficial as well. 

A bird's droppings should be about 1 white. You can soon tell when his food is not what it should be by the character of his droppings. When they are too loose stop the green food. 21

ABOUT FEEDING DAINTIES 

Most people like to give their pets a treat now and then. 

Seeds, gravel, plus a little green food should always be the stock diet, although in the moulting and mating seasons and when a bird is ill special feeding may be necessary. French's Song Food and French's Bird Biscuits are treats most canaries enjoy. French's Sunshine Food containing Cod Liver Oil is also a fine treat especially during the winter months. Do not feed your bird hemp seed regularly because it is fattening and may bring on ill health. Never feed a bird sugar or anything from the table except perhaps a small sliver of boiled carrot or a piece of fresh lettuce.

BIRDS NEED LIME 

It is well to keep a Cuttle Bone in the cage at all times. 

Now and then crumble up a bit of old plaster or chalk and scatter it in the gravel-this should be done especially if the bird's bowel movements are loose. Precipitated Chalk which is obtainable at any drug store should be placed in the drinking water occasionally if the diarrhoea persists. You need have no fear of the bird taking more of it than he needs.

EXERCISE AND PLAY 

Birds in small cages need exercise. Tie a few strands of worsted to the top of the cage for your canary to tug on and sometimes dangle a small bright key or other metal object from the top of the cage. A small bell suspended in the cage will sometimes attract and hold the interest of the bird when nothing else will. 22

PERCHES 

We strongly recommend flatish oval perches. If the perch is too thin there is not sufficient girth for the bird to grasp and hold itself erect and its claws in meeting are often forced out of shape and crippled. Nor can a bird grasp a perch that is too thick. The perches should be made of pine or soft wood. 25

Make sure that the cups are clean and well-supplied with fresh seed and water. For the sake of cleanliness the vessels containing the soft food and water should be of glass or crockery, with smooth and rounded interiors. 

At least once a week give the body of the cage a thorough cleaning, as accumulations of dirt and excrement around the bars form an excellent breeding place for mites and lice.

The bird's feet should be examined to see that they are clean and healthy. The feet of birds inclined to shirk their bath often become caked with filth from the bottom of the cage, and if this condition is found to exist, the feet should be soaked in warm water, and the accumulations gently removed. It is then well to rub a little sweet oil into them.

The claws should be given attention and trimmed when necessary. (See page 47.)

A habit of feather pulling may be caused by lice, often it is caused by improper diet. (See page 49.)

Canaries like attention and excitement. Talk to them frequently. They are usually fond of music, too, and will often try to drown it with their own notes. 

If a bird sits listlessly on his perch, does not eat, and does not appear to be happy, there is something the matter.

First, investigate his droppings. These should be about one-third white, and the balance natural color, not too soft or not too hard. A bird that sleeps at night perched upon two legs, instead of upon one, will probably be found to have a bowel trouble, or something the matter with his digestion. 

Mice are especially fond of Bird Seed. At night when 26

it is quiet, they sometimes enter the room where the bird is kept and eat the seeds which have fallen on the floor. 

If the cage is hung near a curtain or other drapery, they will endeavor to reach the cage by climbing up these hangings and jumping on to the wire or cord from which the cage hangs and so obtain access to the cage. A bird is thus frightened and disturbed at a time when it should be asleep. As a result it will be drowsy in the daytime, lose its gay spirits and fail to sing. Unless prompt action is taken, you will lose your pet through loss of sleep.

Care, therefore, is necessary to see that the cage is not hung in a place where mice can get to it. In addition, your birds may starve themselves as they will not touch seed which mice have interfered with.

HOW TO MAKE EGG PASTE 

Egg paste is made by taking equal parts of both the yolk and white of hard boiled egg, mashed fine, and mixed into a paste by adding a few drops of olive oil. 

HOW TO GIVE A DOSE OF MEDICINE 

Carefully and quietly approach the cage. Open the door, hold the cage firmly with your right hand and take the bird in your left. The bird should be held firmly but gently in the palm of your hand, your index, ring and little fingers being used to keep his wings and feet down. 

This leaves the thumb and forefinger free and if you will use them to press gently on each side at the base of the bill the bird will open his beak. The medicine may then be dropped on the back of its tongue by a medicine dropper.

HOW TO TRANSPORT A BIRD 

If the bird is to be removed to another house, protect the cage thoroughly with a piece of stout wrapping paper. 

Do not permit any aperture through which a draught 28

might strike the bird, but puncture a few holes in the paper covering the top of the cage in order to provide ventilation. Never make holes in the paper which covers the sides of the cage.

HOW TO GIVE A BIRD A BATH 

A bird should be trained to take a bath in a regular bathing dish at least three times a week. Most imported birds having spent sometimes several months in the small wicker cages in which they are imported form the habit of bathing in their drinking cups and consequently have to be trained to use a bathing dish, which by the way, can be any flat-bottomed small dish which you care to use for the purpose and which you will be able to pass through the door of the cage constantly. 

In very warm weather in the summer the bird will enjoy his bath each day and suffer no harm thereby but in the winter it is well to allow the bird a bath only two or three times a week and then only in mornings so that the bird has plenty of time to thoroughly dry before going to sleep. The water in the bird bath may be cool, but not cold. 

Never force your bird to bathe; if he persists in bathing from his drinking cup it is well to avoid filling the cup completely in order to encourage him to take advantage of the bathing dish.

How To SOAK SEED 

Place one teaspoonful of seed per bird in water and allow it to stand overnight. In the morning rinse it in two or three clear waters and dry up as much of the excess moisture as you can with paper towels and place the 29

seed in the seed cups. Renew all soaked seed given daily, for soaked seed spoils quickly.

COLOR FEEDING OF CANARIES 

It has long been the custom for bird-fanciers to add to the bird's food during moulting certain substances which will give to the bird's plumage colors ranging from deep yellow to orange or red. The R. T. French Company is now supplying for the purpose a product which can be used in the bird's food during moulting. In this way, one can in a perfectly harmless way, give many beautiful shades to the plumage.

To do this one must start feeding French's Color Food at the beginning of the moult. The food is prepared as follows: To one hard boiled egg add two or three crackers and a half-teaspoonful of French's Color Food. Mix well together with two or three drops of olive oil to form a paste, and supply each bird with a teaspoonful of the mixture daily. Remove the uneaten portions each morning, and give a fresh supply. After the first week, gradually increase the amount of the Color Food until the desired color is reached. The amount may then be gradually decreased until the end of the moult, when it should be discontinued. French's Moulting Food and French's 

Color Food in equal amounts, moistened with a drop or two of olive oil is a very effective mixture and is relished by canaries.

The English have been most successful in coloring their canaries a rich orange color, sometimes almost to a red, by the use of this Color Food, or similar articles. The way it is done in England is, when the birds are first hatched the parents themselves are fed this food-egg, with a 30

little bread, and milk, mixed together, with the milk carefully squeezed out, so as to leave a dry, crumbly substance. This is dredged with this crimson Color Food and the birds are fed on it, and when the young birds' feathers begin to come, they show the result of this feeding in being a rich orange shade. When they begin to feed themselves, after they are old enough to be separated from the parents, they should be given no food for a long while, excepting soft food, which contains a considerable amount of the Color Food, which is absolutely harmless, being only a powdered vegetable substance.

After the color is firmly set, no further attention need be paid to them until the bird moults again, and then during each moult he must be fed Color Food provided you wish to maintain the color, otherwise, the old colors will bleach, and the new feathers will come in their natural color.

Once or twice a week, a small portion of flaxseed should be mixed with the regular seed. Other delicacies should be withheld, as they may induce the birds to neglect their Color Food. Use great care to exclude draughts. Avoid keeping the birds in a strong light, as this may fade the tints you are trying to obtain. Breeding CANARIES 

Mus S HAS already been said, this little book is written Afor the benefit of the amateur, and if the suggestions which are made here are followed, with the aid of a little common sense, there is no reason why anyone should not have a successful season and raise, from one pair of birds, from six to fifteen young.

Wild birds when they nest seek seclusion and quiet, and many, if disturbed, and made to leave their eggs, will desert the nest entirely. Although Canaries have been bred for years in cages, yet the birds will do very much better if kept quiet and free from disturbance. 

There is no greater pleasure to those who love these little creatures, than watching them during the time when they are raising their young, and although it requires considerable care and a little judgment, breeding them is something that any one may do. 

THE MATING SEASON 

The spring of the year is, of course, the time when birds mate and St. Valentine's Day is said to be the day when they plight their troth. This may be in the wild state, but in breeding in cages, which is in a measure artificial, a little depends upon the climate. Birds that are brought forward to lay and mate too early are liable to have their eggs and their young chilled, and while the birds may be allowed to see each other and know each other early in 32

February or in March, it is better for them not to mate and commence their nests until the thermometer gets somewhere around 50° out-of-doors. In such a climate as in New York City, that would be from the middle of 

March to the first of April, as the season is cold or mild.

BREEDING CAGE AND NESTS 

The best cage for raising Canaries is a metal one of box shape, with a sliding partition in the middle. The size most favored by experience has a length of about 2 ft. 

4 in., a depth of 14 in., and a width of 10 in. At the bottom there must be a drawer. A cage of this description can be bought at most bird-fanciers' stores. Nothing better can be had with a view to safe and convenient rearing. The nest boxes can be hung in the middle of the cage between the two perches, so that the top of the nestbox is level with the perches. The nesting material should consist of specially prepared nesting hair and moss. 

French's Nesting Hair is ideal for the purpose. Care and attention in matters like these will ensure the comfort of the birds in building their nests.

SELECTING THE BIRDS 

In the selection of a pair it is well that one should be yellow and the other buff. The cock bird should be a strong, healthy singer and the hen should be young and characterized by liveliness and sprightliness. For best results neither of the birds should be over five years of age. Put the cock on one side of the partition, and the hen on the other.

BREEDING SEASON 

By the middle of March, a wire partition should be sub- 33

stituted for the wooden one, so that the birds can see each other. A little niger seed or a few dandelion leaves, (if procurable) may then be given to the hen two or three times weekly at the beginning of the breeding season.

These are preventives of egg-binding, and tend to bring the bird into breeding condition. 

From the forepart of March, a hard-boiled egg mashed up with one or two unsalted soda crackers of the Uneeda 

Biscuit type, one drop of Olive Oil, and a few Poppy

Seeds (Maw) can be given to the birds daily. 

It is well, also, to sprinkle some finely ground shells or old mortar with the gravel daily as a preventive of soft-shelled eggs; or, alternately ordinary hen's eggshells, dried and ground up, may be used. (To avoid contamination by poultry diseases, eggshells, if used, should be dried in a hot oven 1½ hour.)

About April 1st the wire slip can be drawn so that both birds can run together. If this mutual acquaintance on their part should lead to a quarrel, do not be alarmed; they will soon get friendly.

At this time it is a good thing to clip the hen's claws to prevent possible injury to the eggs during incubation. 

See page 47 for directions.

The breeding cage should be placed in a room free from draughts and the nesting materials should be placed in the cage, little bits of wool, cows' hair, Southern moss, and odds and ends of that sort will suffice. WHEN THE FIRST EGG Is Laid 

Don't be in a hurry for the hen to lay. That can be expected only at the proper moment. No matter how much you worry about it, she will produce her egg at her own time and no sooner. When you think the favorable moment is near at hand have ready a dummy egg, a small marble or a ball of painted wood the size of a canary egg. As soon as the first egg is laid take it out of the nest very carefully and replace it with one of the dummy eggs aforementioned. Remove the second and third eggs likewise. The eggs should be removed with a teaspoon and placed in a dish or small box containing a bit of nesting material or bran. Turn the eggs daily. As a general rule the bird will lay an egg every morning, until her clutch, consisting of from two to five eggs, is completed.

When the third egg is laid, place all three of the eggs in the nest and let this be done in the afternoon. When the hen takes to the nest entirely, the birds should again be divided off by the wire partition and the hen's bath withheld for the first seven days while she is sitting. After the eleventh or twelfth day, allow the hen to bathe dailyif the weather is fairly warm-as the moisture from her feathers tends to soften the egg shells aiding the hatchlings to pick their way out. The bath water should be room temperature.

On the thirteenth day a little soft food should be given to the hen bird. On the fourteenth day, take the nest box down to see if the hen has hatched any of her eggs.

If she has, soft food should be given three times a day at intervals of three hours. This must be fresh, however, and under no circumstances should the food be used if sour. 39

AFTER THE HATCHING 

Give the young birds soft foods exclusively for the first few days, perhaps a week. By soft foods is meant French's 

Nestling Food, a little fresh bread dipped in scalded milk, and the Yolk, ONLY, of a hard boiled egg with which a few finely ground cracker crumbs have been mixed. French's Bird Seed should be fed as usual but omit the Bird Biscuit in favor of Nestling Food for the first six weeks. At the end of perhaps a week in addition to the soft foods, each day give each little bird one-half teaspoonful of French's Sunshine Bird Food and some

As the little birds gain in vitality, gradually increase the amount of cracked seeds given and decrease the soft foods until they are about six weeks old after which time they are able to crack their own seeds and may be placed on a whole seed diet such as that enjoyed by older birds in good health.

When the little birds have been hatched six or seven days, their eyes will have opened and when they are around 10 days to 2 weeks old the partition can be drawn out and the cock bird allowed to rejoin the hen. 

He and she will unite in feeding the young. However, if you notice that the hen is not feeding the young as she should, or if she doesn't stay on the nest sufficient to keep the chicks warm, the cock should be allowed to return at once. If both of the birds neglect to feed the young ones, it will be necessary that you do so yourself. 

Pick the food up on the end of a soda straw or small stick and drop it into the open mouths of the little birds. Continue to do this until they are able to take care of themselves. Sometimes the hen neglects feeding the young, 40

because of a lack of green food in her diet, and when this is given she resumes the feeding. 

When the birds are a month old, they may be put in a separate cage from their parents. However, if the hen takes to the nest entirely a second time before the birds reach the age of one month, they should be separated from her immediately and if necessary placed with the cock until the second nest is due to hatch. 

When the little birds are about sixteen days old, the hen will probably show signs of wanting to go to nest again. You must, therefore, place another nest, some nesting material or fresh moss in the compartment, but away from the young birds. Be sure that sufficient material is supplied; otherwise, if the hen runs short she will not hesitate to pull the feathers of the young birds. When the hen again lays her first egg, it should, as in the former case, be removed. When she has laid her third, all three should be replaced in the nest. As in Round One, when the hen takes to her nest entirely, the cock should be separated from the hen, her bath withheld for seven days, and when she hatches her second nest of eggs, the same routine should be adopted as with the first nest. 

Three or four nests of young birds can be hatched but many breeders have best results by not allowing more than three nests a season.

At the age of eight weeks French's Color Food should be given to the young birds and continued until they have finished their moult. Young birds hatched from

Rounds 2, 3, and 4 should be treated in the manner already described. The old birds should be placed on 

French's Color Food the second week of July. You will then be assured of perfectly colored birds. 41

DEAD BIRDS 

Keep on the lookout for any dead birds, that may be in the nest after hatching. Be sure to remove them.

TROUBLE TO WARD OFF 

It is most essential never to handle hen birds while they are breeding, but before breeding always give the bird a thorough dusting with French's Bird Lice Powder. Take care to dust under the wings and around the neck, making sure that the powder penetrates to the skin. Before replacing the eggs after the third one has been laid, dust the nest also with Bird Lice Powder.

EGG-BINDING 

Another serious trouble, and one that is not at all uncommon, is to find a hen that you expect to lay again, trembling and crouching in a corner of the cage, suffering from what is commonly called "egg-binding." She should be attended to at once. Take the hen very gently in your left hand and put three or four drops of olive oil in her beak. In serious cases put the same amount in the vent, using a flight or tail feather for this purpose. (If you do not happen to have a spare feather for this purpose, clip one of the long wing feathers with a pair of scissors but do not pull it out.) Replace the bird gently in the cage, the corner of which should be well covered with moss, etc., keeping the surroundings fairly warm.

After a short time, the egg will be safely passed. The best prevention is to feed the hen dandelion leaves, watercress or boiled carrot at least three times a week while the birds are breeding. A small piece of pork lard on the end of the perch is also useful especially on a cold day. 42

Diseases AND OTHER OF CANARIES 

CAGED BIRDS

S A general thing, the diseases most usually contracted by Canaries are due either to improper feeding, resulting in indigestion and kindred troubles, or to a cold which may result in asthma, consumption, etc. The means of preventing these troubles have already been mentioned, but, even with the best of care, a bird will occasionally become ill, and some of the more frequent diseases with the methods of treating them are therefore detailed.

ASTHMA 

This is usually caused by a chill, and is aggravated by impaired digestion. As in man, it attacks the bird periodically, and may be distinguished by the peculiar wheezing sound which the bird makes. The disease must be taken in hand at once, as it is sure to result seriously if not treated. Keep the patient in a warm, dry place, partly covering the cage on damp, foggy, or chilly days, and feed only on French's Bird Seed and Biscuit, with about a teaspoonful of an egg-paste given fresh daily. 

Mix in about as much red pepper as can be picked up on a ten cent piece. A dose of Bird Tonic should be given in the drinking water, which should be warm. If the wheezing is severe, make an infusion of flaxseed by soaking a teaspoonful of unground flaxseed in a cupful of 44

boiling water, and allowing it to stand several hours. 

Pour off the water and place it in the drinking cup for the bird to drink. 

The bird should not be permitted to sing at night.

Bathe the nostrils with tepid water containing a little boric acid, and add three drops sweet spirit of nitre, four drops glycerine, and six or seven drops lemon juice to the drinking water.

BALDNESS 

This may be due to Lice, Old Age or General Debility. 

Make sure that your bird is entirely free from Lice. (See page 50) and if he does not have these pests, treat him as follows: Daily give him one teaspoonful of French's Bird 

Seed and sprinkle 1 to 4 of a teaspoonful of French's

Bird Charcoal on the floor of the cage with the Bird Gravel. See to it that he has plenty of green food and keep a piece of Cuttle Bone in his cage. Cover a five-cent piece with Epsom Salts and dissolve it in a cup of hot water. When cool, give it to him in place of ordinary drinking water. This should be done daily for ten days. 

Anoint bare patches daily with French's Bird Ointment. Before applying this, give a thorough dusting with 

French's Bird Lice Powder, rubbing it in well with the tips of the fingers but not pressing too hard. Add eight or ten drops of French's Bird Tonic to his drinking water daily until he is normal again. 

BRONCHITIS 

This is usually the result of neglected cold, and is attended by feverishness, restlessness and a dry cough, with 45

a rattling noise in the throat. The inflammation causes the bird to drink often, and it is well to give it the flaxseed tea mentioned under Asthma. Keep the bird warm and free from draughts, and give 8 to 10 drops of French's

Bird Tonic in the drinking water, or in flaxseed tea. 

COLD OR CATARRH 

This is the result of a chill, and is usually brought to notice by the bird sneezing and sitting with its feathers puffed up. It must be attended to at once, as it leads to asthma, bronchitis and consumption. Keep the patient in a warm room, free from draughts. Give flaxseed tea in place of ordinary drinking water, and add to it 10 drops of Bird Tonic. Feed on Bird Seed and Biscuit, with a teaspoonful of egg-paste given fresh each day. A very good remedy for colds is 1 drop of tincture of aconite and 6 drops of glycerine thoroughly mixed in the drinking water daily until the bird is well. 

CONSTIPATION 

Give the bird plenty of green foods, sweet apple, chickweed, watercress, etc. Add a few drops of castor oil to the egg-paste, and give fresh daily. Sprinkle 4 teaspoonful of French's Bird Charcoal with the Bird Gravel, daily.

CONSUMPTION 

This is the result of neglected colds. The bird gradually wastes away, and unless treated quickly will soon die. 

Use the same precautions as in cases of colds or bronchitis, with special attention to warmth and a nourishing diet. In addition to French's Bird Seed and Biscuit, give the egg-paste, to which may be added a few drops of cod- 46

This may be caused by the bird having been confined in a cage which is too small or not properly cleaned, or by use of bad perches. In such cases it attacks the limbs. A very good remedy is to bathe the legs in warm water containing a little Colman's Mustard; give the bird three drops of olive oil, using a wooden skewer or bodkin needle.

When the cramp is in the stomach, it is generally due to the bird having eaten something that does not agree with him. For relief, give 3 drops only of olive oil, and place a small lump of gum arabic in the drinking water. 

An excellent food for the bird at this time is a small piece of white bread which has been dipped in milk that has been boiled and allowed to cool. Squeeze it quite dry before giving it to the bird. It must be sweet, and under no circumstances, must it be permitted to remain in the cage long enough to become sour.

CUTTING CLAWS 

The claws should be given attention, as, if not trimmed, they grow to abnormal length, and prevent the bird from perching comfortably. Trim with a small finger nail clipper. To perform this little operation, take the bird firmly in the left hand, holding its wings down, and between your third and fourth finger draw out the leg. 47

This frequently results from eating stale or sour food, or unripe or decayed green food, or even bad or stale water. 

Give the patient one drop of castor oil on the back of its tongue—but no more. Place a small lump of gum arabic in the drinking water. For food give a piece of cracker soaked in fresh milk. INFLAMMATION OF THE BOWELS

MOULTING 

Moulting is not a disease, but the future health of the bird depends largely upon the care given it during this period. 

Whatever weakness the birds may have is sure to show up at this time, and unless particular care is given, they are likely to come through it with some troublesome or incurable ailment.

Moulting usually starts in July and lasts until November. The first symptoms to be noticed are a general lassitude and drowsiness, a sort of tired-out appearance. 

The bird will frequently hunt about in its seed-cup or at the bottom of the cage, as though searching for something it cannot find. In a few days you will probably find a few loose feathers in the bottom of the cage, and you may then feel assured that moulting has commenced. 52

Unusual precautions must be taken against draughts, as a chill may stop the moult and kill the bird. On the other hand, fresh air and sunshine are essential. Give baths freely but only in the mornings of the warmest days and do not give the bath near an open window. 

Keep a Bird Biscuit in the cage, and add 8 to 10 drops of French's Bird Tonic to the drinking water occasionally. Use only French's Air-Washed Bird Seed to which it is well to add a small amount of flaxseed. Once or twice a week give a little egg-paste or French's Moulting 

Food and one-half teaspoonful of French's Sunshine Bird 

Food twice weekly. A piece of sweet apple will be enjoyed and beneficial. A small piece of boiled carrot is also good. Give little or no other green food, except possibly a small piece of lettuce, taking care to cut away the thick part of the stalk.

Sometimes, even with the best of care a canary does not return to full song after the moult, although otherwise apparently in good health. Give the "Loss of 

Voice" treatment, page 52, and encourage him to sing by playing a radio, victrola, piano or by whistling or humming softly to him.

The observance of these hints will usually bring the bird safely through the moult, unless some weakness is brought to light and aggravated by the moulting process. 

ESCALY LEGS 

This is a condition that ordinarily afflicts only the older birds. Take the bird in the left hand and bathe the legs in warm, soapy water for a minute or so. Then wipe them dry and apply French's Bird Ointment, gently rubbing it in with the finger and thumb, and taking care no 53

ointment adheres to the feathers. Replace the bird in the cage without wiping off the ointment. An alternative method is to smear the legs with paraffin, using a small paint brush. One or other of these treatments should be repeated for two or three days, until the dirt and germs which have accumulated under the scales have been released. Then take the bird, and with the back of a penknife rub the scales very gently up from the under side, and it will be found that most of them will come off, but don't press too hard or you will cause the tender skin to break and the legs to bleed, which should not occur if tender care is used. A very gentle pressure is all that is necessary, provided the treatment has been sufficient. If all the scales do not come off, the process may be continued. When they are gone, or almost gone, continue to grease the leg daily for a few days until it is healed. 

SETTING BROKEN LEGS 

When a bird's leg is broken remove all the high perches and swing from the cage and place it in a quiet, dimly lighted room where the bird will be away from all excitement and not teased or frightened in any way. If the leg is broken between the joints, take the bird in your hand and gently pull the leg away from the body until the ends of the bone meet. Carefully press them together and place a splint of toothpicks or a quill which has been split open around the leg. The splint should then be bound in place with a thread but care should be taken not to bind it too tightly otherwise circulation will be stopped. Allow the splint to remain untouched for a period of three weeks and then remove it entirely. It is impossible to set a broken joint. 54

GOLDFISH 

In his experimental tanks of both Goldfish and Tropicals, the writer has always endeavored to approximate ideal home conditions. Four simple "Don'ts" will cover the essential Goldfish needs:

Don't crowd your fish. For each inch of Fish in the tank, exclusive of heads and tails, have about eighteen square inches of water surface. Oxygen is absorbed by the water at the surface and the rate of absorption is in direct proportion to the amount of surface. A ten gallon jug completely filled to the neck can absorb oxygen at only a small fraction of the rate of the water in a ten gallon straight-sided tank.

Don't overfeed. Any food remaining uneaten in thirty minutes should be removed with a siphon or dip tube.

Don't allow sudden temperature changes. Any water added to the tank to maintain the proper water level should be the same temperature as the water in the tank. 

Keep the tank in a room where the temperature is fairly constant, especially in winter.

Don't place new fish or aquatic plants in the tank immediately as they may be diseased. Keep the fish to themselves for a week in a separate tank and observe them. New plants should be washed carefully before being placed in your aquarium. 60

TROPICAL FISH 

It may be surprising to some who are familiar with seeing only goldfish in an aquarium to learn that now there are approximately 100 varieties of tropical fish obtainable. They will never completely replace goldfish but are at least equally popular with thousands of aquarium fanciers in all parts of the country. 

As a rule Tropical Fish require warmer water than Goldfish and if it is possible, keep the temperature of the water at 70 to 75 degrees, night and day. Tropicals require less oxygen than Goldfish but here again too much crowding is harmful. Planting the aquarium with aquatic plants such as sagittaria, vallisneria, and the floating anacharis and riccia is essential for properly balancing the aquarium and providing a means of breeding and rearing both the livebearing and the egglaying species. 

In feeding Tropicals, use a regular tropical fish food, giving a small pinch twice a day and occasionally feed a few white worms, which may be purchased from almost any dealer, or small amounts of finely chopped salmon or shrimp.

Diseases of these fish are due chiefly to the limited knowledge, carelessness and inattention of the owner. 

Minor accidents, chills and improper feeding are also contributing factors.

When a fish is sick it can be told by its listlessness and the carriage of its fins. A healthy fish has its fins well spread and an ailing fish droops its dorsal and contracts the caudal fin. Remove sick fish immediately.
