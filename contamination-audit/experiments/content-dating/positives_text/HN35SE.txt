DIGEST OF FINAL RECOMMENDATIONS 

The purpose of this preliminary report to the Board of Education is to present in very condensed but definite form a sufficient report to provide the Board with the essential basis for budgetary action. This is in accordance with the specifications of the survey. While the date of this preliminary report is somewhat later than would have been preferred by both the Survey Staff and the Board, the delay has been necessitated both by the failure to seal the arrangements for the survey as early as had been originally contemplated, and by other delays incident to such things as continued :: meetings with the Advisory Committee, prolonged periods of conferences with individuals within the school system, and other factors quite common to survey undertakings of such large and complicated scope. In the course of many conferences and meetings with the 

Advisory Committee at which members of the Committee of the Board of Education on the Survey were present, extensive data and reasoning have been presented by the Survey Staff to the end that a rather complete understanding is present on the part of members of the Board of Education and of the Advisory Committee both as to the deep-seated problems which lie in the Hartford Salary situation and the facts upon which the Survey Staff has depended in arriving at its conclusions. There have also been extensive presentation and discussions of the fundamental principles underlying good school salary policy. 

At the outset it should be made clear that the meetings with the Advisory Committee have been most helpful to the Survey Staff in formulating its recommendations and in revising some of the tentative recommendations which have been previously presented.

The Survey Staff is very grateful for the continued interest and thought and advice of the Advisory Committee both as a whole and on the part of the individuals comprising it. The Board of Education through its individual members, the administrative and teaching staff of the school system, the individual members of the Advisory Committee, and other citizens have contributed greatly to the final development of salary recommendations on the part of the Survey Staff. It should be noted that the tentative recommendations themselves which have already been presented bore the imprint of the thinking and advice of the Advisory Committee and others mentioned above. The Survey Staff has throughout the course of its work been guided not alone by the many facts that it has accumulated but also by the advice and thinking of people, both teachers and lay-citizens. 

The tentative recommendations themselves bore this mark. The final recommendations of the Survey Staff represent some changes from tentative proposals but it should be borne in mind that at every point the recommendations herein set forth have been weighed systematically against the suggestions of the Advisory Committee. In the material to be presented in the following pages the Survey Staff will present its points as briefly as possible, reserving complete treatment for the final report. Most of our

attention will be reserved in this preliminary report for a presentation of the final recommendations concerning instructional and administrative salaries.

In the four extended meetings with the Advisory Committee and in many conferences, the nature and the importance of salary policy both to the schools and to the city have been continuously kept in the forefront. Throughout the course of the survey the emphasis has been on principles and facts, conditions and how best to meet them. The Staff has sought real understanding of the 

Hartford salary situation and its background especially the history of the problems and local policies in this area at the time of and since the Strayer Survey of 1936. The local situation has been viewed, also, against the backdrop of developments in school salary policy in American cities and school systems generally. 

As must have been evident, there has been no neglect of the general economic situation the high spirals of prices in the net of 

which school workers find themselves.

The Ultimate Criterion 

Above all, from the first session with the Board one thing has been so repeatedly stated as to leave no room for doubt: the ultimate test set by the Survey Staff has been to design salary schedules which, within the framework of fiscal reasonability and economic justice to personnel, would best promote the educational growth of Hartford children and youth.

It must have been apparent to the members of the Board, to the school personnel, and to the Advisory Committee, as it was to our Staff, that during the course of the survey deliberations there were manifest some cross currents of opinion and advice. 

While there was a general unity of purpose apparent in the Advisory Committee's thinking, there was considerable diversity in the suggestions of specific purposes and detailed measures. 

These diversities have been both enlightening and confusing, as have similar expressions commonly in other projects of a like nature elsewhere. They have been enlightening because they have represented kinds of data to be considered, and because they have been sincere expressions based on varying degrees of familiarity: and experience with the intricacies of the salary problem. They have been confusing because they have sometimes been extreme and because they have indicated differences in points of view which are often tied up with considerations somewhat extraneous to the central issue expressed by our ultimate test the best for the schools, the best for the boys and girls, and the best for the future of Hartford itself. 74

The 

In the circumstances, your consultants have done just what, for example, every good Board of Education should do.

Survey, not unmindful of the diversities, has grappled with the considerations comprising the central core of the issue, and not the extraneous elements. It has taken a straight path which is an educational path. It is a path which, according to our understanding of the facts, fiscal adequacy in Hartford has cleared, and one which leads not only to economic justice for teachers and administrators, but also promises to be best for boys and girls. 

In other words, the Survey has not compromised with the ultimate criterion. The limit of our own compromise has to be what the real educational needs impose. This is on the theory that the good education undergirds the good city. If our recommendations come out somewhere in between the extremes, it is not because a compromise was purposed; it is because according to our best judgment of the facts, both in and confronting schools, the Plan for Action here recommended is the best one we can now devise for Hartford School Salary Policy. This seems to the Staff to be what the specifications called for.

An Adjustable Basic Salary

There are two factors which have had important influence upon teachers salaries during the past decade. The first was the obvious need to raise the level of teachers salaries if better trained, abler people, were to be recruited to the profession, The second was the necessity for adjusting salaries to rapid changes in our economy. Basic salaries have been basic in name only. Boards of Education have had to wrestle with salary policy each year and sometimes oftener. The constant necessity for revising and patching salary schedules has taken much valuable time from teachers, administrators, and board members, which time might have been devoted better to the needs of boys and girls in the community.

Traditional Step-Type Schedule Not Recommended: 

The It is possible for teachers to be at any point in the range between minimum and maximum for their classification. traditional steps on the schedule have been purposely avoided. 

It is almost impossible to avoid the feeling among many teachers and laymen that on the "Step Type" schedule each step is related to years of teaching experience. Our research and that of others indicates that there is too little relationship between teaching efficiency and experience beyond the first few years of a teacher's service to justify the equating of salaries year by year in terms of experience.

Scheduled Provision for Special Competence Recognition 

One of the specifications for this survey called for a consideration of the question: "Should there be provision for special recognition of competence or merit: if so, at what stage in the schedule; what would be included in competence or merit; how recognized and how administered; extent and method of teacher participation in merit decisions; plan of appeal from decisions on this and other salary matters?"

This leads to the prescription of a single basic schedule with conditional increments which will carry any teacher who does satisfactory service from a decent minimum to a rewarding maximum. Implicit in this thinking is the fact that it is necessary for the school to have a well-established concept of what constitutes satisfactory teaching, and that no teacher who does not meet the requirements of this first basic level shall be retained in service.

Above or in addition to this first level of competence the basic level of satisractory work it is proposed that there shall be established pioual parallel scales of payment for higher levels of adjudged corpetence, open to all who aspire to improve themselves professionally and who have the capabilities required for such classifications. These parallel competence scales will carry the more competent teacher salarywise more rapidly upward toward higher maximum figures, Such figures may well be more than the public is prepared to pay to the less able teacher, but what the public must pay to the outstanding.

Specialized Positions Classified as Teaching 

The standard salary range, with its provisions for adjustment for economic conditions, and with opportunities within the total salary policy for the recognition of special competence, should serve as broadly as possible for all positions which though somewhat specialized are basically equivalent in type and requirements to the regular teaching classification. In turn, the schedule should be good enough to make this feasible. It is also better to vary the load assigned to a position, where necessary than to differentiate salaries. 

Recommended: It is recommended that the following positions be classified in salary under the instructional schedule: 

Psychological Examiners, Testing Staff, Speech and Hearing Teachers*, Visiting Teachers*, Remedial Reading Teachers, Head Teacher of Home Instruction*, Teacher-Certificated Attendance 

Officers, School Nurses, Dental Hygienists (if of Bachelor's degree status or better), Physical Education Teachers, Librarians,

Welfare Worker, Opportunity Class* and Coaching Teachers*, Gui dance Teachers and Counselors*, Penmanship Consultant.

Positions starred (*) now carry differentials of $100-$200 above regular teaching positions. It is not recommended that these amounts for present staff be taken away in placement on the new schedule, but that the maximums for each preparational class in the new schedule be applied to the above specialized positions, with the exception that if initial placement in any case carries a position over the established maximum the salary thus placed shall remain for the particular present incumbent. Substitutes

It is recommended that Continuing Substitutes, those regularly employed for a term or more to take the place of a regular teacher temporarily on leave, be placed on the standard schedule for teachers (BLS 150) at not less than $2475 and not more than $3000, and be eligible for the economic-level adjustments in the schedule, i.e. in 1949, BLS 170.

It is recommended that Temporary Substitutes, those substituting day by day and for short irregular periods, be paid at the flat rate of $10 a day.

Administrative Salaries 

This Survey has studied with great detail the salaries of administrative and supervisory personnel. The Survey has considered the existing classifications and the positions; its recommendations are not based on personal incumbency but they are applicable thereto. Generally speaking, however, it can be said that the fundamental positional classification existing seems in accord with recognized good practice; that two classes of central, system-wide, staff below the level of assistant superintendent, namely director and supervisor, seem desirable; and that in general two classes seem sufficient. The present salary scales show too much congestion among the director and supervisor grades. 

Survey cannot wholly and at once rectify this past accumulation, but its proposals will do so in time. The 

Hartford's school administrative salaries in general are too low by high-grade standards and in relation to the strategic importance of modern educational leadership. It is going to be of increasing necessity that administrative and supervisory salaries be raised to higher levels, if skilled leadership based on scholarly qualities, experience, broad knowledge, human understanding and advanced technical preparation is to be recruited to keep in pace with the problems and needs of the school system. in this day and age.

Hartford tends to recruit its administrative and supervisory staff-- both system-wide and in schools-- in the opinion of the Survey, somewhat too extensively from within the system. 

This is a dangerous local trend to be on the alert against, even though the practice is comfortable and to a degree has merit.

PART TWO ANALYSIS OF THE SURVEY PROBLEM 

In effect, the Digest, previously presented, constitutes Part One of this Report. This Part will deal with salary background, principles and procedures, and with a summary of factual data relating to the personnel.

1. THE BACKGROUND OF SCHOOL SALARY POLICY IN HARTFORD 

The educational progress of a school system may often be read from the history of the community's school salary policy. 

Where school salary arrangements approach a reasonable form of adequacy, growing in stature with conditions, and demonstrating withal a proper consideration of the prudential principle, educational developments in that system are likely to proceed apace. 

Conversely, educational regression nearly always accompanies the payment of inadequate salaries, or ill-arranged salary policy. The two, educational progress and realistic salary policy, are inseparable. Today's leaders in the field of education recognize that the salary schedule comprises one of the most vital single elements in school fiscal matters because of its far-reaching effect upon the quality of recruitment and performance. They know that the boys and girls of the community are the real recipients, through better teaching and better educational services, of the ultimate benefits of any sound, adequate, durable, and flexible salary plan. 

Over the past score of years, Hartford's educational ascendency may be traced to those periods during which a concentrated effort was made to meet sehool salary needs. The lack of a parallel ascension in other years coincides with a laissez-faire attitude on the part of the city toward the school salary problem. The alert citizen and the interested parent will not miss the significance of this observation. In its light they will perceive that the salary policy is more than a plan for paying school personnel; if competently designed, it becomes a practical, potent instrument for furthering professional objectives.

While Hartford's educational leadership has been somewhat retarded by moments of salary inertia, there has been a distinct, albeit irregular, upgrading of salaries and, it is believed, growth in educational efficiency in the city's schools since the era when the independent school districts were consolidated (1934). Indeed, a start was made earlier. The result has been that Hartford has tasted some of the fruits of positive educational leadership. 

Having one attained such a position, the community comes face to face with the fact that the school system cannot afford NOT to pay adequate salaries today. Cannot, that is, unless it is willing to relinquish its educational gains and content itself with a substandard education for its children.

In order that the salary situation may be clarified for the period under consideration, it seems advisable to summarize here the salient features of the Pre-Strayer, the Strayer-Elsbree, and the Post-Strayer eras.

Minutiae which have previously appeared in published reports, are purposely omitted.

22. 

Pre-Strayer Some of the inconsistencies that prevailed in the different salary schedules under the independent district plan persisted for some time after the consolidation was effected. For example, in the positional type schedule then employed, there had been no special classification for junior high teachers: instead, the salary of such a teacher depended upon the previous teaching status of the individual. Some simplification of salaries resulted from the consolidation, however, such as a single wage scale for all elementary teachers.

Although elementary salaries were made uniform at the time of the consolidation, salary levels were generally quite low in relation to those paid in forward communities. Only the salaries paid high school personnel were comparable to those of neighboring cities.

Strayer-Elsbree Through the introduction of a single salary, preparational type schedule, closely keyed to the previously existent senior high salary figures, the Strayer study (1937) materially improved the levels of elementary and junior high salaries. Of course, those teachers with advanced preparation were raised to the higher ranges, while those with two years or less preparation beyond high school were only upgraded slightly. The gamut of basic salaries ranged from a $1200 minimum for less than two years preparation to $4000 maximum for those (almost none) with doctors' degrees.

In addition, the Strayer-Elsbree Schedule rectified many of the earlier inequities and provided a sound basic minimum-maximumincrement structure for the period at which it was devised. Upon this basic single-salary plan was superimposed a system of differentials for principals and supervisors, while other administrative officials were assigned discrete schedules. 

The Strayer-Elsbree plan carried some incentive for the further educational growth of Hartford teachers through the higher salary scales for advanced levels of training. Then, too, the improved elementary salaries began to attract teachers to Hartford, thereby placing the administration in a favorable position to compete for the educational services of outstanding personnel. The net result was that Hartford began to assume a position of educational leadership at all grade levels and other communities were to examine and imitate Hartford practices, including her salary practices. Hartford's educational stock was high at that moment. Post-Strayer Then came the economic disruption engendered by the war. In spite of its basic soundness, the inflexibility and inadaptability

of the Strayer-Elsbree Schedule made itself manifest under the distorted conditions of an inflated economy. 

In consequence the years since 1941 have been marked by the frequent necessity for sporadic patching of the salary plan. Eight different adjustments, some temporary, others permanent, have been made in the intervening years. All have been made with the enervating effect that the annual bargaining for the adjustment of teachers salaries inevitably has upon the education of the children. 

The settlements that were made seem to have been arrived at largely on the basis of expediency rather than through the development of a well-conceived, durable master plan that would foster worthwhile professional objectives. 

Present Schedule

The present schedule (1948) in its essence, contains but minor modifications of the Strayer-Elsbree Salary plan. True, the ranges are higher by the total amount of three permanent increases over the past several years (approximately $740 for the minimum salary levels). Anywhere from two to six steps have been eliminated from the minimum-maximum-increment structure, thereby reducing the time required to attain maxima, but no great departure from Strayer- 

Elsbree salary methodology has taken place.

The modifications which have been made may be described as purely monetary expedicnts. There has been no provision for inherent elasticity to enable the schedule to cope with changing economic conditions. Other than the already provided movement to higher preparational levels, there has been no added incentive for the promotion of professional improvement nor the advancement of teaching through conditional increments in the schedule itself. 

Nor has the present plan sought to encourage and reward the outstanding teacher for service which is of unusually high calibre. 

So the struggle has been to follow economic and somewhat essential group pressures without much pre-planning or scientific approach, from year to year. There have been cost-of-living adjustments, more like bonuses, but these have been rough and ready, hit or miss, uncalibrated increases, which have not been lodged in fundamental policy. Sometimes, indeed, these guessed adjustments have been rendered "permanent". Concentration upon such a course has tended to lighten the accent upon the more important matter of skillfully utilizing salary policy to promote and to nurture educational objectives, and at the same time to render economic justice to the individual teacher.

Today Hartford is now confronted with the obvious need for a system. tic, long-term salary plan, attuned to economic fluctuations, and de signed to meet new professional conditions and needs. At the sam

2. THE SURVEY PROBLEM PRINCIPLES PROCEDURES 

Con- 

It is reasonable to ask at this time "why a Survey?" What is the basic problem which prompted the need of a survey? sideration of these questions, some further clarification of guiding principles, and brief statements concerning the more important procedures employed in the study, constitute the purposes of this section. 

The answer to the question, "Why a Survey?, was implicit, if not explicit, in the section preceding this. Yet the question has a value which can still be pursued. In 1936 Hartford had a thorough and comprehensive study of its schools. Included therein was a study of salaries, resulting in the adoption of schedules which have represented the body of salary policy since that time. 

Since 1936, however, much has happened. The impacts of vastly changed economic conditions has been felt heavily in all school systems. Who has not heard of "The Teacher Crisis"? 

During this period none of the salary schedules adopted by school systems has been able to hold up against conditions. Hartford has been no exception. There has been much patching; but it has gotten so, increasingly, that each year avowed salary policy could not be relied upon as the basis of annual budget formulation. Long periods of negotiation had to be gone through. Naturally, the question arose "Why not seek through a re-study of the instructional salary problem to recapture an effective salary policy?". In effect, and briefly stated, it was to achieve a school salary policy which could be much more of a settled course of action that led to this Survey. This, however, is a large order; and it would seem desirable to explore farther into its various aspects. In doing so the 

Survey will draw upon portions of materials presented at an allstaff meeting in Hartford on September 13, 1948.

No area of public policy in education is more important or permeates more deeply into the very body of the public schools than does salary policy. If citizens ask themselves earnestly and unceasingly, "What kind of schools do we want for our children?" they will find themselves in a sort of continuum of constructive educational design which undergirds and promotes the qualitative growth of the community. This, in a democracy is the inevitable growth-resultant of paying attention to problems of educational policy. 

But no amount of such attention to the question of "What kind of schools" can alone yield results. Inevitably the question has

to be faced: "What personnel--teachers, administrators and special service staff--will be the kind that will make possible the realization of these better schools for children?" Staffing of the school is as important as its educational content; in fact, it is more so, because given highly competent teachers and administrators, they will be of the very stuff that makes the design of the school system hoped for.

Out of staff comes the school as the unique public factor in making the community, the state, and the nation. This can hardly be gainsaid. Yet the conditions of these times place unusual and stringent demands upon the school and upon the staff that would attain highest-level competence. Change, and a speed-up in the tempo of change now require an alertness and a constant application to the study of the external conditions impacting on the schools beyond anything heretofore known by any generation of school teachers. 

The contributions of modern science are vast, and they very largely constitute these external conditions. Physical science is so permeating of our life and times that its meaning in the education of children and youth has to become increasingly the stock-in-trade of all teachers and not merely that of the specialist. 

The task of education includes the challenges which the physical sciences throw to the social sciences; and the teacher, as key in this interaction, has today a far greater and more complex function to master to the point of competence than he ever had before. 

The research findings within the field of education alone; the constantly developing body of educational science; the newer tools, equipment, and materials available for use in teaching; the changing and developing technology of education; these -- to say nothing of the unquestioned importance of a broad general educational background are cumulative in their scope and all in all demand of the teacher a constant professional movement of study, both institutional and in-service, for which no amount of experience alone can possibly substitute. 

It must be clear, therefore, that in these days the role of personnel is strategic in building progressively good schools for the progressively good community. School salary policy can make or break this strategy. What Schools? What staff? What salary policy? These are three great questions of internal educational policy. If one starts with the first, one inevitably ends up with the last. If one starts with the last, as in this Survey, one must constantly have his eye on the first, because school salary policy is not good unless it yields the good teacher and the good school. This might be couched in the form of a principle: School salary policy is a relatively "settled course of action' for channeling public moneys into personnel so that funds will yield their maximum in the educational growth of children, youth, and adults coming within the influence of the school system. This principle makes the improvement of the schools the ultimate criterion in the development of the salary schedule. It is broad enough, unquestionably, to encompass economic justice to the teacher. In the last analysis what is best for the pupils is best for the teachers. This is a guiding principle of this Survey. 11

- Closely related to this criterion for the development of school salary policy is the "quid pro quo" principle which the Survey staff has often referred to in the course of conferences and meetings. Freely expressed this means "one thing for another". 

The expression has also a qualitative tinge. The people to whom the public schools belong cannot expect, as has been altogether too prevalent an expectancy in school systems generally, to get quality service without quality pay; for example, to meet the ultimate criterion without heavy expenditure. Conversely, it is trite but still good to say that the teacher should be worthy of his hire; and this is especially meaningful when the hire is high. Certainly at high level salaries the teacher should expect to be continuously in the process of improving himself and raising his level of competence. Teachers should be paid well and the purchasing power of their salary dollar should be reasonably protected and stabilized. 

Citizens can well afford to be liberal in their salary policy because this will place the people of the city in the position of being able to demand with a wholehearted and genuine sincereity the quid pro quo" of continous growth in-service toward higher and higher competence on the part of the teacher. Teachers may well afford these conditions as the "quid pro quo of higher-level salaries. 11 Reference has been made to the idea of policy as "a settled course of action". The salary schedule, which is the more common term in school systems, is a device or a mechanism expressive of avowed salary policy. Now, of course, nobody expects that in these times of fast change, social complexity, and economic dislocation, a completely settled course of action in school salaries can be or should be attined. Salary schedules have to be adapted to conditions, as do curricula and educational services. Yet it is true that one of the real reasons for this Survey was to achieve a relatively more settled course of salary action than has prevailed in recent years. The Survey has called attention frequently to the enervating effect of constant salary controversy and efforts of staf: for salary adjustments. These controversies and efforts unquestionably take time and take energy off the very things for which the personnel are employed. And this condition also has its return meaning for citizens; i.e., to support salary policy that is good, and that keeps salaries well in adjustment with economic fluctuations. 

Survey Arrangements and Procedures

The current study of Hartford instructional and administrative salaries got underway about the first of June of this year. Arrangements for the Survey were made by the Board of Education and the Superintendent of Schools under a special appropriation voted by the City Council. Contractual arrangements were made with the 

Harvard Field Studies Center to include in the Survey all instructional salaries, including those of both the teaching and the administrative, supervisory, and special service personnel. The study

· was note to include the non-certificated personnel of the school system. Concurrently, it is understood that a study is being carried on by the municipal administration covering much of the non-certificated personnel. There are still some other positions, such as those in the medical and dental service, which need further consideration, but to which only passing consideration has been given in this Survey.

A rather complete set of specifications were drawn up by the Board to set forth the special areas and problems which the Survey should study. These included such areas of concern as the following: (1) financial recognition of employee dependents, (2) adjustment plans for relating salaries to the increase or decrease in the cost of living, (3) requirements of continuous study for professional improvement for normal advancements on salary schedule, (4) special recognition of competence or merit and the conditions thereof, (5) payments for duties beyond classroom work, (6) factors of employment, such as sick-leave, hospitalization insurance, and pay periods, (7) the basis for placing new teachers on schedule, (8) the relation of the step on schedule to the person's years of experience, and (9) provisions for sabbatical leave.

This list is very comprehensive and represents most of the areas of issue requiring attention in any salary survey. It should be said, however, that a salary survey is even more than the consideration of these areas. A practical and workable salary schedule has to be developed and supported. This involves the levels of salaries to be embraced within the minimum-maximum ranges, the size and number of increments, and the conditions for their attainment. 

The Survey also has to be concerned with the details of the enactment and administration of salary policy, and with important fiscal considerations;

With the wholehearted approvel of the survey staff, it was decided by the Board of Education to designate an Advisory Committee composed of members of the staff of the school system and lay citizens to cooperate with the Survey Director through suggestions and reactions to proposals. It was the wish of the Board, however, that the Director should carry full responsibility for his final recommendations and report. Four long meetings of the Advisory Committee were held, and there was a countless run of individual and group conferences during the period. All final recommendations of the 

Survey, appearing in the Digest presented earlier in this report, were first reported in tentative form to the Advisory Committee. 

As noted in the Digest, the deliberations and suggestions of this Committee were of great help to the survey staff. In the course of this Survey the staff explored deeply into the factual salary situation and related factors. Great pains were taken to assemble all pertinent data concerning the teaching and administrative personnel with relation to the salary problem.

Summary of Specific Requisites 

In It is not alone by general conditions and principles that the Survey has been guided, but out of them it is possible to distill particular requisites which seem to be indicated in designing effective school salary policy for Hartford in these times. School salar schedules have come to have common characteristics. This survey has adhered to many of these, but it has also realistically moved to a considerable degree away from the common requisites and common design. It has done so in keeping with what it sees as advanced trends, as well as general and local conditions. 

The more important of the presently indicated requisites for the shaping of salary schedules, as the Survey sees them, are the following:

(1) The basic classification of the teaching personnel for purposes of salary should still be educational and professional preparation. This is accepted as a requisite, which has come to be common through long experimental development in school systems. One reason for a preparational differentiation in classification lies in the fact that what constitutes standard preparation is still in the process of growth. Hartford's minimum standard now is that which is represented by the Bachelor's degree, but many local teachers have less and many have more. Eventually a preparational classification may not be essential. (2) The Survey accepts the idea of the basic single salary schedule, with no positional differentiation. In 

Connecticut this involves, also, the legal requirement of no discrimination on account of the sex of the teacher. (3) The minimum-maximum-increment structure is requisite. 

The minimum salary should be sufficiently high to allow the school system to recruit highly able and promising beginners in teaching. (4) The starting salary policy should be flexible enough to permit the recruitment of teachers from the regional and national market, and to permit the school system to meet special circumstances which may attend the filling of a particular position. (5) Salary levels and increments should be large enough to enable the teacher to have a going financial security, desirably with undivided occupation given to the job of teaching and to in-service training. This does not deny work experiences designed to contribute to teaching competence. (6) Maximum salaries should attain levels which will maintain a high degree of holding power for capable and mature teachers. (7) The salary schedule itself should be so constructed as to promote the continued professional and personal growth of the teacher. It should provide ways for discovering, encouraging, and rewarding the superior teacher; conversely, it should help to protect the child from the consequences of incompetent teaching.

(8) The salary schedule should be able to protect the basic teaching position from being weakened unnecessarily by its loss of good teacher-incumbents, simply because financial pressures attract them to administrative or other positions. (9) Salary policy, as a whole, should provide ultimate salary levels, and include in its design provisions, which will help to make the career of teaching attractive to persons of very high competence. (10) Devices should be at hand to give continuity and stability to basic salary policy and to protect it from the buffeting of temporary and external economic conditions. (11) Functional personnel administration is a requisite, and this involves broad staff participation and organizational mechanism for the same.

Viewing Hartford's present school salary policy against these principles and requisites, it is apparent that some of them have already been given considerable recognition. It is not so much that new principles must be established as that these must be seen in new light, and that new standards as requisites must be employed for their continued and effective operation.

In the light of the foregoing principles and requisites special attention is given and recommendation made in this Report for the adoption of three components of school salary policy which are new to Hartford. They are: (1) A systematic device for stabilizing basic salary policy against the effect of fluctuating economic conditions; (2) A reasonably objective device for discovering and rewarding teachers of high competence; and (3). The requirement of continued professional preparation as a condition for receiving increments.

With Relation to Teaching Personnel 

A salary policy built upon an exhaustive study of the facts in the particular situation is likely to be a sound and lasting policy. Good procedural practice requires that the facts be unearthed, be organized individually and in related groups, and be distilled of all pertinent matter that may prove directional in salary deliberations. The facts must be utilized constantly as a compass whose needlè indicates the unbiased road toward what is reasonable, adequate, and equitable. Therefore the usefulness and import of fact gathering and analysis is paramount. 

Throughout the period of this salary study the survey staff has pursued its work on the principle that the calibre of its ultimate proposals would be in proportion to the qualitative and quantitative data collected and the intelligent employment of those data. The appearance of the twenty-one factual tables in the Appendix is evidence of the quantitative data that were collected in conjunction with this study; yet it must be realized that these comprise but a part of the total amount of data derived from many sources.

Although only a portion of the data can appear herewith it must be said that every shred of reliable information unfolded was carefully scrutinized, weighed, catalogued, discussed freely, and its implications added to the final salary composition if it had any bearing upon the Hartford salary problem. Some of the more latent material served the thinking of the survey staff fully as much as did the data which appear in the twenty-one tables. 

Some of the more patent facts regarding the origin, dependencics, experience, preparation, age, and professional improvement of Hartford teachers appear in these tables. All portend much to the maker of salary schedules for each is a vital element in the attainment of the objectives which underlie sound salary policy. 

Origin of the Teaching Personnel 

Regarding origin, 46% of the elementary, 36% of the junior high, and 17% of the senior high teachers have been enlisted from 

Hartford. Another 35% of the elementary, 23% of the junior high, and 28% of the senior high teachers have been drawn from Connecticu excluding Hartford. All told, 68% of the teachers originate in 

Hartford, greater Hartford, or Connecticut. See Appendix, Table 1, for further details.

Thus, up to recent years, Hartford has successfully met the competition of nearby communities for the services of capable teachers. However, there is considerable evidence, augmented by the decline in applications for elementary positions, that the

-. complexion of this picture has worsened and will degenerate still further in the face of constantly advancing salary levels elsewhere, unless Hartford salaries have a substantial upward revision. 

It is not undesirable that Hartford continue to draw about onethird of her elementary teachers from beyond the bounds of Connecticut, but the Hartford schools should be in position to attract for this influx those teachers who would be ranked in the highest quartile in ability, wherever they may be.

The reason for the smaller percentage of teachers indigenous to Connecticut now in the Hartford senior high schools may be traced to a variety of causes, chief among which is the fact that, in 1937, the Strayer Survey found that the high school teachers alone were paid salaries which compared favorably to those of other New England cities. Historically, then, Hartford has had a fairly lengthy period during which some of the best high school teachers from all sections of New England have been attracted to Hartford. In this case, Hartford has not lacked applications from the city and its environs, it has simply been enabled to be more selective by virtue of its better salary schedule. 

As the competition for the services of qualified teachers becomes more and more intense, Hartford must ensure, largely through her salary policy, that her boys and girls increasingly have the advantage of top-notch teachers selected from a full field of teacher-candidates in Hartford, in Connecticut, in New England, and beyond. From wherever they may come, they must be selected because of their preparation and teaching ability, not their availability. 

Facts on Dependency

Dependency can be a vexing matter in fixing rates of compensation for school personnel. To reach an intelligent decision regarding the advisability of the inclusion of a dependency clause in any salary plan, the data must be probed and analyzed. 

A comparison of the dependencies of men and women teachers, both married and single, corroborates the contention that male teachers have much the heavier responsibilities in this connection. 

Thus, while 114 married women teachers have 50 dependencies, 161 married men teachers have 361 dependencies. Similarly, while 508 single women teachers have 162 dependencies, 29 single men teachers have 13 dependencies. So the ratio for single men teachers is about the same as that for married women, about .4 dependency each. 

Single women have about .3 dependency each, whereas married men teachers have 2.2 dependencies each.

Food for thought lies in an investigation of the salary levels of teachers having the greatest number of dependents. It is seen that the largest relative number of married teachers are in the group that receive from $4040 to $4690. This is also the group with the most dependents, having 207 dependents for 111 teachers. It also happens that the teachers in this salary

category have the highest relative professional training. It can be co-derived that the preparational type schedule has provided a means for the teacher with dependents to raise his salary level through upgraded professional improvement and that this group has seized the opportunity thus presented.

Among the single teachers in Hartford, dependency is not a serious problem. Even in the salary group with the highest incidence of dependents (those earning from $3440 through $3990), there are only 72 dependents to 176 teachers. Relatively, the greatest number of single teachers are in the salary range from $3740 through $4390. In this bracket, 228 teachers have only 80 dependents.

The foregoing facts governing the dependencies of Hartford teachers would seem to indicate that, instead of a dependency differential appended to the salary schedule, a more rational policy would be to pay an adequate, decent, professional living wage for all teachers and to supplement that by opportunity for added compensation through higher professional service and preparation. Experience

The matter of experience as a determinant of salary level is another problem which cannot be easily resolved. At the extreme poles are the proponents of experience and the adherents of pure preparation. The pro-experience group holds that the teacher with experience is more valuable than the one fresh from college. Some go as far as to maintain that a certain number of years of experience should be equated with the next higher preparational level. 

Many experience proponents allege that the number of years of experience should correspond with the "step" on the salary schedule, or that a super-maximum should be set up for those with a certain number of years' experience. The pure preparation adherents, on the other hand, discredit experience as a contributory force in the betterment of the classroom performance, and state that twenty years' experience too often is but one year repeated nineteen times. They add that there is little or no correlation between experience and adjudged competence.

Somewhere between these two extremes must lie a salary arrangement which, while it takes cognizance of experience as of definite value, reduces the automaticity of increments by attaching professional improvement conditions to their attainment.

Since experience is a matter around which there are likely to be some feverish disputations, there are presented in the Appendix seven factual tables which should clarify the experience situation in Hartford. A few observations from these tables should serve to point up the more important elements regarding experience and leave to the reader the examination of the tables for items o individual interest.

The purpose of this section is not to duplicate the Digest but rather to extend the interpretation of certain aspects of the basic plan for teachers' salaries. A Different Design Requires a Different Format 

Over a considerable period of years Hartford citizens, teachers, and officials have gotten used to seeing a salary schedule expressed in a certain way - chiefly in columns representative of stages of preparation and with salary steps in each column. That this survey has moved away from the traditional format may at first sight make the proposed plan seem complex and disconcerting. In fact, however, the Concise Summary" (See p. 12) is a simple expression of the salary schedule. In reality it is more explicit than the traditional format, while also expressive of several more elements of principle. (See P. 30).

The old format developed in large part out of the time and concept of increments in salary going willy-nilly with years of experience. Even when, as later, schedules cautioned against "steps" as representing "years" they were not uncommonly so interpreted. This pattern lent itself also to the application of a single principle only, such as that of varying salaries by types of position (elementary and secondary) or, as more recently, by preparation. In the former case, as made clear in a previous section, this survey has departed in principle from the automatic, year by year, relation of salary to experience, even though, if conditions are met, such relationship is still achievable. In the latter case, the survey has added other principles. In every way, it seems to the survey that in the abandonment of the old and traditional format the present expression of salary policy is both necessary and less likely to misinterpretation. 

Classification of Teachers is Still Preparation.

Education today requires a forward looking, improved policy of standard personnel service. Teaching is a science and a profession demanding able and carefully trained personnel. Doctors, lawyers, the clergy, and many other professional people must study two, three or more years beyond a bachelor's degree before entering practice. Not long ago two or three years of training beyond high school was the common requirement for teachers in public schools.

Today a bachelor's degree from a teachers college or university is the usual requirement. The trend of the times clearly indicates that at least a master's degree will be the minimum requirement for all teachers in the near future. It is understood that such a plan is now being considered by the Connecticut State Board of Education. The survey staff therefore recommends that the 

Hartford Board of Education maintain its policy of employing only teachers who possess at least a Bachelor's degree and in addition, that the salary policy adopted by the Board include provisions for rapidly upgrading new teachers to a master's degree, while providing incentive for continued in-service study and professional growth. The survey staff also recommends that provisions shall be included in the salary policy for teachers now in service which will encourage the continuance of in-service training and at the same time enable teachers of long service to receive salary recognition without undue penalty of insurmountable hurdles. 

Accordingly the staff recommends the following five classifications of teaching personnel based on professional preparation, as stated in the Digest:

The Idea of An Adjustable Basic Salary 

As noted in the Digest, the two factors which have been clearly operative in the salary situation during most of the past decade have been (1) the raising of salary levels with the hope of better recruitment, and (2) adjusting levels to changing price indexes. Basic salaries have been "basic" only in name. Under present conditions the survey staff has recognized the folly of recommending a simple basic schedule which might well become ineffective, as most schedules have, in less than a year's time. 

The thinking and research of the survey staff has led it to believe that the most sensible approach to the problem in the circumstances is to establish a Standard Salary Range to which adjustments can be systematically made as changes take place in the cost of living. 

Thus, an Adjustable Basic Salary would be achieved would, except for new recognitions of educational need, be kept automatically adequate and reasonably in adjustment in terms of its purchasing power for the employee. Furthermore, the adjustive mechanism, as well as the Standard Salary Range, would be within the scope of established policy, whereas hitherto only the "basic" was thus laid.

Adjustments, as such, were always left to more or less haphazard negotiation.

Placement of New and Present Teachers on Schedule 

The Digest has been explicit on the schedule-placement of teachers new to Hartford, and on the plan of "cut-over" of present personnel to the new schedule.

As to the former, it is reiterated here that with a highlevel adjustable basic schedule it would be nothing short of folly mechanically to tie the hands of the school system by rules limiting the salary placement of new teachers to some formula in terms of experience outside Hartford, in terms of dollars plus what the applicant is receiving, or the like. Generally speaking, new teachers will be employed at the lower end of the schedule. At the same time Hartford's supply market should be countrywide, rather than local. Furthermore, if common practice be the guide, the tendency toward prudence in initial salary placement will be likely to be over rather than under worked. 

One of the great things about a high-level schedule is its potency in staffing schools with the best there is in the way of teachers. The survey is emphatic concerning its recommendation in the Digest to "pay what it takes" to get the right person being prudent in so doing. The only hesitance about this seems to be 

---

the fear that someone will be brought in whose salary, judged in terms of "steps" and experience, will be higher when so gauged than one already in the system. Such sensitivity simply ought not to exist in a professional teaching body. If it does exist, it ought not to prevail. As a clear matter of fact, the very economics of supply and demand, in addition to purely educational desirability, virtually forces the type of policy here recommended.. 

The "cut-over" for present teachers is liberal. It is based on the principle of going to the new schedule at present salary, plus adjustments. This plan does not follow the cut-over" theory that a teacher should be placed on the new schedule at the same "step" on which he is in the old schedule. 2. RELATING SALARIES TO THE COST OF LIVING 

Prior to the war, in the so-called normal years, it was commonly observed that the effective life of a good salary schedule was about five years. Beyond that, even the good salary schedule was likely to have developed various internal mass inequities. 

Levels or classifications, and other elements within the schedule became increasingly meaningless; or, as has happened more recently, the whole schedule has broken down in the face of rapid changes in economic conditions. 

SO In these days of abrupt and precipitous changes in the national economy, the prospect of establishing a salary schedule of reasonably long life is indeed dim unless to be undergirded by some plan to insure economic stability. A salary schedule, however good that survives for only a year or two has neither served the community well nor protected the schools from the enervating effect of the annual barter over teachers' salaries. During the last six years, in community after community, the rising price-wage cycle has forced major alterations of salary policy almost annually much so in fact, that comparative salary data for school systems simply cannot be kept up-to-date. For all practical purposes, the life of a salary schedule in these days has too often proved to be one year. From the standpoint of the ultimate criterion the educational welfare of boys and girls this annual over-hauling of salary policy, with its tensions, uncertainties, and the draining away of teacher time and energy from the classroom and the child, exacts a hidden cost no schools can long withstand. 

Therefore, in order to establish a more settled course of action which may lengthen the life of the Teachers' Salary Schedule herein proposed, it is the considered recommendation of the survey that the City of Hartford, through its Board of Education, attach its Salary Schedules to the Consumers' Price Index -- All Items as published by the U. S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. 

Once this recommendation is made, at least four questions arise immediately: (1) Why use the Consumers' Price Index of the 

Bureau of Labor Statistics? (2) How should teachers' salaries be attached to the Consumers' Price Index? (3) What happens if the 

Index goes up or down? (4) Will this serve the best interests of the teachers of Hartford end of the citizens generally?

Why Use the Bureau of Labor Statistics Index? 

It has been the history in Hartford, as elsewhere, that increased compensation for teachers, either in firm salary increase or in temporary "bonus" adjustments, has followed inexorably upon the rising cycle of prices. When the lag between price rise and salary rise has been great, either in time or amount, teacher moral and efficiency have declined; when the correction has been made under threat of either party -- the community or its teachers schools have been alienated from the people they serve. Teachers' 

---

- salaries will undoubtedly continue to be adjusted, either by logic or barter, to major changes in the cost of living. It is the intent of the survey's recommendations (See Digest pp. 4-6) to subst: tute a just and understandable system of adjustment for the prevailing method of expedient barter. 

In the past, cost of living adjustments have been arrived at in general through two devices and combinations thereof: (1) Flat increases in salary permanent and irrevocable except by general salary cut; (2) Temporary cost of living "bonuses" bearing some percentage relationship to that part of the teachers' salary deemed to be sufficient for subsistence. 

The former method of flat salary increase has proved ineffective as a sole means of adjustment. Most such adjustments have resulted in half-way measures due to the caution exercised by municipalities as they anticipated the return to a normal economy. 

Nor has the latter method -- annual percentage adjustments -- solve the problem. In most cases the fixed percentage has been arrived at after long and heated debate and has been based on an inadequate "subsistence componment" of the total salary. Not only have such plans failed to bring real relief to the economic plight of the teacher, but they also have had the effect of penalizing disproportionately those persons of long and mature service whose salarie are substantially above the bare subsistence level. 

In this connection it is observed in Hartford, for example, that the difference between minimum and maximum salaries under the 

1937 Strayer-Elsbree Schedule for teachers holding Bachelor's degrees was $2,000.00. $2,000.00. To maintain in 1948 the purchasing equivalent to this 1937 range would have required $3400.00. Yet the differenc between minimum and maximum salaries existing under Hartford's present salary schedule is only $2,100.00. The net effect of the salary and bonus adjustments which have taken place in the last seven years in Hartford, has been to reduce sharply the true salar; range at the expense of teachers of longest service. 

What is Meant by Cost of Living and How Are its Changes Measured? 

The term "cost of living" means different things to different people. To some it is the total amount of money spent by an individual or a family during a given period of time, such as a month or a year.

To others it is the amount spent (or required) during such a period for the goods and services which the individual or family considers essential the amount needed to maintain one's accustomed mode or level of living. There can be no one cost of living which is reasonable or proper for everyone. Economists and others interested in the economic status of occupational groups usually think of the cost of living as a sort of average or typical cost for the members of a particular group. 

---

the When the cost of living is devined as the total dollar-cost of the commodities comprising a certain mode or level of living, changes in it are measured by comparing the costs of such commoditi on different dates. This general procedure is followed by a number of agencies in preparing cost of living indexes for certain broad occupational groups in areas of varying type and size. At least three of these agencies operate in the nation as a whole -- 

Bureau of Labor Statistics in the U. S. Department of Labor, the National Industrial Conference Board, and the Bureau of Agricultura 

Economics in the U. S. Department of Agriculture. There are also a number of state agencies whose cost of living indexes are limited to individual states and the communities in them. No agency to the survey's knowledge publishes separate indexes for the main geographical regions of the nation, such as the Northeast, the Middle 

States and the Far West.

With its indexes the Bureau of Labor Statistics attempts to measure changes in the cost of the goods and services typically purchased by the families of wage earners and lower-salaried clerical workers in cities. Basic information on the kinds and amounts of commodities to be covered was obtained by the BLS through a comprehensive curvey, during the years 1934-1936, of the yearly incomes, expenditures, and savings of 14,469 such families in cities over 50,000 in population. Each family included in the survey was composed of two or more persons, had been living in the same community for at least nine months, had received no "relief" during the year covered by its report, had had its chief earner working for at least thirty weeks of the year, and had received an income of at least $500.00 during the year. The average yearly income reported by these families was $1,524.00. The information was used in determining the items to be priced for the indexes, the stores in which prices were to be obtained, and the relative importance to be attached to each item priced. The indexes themselves are most representative of typical families, and less representative of families that were much above or below average in the survey of 1934-1936. 11 The 'consumers' price index for moderate-income families in large cities", formerly known as the cost of living index, measures average changes in retail prices of goods, rents, and services bought by families of wage earners and moderate-income workers in large cities. Time-to-time changes in the prices of goods and services are weighted by 1934-1936 average expenditures of familie: whose incomes averaged $1,524.00 in 1934-1936. City data are combined for the United States with the use of population weights. 

The indexes are not claimed to indicate whether it costs more to live in one city than in another. They do, however, indicate the relative increase in prices in those cities. Is the BLS Consumers' Price Index a Reasonably Accurate Gauge For Teachers Salaries? 

This question has been debated at length over the years. 

Various studies have pointed out the fact that the weight given to food in the BLS Index is heavier than would be derived by a close analysis of teachers' budgets. Likewise, there is good reason to believe that teachers spend more for books, magazines, travel, the arts, and other professionally connected activities than does the typical family on which the Index is based. Rent, too, is a factor which is probably given insignificant weight, since many teachers particularly women teachers, board away from home. Yet, studies by the National Education Association and other organizations have clearly indicated that, in general, the BLS Index is the most sensitive and accurate of the available indices, and is descriptive enough of changing prices to be useful for gauging the salaries of educational workers.

In actual practice, communities have adjusted teachers' salaries to the BLS Index, either the national ALL ITEMS index, or a special index for a particular city. (There is no special index for Hartford). Among such communities are the following: Phoenix 

Arizona; Stamford, Connecticut; Brookline, Massachusetts; Dearborn 

Michigan; Oak Park, Illinois; Mesa, Arizona; Montibello, Californi 

Lansing, Michigan; Milwaukee, Wisconsin; the Fordson District 

Dearborn, Michigan; and South St. Paul, Minnesota.

What is the Standard Salary Range? How Derived? 

For teachers and all personnel so classified the Standard Salary Range (ELS 150) recommended is $2475 - $5225. For administrative and other personnel various ranges are recommended, but in each with an assumed equivalence to a 31S index at 150. The recommended plan involves adjusting any salaries within such ranges (BLS 150) to payable salaries in 1949, which, with some tolerance by virtue of the technique of adjustment, will have equivalence, or parity, under price conditions expressed by a BLS index of 170.

At this point a number of questions arise. What was the process of conversion by means of which the Survey arrived at assumed equivalence to a ELS index of 150? It was thought that the Strayer-Elsbree Schedule for those with Bachelor's degrees, or better, was fairly well adjusted to conditions applying in the teacher salary field when the Index was at 100. The year 1937 was half-way in the period 1935-39 which the BLS still uses as equal to 10J. The Strayer-Elstree range was $1500 (Eachelor's minimum) to $4000 (Doctor's maximum). This range was generally considered good at the time. With this range, however, the survey staff reasoned and concluded in its judgment that the qirimu wOS too low as a base; and it therefore substituted $1800 (BLS 100). 

It substituted for the virtually unused Doctor's degree as top classification, the Master's degree, plus one year of preparation for teaching, and kept the $4000 as maximum (BLS 100). Thus a range was determined, based on the accepted Strayer-Elsbree Schedul with slight modification, of $1800 - $4000 for standard personnel. 

Next the Survey made an adjustment in this range, both minimum and maximum, from BLS 100 to BLS 150. To a $1600 portion of the minimum ($1800) there was added half the difference between $1600 and $1800, getting $1700, as the "subsistence component" of minimum salary at BLS 100. The $1600 figure in the sense used is not out of line with thinking and practice at the time. Next, 150/100th of $1700 was taken yielding $2550; and to this was added the $100 of the difference between $1600 and $1800, not used in the "subsistence component". This brings out a minimum of $2650 (BLS 150). 

Similarly, the staff proceeded with the maximum of the BLS 10 range, namely $4000, and derived $5400. In this way the Standard 

Salary Range (PLS 150) would recome $2650 - $5400. This was then checked from a number of angics reasoned with, so to speak -as to its goodness, and as or a time when the B.L.S. stood at 150, circa 1946-1947. This was not far from the time of the common recognition of a "Teacher Crisis". Would this be fair and desirabi salary then -- not too high, not too low? The Survey considered it very satisfactory but as indicated in the Digest (p. 5) made modifications downward by $175 at each end in deference to the practical situation, fiscally speaking, and especially in view of the inadequacy of present state aid for schools. Therefore, the 

Standard Salary Range immediately recommended was settled upon as $2475 to $5225 (BLS 150).

It is from this range that any teacher's salary falling within it will be adjusted to BLS $170 by the technique of " cut- over" and adjustment for economic trends as stated in the Digest (pp 5-8). The only basic difference in the adjustment to 170 from that operating in the description given above is that the initial "subsistence component" in the 170-adjustment was taken at $2400, instead of $1600, the same representing the rise from index 100 to index 150. Similarly, the adjustment will be made to BLS 170 for salaries of other personnel lying within other salary ranges at BLS 150. In some cases, however, slight changes in other aspects of the cut-over" were introduced, e.g. in cases of administrative personnel recommended to go on year-round employment. 11 

Why Key Standard Salary Range to BLS 150? 

The question naturally arises 

- and has many times during the Survey, as to why the standard salary range should be keyed to a base of BLS 150. First of all, it is obviously necessary to have some point of departure if a real policy of adjustment for the cost of living factor is to be established. If one is to adjust, he has to adjust from somewhere. It would seem desirable, if possible, to assume some period approaching something like a norm, economically speaking, as the period of departure. It would seem desirable to establish some conception of normally good salary levels for such a period, at least in the changing light of public understanding and prizing of general teacher worth. In such event a base would be available from which to make adjustments according to policy involving the use of the indexes. Of course, one has to recognize the clear social trend of gradual growth in the public recognition of what constitutes adequacy in teachers' salaries, and that anything in the nature of "normalcy", either in salaries or in economic conditions, is at best a changing conception of "normalcy", There is also the variable of the local fiscal situation. 

Prior to the notable rise in the BLS index, back (say) in the early 40's, when the 1939 condition was close at hand, it seemed relatively simpler (to put it mildly) to envision for a particular community something in the nature of a fair and reasonable salary range for the nearby period when the indexes were around 100.* Now, however, with the period of BLS 100 much farther 

See, e. g. "School Salary Policy in Meriden (Conn.)" 1943, in which the problem of cost of living adjustments was explored by members of this survey staff. away, and amidst many dislocations, it is practically impossible, and possibly impractical, to do what could (say) in 1941 or 1942 be done with some confidence. This is one reason why the Survey has taken a different approach to the problem.

It seems to stand to reason that economic levels will not for long, if at all, return in the foreseeable future, to the BLS 100 level. Full return, at least, has not been late history; recession is another matter. Even the ratio of 1935-39 (at 100) to 1913 (at 70.7) is 1.41 plus. Considering economic trends, and the tendency toward planning and controls, it is held justifiable to make a choice of BLS 150 at which to key the Standard Salary Range.

This is the assumption of the Survey. Having made this assumption the task of the Survey was to arrive at a conclusion as to what salary range might well be chosen as fitting BLS 150. 

In preceding paragraphs it has been stated how this range was established by proceeding from an actual and publicly accepted range as of 1937. Again, this was checked and tested as a practical decision.

In judging the adequacy and reasonableness of this Standard Salary Range (BLS 150) it should be understood and recognized that it is not here that the Survey leaves its recommended salary policy for teachers. Such a range would not be taken if adjustment to whatever current economic levels prevail were not planned; or, as now, adjustment were not to be made to BLS 170. It is in light of the very policy of regulated adjustment that the adequacy of this range has to be seen.

What Happens if the Index Goes Up or Down? 

Reference has already been made to adjusting salaries annually, prior to budget time, and based on the average of the ALL 

ITEMS Index for the three-month period August, September, and

October. As long as the present level of inflation remains relatively stable no severe problems should arise. 

In the proposed plan the so-called "Subsistence Component" will be flexible. The basic Subsistence Component will be $2400.00 

For persons of higher salary, the Subsistence Component will be increased by one-half the difference between $2400.00 and the full salary, as computed within the BLS 150 ranges. By introducing variability into this factor, the effect of inflation (or deflatio: will be more equitably distributed over the entire staff and not operate in marked disproportion on those teachers at the higher salary ranges.

In the event of extreme inflation or deflation occurring abruptly, no systematic tie-in to the Consumers' Price Index is known to the Survey which will surely save hermless either the city or the teachers. The variable Subsistence Corponent should have the effect of reasonably atturing salaries to the cost of living while it remains within moderate limits. If, for example, the Index should rise substantially above 200, there is good reason to believe that the whole problem will have to be rethought by all parties concerned. It is equally true that if "the bottom drops out" and we plunge downward, reappraisal will be required.

Runaway inflation or depth of serious depression are factors which, though not anticipated, no claim is ventured that this salary plan, or any other, can adequately deal with in advance. 

The Survey Staff recommends that should the BLS Index rise above or fall below the limits included in this proposal (viz. 200 to 

130) the Board of Education should then open the matter for reconsideration. The highest point on the Index for which this plan is intended to operate is 209.9 and the lowest 120.1. While the plan might well operate above and below these levels, such extremes would amount to real dislocation and well might call for reevaluation of the entire salary policy. 

Will This Serve the Best Interests of the Teachers of Hartford and of the Citizens Generally?

It is thoroughly understandable that many teachers may have misgivings as to the equity with which the downward correction for salaries will operate in the event of decline in the Index. When one regards the fact that teachers' salaries, historically and traditionally, have lagged behind comparable salary rates for many other vocations requiring the equivalent or lesser skill, one is disposed to regard any upward correction as "payment long overdue" and to regard downward correction as a return to the discriminatory low levels of past practice. Yet if the past is any criterion, it is naive to suppose that teachers' salaries can be forever pegged & inflationary levels if all else sinks about them. Even if this and there are compelling reasons why teachers' salaries should not be expected to subside as rapidly as those in profession less underpaid -- the time and energy required by the school system in defending itself can come only from one source: the time and energy that would otherwise have been spent on the education of children. were so 14 

If and when the Index drops to 160 or below, the first point at which downward correction from the present BLS 170 rating takes place, total compensation will be reduced from that recommended by the Survey.

It should not be overlooked, however, that if and when this event takes place the purchasing power of all the dollars constituting the teacher's pay will have been increased, inasmuch as the downward correction will have taken place only on that porti of the salary represented by $2400.00, the basic Subsistence Component, and one-half of the total salary above $2400.00. Just as the teacher's salary does not receive full correction on the way up, by the same token the full impact of deflation will not be felt on the way down.

As far as the citizens of Hartford are concerned, including the children of Hartford, systematic adjustment of teachers salaries may be buttressed against larger salary cuts than might otherwise prevail, the real return to the City in terms of educational service rendered for the tax dollar should, whatever the economy, be substantially increased. If teachers produced tangibl:

- and concrete products which could be actually weighed and measured, a non-systematic, opportunistic approach might be tolerable. But teachers deal with human beings. The product is intangible yet is the very stuff of our survival. It is sheer waste of the tax dollar to permit and encourage the diversion of the teacher's energy away from the purpose of his employment the education of children.

On the other hand, deflationary factors are becoming increasingly insistent: bumper crops; increased consumer resistance in many lines; and prospective further foreign currency developments which may provide additional competition of foreign-made goods. SCHEDULED PROVISION FOR SPECIAL COMPETENCE RECOGNITION 

One of the specifications for the Survey called for a con- . sideration of the question: "Should there be provision for special recognition of competence or merit: if so, at what stage in the schedule; what would be included in competence or merit; how recognized and how administered; extent and method of teacher participation in merit decisions; plan of appeal from decisions on this and other salary matters?"

It is fitting that this specification be included because practically all discussions of teacher salary policy today lead up to the question of relating salaries to competence. 

The urge for better salaries for better teachers is not new. Differentiation on the basis of competence was not, however, a great issue as long as teachers' salaries were quite universally low. But, when the spiral of inflation and the teacher shortage gave the professi a lever to break through public apathy which had held down salary levels, and particularly as laymen began to participate in the study of teacher salary policy, the competence theme received emphasis. Public opinion is now increasingly hesitant about approving salary ranges which reach into high professional levels without safeguards against mediocrity.

1. Competence schedules will stimulate the professional improvement of teachers and will promote better teaching. 2. 3. 4. 5.

There is need for sufficiently large rewards for master teaching to make it a satisfying career without the teacher feeling that he can only get ahead in the world by becoming an administrator. 

Competence schedules appear to carry much of the promise for more basically effective long-term recruitment of ability personnel to the teaching profession.

Competence Features in General Use. 

1 Certain features in general use in teachers' salary schedules are actually intended to relate payment to competence. For some time it has been common practice to vary the attainable maximum salaries in accordance with the preparation status of the teachers, generally in terms of degrees. It has also been customary to require recommendations from principals for the awarding of increments, although the requirement has generally been limited to that of satisfactory service". In many schedules there have been provisions for 'super-maximums" for outstanding service, and in some cases stipulations are made that extra increments can be given for meritorious work. 11 The basic theory behind the preparation type schedule is payme in relation to competence with preparational status taken as one factor of competence which is readily measurable. This theory does not assume that measurement of preparation or training is an exact measurement of competence, but does assume that, all other things being equal, the better prepared teacher is the better teacher. 

Previous Competence Schedules. 

There have been a number of attempts to relate salary to competence through scheduled provisions. Only two will be discuss. here since they illustrate past thinking.

An early attempt to devise a workable merit schedule resulted in the adoption in 1929 by the Board of Education of Pittsburgh,

Pennsylvania, of a schedule providing for five "superior teaching levels" with limiting percentages of the number of teachers who would be eligible for each level. These levels which required competence judgments represented approximately the upper half of the schedule-range.

· · The law recently passed in New York State which stipulated merit ratings at the end of the sixth and certain subsequent years of service also had percentage safeguards as to the number of teachers who must be moved upward.

These plans were both of somewhat similar type in that each presents hurdles in the path of each teacher up the increment scale requiring a competence rating which must be passed if each hurdle is to be surmounted. The New York plan had no limitation on the percentage of teachers who could be brought past the hurdles. The 

Pittsburgh plan had such limitations. The Pittsburgh plan passed out of existence in a few years. There is a question, as in all attempts at advanced policy, as to how the New York law will work out in the successful establishment of merit schedules on the local levels. One thing it has done is to focus attention upon this important problem. The attempt should not be sold short. Generally speaking, the "plateau.system" has not, to date, had much acceptability, but neither for that matter has any plan. Obviously, by their very nature, plateau limitations tend to be inherent for a given staff in salary-competence plans. Yet the thing to do is not to close up whole group thinking to the study of the problem or trial of improved designs. 

The Known Difficulties.

Study of reactions to previously conceived plans, and conclusions from study groups which have been earnestly at work upon this question, indicate that the major difficulties to be overcome are the following: 1. 

With reference to what has been tried in the past: (a) The rating has been a fixed procedure not sought by the teachers but imposed by the regulations and required of all members of the staff at stated intervals. It thereby becomes a mental hazard (b) The "plateau" type of intermediate maximums serves to hold back groups of teachers who fail to get the desired ratings without any hope for further progress along an increment scale, even though their work is satisfactory enough to retain them in service. (c) The ratings done by the supervisor or the administrator are generally a morale destroying factor and serve to disturb the administrator-teacher or supervisor-teacher relationship. (a) Plans restricting advancement to higher levels to a fixed proportion of the staff tend to saturate these upper levels with teachers who have once jumped the hurdles, and make it impossible for young teachers to have an opportunity to move upward.

2. 

With reference to the broad field of relating salary to competence, the major difficulties are the following: (a) The problem of determining what competence in teaching really is; of establishing the factors which contribute to its development and existence; and of finding its measurable components. (b) The problem of evaluating competence in the teaching situation. (c) The problem of developing a workable schedule structure which will relate salary to competence. (a) The problem of acceptability which include the psychological aspects of the entire situation.

More specifically the arguments generally proposed as contraindicative are that: 

1. No way has yet been found to measure competence. 2. 3. 4. 

Competence evaluations make the teacher apprehensive.

Evaluations by principals and supervisors will lead to a disturbance of staff morale through fear of bias,personality conflicts, jealousies, and apple-polishing". 

Classification of teachers by competence will lead to parental discrimination against those not classified on higher levels. 

Overcoming These Difficulties.

The difficulties and arguments enumerated above must be largel or completely overcome before any competence schedule will work and fully as important, before any plan will be acceptable to the teachers.

The question of defining competence and measuring it must be recognized to lie in a field of thinking which is not an exact science. For this reason the only practical procedure is to use the most objective measures known for any contributing factors for which such measures have been devised. Where subjective evaluation are necessary, every safeguard should be taken to reduce unreliability by making the evaluations comprehensive and unbiased. 

Objective measures are available for extent of preparation and for mastery of technical skills, knowledges, and other important contributing elements. Behavior can be recorded and studied and procedures can be observed and appraised by persons, who, through experience and study of the problem, can presumably achieve a high degree of expertness in subjective appraisal.

Teacher apprehension can be substantially reduced by making the appraisals voluntary on the part of the teacher and by permitting re-appraisals at the option of the teacher. 

Disturbance of staff morale can be largely avoided by having the final judgments made by persons other than the principal or supervisor, preferably from outside the regular school staff. 

Parental discrimination or unwarranted loss of reputation may be avoided by minimizing the idea of classification and emphasizing the philosophy of continuity of professional growth. 

The Recommended Competence Plan in Outline. 

The Survey holds that there should be some distinction betweer levels of competence and that the compensation of teachers should bear reasonably close relation to these competence levels. Fundamental to this thinking is the idea that the first level of competence denotes the teacher who is doing work which is sufficiently satisfactory for him to be kept on the staff and to receive increments for a substantial portion of his period of service. It is assumed that the teacher is willing to try, through in-service training, to increase the quality of his work from year to year. 

This leads to the prescription of a foundation schedule with conditional increments which will carry any teacher who does satis factory service from a decent minimum to a rewarding maximum. 

Implicit in this thinking is the fact that it is necessary for the school to have a well-established concept of what constitutes satisfactory teaching, and that no teacher who does not meet the requirements of this first basic level shall be retained in service 

Above or in addition to this first level of competence the basic level of satisfactory work it is proposed that there shall be established optional parallel scales of payment for higher leve of adjudged competence, open to all who aspire to improve themselv professionally and who have the capabilities required for such classifications. These parallel competence scales will carry the more competent teacher salarywise more rapidly upward toward higher maximum figures. Such figures may well be more than the public is prepared to pay to the less able teacher, but what the public must pay to the outstanding.

From the point of view of stimulation and incentive, both to teachers in Hartford service and to those who may be interested in teaching in Hartford, the establishment of parallel competence scal is justified. They should stimulate great professional growth and highly expert teaching. To fulfill this objective they must reach previously unconceived levels of professional compensation. Obviviously they must be workable without administrative complications without conflict with supervisory relationships, without overloadin the fiscal ability of the school district, and without raising the bugaboo of bias or influence.

---

Supplementing the basic schedule with a limited number of parallel scales for different levels of competence, each established as objectively as possible, should give all teachers a continued incentive for improvement. Ultimately there should be provided more than a single scale above the basic level, since the justification for the establishment of competence schedules is not in the differentiation itself but in the promotion of continuity of growth in professional proficiency. 

Structure. 

A parallel scale for a level of competence above the basic level of satisfactory service can be more simply described as the payment of an increased increment through the addition to the basic increment of what might be called a competence increment. This competence increment may vary in size for the various levels in competence, and presumably will be available only for the period of years in which the individual can continue to qualify on a particular level. Re-evaluation must be prescribed often enough to keep a teacher from growing stale, but not so often that it will make the evaluation procedure an expensive and burdensome process.

The parallel scale for the first level of competence above the standard schedule shall provide: 1. 

An initial additional increment of $250.00 over and above the increment due on the basic schedule, if any, for the next salary year following qualification. 

2. Annual additional increments of $50.00 for each succeeding year, as long as the individual continues to qualify, until a maximum of $950.00 (BLS-150) is attained in competence payments. 3.

These competence payments shall continue only as long as the teacher continues to qualify for placement on the. competence scale. If at any time a teacher fails to qualify for renewal, his salary shall revert to that at which he is listed on the basic schedule. 

The Survey recommends the adoption of only one parallel competence scale at the present time but contends that a complete competence provision should involve at least a second parallel scale to be established at some not too far distant future time. Such a second scale will be treated only in general terms in this report. 

Structurally it would involve a second additional increment, perhap of $50.00 per year, extending to a maximum about $500.00 (BLS-150) larger than the one listed above. 

The Optional Feature. 

It seems desirable to have the competence scale optional in more than one sense. With a reasonably adequate basic salary

. schedule for satisfactory service, it would really be possible to eliminate the competence features of the salary schedule entirely without invoking hardship upon the staff as a whole. The inescapable fact is that competence in any group of teachers, as in a group of workers in any other profession or occupation, exhibits a distribution which has a wide range between extremes. Even when those who are not considered worthy of employment are cut from the bottom of the distribution, there will be a certain proportion of almost every staff for whom levels of competence above the basic level of satisfactory service cannot be assigned. This is, however, the statistical approach. From the point of view of the individual teacher, anyone is eligible to qualify for levels abovo the basic.

The prescription or imposition of evaluation upon all who are on the staff would inevitably lead to apprehension on the part of the teacher who has not yet prepared himself for a higher rating to the point which is theoretically and inherently possible. Therefor it seems best to have the evaluation entirely optional on the part of the individual and available at almost any time. 

From the point of view of the school and of the teachers as 2 group, it may be well to have the adoption of the full extent of the competence feature optional inasmuch as the level beyond the first would be attainable only to one who had already been on the first level for a period of time. It is evident that only the first optional level can be activated at the time the new schedule may be adopted. When the time comes for the second competence level to be considered, which may well be some five years or more into the future, it would be well to have the possibility of incorporatin into the specifications and design of that second level whatever may be learned from experience with the operation of the first level It is proposed that any teacher who wishes to apply for a rating on the first parallel competence scale be allowed to make application by filling out a suitable form to be filed with the 

Assistant Superintendent (or Director) for Personnel. This form should include the essential facts regarding personal and professional record, preparational status, etc. Any teacher who fails to qualify at any time may apply again from year to year.

Qualification and Evaluation. 

If competence scales leading to higher attainable salaries are to be incorporated into the official salary policy it would be folly to set them up with trivial requirements. The competence scales are not a part of the basic schedule. They are something over and beyond. They do not need to enter into the determination of group satisfaction with the basic salary schedule which is recommended. Therefore, the requirements for admission to competence levels can be substantially difficult to meet. Unless these requirements are hard, the added payments will not be competence payments at all and the entire purpose will be lost through the degeneration of the situation into a subterfuge for an overall

62%- increase in the basic schedule. 

Since the competence schedules will result in maximum payments for selected groups at levels substantially higher than generally paid previously for teaching, it is doubly important that only the best in all aspects of the teaching profession be allowed to attain them. This means that a rigid set of conditions must be established involving evaluations based not only upon actual records of observation of teaching behavior, but also upon measures of intelligence and knowledge of professional information, facts, and principles. The basic schedule requires continued in-service preparation. The competence schedule should have some measures which will insure that the in-service preparation has not been a matter merely of form, but that the opportunity to improve has been securely grasped with a resulting increase in professional knowledge and know-how. For this reason it is important that suitable objective examinations be employed. Of the available examinations, the National 

Teacher Examinations ("Common Examinations" tests) are deemed by this survey staff to be extremely well adapted to the purpose. 

With this examination used as the first instrument of evaluation and with suitable percentile requirements set up, it would be necessary to apply other evaluations only to those individuals who have met the requirements stipulated under the NTE measurement. 

This order of procedure in itself tends toward economy of time and effort.

The use of properly chosen judges from outside the school system to evaluate recorded personnel data and to complete the evaluation by observation of the teacher at work and by such interviews as are necessary is recommended. The use of outside evaluators will broaden the base of the competence judgments and will serve largely to eliminate the apprehension of applicants for evaluation that personal relationships within the staff will have undesirable influence upon the results.

Inasmuch as the optional parallel competence scale, by its very nature, is not expected to apply to a majority of any teaching staff, its adoption should not be contingent upon majority approval of the teaching staff in case the adoption of various phases of the Survey's recommendations are in any fashion considered as dependent upon teacher acceptance, formally or informally. 

Because the competence feature is designed for stimulation of teacher proficiency and because its optional characteristic presupposes original entrance into its framework by relatively small numbers, it should be incorporated in the general salary plan even if only a relatively few teachers initially give evidence of interest in and desire for its benefits, or, indeed, give approval to this type of salary policy.

How View Administrative Salary Policy? 

The 

The survey staff wishes to reiterate here the fundamentally and strategically important role of administrative personnel. importance of this segment of personnel is out of all proportion to its cost in the total budgetary picture. 

In these days administration has a new and difficult job cut out for itself in the challenges to leadership which lie in human and public relations, and in extending the scope and depth of participation on the parts of the teaching staff and the lay citizens of the school system. This is a difficult objective to reach in any sense of realness. It is one which can be aided by an understanding and cooperative public, including, of course, the parental group. A major essential is also a Board attitude that, while prizing the objective and the results of these relations, nevertheless recognizes the basic division of function between administration and policy enactment, and that the actual guidance of the processes involved is an administrative rather than a Board, or legislative, function. Finally, it is of great importance that teachers, while recognizing the morale-building and professional value to them that their participation in policy development can yield, do not take advantage of it by using it to press for purely personal or group ends.

Just as the applied science of education is developing constantly, as a whole, so also is that of administration; and in these developments there is growing recognition of the change and complexity of underlying conditions, as well as the contributions of the basic sciences and disciplines. Too often the response to this development lags in practice; this seems almost to be inevitable, both in teaching and administration in fact, in dealing with the total teaching-learning situation. In the tendency toward this lag lies the reason why it is so important to have the continuous preparational movement for which this Survey has called. 

But, as the Survey has indicated, the movement alone is not enough; 

---

the substance of the in-service preparation must somehow be better applied in the schools and integrated with the educational program in action. In this task administration, properly viewed, is of fundamental and strategic importance. The full realization of the potential value that lies in the provisions of the salary policies recommended by this survey depends to no small degree upon the leadership of the administrative staff both of the system as a whole and of the individual schools.

As never before, our forward looking school systems are seeking to make themselves accordant in their development to democracy itself. Yet traditionally they are heavily authoritarian. School systems are in transition and in a period when complexities, crosscurrents, and pressures impact upon them. Administration is the midst of these; its methodology is in transition, also, toward a leadership which is likewise accordant to democracy. Its task is really difficult.

The administrative function, with the emphasis upon leadership embracing the methodology of participation, is more difficult, more demanding of its own personnel, than when the emphasis is upon authority, resting upon authoritarian method. The democratic administrative intent and actual movement is, however, much greater than either teachers or the public realize. This is true right in 

Hartford. This movement on the part of administration must be met at least half-way; certainly by the teaching staffs in cooperative action, in their functional demonstrations in each position, and in their general response to and understanding of a sincere and administrative desire and effort.

The design of the personnel and salary policy recommended by this survey is based upon the objective of a school system which is in accord with democratic principles. Let no one doubt this. The salary levels proposed are reasonably high and kept in economic adjustment; whatever conditions are attached to salary attainment are those essential to the objective; the financial stimulus to high competence is present in the plan; and the mechanism for the implementation of staff participation at all stages the Staff 

Council for Personnel Development 

is provided.

But, as must be clear from the preceding projection, the problems of administrative direction and leadership levy their corresponding demands upon all those who hold positions of administration and supervision. Administrative personnel cannot be held, and recruited, in the long run from ordinary ability; nor can the function be dispatched without specialized preparation corresponding to the expectation.

In view of the administrative task, and the conditions surrounding it, the personnel in this field of the school systems concern cannot be maintained at effective qualitative levels without much higher salaries than Hartford has been paying.

This observation applies to all administrative levels. The 

Survey has recognized this and proposed discrete salary scales at higher levels than have prevailed. These scales have been pitched, in accordance with the survey staff's best judgment, at a balance between the present salaries and where they will probably have to go eventually as the future problems of recruitment are confronted by the Board. In future recruitment, the survey staff strongly urges the Board to avoid the too commonly encountered danger of in-breeding through the over-doing of administrative staffing from within the system. The Survey scales are designed to make it possible for the Board to compete in the open market and to maintain a healthy balance between highly selective promotion and the drawing in of new blood.

As made clear in the Digest of Recommendations the proposed adjustments for the cost of living factor are to be applied to administrative as to teacher salaries. Adjusted compensation is provided for moving the entire administrative staff to the necessary twelve month basis of employment. No differentiation in terms of preparational classification has been proposed for administrative personnel, because it is assumed that only persons who meet fully standard personnel requirements will be initially employed. The standard preparational requirement for administrative personnel should in general be equivalent to the highest level set up for teachers, and in no case be initially less than the Master's degree. 

The recommendations clearly include the application of preparational requirements for salary increments in extent equivalent to those applied to the teaching staff, and with functionally adequate and properly specialized directional planning of the courses, or their equivalent, to be pursued.

1. COST PROJECTIONS 

The estimation of future annual cost of a salary policy as applied to a sizeable staff of teachers, particularly in the case of a schedule with conditional increments, requires the establishment of a number of assumptions. Among these are the following: 1. 2. 3.

Assumptions regarding the annual replacement of teachers including (a) the number who will retire from teaching because of age or illness, leave the system to work elsewhere, give up teaching because of marriage, or resign for various miscellaneous reasons. (b) The financial saving made on each replacement. 

Assumptions regarding the number of teachers each year who qualify for conditional increments in accordance with the requirements.

Assumptions regarding the number of teachers each year who will qualify to go beyond the intermediate maximum established for each preparation classification.

There are also certain practical conditions bearing upon cost projection. One, in this case, is that the forecast must be based upon present staff only. Contemplated or possible additions to the staff for extension of services or to accommodate increased enrollments are not included in the calculations. By the same token possible savings through reductions or reassignment of staff are not taken into account. On

Another practical consideration is the degree of conservatio: to be incorporated into the assumptions. If, for example, a teacher receiving $4,000 per year leaves the system and is replaced by a new recruit at $2,500, there is a saving that year of $1,500. If it is assumed that in a given year there will be 10 such replacements; the total saving would approximate $15,000. the other hand, if the assumptions on replacements call for forecasting the replacement of 30 teachers with an average saving per position of $1,500 the total saving would approximate $45,000. 

This shows clearly the possibly large effect in any one year of relatively slight variations in the assumptions. Variations such as this would, of course, tend to even up over a period of years. 

It is necessary also to limit the period of cost projection to a relatively few years over which the operation of existing factors can be foreseen. For this study that period is taken to be five years.

Costs of Competence Scale 

The estimates of increased cost already given in this chapter do not include the cost of the competence scale. These additional costs, however, have been considered and estimated. 

In projecting the cost of the first parallel competence scale it is assumed that the first payments for those who qualify will be made in the 1951-53 budget year. In computing the costs for subsequent years it will be assumed that the ultimate capacity of this scale will be reached over an extended period of years. 

It is believed that about 75% of the total number of teachers on the staff will be eligible in terms of years of experience at any one time. Of this group, it is assumed that in the near future about 30% will be eligible in terms of inherent and actual ability to qualify. It is also assumed that not more than 80% of those who could qualify will apply. Applying these percentages to the number of teachers on the staff as a basis for an approximation, it is reasoned that, during the early years until the competence scale has been in operation long enough to be filled to capacity, not more than 150 teachers will be so listed.

Since a teacher who qualifies for admission to the competence scale and for continued renewals on it requires 15 years to attain the maximum competence differential, it will take 15 years to fill up the scale and to reach a point of probable maximum cost. 

The cost of administration of the competence scale must include payments for the services of the Board of Review. It is not assumed that the school budget will bear the cost of adminis tering the National Teacher Examination.

Hartford have shown no appreciable change since 1933. Implications in Hartford's Metropolitan Trend.

The survey has also given some consideration to the future. developmental and economic problems of the Hartford municipality. 

Hartford, alone, is a physically limited city; but greater Hartford is a metropolitan district of which this city is the core. The trends of residential and industrial relocation outward in the greater metropolitan district give concern to the city and the need of planning. The pattern is not sharply distinct from that of other metropolitan areas. But the pattern can be allowed to mature, as in some areas, toward urban deterioration; or it may be guided and nurtured by policy, as is too infrequent, toward the height of urban cultural and economic development. 71 In the premises a few things seem rather clear. The total well-being of the metropolitan area depends much on that of the core. In this, education must admittedly have a unique role to play more so than is too often remembered. An urban state like 

Connecticut may well take notice of this situation and look well to the adequate financing and structure of public education by attention both to state and local means, and especially as the latter shows any signs of fiscal retrogression in the metropolitan core. This survey cannot extend the analysis of this problem, but it would be remiss if it did not call attention to the currently unplumbed nature of the interplay of forces between the outer bands and inner core of metropolitan districts, and the state's inherent concern therein.

With or without any industrial trade or residential trends affecting Hartford as the core of the metropolitan district, it seems clear that in long-term view the school enrollment situation. beyond the effect of cyclical forces, is not one of general expansion. Fundamentally the future school population is viewed as a slowly receding one. This also is something of a metropolitan pattern. In this situation too often the schools in the core city are allowed to drift into lowered quality, a tendency seemingly naturally abetted by the very recession of the population. Quite the reverse ought to be and can with relative ease be the course. 

If ever there were a time when Hartford's situation dictates an educational policy built on devotion to qualitative excellence, such would seem to be now. To this end qualitative excellence in school personnel is of paramount importance. To this end school salary policy designed to recruit and hold and upgrade quality personnel is clearly indicated. This is the position and the strategy; for Hartford, of this survey.

There is little question that Hartford has reasonably well supported education in the past. The whole strategy of the urban situation now requires that Hartford adopt high level school support, for value received, as fundamental future policy. True, that in the past few years of economic inflation, Hartford has had to spend more additional dollars on education than on any other one public service. Yet, while the total cost of government in 

Hartford has risen 56% in the past nine years, the cost of education has increased only 42%.

The survey recognizes that statistical data on the ability of cities to support education and other governmental services are not without their limitations. There is no attempt on the survey's part to press statistical comparisons beyond their reasonable limits of significance. This is one reason why the survey staff has reflected so broadly about the situation. To be sure, the objective data indicate great ability, absolutely and relatively, to support schools and other services at a high level. It seems as if it must be admitted by all reasonably objective persons that Hartford is without doubt in the top ranks of the fiscally able urban municipalities. This does not mean that Hartford can afford to be extravagant; it does not mean that Hartford does not have its fiscal problems. The survey staff is the first to grant that there ought to be, and can, and will be, much greater state financing of Connecticut school systems on the part of the state. 

Some brief elaboration of the state-aid situation, as it does and as it might affect Hartford's school support, may be of interest. State aid for schools did not amount to much in Hartford prior to the last two years. Due to the increase in the state's provisions of school aid, the amount now due Hartford annually amounts to some over $600,000, or approximately 13% of the current expenses of schools (1947-48). Two years earlier state aid accounted for only 5%. Hence, it will be seen that the increased state aid has meant a small but appreciable relief from the local property tax burden for the support of schools. 

At the same time, it should be noted that for the state as a whole approximately 22% of the current expenses of schools was borne by state aid. To some extent the Connecticut state aid syst has an equalizing effect, meaning that state aid is in somewhat inverse proportion to the local ability of the municipality to sup port schools. Hence, to the extent that equalization prevails, the more fiscally able the community, the smaller the state aid An inspection of state aid data for 1947-48 shows that only two towns Greenwich and Stamford) derive smaller percentages than

Hartford of the current expenses of schools. The percentages of 

Bridgeport, and New Haven are 19% and 16%, respectively.

It must be apparent that the situation with respect to the local fiscal ability to support schools in a town or city is, practically speaking, closely tied up with the problem of statu

8.- At the same time one of the strongest principles governing state aid distributions is equalization. States are very loath, and rightfully so, to run greatly counter to the equalization principle. The problem constitutes one of the serious issues in statelocal fiscal relations.

Obviously, any relative increase in Hartford's state-aid-forschools distribution would materially ease the local problem of school support. The problem, however, is complex; it has many angles and well founded principles are involved. This survey has had neither obligation nor time to enter into any detailed study of Connecticut state aid. If state aid were increased in 

Connecticut, as seems greatly needed and warranted, as judged by over 40% of school support), Hartford might or might not receive a larger distribution, depending on the principles followed, and methods used. One thing, however, that seems certain is that the state would have to look at the problem in equity, from the statewide view point. 

The survey could have gone into many intricacies of fiscal analysis. It is not greatly impressed by that approach, nor is that its function. It has, however, explored the main configuration of the fiscal scene; and it has gauged in perspective the whole picture. After all, it is simply a matter of rather common sensed observation that Hartford's economic condition well supports the contentions earlier made in this section, namely, that Hartford can pay for any reasonable educational program it may desire for its children. 

The survey's further conclusion is and on this the staff has no doubts that the salary policy which it recommends is well within the capacity of the Hartford fisc.

APPENDIX D THE BOARD OF EDUCATION HARTFORD, CONNECTICUT GENERAL SPECIFICATIONS OF A PROFESSIONAL SALARY SURVEY EXTENT 

The Director of the survey will recommend and support salary schedules covering all full-time positions under the Board of Education for which a certificate is required, issued or approved by the State Board of Education. This would include teachers, librarians, psychological workers, school social workers, school nurses, dental hygienists, principals, supervisors, directors, assistant superintendents and superintendent. It will include pay rates for temporary and continuous substitutes and part-time employees in such positions. It will not include non-certified positions. The survey will include a review of all positions for which the present salary stems from the teachers' schedule with the addition of a differential; also, in general, the question of differentials or higher compensation in some form for positions requiring training or responsibility in excess of or discernibly different from regular preparation of a teacher. 

In the support of his recommendations the Director will of course consider such factors as the professional nature of the service rendered, cost of living, comparison with similar positions elsewhere, comparison with salaries or incomes of persons in other professions in Hartford, recognition of preparation and experience, the financial obligations including estimated costs for a reasonable period, the community's ability to pay, and a plan of transfer of present employees from existing to new schedules.

The Board requests that in his study and report the Director will include consideration of the following questions: (1) Can a Hartford schedule legally, and should it, make provision for financial recognition of dependents of the employee? (2) Should there be an adjustment plan whereby salaries at different times are adjusted to increase or decrease with cost of living? (3) For normal advancement on the schedule, should the employee be required to present evidence of continuous study for professional improvement?

The General Specifications as adopted by the Board, are reproduced here only with relation to "extent" and "An Advisory Committee". (A.D.S.)

D ) Should there be provision for special recognition of competence or merit: if so, at what stage in the schedule; what would be included in competence or merit; how recognized and how administered; extent and method of teacher participation in merit decisions; plan of appeal from decisions on this and other salary matters?

8) What should be the relation of the step on the schedule to the person's years of experience?

This question is precipitated by present relation based on past cutovers to new schedules. In general, the person's step on the schedule is lower than his years of experience. In each previous cutover to an improved schedule the individual has gone to that step which gave not less than a stated amount or increase. As a result, the individual has gone, in general, each time to a lower step on the new schedule than on the previous schedule widening the gap between years of experience and step on schedule. In the execution of this study there will be an Advisory Committee representative of teachers and other instructional employees, the Board of Education, the City Council, and other interested groups. These groups might include Parent-Teacher

D Associations, the Governmental Research Institute, the Chamber of Commerce, labor organizations, the League of Women Voters, persons from other professions, and any others desired by the Director or the Board's Committee.
