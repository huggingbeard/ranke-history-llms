FOREWORD 

WHAT man most urgently needs today is to nourish his soul with eternal truths. One false philosophy after another has deluded him, and led him step by step to the chaos in which he actually finds himself. There is, however, a hopeful sign in his new consciousness of reality which may prove an awakening to this need, and is perhaps an initial step to the Eternal Fountain of Truth, in which he will find the antidote for the poisonous fruits of falsehood which have left him sick of soul. 

Our Catholics are, by no means, free from the pernicious results of the false principles that are abroad today. Yet they can easily find a remedy for them in the teachings of the Church, and especially in the perusal of Catholic literature, whose object is to counteract these evil principles. 

But modern life is so exteriorized, so filled with feverish activity, that the average man and woman of good will find little time for the reading of Catholic works, however necessary they may consider them to their spiritual life. 

It is the desire to meet this situation, by the presentation of Catholic truths in concise form that prompted the publication of the following pages as "." 

May the modest attempt find its way to the leisure hour of many a busy lover of truth, and supply subject matter for meditation and serious thought on vital truths that are light and food to the soul! vii

PART I MORAL AND ETHICAL TRUTHS 

- 1.

REALITY 

ALL the facts of existence are included in the word "reality." Living in conformity with reality is facing the facts of life just as they are, as they present themselves to us in our daily living contacts. To live in conformity with reality is to live in conformity with truth, and our Lord has told us, "The truth will make you free." To live in conformity with truth is to live honestly and sanely.

The consciousness of reality seems to have dawned upon our generation with the force of a new personal experience. It is one of the disarming features of this age, which, in spite of many discouraging symptoms, appears to be finding in an impressive way that life is a self-development based on concrete reality. 

Notwithstanding many things to the contrary, we cannot help feeling that light is beginning to break through the clouds of doubt and indifference that has long hung over this earth, and that hopeful signs are pointing to a religious re-awakening. 

The existence of the spiritual world and of a life to come are doubted by fewer people than we are inclined to think. Even for those who have no religious affiliation psychic experiences have been sufficiently real to force the acceptance of the spiritual order. Let us hope that the crisis through which we are passing is the dawn of better things. "The old order changeth, giving place to new, And God fulfills Himself in many ways." - 3

FACING REALITY 

TAKING ourselves and the world in which we live in sober earnest, not letting ourselves be influenced by dreams, desires or memories concerning ourselves or it, is facing reality. Using the means at our disposal to solve our problems, without resorting to 'escapes"; realizing that God placed us in this world such as it is and such as we are, and that He will expect no more from us than we can possibly give, is facing reality.

Knowing that we have duties to perform for which God will hold us responsible; feeling weary and disgusted at times, and asking ourselves "What is the use?"; but still doing our duties with as much courage as we can summon, is facing reality. Seeing our most cherished projects coming to naught, standing on the ruins of our most promising hopes, with nothing to do but begin all over again, and beginning all over again with small courage, is facing reality.

Taking the joys and consolations that God sends us on the way, but realizing that this life is not meant for permanent happiness is facing reality. 

Falling betimes into sin and error, but rising again by recourse to God's mercy, and trusting that He forgets our weakness and loves us still, this is facing reality. Meeting life day by day in its varied experiences of joys and sorrows, trusting God through it all, seeing the working out of His destiny upon us is facing reality. It is living as 

God means us to live, it is laying up a treasure for ourselves that will not fail. - 4-

LIVING IDEALLY 

TO SLEEP in a credulous trust, to be apathetic as to love or pain, to give up one's rights through cowardice or weakness is not to live ideally. To live ideally is to acquire a calm self-possession that renders up capable of governing others, but always, and above all, capable of governing ourselves. It is a striving toward an ideal without fear of pain. 

It is being faithful through pain and trial even unto death.

There is a danger of losing sight of our ideal in the mist that gathers round our soul when trying circumstances touch us personally and intimately. 

It is a sublime achievement to keep ourselves calm and cheerful in such circumstances. It is wise to prepare our hearts for trial before it comes, for no life can continue long without it. 

The mountain-climber sets out by walking joyfully over a verdant pathway covered with flowers. 

The birds sing around him, and all nature is bewitching. But as he climbs higher and higher the birds become silent, the verdure becomes more scanty until he has reached a stage where all growth disappears and there remains only the silent and desolate rocks. This is a picture of man's life in its ascension to the summits.

Work and worship are the watchwords of our ascension, with all the courage and joy we can put into each. The ascending slope of life is often a 

Via Dolorosa or a Calvary, there are many falls on the way. But we shall find the merciful arms of death as the final relief from the upward struggle and the weight of our burden. Every stage of the way, however, has its little halting places and its little songs in the night. But the evening of life is the best. When we have painfully toiled up the - 5 -

heights we shall find the setting sun behind us, and soon we too shall go down into the great sea to awake, please God, in the dawn of a brighter tomorrow.

HAPPINESS 

MAN being created for eternal happiness, it is natural for him to seek happiness in all things. 

And God, who knows the needs of our bodies, our hearts and our souls, does not forbid us to seek a certain happiness, even in temporal things. The laborer, He tells us is worthy of his hire. And no one advocates more eloquently than the Vicar of Christ, the Pope, the rightful claims of the man who must earn his bread, with regard to his physical well-being. 

But we read in Holy Scripture, "Not on bread alone doth man live, but on every word that cometh from the mouth of God." Man's feverish pursuit of worldly happiness too often leads him away from the true source of happiness. He seeks it everywhere but where it is to be found. The poor seek it in wealth, the rich in luxury, the ambitious in power, the intemperate in drink, the vainglorious in external show, the impure in unlawful pleasure. 

But all these seekers, like the Prodigal Son, eventually find that they have been feeding on the husks of swine. Happy will they be, if sickening of these, they remember their Father's House; for here alone is true happiness to be found.

The degree of happiness that God wills for each one of us here below depends upon our degree of self-restraint and our willingness to sacrifice the passing advantages of time, when they become an obstacle to the pursuit of our eternal destiny. In a word, happiness depends on our maintaining right relations both to the things of time and to the things of eternity. -7- 

---

SOURCES OF JOY 

THE human heart without joy is as much a desert as earth without water. It is because God does not approve of melancholy and depression of soul that joy is so often mentioned in Holy Scripture. “I tell you this," says the Master, "that your joy may be full." And the great Apostle repeats after Him, "Rejoice in the Lord, again I say to you, rejoice."

It is each one's duty to contribute his share to earth's joy. Its natural supply of sadness is always in excess. But joy is distilled drop by drop from little things along the way; the perfume of a flower, the song of a bird, the music of a brook, the streak of blue behind the clouds; all these the eye of faith discerns and makes them the subject of a Te Deum. 

Our joy is in proportion to our appreciation of small advantages. However tiresome the day is it has hours of rest. However stormy our skies there are days of calm and sunshine. If the day was boresome the night brings serenity, and each day is between two nights. If the night lies heavily on our hearts, the day will soon break, and a night is between two days. If youth has its dreams, old age has its memories, and if youth has its hardships, age will bring relief. Look at the roses and neglect the thorns. But this above all, when the earth is without a cheering ray, Heaven is awaiting us above; there the sun is always shining and joy flows in an unceasing torrent. - 8 -

HAPPINESS AND HOLINESS 

THE Christian life here below is a succession of struggles, resolutions, and frequent falls. But, thanks to the help of divine grace and continued combat, it is a forward march to the goal. The struggle is against creatures, who try to hinder us on our way. But though living, conscious obstacles, they need not stop us if we are docile to grace. Creatures have only a passing beauty. Their light soon fades.

But if we allow their attraction to lead us astray, after we have sacrificed everything to it, they will pass and leave us with the regret of having served them.

The world will never understand that holiness is the only real happiness there is here below. As

God multiplies His graces in our soul, joy opens our hearts to His love, enlightens our mind to see 

Him more clearly, quickens our gratitude and gives a certain elasticity to our faculties. All this removes from our hearts the alluring beauty of earth's idols by the gladsome disenchanting light God's grace throws upon them. Even the regret God sends us for our past transgressions by His grace fills our hearts with joy and gives us a sense of security for the future. - 96 .

FREEDOM 

How threadbare the word "freedom" has become today! How little of its original dignity does it retain! And yet it is a precious gift purchased for us by Christ, who affirmed that "The truth will make you free." The spiritual masters of the Middle Ages frequently described the Majesty of God by the expression: "God, the Free."

Our idea of freedom is to be without restraint, to have the right to choose and act as we please. 

But we must understand that freedom cannot exist apart from law. There is no freedom without law, but there is freedom within the law. Non-compliance with law, or resistance to it for man, whether the law be physical, social or moral, means deterioration or destruction, but surely the loss of freedom. Freedom is preserved by law, and our own freedom is conditioned by our knowledge of the law.

Matter is governed by physical laws, mind by moral laws, and our freedom, our very life, is involved in obedience to these laws. Failure to live under law spells disaster to soul and body. God is the author both of physical and moral laws. Physical laws are often based on theories that are uncertain, for man arrives at their knowledge, as a rule, through long and painful research. The moral law is founded on the truth of its Author, and admits of no doubt. It is too vital to our eternal destiny to be left to uncertainty.

It is folly to dream of social conditions that would emancipate us from law in this life. Our safety of soul and body lies in obedience to it. -10-

WHEN WE ARE FREE 

INTELLECTUAL environment, custom, tradition, public opinion, passion and the influence of example create a powerful subjection for the human mind from which few can escape. The political, cultural, and economic circle in which we find ourselves, in which we are forced to live molds our entire psychology. Yet even from such a bondage we can disengage ourselves. But how?

There are two ways of escaping from the slavery of external circumstances: we can break our bonds by outwardly overcoming the circumstances that holds us back; or by a free renunciation we can raise ourselves above them.

The servitude of environment comes from our temporal needs, from which we cannot easily escape; but our eternal interests raise us above them. In the teachings of the Catholic Church eternity enters time. While she approves of our taking care of our temporal interests in a normal way, she exhorts us, by her moral code as well as by her ideals of Christian perfection, to rise above the things of time.

Living according to the mind and teachings of the Church makes us free, with the freedom of the children of God. The Church enters into relations with every age.

She is not old-fashioned-as moderns accuse her of being. Her concerns, however, with the affairs of time as such is but to oppose what is to be condemned, to place her full strength against whatever threatens the freedom of men's souls. -11-

OUR GREATEST ENEMY 

TRUTH itself has taught us that a man's greatest enemies are those of his own household. Our greatest household tyrant we find in our own heart.

This tyrant is our personal character, with its evil inclinations and passions, which place a thick veil between our intellect and the light of knowledge that would liberate us. On account of our passions truth can reach our minds only by feeble rays.

These passions build up our prejudices, thus weakening our logic and controlling our intellectual freedom.

Our personal character colors all our thoughts, influences our experiences, and limits our self-development. No philosophy or self-discipline will, however, release us from its tyranny. Our freedom from it will come when our eyes are open to absolute perfection, to perfect idealism, and to our utter dependence on the Author of that perfection and idealism, Jesus Christ. His doctrine presents to every state and condition of existence the essence of pure perfection to which it may aspire. It is in the mirror of this perfection that we shall see our limitations. And to the extent truth enlightens and humbles us, to that extent we shall be liberated from our tyranny. -12-

IN THE LIGHT OF THE SPIRIT 

IT TAKES a long time for some souls to acquire a sense of the supernatural. Many people live and die without understanding much of the things of the spirit, while others have this understanding almost from their birth. There is a reason for this condition of things that would require too much discussion. 

Fortunately God judges men according to their lights, and that good will weighs much in the Divine balance. Some of the saints were not saints all their lives. And the best of us could say: "Angels know I have been far, After where wild roses are."

Yes, many of the saints wandered far after the false glamors of earth before they saw and understood. But an hour came for the wanderers, a blissful hour, when a divine light flashed through their soul, and a grace touched the heart of each poor erring sheep and he recognized the Voice of his Shepherd, and refused thereafter to listen to any other. 

If for us that hour has not yet shone let us watch for the light, lest we miss it. It may be but a feeble ray, but if we follow it faithfully it will become a great sun that will absorb all the false lights that have hitherto allured us. -13-

OUR NATIVE TRAITS 

EACH man and woman we meet is a distinctly individual type of humanity, and not just a human being in general. Each one embodies a special form of personality, an expression of human nature that is never repeated. This peculiarity of his manifests itself in every word and action, determining his attitude, his outlook and exterior deportment.

It is the duty of each one to study his and her special type of temperament. It indicates our limitations, is the source of our defects and weaknesses, but also of our strength and worth. It is in our special individuality we shall find what God requires of us, the place we should occupy, our special vocation, our mission here below. 

If we pursue the natural downward propensities of our temperament they will lead us away from our destiny. On the other hand, if we discover our native virtues, our upward temperamental attractions, cultivate and follow these we shall be traveling toward the goal. A harmonious development of our individual powers makes for virtue and freedom. - 14-

PRIDE AND VANITY 

SELF-LOVE is not in itself sinful. God commands man to love himself, for He placed in his soul the sentiment of self-esteem and self-preservation. Well understood and properly regulated, self-love is a help to virtue and a prop to public and private morality, without which man could not cultivate honor and lofty ideas. But failure to control selflove properly leads to many errors and disorders, notably to pride.

Pride is an exaggerated estimation of our own excellence and capacity, with a disposition to despise others as beneath us. It was the sin that created hell, and closed the earthly paradise; for it was the sin of the angels and of our first parents. 

God hates pride above all sins, for, more than the other sins, it is directly opposed to His glory. To offset the damage wrought in the world by pride the Son of God had to humiliate Himself, become incarnate, and live a suffering and despised life which He ended in the supreme humiliation of the Cross. Pride is characteríed by contempt for lawful authority, and a disposition to rebellion and revolt when there is question of subjection or humiliation.

Vanity is the pride of small minds. It is less sinful than pride, but may develop into pride if not corrected. It is a kind of mental intoxication; and indeed, a vain man and a slightly intoxicated man have many points of resemblance. They are both satisfied with themselves, and with the world, as long as their pretentions are unmolested. They are both, to a certain extent, inoffensive; and provoke a smile rather than indignation.

Nearly all men suffer more or less from vanity and pride. Their cure comes from divine grace - 15-

through prayer and meditation on the truth. Everything that makes us truer makes us freer from pride and vanity.

THE EXERCISE OF CONTROL 

OUR soul and body having to dwell together during our sojourn here below, it is of the greatest importance to decide which of them is to exercise sovereign control over our life and actions. St. Paul tells us that if we live according to the flesh we shall die; but, if by the spirit we mortify the deeds of the flesh, we shall live. It is, therefore a question of salvation or loss for us to determine whether we will be ruled by the flesh or by the spirit. 

The subordination of the flesh to the spirit is achieved through discipline and self-denial. It is to help us in this difficult work that the Church ordains fasts and abstinance at different seasons of the year. These are not ends in themselves but a means to bring the senses under the control of reason. No form of self-denial is in itself perfection, but only a means of perfection. The discipline practiced by the saints, in regard to eating, drinking, sleeping and the sublimation of the carnal instincts, had for object their advancement in holiness, the summit of which is union with God. 

Mortification, with this end in view, purifies the senses, clarifies the mind, subdues sinful desires, stimulates ardor for spiritual things and makes the spirit, which it renders humble, merciful and indulgent in all its dealings, master of the flesh. -17-

OUR FAULTS 

ST. PAUL assures us that to those who love God all things work together unto good; to which St. 

Augustine adds: "even our faults"; and he had reason to know this.

Who can determine the part his faults or transgressions have had in the development of any virtue he possesses? Was it not from our very weaknesses that we have learned to be charitable and indulgent to those of others? Having ourselves experienced human frailties we do not wish to have two standards by which to measure ourselves and our neighbor. It is, perhaps, to our falls that we owe any degree of humility we have; and it was from them that we learned the need we had of God. Our love for God is in part due to our transgressions, for he loves more to whom more has been forgiven. 

It is wisdom not to let our faults, or the falls we sincerely regret disturb our peace of soul, as long as we are struggling patiently and humbly to rise above them. St. Teresa's Bookmark should often be the subject of our meditation in this regard. "Let nothing disturb thee. 

Let nothing affright thee. 

All things pass away. 

God never changes. 

Patience wins all things. 

Who has God needs nothing more; 

Alone He sufficeth." - 18 -

DOING AND BEARING 

MEN judge each other by the greatness of their deeds. The mighty men of earth are those who accomplish great actions. In the scales of God, however, all else being equal, suffering weighs more than action. Suffering requires more will-power and more divine aid than action. Suffering is a thought of God; it is not man's idea of greatness. 

It is something to which God has given a mysterious power to raise, to purify, to humble, to beautify, if man learns how to use it. And God's ways are exalted above the ways of man. Christ was wonderful in His actions, but He was still more wonderful in His sufferings. It was on the cross that 

He became our Redeemer. It was His sufferings that paid our debt to the Divinity, and opened 

Heaven to us.

The tragic thing about suffering is that man so often fails to see its meaning, that he has to suffer so much, and that he profits so little by it. Learning how to bear the pains of life takes away half their sting. Seeing the Hand of God beautifying our soul by their means makes them, if not desirable, at least acceptable. It is the uselessness, the meaninglessness of suffering that produces rebellion in the will. Action appeals to man. Through it he sees, or hopes to see, the fruit of his labors; and it consoles him to know that his labors and efffforts are not fruitless. Only the light that comes from the Cross of Christ gives meaning to suffering, subdues the will and calms the mind. - 19-

PASSIVE AND ACTIVE VIRTUES 

WHEN the meaning of suffering begins to dawn upon us a little we begin to understand why passive virtues, like humility, patience, meekness and the different forms of self-denial occupy such an exalted place in Christian ethics.

Christ during His mortal life blessed these virtues both by word and example. At the same time He did not canonize indolence and lack of effort and diligence, all of which result from weakness of will. 

We know that before Christ anointed His Body with the Blood of His Passion, He anointed His Hands with hard labor, and that He reproved the idle servant for hiding his talent. We must not, however, overlook the fact that nature suffers less in the exercise of the active virtues than it does when forced to bow patiently under long and trying physical and moral suffering. Who does not understand the sum of virtue required to submit to prolonged injustice and persecution? -20-

SUFFERING 

SUFFERING in the moral order is like night in the physical world. When the sun sets the land is draped in shadows that blur the outline of the landscape and hide its splendor and color. But it is when all that charmed the eye here below is saddened that we raise our eyes to the heavens and learn to love the starry vaults that were hitherto unknown to us.

In the same way when trial, sorrow and affliction cast a veil over all we held dear in time we raise our eyes to God, and in the light of faith see eternal truths that would have forever escaped us had not the cross shattered our earthly and illusive dreams of temporal happiness.

God's designs are not ours. He has willed that 

His eternal mansions be peopled by adorers with whom He has shared His Cross, and who have seen in that Cross the pledge of the happiness He promised them would be eternal. But He has not promised us material happiness nor joys for all our lives here below. To place our happiness where we ought, says Bossuet, is the source of all good; but to place it where we ought not is the source of all evil. And the saints, and all holy souls who have suffered well, assure us that God has placed happiness in the suffering He sends us. Jesus 

Christ Himself points out to us the different forms of happiness to be found in the cross in His Sermon on the Mount, and which are enumerated in the Eight Beatitudes. -21-

CARRYING OUR CROSS 

THE manner of carrying the cross divides man, so to speak, into two classes, for suffering either makes or mars men. Those who take the cross as a gift from God, and pass through the fire of pain nobly and bravely, or at least resignedly, are purified, strengthened and sanctified. Their influence on other souls will be in proportion to the length and bitterness of their trial and the manner in which they bore it.

Certain souls suffer little in this life in comparison with others, but no one escapes the cross under some form or other. Those who suffer little never rise beyond mediocrity in virtue, and their influence for good in this world is neither strong nor lasting. There are others who refuse to accept the cross God sends them; and these fill the world with irritable, peevish, ungracious and embittered murmurs. They are in continual conflict with Divine Providence, which they blame for the pains that cut them off from the pleasures of life. They are the victims of unspeakable mental anguish, and a source of distress to all who know them. There is no relief in nature for those who rebel against the inevitable.

It is beyond doubt that many cases of neuroses, phobias, and obsessions are the result of a conflict between a sense of duty and self-preservation, or between self-assertion and humiliation, with the inability to end the conflict in a way which conscience approves.

Our modern sanatoria would have fewer cases of mental disorders if men and women could say, "Thy will be done" to the crosses 

God sends to them." - 22

THE INEVITABILITY OF PAIN 

WE MEET pain of some kind at every stage of life's journey. Our daily contacts often give us much to bear. Without meaning to be so, we are all, in some way, a trial to each other's patience. 

We jar the susceptibilities of even our nearest and dearest, as they do ours, so defective is our fallen nature. Even our good qualities, when they fail to harmonize with the temperments of those with whom we have to deal, are a source of pain to us. 

Our very efforts to do good around us have their measure of suffering. But any work of zeal for 

God's glory must be marked with the sign of the cross, or it is doomed to sterility. Yet should such a work crumble under trial, some seeds of salvation will have fallen from it that will bring forth fruit in a manner we could not have foreseen. 

There are those in whom the sight of pain causes revolt. They flee from suffering if possible, even should the flight entail sin. Such souls are flying from peace which they will never recover until they have learned to accept the mystery of the cross. 

Our century has worked hard to eliminate pain from human life. It is a noble task to lessen the sufferings of the race; and science has contributed largely to this. Still humanity continues to suffer physically and morally, even in ways that seem to be a result of the efforts to eliminate pain. To lead the life Christ came to point out to us, and which will eventually reach the goal where pain can no longer touch us, we must heed His injunction: "Take up thy cross and follow Me." -23·

CAN WE SUFFER AND BE HAPPY? 

HAPPINESS is the dream of the human heart and the dream is no illusion, for we were created to be happy. God made man for eternal happiness, and the desire of happiness carries him toward his destiny. But he unfortunately associates happiness, with pleasure, especially the satisfaction of the senses. It is hard to persuade him that it is possible to suffer and yet to be happy. This is a truth hidden from the majority of men; for they flee from everything that savors of pain.

There is only one real obstacle, however, to happiness; and that is whatever stands between us and our eternal destiny. And suffering sometimes does become such a hindrance if we do not learn how to dominate it. It becomes, on the other hand, a blessing, a means to real happiness, if we accept it as sent by God, and as an expression of His benevolent designs upon us. Our sufferings are measured by Him to fit our spiritual needs, and proportionate to the degree of glory to which He has destined us. By means of it we fill up, according to the words of St. Paul, "what is wanting in the sufferings of Christ for His body which is the Church." 

The Gospel assures us that the Apostles did not understand the meaning of suffering before they received the Holy Ghost. When Christ told them that "the Son of Man must suffer many things," they did not catch the meaning of His words. But when the Holy Spirit enlightened their minds they began to rejoice in prison, scourge and death. Suf fering according to God's will brings unspeakable happiness even here below. Having all the pleasures of this world in opposition to His law brings unutterable misery, even in this life. -24-

A SANE ATTITUDE 

THERE are souls that are crushed by adversity and that might have been saved from a tragedy had they taken a saner view of things especiallly of the role of pain in human life, its benefits to the soul and its saving fruits for those who accept it in a Godlike way. It is wise at times to reason with ourselves even in a philosophical way. We should speak to ourselves as we would to a refractory child, and tell ourselves that, even for the most fortunate, life carries a considerable deficit in the sum of satisfaction it offers to our desires. We should remind ourselves that no matter what affliction comes to us, or how dark our night, there are still duties to be accomplished, and that concentrating our attention on these will strengthen our will and prevent demoralization. Difficulties are only opportunities for the development of greater strength to struggle with them. Every difficulty with which we strive enegetically leaves us a double benefit, even from a natural point of view: it enriches our experience and increases our intellectual vigor, thus leaving us less vulnerable to pain and suffering, while it fortifies our courage and patience. -25-

HIS WILL IS OUR PEACE 

THE masters of the spiritual life counsel us to leave the past to God's mercy, the future to His

Providence, and, aided by His grace, to do the best we can in the present. They call this holy abandonment. This condition of mind implies a peaceful regret of our past sins and errors, and, to repair the time we have lost, an endeavor to make particularly good use of the present. This disposition leaves us ready for the worst that can happen, and hence bespeaks a deep peace of mind and a holy joy of heart. It removes the keenest anxiety with regard to the future, and softens the bitterest grief that can come to us; while it animates all our activity and fills the soul with the joy of the Holy Spirit. 

There are but two powers in the world: the will of God and the will of man. When these two harmonize the result is peace of mind, and contentment with whatever comes. When they are at variance the soul is disquieted, and there is suffering in the present and little hope for the future. All our happiness here and hereafter depends on the relation of our will with the will of God. To abandon ourselves to this Divine Will does not prevent our trying our best to attain to temporal happiness according to the law of God, nor does it forbid our praying ardently for deliverance from all evils of soul and body. -26-

DEGREES OF HOLINESS 

ALTHOUGH God calls all men to perfection, there are degrees in this perfection. The work of grace in souls is as diverse as is human personality; and we hear our Lord assure us that in His Father's House there are many mansions. 

What is, however, supremely important for us to know in this respect is how to correspond to the grace we receive from God for our particular advancement in holiness. If we are faithful to this grace, that is, if we offer no resistance to the lights and helps that God gives us, He will inevitably lead us to the degree of holiness and perfection to which, from all eternity, He has destined our souls. To be thus faithful we must have deep reverence for the work of grace in our own souls and in the souls of others. We must cultivate a sentiment of piety and devotion, and we must glorify God in His saints.

The only real peace this life affords comes from holiness of life. The more deeply we enter God's friendship the happier we shall be. Those who have not yet made God their friend by excellence seek their happiness in earthly friends and in the pleasures of this world. They dislike to look into their own hearts for fear of the reproaches they may receive from their conscience, and so they neglect the care of their interior life. We must remember that every act of fidelity to God, every response to His grace raises the soul to a higher degree of holiness, until it has finally reached the summit to which God calls it. - 27-

SOLITUDE AND SILENCE 

ALL founders of religious orders have prescribed silence as the guardian of discipline and the atmosphere of prayer. There is no interior sanctity without silence. But the silence of the imagination is more important to this effect than the silence of the tongue. It is to secure interior silence that exterior silence is observed. Solitude and silence are educators and liberators for whoever would live according to the Spirit.

It behooves us to learn the value of solitude and silence, for sooner or later they impose themselves upon our lives, as the shadow of our days begins to lengthen. The realization of life's insecurity brings its disillusionment, for God wishes to detach us from the material and to show us that He Himself is the Life of all living. In the solitude of this experience if we listen well we shall understand a whisper in the silence, "I am here."

The touchstone of a soul is its ability to bear solitude and silence. Its attitude toward these are indicative of its leanings with regard to virtue. 

The men and women who have moved the world to its depths have come from the solitary heights. 

The most lasting influences have been elaborated in silence and solitude. The higher we ascend, however, the more solitary we shall be. But to radiate light and heat, souls must climb the lonely heights. The sun is alone; and from Thabor and

Calvary the heart of the world is kept from freezing. 

-28-

THE BLESSINGS OF SOLITUDE 

MANKIND will regain its moral bearings when men and women have learned the benefits of selfpossession resulting from a quiet and disciplined heart. But these benefits are not the fruits of our modern noisy and feverish life. The soul needs action it is true, but its most pressing need is to see itself in God in the quietude of contemplation and prayer. It is in this blessed atmosphere that it renews its vitality and strength just as the body does in sleep.

Solitude in itself is but a means and not an end. 

Those who are forced to seek it, without a desire for the blessings it affords, find in it but the atmosphere of a prison. It is in solitude that the saints. find holiness and that all souls of good will find the Living Waters of which the Gospel speaks. Solitude is the homeland of the strong, in whose silence the ancient philosophers heard the "music of the spheres." It is the soul's meeting place with God; for Holy Writ tells us He is not in the tempest, but in the gentle breeze. He is found in the quiet and seclusion of the heart of those that seek Him. 

In this divine communication of the soul with its God is the happiness found which man seeks in vain outside himself in a world where happiness is but a dream, a mirage that escapes the grasp of all who would seize it. -29-

OUR AVERSION TO SOLITUDE AND SILENCE 

IT IS only as men approach the higher stages of the spiritual life that they realize the friends that solitude and silence are to the soul. Even religiousminded people dislike to come face to face with themselves in solitude. Man drags after him the misery of his own greatness. He aspires to the Infinite; he desires the Absolute. But within his own heart he finds only painful limitations and a distressing void. What he meets there discourages or humiliates him. So he becomes a mendicant from his birth, begging from his fellow man what he cannot find at home. He therefore derives small comfort from solitude and silence. Besides this, he is not a pure spirit. It is through his senses that he feeds his intellectual life. This too helps to make him a social being.

Furthermore, our modern methods of life make solitude distasteful to us; for it forces upon us a desire for movement and change of scene so that the less seclusion we experience the better we feel. 

The advantage we derive from the change is not the question; what imports is that we avoid coming too often face to face with ourselves. Movement, distractions, boredom is the cycle of our day. Living for us has become synonymous with noise, exteriorized effort, and a consequent stifling of the spirit by matter. Our social mingling has resulted in the communication of mediocrity, and a lowering of intellectual and spiritual ideals. -30-

FAILURES 

LOOKING back upon their lives, as the shadows begin to lengthen, the greater number of men and women see in them but failure, even should the world judge otherwise. They see but unfulfilled plans, but unrealized ideals. How very few have been able to carry out the projects of youth! But are these failures, according to the judgment of the world, failures in reality? In other words, are they failures in the sight of Heaven? The life of Christ according to the world was a failure. He was born in poverty, lived in poverty and died in poverty. 

He was despised by the authorities of His country, apprehended, condemned, put to a shameful death. His progress through this earth as the world saw it, was anything but a “success.”

The judgment of this world with regard to the success or failure of a human life, means nothing when we come to understand the real meaning of life before God. When we remember that our life here is but a probation, a place of preparation for actual life, a training school for eternity, we become indifferent to the world's idea of success. We may have failed utterly in a human sense, and have achieved a glorious victory before God. On the other hand, the greatest success in the eyes of men may be a complete disaster before God. It is what God may think of the value of our lives that should concern us, and not the passing and false appreciation of the world. -31-

OVERCOMING EVIL BY GOOD 

ONE of the surest and best methods of fighting against our faults and evil inclinations is to nourish, cultivate and fortify our native qualities and good inclinations, for surely there are some of these side by side with the defects of every soul. 

We have only a given sum of strength and activity, and if we use this energy in cultivating our good qualities we withdraw it from fortifying our evil propensities. The nourishing of our virtuous dispositions is often the starving of our evil ones. 

The apostle tells us to overcome evil by good, and this system of the balance of power is perhaps the only successful one in human betterment. To attack the evil that is in man's nature by openly opposing cold logic to the fascination of a passion, to try to extirpate error by force of arm, as it were, offers but small promise of success. 

As long as this world lasts good and evil, as two adversaries, will be in opposition one to the other. 

Until human nature is rehabilitated under other skies, evil will be one of its distressing characteristics. We need not dream of making it disappear by direct combat. The best we can hope to do is to withdraw its life forces and use them on worthier enterprises. Leave error to its own resources, but withdraw truth from its influence. 

Suffer the evil we cannot prevent, but hasten, ourselves, to do good. - 32-

PATIENCE WITH OURSELVES 

WE easily realize the importance of the exercise of patience with others, as well as with the daily trials that beset us. But we understand much less the benefits of patience with ourselves.

Impatience with ourselves consists in being annoyed by our drawbacks and imperfections, in feeling irritation at our lack of success, when we have done everything possible to correct our defects or to insure success. This impatience is also manifested in our painful surprise at our want of selfcontrol on account of our habits; or in our depression at the feelings of anger and impatience we experience with all that thwarts us; or again, our annoyance at our lack of progress in our exterior or our spiritual life.

These feelings have their source in self-love, in lack of humility and a want of true estimation of the difficulties of self-improvement.

Among the means of cultivating patience with ourselves might be mentioned frequent meditation on our own nothingness, on the meanness of our past lives; thanksgiving to God for having preserved us from some of the great crimes we might have committed, were it not for divine grace; but especially prayer for patience with ourselves. The results of this patience is humility, consideration for others and evenness of temper. -33-

OUR MUTUAL DEPENDENCE DIVINE Providence has so arranged our destinies here below that we depend upon one another in a way in which we cannot close our eyes to the demands of charity. In our earthly condition we see multiplicity and diversity of parts, weakness and strength poverty and abundance, ignorance and knowledge leaning one upon the other. But an ulterior principle guides, co-ordinates and molds all into a harmonious whole. 

We are as much members one of another as we are members of Christ. So whether we will it or not, our life in Christ cannot remain isolated. 

There is no place in Christ's Kingdom here below for selfish individualism. We cannot, as members of that Kingdom, maintain forbidding frontiers.

We cannot truly love Christ unless we share His ambition for the extension of His reign. It was to see all His members ascend with Him to the Father that He took our humanity. We cannot say we love Christ if we exclude from our love any one He loves, if we are indifferent to the sufferings of any of His members. -34-

LAY APOSTOLATE AND HOLINESS 

THE objective of all lay apostolate-while it includes both the spiritual and corporal works of mercy -is to bring souls to God, and God to souls. This form of Catholic Action requires workers of intelligence, talent, savoir-faire and psychological penetration. But if behind all these things there is not a powerful reserve of personal sanctity, in spite of much noisy and feverish activity, there will be no enduring fruit.

The lay apostle needs personal holiness more than the priest in order to do good to souls. Of course, the priest by his very state is obliged to tend to holiness of life; but his work with souls is not conditioned by his degree of sanctity, though his influence for good is augmented by it. It is the grace produced by the sacraments administered by the priest that brings forth fruits of sanctity in souls. For the efficacy of the Sacraments do not depend on the holiness of the priest who administers them, but on the grace of God of which they are the channel; while the fruits of lay apostlate does depend upon the influence that radiates from the personal holiness of the apostle, to which God's grace, of course, is added. It, therefore, behooves those who undertake works of zeal for souls by direct contact to begin by putting order in their own house. -35-

RADIATING CHRIST 

AS THE tree casts its shadow, not only on its immediate surroundings, but across brooks, streams and barriers that itself can never cross, so man exercises an influence for good or evil, not only upon his intimate environment, but upon the lives of men and women that he has never seen nor ever will see in this world. No man lives his life merely for himself. This is a serious thought for the wise; but for the Christian and Catholic it suggests possibilities for the exercise of zeal that bring forth rich harvests for eternity. Their

Owing to the facilities offered by the material progress of today, agents of evil have multiplied their activities to an extent that terrifies. object is to exterminate the reign of Christ in souls. 

To do this they employ hate, for Christ established 

His reign by love. To their hate we must oppose our love, the supernatural love that Christ has implanted by grace in our hearts. 

There is no force in the world like love. Any influence we can exercise on men's souls will be exercised through love. It is the only power to which men willingly yield. But this love is not essentially sentiment or emotion although it may include these. It is before all the love of the will, and springs from our love of God. To the extent to which this love animates us, to that extent we shall give Christ to our brethren. -36-

SACRIFICE AND CONQUEST 

THE basis of all great achievements is sacrifice. 

No great work was ever carried out without it. 

Unless we resemble Christ in some way we cannot be saved. But to resemble Him we have to undergo some form of crucifixion; of mind, of heart, of soul or of body. Neither can we save others without tasting the mysterious fruits of the cross.

To win the world back to God it is not enough to be great speakers, great writers or great men and women of action, however useful these things are. 

What we need above all is not so much men who know how to mount the steps of the rostrum as men and women who know how to mount the steps of Calvary.

The young neo-pagan generation have learned the value of hardships, labors, sacrifice. They know they must be ready to give their lives for the questionable causes to which they have devoted them and are willing, to give them. Can we Christians be any less ready to sacrifice ourselves for an unquestionable cause? The voice of Christ is calling up a new generation, as He called in apostolic times to men to follow Him. Leave the beaten track, He commands, abandon your selfish aims, your ease, your comfort. Accept to be different from the multitude that is following its instincts and passions. Part with all that is not Me. On this condition will you be a conqueror; but a conqueror whose laurels will not wither, and whose conquest no man will take from you. -37-

LOVING THE UNLOVABLE 

TRUTH itself tells us that he who loves not his brother abides in death. And the second half of the Great Commandment is "Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself." But we all know that we have very unlovable neighbors, and, do what we will, it seems impossible for us to love them. How are we to harmonize the precept of the love of our neighbor with this impossibility? 

We must first remember that "feeling" does not count when there is question of supernatural love. 

And the love of our neighbor that the precept implies is a supernatural love. It is impossible not to experience feelings of aversion for those who for any reason, are repulsive to our sensibilities. These feelings come as unbidden as do physical pains from sickness or injury. What is of real importance, however, in the matter is the functioning of our will. This is the only power of our being over which we can exercise real control. 

We can react to aversion, to antipathy, to personal injury by not returning evil for evil. We can, by the help of grace, return good for evil by praying for our unlovable neighbor or by helping him in his necessities should the occasion offer. 

Worldly-minded people might consider this weakness; but let them measure the degree of virtue and courage it requires to act thus, and how little we need to practice the law of retaliation. -38-

MISUNDERSTANDINGS A MISUNDERSTANDING is a mirage that rises in a most peculiar way between closest friends or nearest relatives and causes them to see things, that were hitherto clear, in the strangest lights. It blinds the eyes, stultifies the mind and freezes the heart. Under its influence it is impossible to discover in their natural outlines things that were but yesterday as plain as day; for the imagination has twisted everything out of shape. As a result, hearts that were bound to each other by mutual trust gradually become ice-bound, and if the misunderstanding is not removed they will soon be the worst of enemies. Everything now is falsely interpreted, trifles are exaggerated, every little action is attributed to an evil intention, and a war, worse than Don Quixote's, is being waged against phantoms. To injure the supposed enemy, secrets that were jealously guarded are betrayed and a most lamentable situation created.

Very often, this misunderstanding was willed by one or other for personal reasons that urged the mind to misinterpret the actions of the other person, because they are not in keeping with the ideas of the person taking offence. But more often misunderstandings are the result of trifles. Self-love and pride are usually at the bottom of them, but sensitive natures more than others, fall a prey to them. People who cultivate a broad outlook on life, who have learned to understand human nature, and to expect the minimum from it; but especially those who have learned the practice of the virtues of charity, humility and self-denial escape the pains of misunderstandings because they have put into practice the admonition of Holy Scriptures: 

Let not the sun set on thy anger. -39-

SEVERITY AND INDULGENCE 

SEVERITY is one of the virtues of youth, indulgence is the fruit of maturity. In youth the source of law is in our own strength, and we impose this law upon others with domineering rigor. 

As we learn life's lessons and our own weaknesses, if the knowledge has not been fruitless, we become more lenient in our dealings with others. Our notions of right and wrong remain the same, for truth is absolute; but culpability becomes relative in the eyes of good sense. Hence the injunction, "Judge not," becomes more enlightening. The mind recognizing its own impotence, and the heart its own weakness join daily in teaching us not to condemn. -40-

DEALING WITH SOULS 

SHOULD God call us to the delicate work of dealing with souls, of influencing them for good by the exercise of our zeal, it is of great importance to know that the work of divine grace in souls never destroys nature. No matter how holy men and women become they still retain their native temperament. God's grace builds upon nature, elevates, transforms, sublimates it, but never supplants it. In our contacts with souls, then, we must expect to meet a great variety of personality, a wide diversity of outlook and opinion all of which may be in perfect harmony with God's individual designs on these souls. No artist repeats himself, and we must not be surprised that the Sovereign Artist forms no two souls in the same mold. His design on each is distinct; the rôle He assigns to each is personal. By a multiplicity of mysterious ways He draws souls to Himself.

We must look with deep respect on each of our fellow-members in Christ's Mystical Body. To deal successfully with a soul we would influence for good, we should make ourselves acquainted with his history, his physiognomy, his physical and moral temperament, all of which God took into account when He annexed Him to His Mystical Body. -41-

CHARITY BEGINS AT HOME 

LOOKING at the world as it is today, and realizing its moral and spiritual poverty no less than its material misery, there is an urge born of natural kindness, strengthened by faith and charity to do something great in the interests of humanity. Noble hearts experience a yearning to help the struggling, to form projects for the betterment of the poor or for the reconciliation of groups and classes; in a word to serve humanity with unselfish devotion. 

This is a beautiful ideal, but there is danger hidden in it. If we take the single injunction of our Lord: "Love one another," and allow the whole teachings of the Gospel to resolve themselves into that one text, forgetting all the others and making it the "Open sesame" of the enigma of life, we allow ourselves to fall into an illusion. 

We have heard it said that "Charity begins at home." This is most true in the sense that we must make the care of our own soul our first and most important consideration. If we forget our own spiritual well-being in the preoccupation of our neighbor's our charity is out of order. "He brought me into His cellar of wine," says the Spouse of the Canticle, "He set in order charity in me." 

If we put our social sentiments before or above that of our own spiritual concern our charity is not in order. We must love our neighbor's soul more than our own body, but we must not put his physical welfare before that of our own soul. ― - 42-

VIRTUE AND VICE 

LIFE is made up of an infinity of almost imperceptible ordinary actions, which are the source of our merit or demerit. The life of the just man is a chain-work formed of these actions done through a sense of duty in view of pleasing God. Each action done through a desire to accomplish the Divine Will is a degree of merit in the sight of God and a corresponding pledge of an eternal reward.

Acts daily repeated in this sense produce both in the faculties of the body and of the mind a habit of virtue that is strengthened with the years. At the outset they are performed with a degree of effort, but by repetition the effort gradually lessens and the habit becomes second nature. 

Vicious habits are formed in the same fashion. 

At first they are isolated acts, but by dint of repetition they become powerful chains that bind the powers of soul and body. To regain his freedom from habits of vice, a man must make more strenuous efforts than is required to form original habits of virtue. Very often this effort is greater than he is capable of making. Hence, a powerful grace of 

God is necessary to break his bonds. However, if he has the will to break them, and makes use of prayer, God's grace will not be lacking. -43-

PLEASURE AS A MOTIVE 

IT IS the motive for which an action is done, the intention one has in doing it that determines its merit or guilt before God. The chief motives or objects man has in his actions are pleasure and duty. Pleasure is, of course, the lower of the two. 

And yet pleasure is not in itself an evil; for God has attached pleasure to the fulfilment of almost every human action both of body and mind, especially if these actions are essential to the life of the individual or for the good of society. This pleasure is therefore a means to the end to which God attached it, but it is not an end in itself. 

To act merely in view of the pleasure one finds in acting is not worthy of man. This would be simply following one's instincts as do irrational beings, who have no higher powers. Such a rule of conduct in man is a debasing of his nature and a deterioration of his character by the weakening of his will. It is because man is over-actuated today by pleasure that we have so much effeminacy of character, for inordinate pleasure weakens morally, as it defiles spiritually. This explains why a degree of hardship and pain is necessary to man's physical, moral and spiritual well-being. 

When God wishes to punish an individual or a nation He abandons them to their pleasures. We read in Holy Scripture how God punished Israel in these words: "My people heard not my voice, and Israel hearkened not to Me, so I left them according to the desires of their hearts; they shall walk in their own inventions." And St. Paul expresses it, "God gave them up to the desires of their hearts." -44-

WALKING IN TRUTH SAINT TERESA's definition of humility "walking in the truth," makes it clear that while this virtue commands us to study our character to find our defects, it does not demand that we ignore or belittle the good qualities and natural virtues with which God has endowed us. Whatever these qualities are we must know that we carry them in earthen vessels which are easily shattered. 

That humility is truth condemns all false humility, deceit and insincerity in speaking about our faults, as well as all vainglory, vanity and silly display of any qualities we possess. What God requires of us is that we appreciate His gifts to us, and that we make good use of them for His glory and the benefit of our neighbor.

From The Parable of the Talents we know that God wants nothing idle in His creation for He condemns the slothful servant who hid his talent. 

Every gift carries its responsibility. The wise know this, and try to realize what profit they possibly can for God, their soul and the souls of men from their native gifts. The foolish squander these, either by not using them, by misusing them, or by vain and foolish display of them. -45-

PURE AND STRONG 

THOSE Who think of chastity as a spiritless virtue, a quality of weaklings, need to re-organize their standard of values. That this virtue is thus considered is a sad reflection on our age. Tennyson makes Sir Galahad say of himself: "My strength is as the strength of ten, Because my heart is pure."

There is no stronger or more positive element in human nature than self-control. And no one can admire strength who cannot admire chaste men and women. It is through his self-control that man is distinguished from the brute, which, having no reason, is wholly guided by instinct. If living is an art, those who have learned to control their instincts have truly mastered the art. 

Chastity is not only in the body; it is also in the soul. It is chastity that gives the spiritual note to personality. It gives expression to the face, luster to the eyes and dignity to the whole personality. - 46-

KEEPING A BALANCE 

THERE is talk today of a new type of humanity that neo-paganism is bringing forth, a product of sunshine, outdoor life and freedom, characterized by physical fitness, and radiant with all the rhythm and harmony that belong to perfected animal life. 

It recalls Rousseau's "Return to Nature," or 

Nietzsche's "Superman."

In earlier years of our civilization, an opposite type was overstressed when the physical side of man was much undervalued in favor of mind and soul development. All things can be overdone, for it is difficult for man to keep within the golden mean. And it is to be noticed that life conditions that have undergone an unjust repression take revenge by a violent reaction in the opposite direction; one tyrant is overthrown by another. 

But this new "over-materialized" type is out of all proportion to the former "over-spiritualized" type. Man is a composite of mind and matter, and a wise balance must always be kept between the two. But, even then, mind must dominate matter, the soul must rule the body if man is to attain his destiny, which is a spiritual one. By his soul, man is "a little less than the angels," and by his body he is a little more than the irrational animal. He is a frontier between Heaven and earth. But a frontier denotes contact as well as separation. To achieve perfect integrity man must keep a wise balance between the angel and the animal that is in him, with the angel always in control. His head must be in the azure, but his feet must be on the earth. — 47 —

GROWING OLD 

THE golden dawn, the dewy rosebud, the fragrant Maytime blossoms are all tender, lovely, desirable things. But, as we contemplate their charms, we are seized with sadness as we remember how fleeting they are. We reason, however, that dawn is but the herald of the day, and should its misty charms remain we never could have noon. Though beautiful, the rosebud is but hidden grace and fragrance; and should Maytime's blossoms linger we never could have fruit. In fact the beauty of all these tender things is in their very passing to give place to the mystery of maturity. 

We love the graces of childhood, and the radiant charms of youth. But they are only states of promise in the process of perishing. We must cherish them, keep them as long as we can, but cheerfully resign them when they must pass.

Youth like the rosebud is but a thing of promise, and that promise must be fulfilled. Like the ages that succeeded it, it has drawbacks and advantages, for each age bears the seal of all earthly things.

Each age too has its share in the working out of God's designs on our souls, in the fulfillment of our eternal destiny. We must therefore appreciate the advantages and opportunities each offers toward this end, and bear with patience that which is imperfect in each. Then when old age comes to us we shall see in it the forerunner of a glorious dawn, a springtime, an eternal youth, the joys of which will never flee, the charms of which will never fade. -48-

MATTER AND MIND 

ALL creation, broadly speaking, is composed of matter and mind. The two substances, spirit and matter, are contrasts, and act according to entirely different laws. Both were created by God and each is an expression of His wisdom and will. 

Man is a composite of matter and mind. He thus draws his being from two difffferent worlds. 

By his soul he belongs to the spiritual world, by his body to the material world. God has created other beings that are purely spiritual, such as the angels. He has created purely material beings, like earth and rocks. Man, in creation, holds a place between these two. His soul, which is a spirit, is united to his material body, and by this union the powers of the soul, which are the intellect and the will, operate through the senses of the body and give these an activity of high quality. 

Man's soul, though purely spiritual, was created by God for union with a material body. Through its union with matter the soul is, during this life, unable to act independently of matter according to the law that governs spiritual life. It is solely through the senses of the body that the intellect receives the seeds of knowledge from the outside world. These observations of the senses are called sense perceptions. Through the intellect the soul develops these perceptions into generalizations or logical concepts and conclusions. Thus the soul benefits by the senses of the body in securing knowledge; and by the operation of the soul upon the sense observations, man becomes a reasoning being by which he is distinguished from the rest of the animal world. -51-

HUMAN LIFE 

SCIENCE has not yet found an answer to the oftrepeated question, "What is life?" Animals we say have sensitive souls, by which they feel and move. 

Man alone has a spiritual soul, on the nature of which there are diverse concepts. 

The Church teaches that man is a composite of matter and mind by the union of his material body with his spiritual soul. The Platonic doctrine, taught of Origen, that the soul had a previous existence before birth is condemned by the Church. 

Her only teaching regarding the soul's origin, but, which she never defined as an article of faith, is that the human soul is created directly by God and infused into the body, as soon as the body is in a condition to receive it.

The Church also disapproves the teaching that the soul could be transmitted from one's parents; for a rational and spiritual substance could not be generated from a material substance. And, of course, the theory that the soul is a combination of parts, coming partly from the father and partly from the mother, could find no acceptance by the Church; because the soul is a simple substance, and as such, cannot be divided.

Though man's soul and body will be separated by death, they will be re-united at the resurrection, and, as a glorified compound of soul and body, man will remain for all eternity in heaven. The human body being thus destined for re-union with the soul in a glorified state, the Church teaches us to treat our bodies with respect, and to regard them as an object of dignity. It furthermore exhorts us to discipline our bodies in the way of perfection, that they may become perfect instruments of the soul, both of which must be preserved to eternal life. -52-

THE HUMAN BODY 

THE Catholic doctrine, though the most spiritual religion in the world holds the greatest respect and consideration for the human body. She condemns the errors taught by the Manicheans, the Montanists, the Albigenses, and later by the English- American Puritans and other sects, that the human body is to be looked upon as an evil thing. The 

Church teaches that the progress of the soul depends on its union with the body and that all growth in holiness ceases for the soul when it is separated from the body by death. Whatever the lot of the soul after death it is the consequence of its union with the body in time. Hence the importance of the rôle played by the body in sanctification. Every degree of sanctifying grace, and consequent merit attained by the soul in this life requires the concurrence of the senses with the soul. 

The practice of virtue which contributes to our sanctity depends on the body. It is the body that is chaste and temperate, strong and courageous. 

The quality of our prayer depends on the higher senses, the imagination and the affections. The body bears the pain and mortification that sanctify the soul, and contributes to divine worship by exterior reverence and attention. 

The respect the Church teaches for the human body is based on the Incarnation in which the Son of God assumed our flesh as a means to our Redemption, on the fact that He took His human body to Heaven with Him at the Ascension, and that for all eternity it will be united to the Second Person of the Holy Trinity. And also on the fact that the souls of those who are saved will live eternally in a spiritualized glorified body in Heaven. -53-

THE HUMAN SOUL 

WHEN we speak of man's soul as a spirit it is hard for us to form an idea of the term "spirit," for we are accustomed to associate a reality with matter. We know that God is a spirit, and the angels are spirits; but man's soul is as truly a spirit as God or the angels. Man's soul, however, being created for union with a material body is of the lower order of spirits.

It is by the consideration of the attributes of a spirit that we can best understand spirit nature. 

Among these attributes might be mentioned, particularly, activity. The activity of a spirit is intellect and will. The power of a spirit is the strength of its will; its size is the greatness of its intellect. 

Because of its incorruptibility a spirit is immortal.

A spirit sees and knows everything directly and without any intervention. Its knowledge is infused from above. It neither hesitates in action, nor changes in determination. And its action is to the full extent of its knowledge. A spirit has agility by which it moves through space with the swiftness of thought. A spirit cannot suffer pain, as human beings can. Material things are no obstacle to its movement and penetration. But these are but a few of the attributes of spirit life. 

Man's soul when released from the body has, by its nature, all of these attributes. But they are latent as long as it is held in union with the body in this life. -54-

HEREDITY AND THE SOUL SCIENCE and theology both confirm the popular belief that the defects and qualities of parents and forebears are transmitted to their children. But since the soul is created directly by God we are inclined to think that it should be free from the laws of heredity; that man should be what his soul made him and not what his parents made him. 

Theology, however, teaches us that God shapes the soul He creates according to the body into which He infuses it. He forms it in keeping with the predispositions of the nature to which He joins it. And, like all God's creatures, souls are very diverse as to quality. The soul, infused into the already living organism, does not change this organism. It only raises it to a higher life. But this higher life is carried on in the inherent qualities, dispositions, characteristics of the body into which it is infused. The soul working through the body in which it is placed does not alter the qualities or defects it finds in that organism. 

However, there are self-evident truths that all men believe and accept; these come from the workings of the soul and are not affected by heredity. 

Neither are acts produced by man's free will modified by the laws of heredity. Hence man's free will has a certain control over the dispositions of heredity. -55-

WHY WE ARE HERE 

EVEN if we know the material of which a machine is made and how it works, we cannot tell much about it if we do not know the object or purpose for which it was made. To know this we shall have to seek the information left by the inventor. 

We know little about man until we know why God made him. To know this we shall have to consult the facts God wished the world to know about him. These facts are the ideas God had in mind when He made man, and we shall find them in Revelation. Outside this source of knowledge we have no means of knowing why man is here. 

No genius would leave an invention behind him without giving some inkling as to its purpose. God who is supreme intelligence could not have left us ignorant of such an important truth as His reason or object in creating man. To live with the minimum of intelligence we must know why we are here, otherwise our existence is pointless. We have a hint of it in our craving for happiness. And

God who put that craving in our hearts did not do so without the intention of satisfying it. So we readily accept the answer of our penny catechism to the question: Why did God make you? God made me to know, love and serve Him in this life, and to be happy with Him for ever in the next. -56-

MAN'S PLACE IN THE UNIVERSE 

As a result of the modern trend of considering man but as a cog in the wheel of state, as well as of the tendency of modern writers to speak with contempt of the worth of the individual, we need to be frequently reminded of the reality of human dignity.

In spite of the imperfection of man's fallen state, he is still the microcosm of creation. By his relations with the material and spiritual worlds he is a recapitulation of the whole creation. He has common substances with the mineral world. He grows, develops, fades and dies like the plants of the vegetable kingdom. He has common traits with the rest of the animal kingdom, drawing, like them, his nourishment from the soil, and sharing their sensitive life. Like the angels, by his powers of intellect and free will, he understands and acts according to the laws of reason. By these same powers of soul he mirrors even the likeness of God. 

Because of all these attributes and endowments he is spoken of by the Fathers of the Church as the high priest of creation, through whom all living creatures offer homage and adoration to the Creator. -57-

LIFE'S GOAL 

THE atheist tries to persuade himself and others that the grave is the goal of human life. But the question: "What comes after death?" troubles many would-be atheists. Some try to get information on the matter by consulting "the dead." But the knowledge gleaned from spiritualism is most deceptive and meager. It does, however, prove the existence of a spirit world.

To know what is at the end of a road we consult some one who has first hand knowledge from having been there. God, who is the author of life, has sent His Son to give us the knowledge we need as to the end of life, the goal to which we are travelling. He has assured us beyond the shadow of a doubt of the existence of a Heaven in which we shall be eternally unspeakably happy, and of a Hell in which we shall dwell in everlasting misery, according to the use we make of our time here below. Without forcing us, for He has given us the freedom of choice, He urges us to do all that depends upon us to secure the one and to avoid the other. - 58 -

EDUCATION AND SUCCESS 

THE object of most parents in the education of their children is to give these a means of making a success of life. But what in reality is success? An invention is a success when it carries out the idea of the inventor. A camera is a success if it takes good pictures. Man's life is a success when he attains the end for which his Maker intended him. And that end, we learn, is to know, love and serve God in this life and to be happy with Him for ever in Heaven.

Education, then, is a means to success when it prepares man to attain his end; when it points the way to his eternal destiny and teaches him how he may best reach it. This should be the first object of education. To prepare man to hold a rank in society, and to perform his social duties is only secondary. But any formation he may receive that turns him away from his end is decidedly detrimental.

All methods of education that fail to inculcate a knowledge of God and of the eternal verities, however efficiently they prepare man for this life, are hopelessly incomplete. They might be compared to manufacturing processes for turning out airplanes, which crash to the ground as soon as they take to the air. -59-

SCIENCE AND RELIGION 

To the true scientist and the same thinker there is absolutely no clash between science and religion. 

Every intelligent individual knows that science can prove nothing, whatever its negative assertions, with regard to the existence of God, the immortality of the soul, the origin of the universe or the reality of free will. Science may affirm that the telescope or the microscope do not disclose the existence of God, and that surgical operations give no indication of a spiritual soul. But all true thinkers know that scientific observation is not the only way of discovering reality. Yet some anti-Christian professors of science in our modern institutions of learning attack the teachings of the Church in the name of science in such a way as to make one think there was open war between the Church and science. These same "scientists" present the study of such subjects as biology, physics, psychology and astronomy in a way capable of undermining the faith and morals of our young people. Of course these latter lay themselves open to attacks on their faith, not only by going to such teachers, but by their superficial knowledge of the truths of their religion. -09-

WOMAN'S SOCIAL RANK AND THE GOSPEL 

THE Condition of woman was most unenviable before the preaching of Christianity, as it still is in all lands where the Gospel has not yet been preached. The dignity accorded by the Catholic 

Church to the Mother of God has had far-reaching consequences on woman's condition in society. 

Wherever the Gospel was announced woman was raised socially, and her lot gradually softened until it reached the social rank she holds today. The process was long, but like the abolition of slavery, it was the fruit of Catholic culture. 

It was to the love and veneration of the Madonna that women owed the respect Catholic knighthood paid them in the Middle Ages, a respect that continued to grow; and that will diminish only when women cease to revere and practice the virtues which devotion to the Blessed Virgin caused to blossom forth in Christian society. -61-

OUR FALLEN STATE 

THERE are those today who deny the doctrine of the fall of man. But if we preclude this doctrine, what explanation can we give for the inconsistencies of man's nature, his inclination to evil and the vitiated condition of his nature? How can we account for the mental attitude that believes in the goodness of God and rebels against Him? Or how can we explain man's giving himself up to all that is base in his nature when the voice of his conscience reproaches him, and when he knows the degradation and misery he will bring upon himself by abandoning himself to his passions?

We cannot believe that God, who gave man free will, made it easier for him to follow his baser than his nobler instincts. We are forced to conclude that man has undergone a moral and physical shock that has affected his senses, his intellect and his will. Had our holy religion not given us the explanation of our fallen state it would have forever remained a mystery. The doctrine of Original 

Sin, or the sin of our first parent, Adam, through which he lost for himself, for Eve and for us the supernatural and preternatural gifts which God had bestowed upon him, and his consequent fall from original justice is the key to the enigma of our fallen state. · 62 ·

MEMORIES OF EDEN 

THOUGH the doctrine of Original Sin, or the fall of man, cannot, strictly speaking, be proved by reason, it is, nevertheless, not contrary to reason, but fully in harmony with it. Without this doctrine we cannot have an intelligible explanation of life. 

The Catholic Church anathematizes anyone who denies it, and Tradition and the Scriptures confirm it.

It is evident from the traditions of the human race that man possessed marvelous powers in a state of elevation and happiness that are no longer attainable for him here below. There are traces of this fact in the yearnings of mankind for some indefinable nobility it once had; yearnings that are like the sigh of the exile for the home in which he was once happy, and which is still a hazy recollection. 

The poetry and music of every race expresses lamentations for a lost bliss, for a distant Eden, for an "Isle of the Blessed" that is no longer to be found. There are traces of these memories mingled with the folklore of every people. In fact, the ancient literatures of primitive peoples give startling proofs of the original happiness of man on earth and of his fall. Plato makes mention of the

Golden Age, which according to Greek tradition existed at the world's origin. The Romans write of the happy age in Saturn's reign which they sadly contrast with the succeeding Iron Age. Other Latin writers laud in poetic tones the heroic ages when work was pleasure, and pain was unknown, when the teeming earth spontaneously supplied man's every need, and the animals were his docile servants and friends. The sacred writings of the Chinese tell of the first ages of the world when pure pleasure -63-

and perfect peace reigned upon earth, and evil was unknown. The Persian and Hindu writings have similar traditions, many of which resemble the nar rative given in Chapter III of Genesis.

WHY ARE WE PUNISHED FOR ADAM'S SIN? 

AT first sight it seems unjust that the whole human race should have to suffer for the sin of an ancestor; for after all, sin is something personal that cannot be conveyed from one to another. But it must be understood that, besides the act of sin, there is the disposition, or state of the soul, that results from sin. The sinner distorts his nature and turns it away from its final end, which is God. 

His nature thus changed, remains in sin until God restores it.

Original sin in us is not the act of Adam, but the condition in human nature which results from Adam's act, and which is transmitted to us with our nature, which comes to us from our first parents. 

Besides injuring his person, Adam also injured his nature. And though its essential properties are still intact, there remains a corruption which we inherit.

God endowed our first parents with three kinds of gifts: natural, preternatural and supernatural. 

The natural gifts include, understanding, free will and immortality of soul. The preternatural gifts implied, immortality of body, freedom from pain, infused knowledge, and control of the powers of body and soul. The greatest of these gifts, however, was that of Sanctifying Grace, by which man was made Godlike. By his sin Adam lost all but the natural gifts; and even these are affected. — 65 —

THE NATURE OF ADAM'S SIN 

MANY speculations as to the nature of Adam's sin are current in our day. Some have thought that it consisted in a moral lapse that had its origin in an inordinate love of Adam for Eve. This opinion is, however, untenable, as evil inclinations, or concupiscence was the result of the fall. Adam is pictured in Holy Scripture as possessed of great wisdom by which he was aware that all animal nature was something beneath the state in which God had constituted him. His nature, being angelic, was not open to carnal allurements until after the fall. He had intimate relations with God in Paradise, “Who walked with him in the cool of the evening," and gave him a precept under pain of death. 

Neither was Adam's sin a sin of gluttony. The first man in his native integrity was incapable of committing a sin which supposes a lack of control over the appetites of the body. Holy Scripture tells us that the tempter insisted on an idea that had more weight with Eve: "In the day ye shall eat your eyes shall be opened and ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil". From the whole context it was clearly a sin of pride, expressed by an act of disobedience to a precept imposed by God. -66-

THE EXISTENCE OF GOD 

It is hard to believe in the sincerity of people who deny the existence of God. This truth is so clearly impressed on human consciousness that we are led to believe that those who gainsay it are either lying, or their moral life has ceased to function normally.

That there is nothing beyond what our physical eyes can see is hard to believe; for when we contemplate a world everywhere in motion we are urged to ask ourselves: "Whence that motion?”

Then too we have continued manifestations of life, light and love. The heavens, says Holy Scripture, declare the glory of God, and the firmament shows forth the work of His hands. If we use our intellect at all we cannot fail to recognize that there must be a power that creates, protects and governs all things.

The Church insists that the existence of God can be proved by the natural light of human reason, for the Church has the greatest esteem for human reason. St. Thomas Aquinas, following Aristotle, gives these argruments to prove the existence of God: From things that move we can ascend to the 

Mover. From the efficient cause we can go to the 

First Cause. From things that depend on other things we can go to that which does not depend, to the Independent. From beings more or less perfect we can go to the Absolute Perfection. From order and design we can ascend to the Almighty Artist who ordered, designed, harmonized all things. - 67-

GOD REVEALED 

THOUGH reason urges upon us the existence of God, yet we cannot form a picture of Him by our imagination. But though we believe in many things through the effects they produce we cannot therefore picture them to ourselves. We cannot, for instance, form an image of electricity to our mind, though we believe in it by its effects. In the same way God reveals Himself to us in His works. The marvelous mechanism of the human body and of the solar system, for instance, reveals to us His wisdom and power, and hence His existence. 

We can fortunately supplement the knowledge our reason gives us by what God has let us know about Himself through Revelation. The Old Testament reveals God to us before He sent His Son to us in the mystery of the Incarnation. The New 

Testament reveals Him to us in terms we can better understand. For here we see Him clothed in our flesh in all His wisdom, love and power. This part of Holy Scripture depicts His life of thirty-three years among us in all its divine beauty and goodness; then it shows Him to us in the mystery of the 

Passion and Death by which He redeemed us. And finally it portrays Him in His glorious Resurrection and during the forty days He spent on earth before His visible Ascension into Heaven, from which He sent us under a visible form the Holy Spirit that was to animate and govern the Church-His Kingdom on earth-until the end of time. -68-

GOD WITH US 

ONE of the great eternal truths which dawns upon us but gradually as we advance in holy living is the presence of God in our soul. That the Divine 

Trinity, Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, dwells in every soul in the state of grace, as truly as the soul dwells in the body, is frequently affirmed in Holy 

Scripture. 

It is true that God is everywhere in the universe by His power and immensity, in every creature that came from His Hands. But He is present in a different manner in the souls of the just. The same person may be present in the place under different titles, and in different dispositions. In the house a man may be present as a workman, as a servant, as a secretary, as a friend, as a father. In the sanctified soul God may be present as a Father, as a Brother, as a Friend as a Spouse. His presence in the universe is often hostile to the creatures to whom He gave life and being, because of their resistence to His love, as in the case with the evil spirits or the lost souls. In the soul of the just He is present in a special way by virtue of His own gift of Sanctifying Grace, because He finds something of Himself there. This special divine union which God contracts with the soul grows in intimacy as the soul increases in Sanctifying Grace. -69-

THE DOCTRINE OF THE MYSTICAL BODY 

WHEN we think of our Blessed Lord we are apt to picture Him as He walked among men during His mortal life, doing good. Or again we try to represent Him in His glorious life at the Right Hand of the Father, or silent and lonely in the Tabernacle. 

What we forget, or fail to understand, is His mysti Ical life in our souls. Yet, St. John and St. Paul make the doctrine of our living union with Christ the center of their teaching with regard to Christ. 

St. John, to make us understand this union, brings us the figure used by Christ Himself; the parable of the vine: "I am the vine, you the branches: he that abideth in me, and I in him, the same beareth much fruit: for without me you can do nothing."-John 15-5.

This union between the soul and Jesus Christ is the principle, the source, of the supernatural life that is in us. It is expressed by St. Paul, who makes use of a similar figure, when he writes: "As the body is one, and hath many members and all the members of the body, whereas they are many, yet are one body, so also is Christ. For in one Spirit were we all baptised into one body."-1 Cor. 12-13.

The Council of Trent combines these two figures of the two apostles in speaking of our mystical union with Christ in these words: "As the head commands the other members, as the sap of the trunk penetrates the branches, so Christ exercises

His influence at every moment on the just. It is this influence that precedes, accompanies and crowns their every good work, and renders these pleasing and meritorious in His sight." In consequence of this doctrine we are living members of Christ's Mystical Body, the Church, Christ being -70-

the Head, and we the members. This Mystical 

Body comprises three branches: the Church on earth, the Church in Purgatory, and the Church in 

Heaven, or the Church Militant, Suffering and

Glorious, the members of which are in union one with another through the Communion of Saints.

CHRIST THE KING 

JESUS CHRIST came to earth to accomplish a tremendous task, to fulfill a momentous mission. 

Yet He made the earth no richer by any material wealth or comforts He brought. He carried no money on His person; He had no fixed abode; He travelled, for the most part, on foot, and lived in rigid austerity. When He was faint with hunger from fasting He refused to turn stones into bread. 

His disciples lived as He did, and He made no effort to improve their material condition. In addressing the people He spoke to them with simplicity and modesty, refusing to impress them by any display of power and majesty. 

His object was to establish His Kingdom in the souls of men. But though He admitted that He was a King, He wore no crown or symbol of regal power. He was surrounded by no royal retinue, and when He was seized by His enemies He forbade those who would defend Him to use a sword. When the people would crown Him King, He fled. He made it understood that He considered earthly kingdoms as mere trifles.

It was, however, evident that He possessed a secret divine power.

He admitted that He had only to ask His Father for legions, and that His request would be granted. It was known that He had raised the dead; that disease fled at His touch; that the demons obeyed His slightest command; that the hungry crowd had been fed by the few loaves He had multiplied; that the tempest was appeased at His word; that life, death, heaven, earth and hell bowed to His behest. -72-

THE SOUL'S SWEET GUEST 

We know that the soul in the state of grace is the living temple of God, the abode of the Adorable Trinity. But when we think of the indwelling of the Divinity in the soul, our thoughts dwell more on the Father as our Creator and Preserver, and of the Son as our Saviour and Redeemer. The Holy 

Ghost, as our Sanctifier, it is to be feared, occupies our thoughts much less. The Holy Ghost, the 

Third Person of the Holy Trinity, is still for many 

The Great Unknown. We forget what He is to our souls.

From the moment the priest says over the child he is baptizing, "Depart from him (her) unclean spirit, and yield thy place to the Holy Ghost", that Divine Spirit takes up His abode in the soul of the baptized and dwells there for all time, unless He is driven hence by mortal sin. "Know you not that your members are the temple of the Holy Ghost, who is in you, whom you have from God, and you are not your own," writes St. Paul.

The Church speaks of the Holy Ghost in the liturgy of Pentecost as "Dulces Hospes animae", the 

Soul's Sweet Guest, a most appropriate title because of the sweetness He brings into the faithful soul. -73-

OUR NEED OF THE HOLY SPIRIT 

NEVER before did Christians so need to turn to the Holy Ghost, the Spirit of Light and Truth, for protection and guidance as in these our days, when the powers of darkness are working so feverishly to destroy the Kingdom of Christ on earth and in the souls of men. The Holy Ghost is in a special sense the ruler of the last ages of the world. 

We, who would uphold the banner of Christ, in spite of the efforts of the powers of evil to snatch it from our grasp, must have recourse, with ever stronger insistence, to the Holy Spirit to defend the Kingdom of God on earth-His Holy Church. 

What the infernal forces deny, blaspheme, and try to destroy, we must confess, honor and defend. 

To ignorance and doubt we must oppose knowledge of our holy religion and faith in its teachings. To hate an injustice we must oppose love, charity, and holy living. Where truth is destroyed in the minds and souls of men we must try to restore it and to make it more powerful and active.

To do all this we need the light and strength of the Holy Spirit. Let us ask it frequently by the liturgical prayers of the Church, especially by the "Veni Creator" and the "Veni Sancti Spiritus." - 74-

DIVINE LOVE SUFFICES WORLDLINGS are sometimes scandalized when they hear the spiritual-minded speak of a love of 

God that dominates and sanctifies every other love of the heart. They are especially so when they hear it said that the love of God replaces the natural sentiments that exist between married people in the case of persons consecrated to God. 

They consider such ideas as blindness and folly, and pity those who entertain them. For them love is but a thrill of the senses, and appeals but to the physical side of man's nature. To love God as a 

Spouse is at best but a figure of speech, they say. 

But whether or not the worldly-minded admit of the existence of such a love, it is a beautiful and astounding reality. God wishes to be loved as a Spouse by many souls, but especially by those He calls to the altar or to the religious state. To these He wishes to be all things, and that they find in Him their unique treasure, their very life. From them He exacts the sacrifice of all other absorbing affections in order that they may love Him more perfectly. -75-

CAUSE OF OUR JOY 

WE CANNOT always keep our earthly mother near us no matter how much we should like to do so. 

She must leave us some day. But the Mother whom Christ gave us to be our own can be with us always, in all the joys and sorrows of life. Her presence, though invisible, is none the less real.

And, as those who are devoted to her know, she has a way of making us experience her influence and help. It is Mary's influence that makes the heart of the fervent Catholic ever joyful; hence she is addressed in the prayers of the Church as "Cause of our joy."

It has been frequently remarked by Catholic writers that the gloom that has settled on the sects who have abandoned the cult of the Blessed Virgin comes from the fact that they are motherless. 

Our very unworthiness should draw us to our 

Heavenly Mother, for she is the hope of the hopeless, the joy of the joyless. She may not remove our pains; she knows perhaps how needful they are to us. But surely she will soothe them by giving us strength to bear them. She consoles without lessening our merit. - 76 —

RELIGION IN PRACTICE 

RELIGION in its broadest interpretation is that which binds man to God, the word coming from the Latin “religere," to bind again. The Christian who practices his religion is one who is redeemed and regenerated by "Water and the Holy Ghost," and who is bound anew, "re-ligio" to the Living God. Subjectively, religion is the attitude of the man who regulates his life and actions according to right reason and divine revelation. Objectively speaking it is the observance of the Commandments of God and the teachings of the Church, by means of which man renders to God the worship he owes Him as a creature, and doing so, works out his own eternal destiny.

Religion is not "the dope of the people" for it is anything but mere sentimentality or nervous emotionalism. Whoever conceives it as such does not understand the strong, if gentle, thing the

Catholic religion is. And yet religion meets the needs of the human heart for sympathy and love; for it unites man to God by love and gives him the help and strength he needs to bear the pains of life and to fulfill his duties to his neighbor. At the same time it enlightens his conscience as to good and evil, right and wrong. And it is the basis of all moral and social virtues.

In the degree in which a man practices religion subjectively and objectively, in that degree will he fulfill the duties of his domestic and social life; in that degree will he be honest, just, temperate and virtuous before God and man. -77-

WORSHIP AN OBLIGATION 

Do we realize sufficiently the duty of worship, of the adoration we owe to God as our Maker, Preserver, Redeemer and Last End?

The object of our life in this world is to prepare ourselves for the true life that is to come. Our sojourn here is surrounded by many undesirable conditions that prevent this exile from being an ideal state. Life, as we know it, is far from satisfying the craving for happiness that is at the bottom of our being. We are the work of God's 

Hands. He placed us here to know Him, to love 

Him, and to serve Him in view of being everlastingly happy with Him in His glory. But how are we to serve Him?

The Church, the institution He founded to teach, govern, sanctify and save men, has prescribed that part of the service we owe God as worship, the chief expression of which is prayer. And because the Church is charged with the organization of the worship of God she places prayer continually on the lips of her priests and her religious, that the spirit of prayer may not die out on earth. She furthermore enjoins on the faithful the obligation of prayer at appointed times that they may render to God the homage of worship due to His Divine Majesty. -78-

WHY PRAY? 

IT IS a sad condition of the human mind today that men have forgotten how to pray. The spiritual, the supernatural order will not be entirely dominated by the material as long as men and women continue to pray. But our modern activities, our feverish efforts are carried out, for the most part, on natural principles. We are trying to lean on an arm of flesh, and the arm of God ceases to sustain us. Our present generation has been brought up with an indifference, not to say scorn, for eternal verities, and with a lack of reverence for the universal beliefs of mankind. They have been fed on preconceptions, and science-crazed, until they look upon prayer as something bordering superstition.

Even our Catholic people are tremendously in need of being continually reminded of the necessity and duty of prayer. For though they have been taught this duty from childhood, they cannot escape the infection of social environment. Prayer, we must remember is a command imposed by God 

Himself; and no Christian having the use of reason can be saved unless he prays. -79-

PRAYER AND ANSWERS 

OUR faith is frequently tried by the many good people who pray earnestly to God for favors without receiving them. Hearts afflicted by sorrow, sickness, misfortune, and distress of varied kinds have besought heaven in vain, while the impious and unbelieving have often the desires of their hearts granted without prayer. Yet God has promised to hear our prayers. Why then is He deaf to our entreaties?

Let us imagine the conditions of life if every 

Christian and Catholic had his prayers answered.

There would be no poverty, no sickness, no pain of mind or heart, no misfortune of any kind in the Christian world and the churches would be full day and night with devout followers "for the loaves and fishes." We easily realize that a religion in which every request for temporal favors was granted would be a religion devoid of the spiritual element, and wholly without the purpose for which religion exists. Especially would it be without suffering, without the cross, and Christianity is founded on the mystery of the Cross. 

To understand God's reasons for refusing frequently to grant our temporal requests is to understand that He has designs on our souls that surpass our limited views. His end in creating us was to lead us by diverse ways, often mysterious to us, to our eternal destiny. When He told us to ask and we should receive it was with the understanding that what we asked would be in keeping with the good of our souls. -80-

PRAYERS THAT ARE ANSWERED

GOD does frequently grant our prayers for temporal favors when they are addressed to Him in the proper spirit. But He does not always grant them, even when we pray with the required dispositions. 

To understand God's reasons for not granting our requests is to understand the purpose of religion, which, before all, is concerned with God's own interests. And these are always in keeping with our spiritual good and our eternal happiness.

God's end in placing us here is the end for which 

He redeemed us: to bring us to eternal happiness.

But He did not promise to give us this happiness in time.

When He promised to hear our prayers it was the understanding that we should not ask for anything that would conflict with His designs on our souls or thwart our eternal salvation.

Prayers that are addressed to God with the proper dispositions for spiritual blessings will always be answered, at least the blessings in keeping with our eternal well-being will result from them. When we ask for grace to overcome temptations, for forgiveness of our sins, and the punishment due to them, for a happy death, for final perseverance, for the conversion of a sinner-provided he does not place his will in opposition to God's-for the salvation of our souls, all such prayers will be granted, we have God's word for it. -81-

PRAYER AND PEACE OF MIND 

PRACTICAL as we are and subtle in finding out the things that contribute to our physical wellbeing we have overlooked, not only the spiritual but, the natural advantages of prayer. 

A physician who had much experience in dealing with the maladies of body and mind, though little interested in the supernatural aspect of life, wrote not long ago that the best sleep-producing agent which his practice had revealed to him was prayer. It is, he tells us, the most adequate and normal of all pacifiers of the mind and calmers of nerves.

It would be a blessing if such human testimony could reach the ears of those who are seeking peace of mind and quietness of nerves in narcotics. Let us hasten to acquaint ourselves with the science of prayer and prove its efficacy as a restorer of peace of mind. We can bear the tempests that rage around us if our souls, our minds and our hearts are quieted by the peace that God alone can give. -82-

THE LITURGICAL MOVEMENT 

THE Catholic Church is for ever busy renewing her youth in one manner or another. One of her actual movements in this direction is called the Liturgical Movement. By this movement she is reeducating the laity in an intimate, intelligent actual participation in the ceremonies of Catholic worship.

There is a tendency on the part of the laity, as a whole, to be present at public worship, especially at the holy sacrifice of the Mass, without taking an active and intelligent part in the ceremonies. 

It is the object of the movement in question to give the faithful a clear understanding of the meaning of each ceremony by explaining its symbolism and bringing them to associate themselves with the services of the Church as in its early days. 

To do this she is insisting in a special way on the use of the Missal at Mass, in which the faithful follow the different parts of the Mass, step by step with the priest. She is even trying to have the people answer the prayers of the priest in Latin, a vernacular translation of which is given side by 

Iside with the Latin.

To fulfill the obligation of hearing Mass it is required only to be present in body at the entire sacrifice, and to give the different ceremonies at least general attention. People may hear Mass by saying their own private prayers or by reciting the rosary. But the ideal and proper way is to follow the prayers with the priest and to enter into the spirit of the different ceremonies. 88-

MYSTICISM 

A CONTEMPORARY writer remarks that there are few words in the modern vocabulary more abused than the word "mysticism." All the vague sentiment, chaotic wit, weird poesy, and political rhetoric; all the dust heaps of absurdities, all the rubbish from the depths of sensuality to the heights of nonsense that happens to be fashionable are dubbed "mystical" by modern writers. Indeed it is a term applied sometimes to the whole of Catholic teaching by agnostic and anti-Catholic writers.

In its original sense, the word "mysticism” means "hidden"; in its practical and Catholic application it means all that is beyond the grasp of the senses and the intellect, but not beyond human approach. 

It might be defined as the secret experience of the soul in its quest for God. It might also be called the intimate experience of the fervent and faithful soul in the final stages of perfection, in which God sometimes reveals Himself in extraordinary ways.

If a soul is called to the mystical state, these extraordinary favors raise the natural faculties to a condition of divine activity which they could not otherwise possess. Mysticism might, therefore, be described as a state of extraordinary supernatural knowledge and activity.

It is in the last stages of the spiritual life, known as the Unitive Way that God grants mystical experiences to those He calls to the Mystical Life. -84-

MYSTICAL EXPERIENCES 

THE term "mystical experiences" is frequently used in connection with the lives of certain saints in the hagiology of the Catholic Church, and it is well for Catholics to be able to explain just what is meant by the term.

These extraordinary experiences are not granted to all the saints, so we give the name "mystics" to saints and other souls who receive them. These experiences are varied; some are unspeakably delightful, others painful and even terrible. Sometimes the senses and the body share in the joyful and wonderful impressions of the soul. We read of mystics in their passive states seeing and hearing things that took place at enormous distances, and in times long past. Some of the mystics have been raised in the air, carried through space, transported to other worlds, or have been present simultaneously in two places. Even in our own time we hear of mystics undergoing incredible fasts, abstaining from all kinds of food, except the Holy Eucharist for years. Certain perfumes and lights have been known to emanate from the bodies of saints, even after their death. The annals of mysticism, past and contemporary, contain accounts of those who have received the Wounds of the Passion in their own bodies. The Sacred Stigmata-and of their bleeding profusely from these wounds on certain days, and at certain seasons. But space prevents even the enumeration of the mystical manifestations that God has deigned to confer upon certain chosen members of His Church. -85-

PSEUDO-MYSTICISM 

We must not think that all marvelous manifestations of which we read are the result of true mysticism. There have been enough pseudomysticism and psychic fads in men's experiences to make people of sane judgment skeptical when they hear of such an extraordinary phenomenon.

True mysticism is characterized by preliminary stages of purification through which the soul passes before it attains the delights that belong to the higher stages. The essential character of pseudomysticism is the desire to arrive at mystical delights without passing through the painful stages. 

True mystics are rare, for there are few who are willing to pay the price at which the gift is purchased. True mysticism is essentially penitential.

It is never separated from the cross. We learn this from the words of the great mystic, St. Teresa of 

Avila, who speaks thus to her daughters in religion: "I assure you, daughters, those of you whom God does not call to contemplation (mystical prayer), that both by observation and experience, I know that those who follow it do not bear a light cross. 

You would be aghast at the trials God sends them 

The Church exercises much caution in the examination of all mystical experiences. She does not oblige us to believe even in the private and particular revelations and visions of the saints, though she advises us not to reject them too lightly when they are affirmed by the saints. It is, nevertheless, true that many of the saints were deceived. 

Satan is clever in transforming himself into an angel of light in order to deceive even the saints.

This is why the Church is so careful in dealing with mystical phenomena. -86-

THE CHURCH AND THE MODERN MIND 

THE ideas and fashions of the hour by their very freshness and novelty exercise a potent attraction on the popular mind-an attraction entirely out of proportion with their actual worth. As a result, there is a large percentage of illusion in what the world calls modern, up-to-date, fashionable. But the popular mind fails to see this, and despises all that it calls old-fashioned, antiquated, out-of-date. 

For this reason the Church is not popular with the over-excited modern mind. Her serene, timeless, calm attitude toward circumstances and world events irritates the feverish petulant mind. But the Church's attitude transcends the limited vision of the present. Her office is to make the voice of eternity heard above the noise and rush of modern agitation..

The Church is criticized for being too conservative in this age of changing convictions and scientific progress; for she continues to teach the truths she taught nineteen centuries ago. But we must always remember that the Church has for mission to hand down unchanged the truths Jesus Christ revealed to her and which she jealously guards unchanged, and will continue to do so until the end of time. The only change she can admit is the manner of presenting these truths which varies according to the intellectual development of the times. She makes these truths more or less explicit, but she never, for that reason, changes her doctrine. Once the bandage of illusion and falsehood is removed from the eyes of the modern man he acknowledges that the Church is the real nurse of freedom, the custodian of truth, and that she always releases the human soul from the tyranny of the temporal and the bondage of falsehood. -87-

THE CHURCH AND PRIVATE REVELATION 

IT IS the aim of the Church to present unaltered the truth which Christ has revealed and commissioned her to teach to the world. With the death of the last Apostle the work of Revelation was at an end. The Church can add no more to what she canonically calls Revelation. New dogmas which she proclaims from time to time are not revelation, but merely a fuller explanation of that which was given once for all.

There have been in the course of the Church's history private revelations made to the saints. But though the Church does not condemn these, unless they contain something contrary to faith, she does not guarantee their truth. She does, however, encourage the faithful to make use of them for their spiritual benefit, especially those divine communications made to the great saints. These are often quoted in spiritual books, and treated with the greatest respect, but they are not articles of faith, hence there is no obligation on the part of Catholics to believe them, though it would be irreverent to speak of them in a disrepectful way since the Church has canonized those to whom they were made. -88-

THE CHURCH AND SOCIAL ORDER 

IT IS not the Church's primary aim on earth to construct a social order. Her concern is chiefly with the individual. She interests herself in social reform only in as much as it is connected with eternal realities. Christ proclaimed that His Kingdom was not of this world. The Church is the visible manifestation of that Kingdom, whose center is in Heaven.

Why then does the Church have anything to say about the social question? First, because she has to concern herself with the earthly conditions in which her children have to work out their eternal salvation. Secondly because she knows it is her duty to establish and maintain in society the principles of justice and charity brought to this world by Jesus Christ, her Founder. She has the right to inquire into the laws that govern family life, the education of her children and the economic conditions that will enable them to live in human decency. It is less the economic than the moral conditions with which she is concerned, however. The 

Church knows that the spiritual crisis of actual life is the result of social discontent, arising from injustice and selfishness on the part of individuals, and it is to remedy as far as possible this condition that she urges the laity to engage in Catholic 

Action. 

- 89-

THE VISIBLE CHURCH 

EXCEPT when the Catholic Church is persecuted she works openly and all her members are publicly known to be Catholics. Three hundred and fifty million people acknowledge her authority. She is, therefore the most visible and influential international institution that exists. 

Many worldly-minded people see in the Church but "a mere fabric of dogmas, laws, ceremonies and pious practices." They are blind to the beneficient, divinely commissioned work she is carrying on in every land. The inability to see the full strength and beauty of spiritual reality keeps thousands away from the Church. But how far each is responsible for this spiritual blindness is a matter of deep importance to all concerned. The poet reminds us that "There are none so blind as those who will not see"; and there must be many such outside the Church. -90-

THE INFALLIBLE CHURCH 

ONE of the attributes of the Catholic Church is Infallibility. This gift claimed by the Church does not mean that every word spoken by a priest or bishop of the Church even when spoken from the pulpit, is to be considered infallible or regarded as an article of faith. The Infallibility of the Church does not even extend to every opinion or judgment expressed by the Pope. It is promised only to the 

Pope, and the teaching authority of the Church, when they formally define a doctrine of faith and morals.

In The teaching authority of the Church is rigidly limited to truths dealing with faith and morals; that is, to all the truths revealed by God as necessary or conducive to the salvation of souls. carrying out her divine mission of teaching the truth to the whole world it is necessary that the Holy Ghost preserve her from error, so that she may be able to decide with certainty which ethical or theological statements are true and which false. -91-

THE POPE 

ALL human organizations must have a head, a leader a governor. The Catholic Church, being an organization, must have a head, a chief, a father. 

Jesus Christ is the Head of the entire Church founded by Him, which includes the Church on earth, the Church in Purgatory, and the Church in Heaven. But for the Church on earth He is invisible. He is, therefore, represented by His Vicar, whom we call the Pope, a word meaning "Father."

From the time of the Apostles to the present day the Popes have guided and governed the Church. 

They have struggled fearlessly throughout the ages for the orthodoxy of faith and the purity of the morals taught by the Gospel. They have undergone furious persecution from heresy and infidelity; many have suffered prison, exile and death for the freedom of the Church and the maintenance of her teaching. But the Popes have always expected persecution, for the Founder of the Church, Jesus Christ, warned the Apostles that they would be persecuted as He Himself had been. 

The Pope, as the direct successor of St. Peter, claims the power, given to this latter by Christ, to rule and govern the Church, the Flock of Christ, until the end of time. If the claims of the Pope to be the Vicar of Christ are unfounded, he is a real impostor. But these claims are so securely founded that no Catholic could question them. He has, therefore, the right to the reverence and obedience of all Christians in matters of faith and morals. -92-

HAVING A CATHOLIC OUTLOOK 

TO BE a Catholic is to accept the Church as she really is with all her external drawbacks and imperfections on account of her divine mission. She is the Kingdom of Christ on earth, but she cannot escape the tragedy that belongs to all things embodied in matter. Exteriorly governed by men, she partakes of human frailty and the shortcomings of things here below; hence we must expect dissatisfaction in many things relating to her. 

She is part of reality and we must accept her as such. But we must do all we can to minimize her external drawbacks, take refuge in her bosom, and bear with courage the state of imperfection that is hers as part of all earthly things. The more we realize the divine mission of the Church, the fact that she is commissioned by Christ to lead souls to their eternal destiny, the more we suffer from her earthly frailties, and the dangers and disasters through which she has had to pass. It was one of the great sorrows the saints had to suffer which made them sigh for the Church Glorious. 

To have a truly Catholic outlook, then, we must not take part with those who criticize the Church, and find fault with the frailties of her earthly administration. We must do all we can to remedy these; but what we cannot cure we must, as a logical mind should, learn to endure. - 93-

RELIGION AND PATRIOTISM 

THE idea of home, country and heaven are always included in the Catholic conception of patriotism; and logically so, for the home is the unit of the state, and neither home nor state can ignore the Eternal Kingdom. The laws of that Kingdom are the foundation and bulwark both of home and state.

Those who observe the Ten Commandments do more for their country than all the flag-waving, parading and patriotic manifestations, however, much these serve to arouse enthusiasm. To keep the Ten Commandments is to be a good citizen; to forget them is to find the way to the criminal court and the prison cell. It is because we have ceased to make them the guiding principle of our lives that we need so many armies and navies, and that men are engaged in international slaughter.

The honest voter, the incorruptible lawmaker, the conscientious office holder, the unprejudiced juror and the just judge are the produce of the Ten 

Commandments. 

-94-

THE CHURCH AND WAR 

THE Catholic Church favors war only when every other means of obtaining justice has failed. She uses all means of which she can dispose to preserve peace among nations, and to restore it when it is disturbed. The encyclicals of her Popes are records of her condemnation of all un-Christian philosophies that lead to war.

The Church considers patriotism as a natural virtue that should be cultivated by every Christian. 

And patriotism implies loyalty to country which includes the taking of arms, and even the giving of one's life in her defense. Catholics have sacrificed their lives on the altar of every country in all ages, as the annals of history proclaim. 

Although there is a Commandment of God that says, "Thou shalt not kill,” killing in a just war, as in self-defense, is not included in this precept. 

Under the Law of Moses, to which the Ten Commandments belong, God sanctioned the wars waged by Israel in the conquest of the Promised Land. 

Even should an individual consider a war in which his country is engaged to be unjust he is none the less obliged to go to war when he is called by his country; for he cannot, as an individual, decide the justice of a war, and must therefore leave such a decision to the State. Wars, as a means of securing justice, are to be deplored, but they fill the pages of history from its earliest records, and will probably do so till the end of time. They are but another proof of our fallen state. — 95 —

THE CHURCH AND NATIONALITY 

SOME of our pure-blooded nationalists, who in many ways admire the Church, object to becoming members, because they would have to rub elbows with so many "foreigners" within her walls. But when we meet groups of men and women who shrink from contact with their fellowmen because they speak a different language, or have other traditions than their own, we know they have not the spirit of Christ. It is because the Church is Catholic, that is, universal, because she is supernational that her members often appear foreign. 

It is a miracle of the divine mission of the Church, that she is able to hold so many nationalities, so different in tradition, ideas and customs in her membership in spite of the antipathies that exist among them. The catholicity of the Church may yet serve as an antidote for the race hatred and selfish nationalistic ideas that are actually threatening to destroy civilization. 

It is the action of the Holy Spirit, who governs the Church, that keeps her intact notwithstanding the animosities that have always existed among the nations into which her members are divided. -96-

THE CHURCH AND THE MASSES 

ONE of the Church's great stumbling blocks to the cultured, the learned, the "élite," and which, perhaps, keeps many who are not members outside her walls, is her large membership of people who create a disagreeable or painful impression. 

In the Catholic Church one finds the poor, the miserable, the mentally warped, the narrow, the vulgar. In a word, one finds the thousand forms of disability to which poor human nature is heir. We even hear Catholics who have a tendency to snobbery remark that, though the Church is a dignified, noble and holy mother, she has many queer children. Yes, she does open her doors to all classes who wish to enter the march to her sublime goal, no matter how strange the baggage they are carrying. 

But has not our Lord said, "The poor you will always have with you?" It is by the very presence of the poor, the humble and the disinherited, of those who flocked around him when he was on earth, that we recognize the Church of Christ. 

Before God all men are equal, not on the basis of human distinctions or the lack of these, but on the fact that all are unjust and sinners in His sight. "All have sinned", says St. Paul, "and do need the glory of God, being justified freely by His grace through the redemption that is in Jesus Christ." 

God does not look on man for what he is personally; what He looks upon is what His mercy works through man's good will. In judging men He does not use our weights and measures. His standards are far removed from human wisdom. Great saints and mystics have sprung up in the bosom of the Church from wretched human soil, for the Spirit breathes where He lists. -97-

THE CHURCH AND MARRIAGE 

MARRIAGE, in the mind of the Church, is one of the highest forms of spiritual companionship, in which two children of God who love each other dedicate themselves to His Sovereign Majesty and to each other. The primary end of marriage is the procreation of children and their education as future citizens of Heaven. Its secondary end is the mutual assistance of husband and wife through an ennobling and spiritualized love, and the domination of concupiscence through the practice of religion, especially through the reception of the sacraments. 

The happiness of married life depends upon the delicate adjustment of its physical and spiritual ends. But there are many and serious enemies to this happiness in the notions of marriage entertained today. Not the least of these enemies is the idea that romanticism is to be scorned. Experience has proved that a little romance adds much to the sweetness of married relations and throws a glamor around many a grim reality. But the greatest enemies of married life are the examples and teachings of those who would throw aside the doctrine of the Church with regard to marriage and the laws laid down by Christian morality. No marriage can long be happy where the love of God and the respect of 

His laws are absent. -98-

IS THE CHURCH IN HER AGONY? 

As we look over the world today and see the chaos that reigns everywhere we are tempted to conclude that not only is civilization doomed but also Christianity on which it was founded. It is evident that civilization is diseased at the core, and that Christianity, as manifested by mankind in general today, has lost its vitality and vigor. Christian discipline is reduced to a minimum by the Church, and her demands in the way of self-sacrifice are limited to the essential in an effort to avoid frightening the softened, comfort-seeking generation that we are, and for fear of extinguishing the still smoking reed.

But the Catholic Church has the assurance of her Founder that no matter what perishes in this tempest-torn world, she, like the Ark, will float above the wrecks. Her Spirit, around which the world has formed a thick crust, that threatens to stifle her, is still a living flame, and when that crust, which is already breaking, is swept away by the tempest, she will again penetrate the souls of men, which will be washed and purified to receive her action.

We are more and more convinced as world events rush on that the Church is at the dawn of a glorious resurrection; that Phoenix-like she will arise from the ashes with which the world has covered her. 

Her Spirit is already moving invisibly over the darkened waters of the world and vivifying the souls that are prepared for her action. When society is purified from its errors and sins, and political Utopias are swept from the earth, the Church will again pour oil and wine on the wounds of the world; and urged by hunger, thirst and nakedness men will return to her bosom. -99-

IMMORTALITY 

ALL that we know of man here below does not die with him. When his body is laid to rest, the thoughts he uttered or left in writing live after him. The love he called forth in life survives the decay of his body in the grave. But these sentiments or emotions that survive him had their origin, not in his body, but in his soul, that part of him that thinks, remembers and wills. Only the outward covering of his real life is dead, but there is no reason to suppose that his real life dies with his material part.

Man's soul is the only part of creation that longs for immortality, and God gave no longing to the soul that He did not intend to satisfy. It would be sad even to think that this instinctive desire of the soul were doomed to disappointment when all the instinctive desires of the brute creation are gratified. This longing for immortality, which even our earthly loves possess, is an irresistible proof that life does not end when its mortal thread breaks, but that it stretches onward through the eternal years. The voice of the soul that calls for immortality is confirmed by the voice of God in

Revelation, the whole structure of which is based on the fact that man's soul can never die. -100-

THE VISIB" E AND THE INVISIBLE 

WE OFTEN hear shallow reasoners say that there can be no life after this one, for there is no evidence anywhere to prove it. The spirit world and the dream world is the same for them, and so when man gives up the ghost all is over. 

But are the things of the senses, those we can taste touch and see, for instance, the only realities? 

Do we not believe in things we can neither see, touch, weigh nor measure? Are not love, truth, and virtue, for example, realities in which we be lieve, but which we cannot perceive by the senses?

In truth, it is the things that are not seen that are the only realities, for the outward world is but a passing shadow while the invisible world is the substance. The things we see are in continual change, and exist only through change. That which is today, tomorrow is no more. Yesterday the oak was an acorn, and tomorrow it will be ashes, while truth and honor endure for eternity. Any life we can boast of is our life of thought and emotion. 

Our bodies and all outward things are but the husks of our real inward lives. We inhabit a spirit world even here and now. We find our real life and the joy of living in a world peopled by images, thoughts, hopes, emotions and aspirations. -101-

OUR HOMECOMING 

DEATH is the only thing of which we are sure in this world. At the same time, its uncertainty gives to each minute of life the importance of eternity. 

It reduces all merely human things to nothingness, envelopes earth with darkness, and heaven with light. It sets us free as nothing else can. For to be free is to be delivered from bonds, released from a prison, restored to our fatherland. And to the spiritual and wise this life is but a sojourn in a strange land. This becomes more and more evident as we approach its last stages. 

The more spiritual-minded men become, the more they aspire to truth, the more are they convinced of the inability of all their eyes see to satisfy their souls. Hence they begin to look to death as the key to the treasure-house of all their yearnings. 

Death alone can give the soverign truth, beauty and goodness for which the heart of man thirsts. 

There is something that terrifies nature in death, because of the separations it entails. But God never deprives permanently of any real good. If 

He separates us from it for a while it is but to restore it to us beautified, sanctified and for all time. We have accustomed ourselves to look upon death as the end of all things, whereas it is but the beginning of all things. If we have lived an irreproachable life, or made our peace with God before leaving this earth, death should be to us but a home-coming; for God is the home of the soul, its dwelling-place, as space is of the body. Heaven, says St. Thomas Aquinas, is the admirable temple whose only limits are light and love. -102-

PREPARATION FOR DEATH WORLDLY-MINDED people make too too little of death. The bravery of living appeals more to them. The thought of death makes them cowardly and afraid. And yet death, and the way we ac complish that act, is the most important affair of our lives. We cannot, therefore, safely omit some preparation for it.

The preparation for death need not be special nor immediate, for a life dominated by the thought of death would not be consistent with life's highest purpose. Yet a general and remote preparation for death should enter into our scheme of living. 

This preparation should include daily prayers for the grace of a happy death. Before going to sleep at night we should ask ourselves if we are prepared to meet God should death overtake us during sleep, which is a picture of death, and from which many people never awake. An act of contrition, and the indulgenced prayer of the acceptance of death should be the concluding part of our night prayers.

Prayers for the dying, especially for those who are actually in their agony should form part of our devotions. Special prayers to our Blessed Mother and to St. Joseph, the patron of a happy death, should not be overlooked. Men's faces looking into the sunset are golden, says Father Faber; and so are our lives, when we make it a practice to look into the countenance of coming death. -103-

DESIRING DEATH 

WE ARE justified in desiring death, if for many years our life has been characterized by holy living, and if we have placed our treasure beyond this earth, for the heart naturally follows the treasure. 

The desire which is part of holiness, however, must be a yearning to see God rather than to be delivered from conditions that weary us. To be blasé is far from being detached from the world.

It is perhaps a form of worldliness. To speak of death in lyrical fashion as a sweet sleep of forgetfulness is pagan and ungodly. The unhappy man looks upon death as a release from his misery. 

The faithful soul looks upon it as a longed-for meeting with God, as the beginning of all desirable things and not as the end of all undesirable ones. 

It is, at the same time, perfectly lawful to desire the end of our pains here below, to long for release from cares, worries and responsibilities; but especially to be delivered from sin and the possibility of committing it again. But the desire to see God must dominate all these other desires. And they must all have a normal mingling of fear. Death is a punishment ordained by God, and we must fear it, as we should fear all His punishments. Only saints who are in the higher stages of holiness may look upon death without a degree of fear. The fear of death must not, however, be exaggerated.

It must be a holy and reverent fear. It has been noted that God Himself withdraws this fear of His servants before the act of dying. For those who die in God's grace and love, this act of dying is, we have every hope, a surprisingly sweet and merciful grace of God. -104-

OUR ETERNAL IDENTITY 

We sometimes hear people express the wish that they might be somebody else. But such a wish is only a play of fancy, for there is nobody who could in sincerity desire to be anybody but himself. In time and in eternity, under all circumstances, we desire to be our very selves. To be anything different would be to lose our identity. It is even absurd to think we could be anybody but ourselves, for such an idea means the complete annihiliation of all that makes us ourselves. We cannot get away from our own identity, nor could we wish it. 

In eternal life, therefore, we shall be ourselves, as is proved in the Book of Job where we read: “I know that my Redeemer liveth, and in the last day 

I shall rise out of the earth; I shall be clothed again with my skin, and in my flesh I shall see my 

God, Whom I myself shall see, and my eyes shall behold, and not another." The survival of our personality and the continuance of our identity will be the key to our eternal existence. All that is desirable or lovely in our personality will be restored at the Resurrection, and will remain in us in our future state, and that which is not will be destroyed. We are assured of this by Divine Revelation. -105-

PURGATORY 

YEARNING for the presence of God without which the soul that is saved, but not yet in possession of its heritage, cannot be happy is one of the pains of purgatory. The damned do not desire God, for such a desire is an operation of the Holy Ghost in the soul. If the lost hunger for anything it is not for God, for whom they have henceforth and forever but a loathing and a horror. The thirst for 

God comes from grace and the souls in purgatory are confirmed in grace.

These holy souls are in a state of comparative suffering on account of some debts they still owe God, or some claim He has upon them, but not on account of any imperfection in their soul, for as spirits they are now perfect. The disembodied spirit is pure and upright without a defect that requires healing. Sin of any kind is no longer possible to it; but full satisfaction has not been made for the sin it committed in the body, and God awaits this satisfaction before taking the soul He loves to Himself. It is to make the satisfaction due to the sin we committed that we accept a sacramental penance and undergo pain in this life. 

But all souls do not make sufficient satisfaction for their sins in this life, so whatever is still owing has to be made up in purgatory. The sufferings of purgatory are purely spiritual. Fire, as in the case of hell, being intended, in some way, only as an agent of detention to the spirit. -106-

THE EXISTENCE OF HELL 

WE frequently hear the assertion in our days, "There is no Hell"; even uninstructed Catholics, from hearing the statement repeated, begin to doubt the existence of Hell. But though the modernized man denies or doubts the reality of such a place, three hundred and fifty million Catholics are taught that there is a place of eternal punishment called Hell awaiting the soul of all those who willfully transgress the law of God during their life here below, and die without repentance. Catholics accept this belief in Hell upon Divine Authority, for we read such words of our Lord in the Gospel, "If thy hand scandalize thee, cut it off: it is better for thee to enter into life maimed than having two hands to go into Hell, into unquenchable fire" -Mark 9-42.

Where Hell is located, and the manner in which fire produces suffering in the case of a spirit, that is the human soul, we do not know; nor is it necessary that we should know here. What is important is that we believe such a place does exist, and that those who enter there, according to the words of 

Dante, leave all hope behind them.

Belief in Hell is not opposed to human reason. 

If there is a Heaven, there must be a Hell, just as there must be wrong if there is right, or evil, if there is good. If there is a Heaven for the friends of God there must be a Hell for His enemies. Christ affirms just as strongly that there is a place of eternal punishment, as He does that there is a place of eternal reward. -107-

HEAVEN 

THE vision of God, as we know, is the central joy of heaven; for it is an article of faith that in heaven we shall see God face to face in the Beatific

Vision. To doubt this is to cease to be a Catholic. 

But the eternal happiness of heaven is also the actual, total and unchanging possession of life. 

The life of heaven is unchangeable because it is complete and absolutely perfect. Change is one of the outstanding characteristics of our life here below, because we are ever in movement toward or away from absolute perfection. In heaven we shall have attained that perfection, hence there will be no more change.

But there are those who find the essence of their enjoyment here below in constant change, and the changeless life of heaven might not appeal to these. 

What we call change here is a succession of scene and emotion which results in much emptiness. The eternal sameness of heaven is eternal newness.

Light and love are ever old and ever new, and the life of heaven is essentially a life of light and love. 

After the resurrection it will be a participation in the life of the Risen Christ, a complete glorification of body and soul. Before the general resurrection only the soul shares in this glorification. 

The last prayer of Christ should reassure us as to the happiness that awaits us in heaven: "Father,

I will that where I am they also whom Thou hast given Me may be with Me; that they may see my glory which Thou hast given Me, because Thou hast loved Me before the creation of the world" -John, 17-24.
