A NOTE TO THE STUDENTS 

This syllabus is designed for your help in accumulating and assimilating the material for the course in science given at Dillard University. It represents a plan of work for your freshman year.

The syllabus contains a brief summary of the work, grouped into subjects and lessons, considering one lesson to be the assignment for one day. Immediately under the lesson head are readings required for each lesson. In cases where texts are not adequate, the readings refer to articles found at the back of the syllabus. At the end of some lessons are found readings which are not required, but are recommended for students who wish a better command of the subject. In each case the readings refer to the books in the bibliography which will be found in the library. 

The author solicits criticism of the syllabus, and will be grateful for a report of any errors brought to his attention. 11

PREFACE 

Science, as a factor in human life, is increasing in importance each day. It has grown from a few isolated facts known by a few, to an inexhaustible everincreasing store of knowledge so voluminous that no one person can be expected to know it all. In this process of expanding, it has come closer and closer to actual life, and is playing an increasingly important part in our everyday experience. In its beginnings it was purely theoretical, divorced from the life of the day, a small store of knowledge which attempted to explain nature and the universe in comprehensible terms. Now, scientific knowledge is a part of every act which we perform throughout the day. The bed we sleep in, the meals we take, our living quarters, our every act involves some product which bears witness to the scope and broadness of science. Through it man has become increasingly powerful in manipulating his environment so that his life is more comfortable and more enlightened. 

Through science man has come closer to his God, has learned to control his environment, and in doing these things, has reached a more abundant life. 

Not only man individually, but man collectively has been forced to change his life through the acquisition of scientific knowledge. A knowledge of natural laws obtained slowly and painfully through an increasingly more scientific method resulted in the use of natural resources for power. The acquaintance with power brought machines which, in turn, led to the growth of cities. The cities led to a different kind of life, a new class of people, new living conditions and habits, and to fresh problems. The automobile, the airplane, the train, have in a smaller way caused the same kind of profound changes in our life. The changes have been social changes, so we see that science has not only changed our intellectual life, but our social life as well. iii

PREFACE 

In view of the great importance of science to life, it becomes necessary to teach college students some of its facts and methods. The orientation of the 

Freshman to the entire field of science so that by the application of the concepts learned, he will be able to have a better intellectual life, and to furnish a broad basis for later specialization in his college training, is the purpose of this course. More specifically, it is hoped that the student will develop the scientific attitude, understand the nature of the scientific methof, develop a habit of using that method in his daily life, obtain a background of information useful for further specialization or independent study, and acquire a knowledge of the important contributions of science and the relationship of these.contributions to human life.

This is to be done by presenting as an unbroken sequence, the growth of science from the earliest times to a period in which the method for the accumulation of data had been well standardized. In doing this there will be given a basis of facts and insights which will be broad enough and sound enough to furnish the students with a rational, general outlook on man and his universe. 

The subject matter will be comprehensive, covering most of the sciences, yet detailed enough to disclose and illustrate essential thought processes and relationships.

Scientific knowledge, as a whole, is greatly misunderstood. Due to the success of the so-called "popular science" books, the products and wonders of science have overshadowed the importance of the real knowledge in the public mind. Science to the layman now seems to be an array of parlor tricks and "believe it or not" statements which bring forth cries of amazement when they are mentioned: Molecules are so small that a billion of them could be placed on the point of a pin; Ehrlick tried six hundred six compounds before he found his famous salvarsan; the human voice can be transmitted by a beam of light. Such facts as these overshadow the real meaning of science as an intellectual technique. It is the purpose of this course to present science as it is, and help dispel the popular misunderstanding. iv

PREFACE 

There are two generally accepted methods of presenting such an orientation course. The more popular method, called by one author the "layer cake" method, consists of exposing the student to a number of hasty surveys of the special sciences. The obvious difficulty of this method is that the student has too brief an acquaintance with each science to learn more than an accumulation of unrelated facts. He deals only with contemporary knowledge, a field much too broad for such a hasty consideration. The cultural aspects and the basic concepts are ignored, and the student finishes the course bewildered by the great scope of knowledge of which he knows so little.

The second method consists of a more historical approach, offering the backgrounds and foundations of modern science, but ignoring much of the final product. 

It is believed that the ignoring of some of the knowledge of today is justified by the fact that to cover such a large amount of subject matter well is the work, not of one course and one year, but many courses and many years. The student, to understand the periodic table, relativity, the newer aspects of evolution, and many modern conceptions of science, needs a good foundation on which to build. Such a foundation cannot be laid in a short time. It is believed that it is better to build up in the student a thorough knowledge of the foundations upon which modern science rests than to give him an inadequate knowledge of contemporary science. 

Science should be represented not as a closed book, but rather as a dynamic, living body of knowledge which will ever increase on into the future.

Just as the science of today is built on the experience and knowledge of yesterday, so the science of tomorrow will use as its foundations the knowledge of today. The wonders of today will be just as commonplace tomorrow as the wonders of yesterday are commonplace today. The representation of a dynamic science can only be done by a study of the past, as a key to the future, emphasizing that today will be the past of tomorrow. In such a study, the relationship of one science to another can be more easily stressed, as it is possible to show chronologically the unfolding of the sciences from a common

origin. Instead of a mass of unrelated facts, the student is taught the philosophical and cultural aspects of science, the intellectual techniques which man has developed through the ages to explain his universe, the place of religion in science, and the unity of science by viewing scientific knowledge as an interlocking and correlated system of facts based on a few primary concepts. vi

INTRODUCTION 

It has already been pointed out in the Preface that the Science Survey course is an attempt to orientate the Freshman to the entire field of science and the scientific method. This is to be done by presenting the acquisition of scientific knowledge in an historical manner. Starting with the knowledge of primitive man, an attempt will be made to present to the student some of the significant accomplishments, of civilization in mathematics, astronomy, physics, biology, geology, chemistry, and medicine. Wherever possible these accomplishments will be correlated with the customs and philosophy of the time, as a true understanding of science comes about only after the realization that the habits, thought, and the religion of people of the past have served to shape the nature of their knowledge. 

The most efficient way to achieve these ends without dogmatism or partiality is to expose the student to a large amount of selected readings from various authors. 

Experience has shown that the average Freshman encounters his greatest difficulty in learning to read intelligently and in acquiring effective study habits. 

In an effort to minimize these difficulties, lesson A, How to Study, is presented. An outline of lessons A 

and one are offered as an illustration of the form the student may use in outlining the assigned readings. 

The student is expected to outline each lesson, with reference pages, on the right side of the Syllabus. 

Blank pages are provided for this purpose.

Lessons 2 and 3 deal more closely with the scientific method and the scientific attitude. So often we see people who, in their intellectual methods, are dogmatic rather than reasonable, prejudiced rather than open minded, and who abruptly jump at hasty conclusions rather than carefully sifting the evidence. 

As one assimilates these lessons, he should attemp to interpret his own thoughts and actions in the light of what he is learning of the scientific method, and attempt to pattern his actions along scientific lines.

FIRST PERIOD OF GREEK SCIENCE 

Following the civilizations of ancient Babylonia and Egype came a Greek civilization populated with freethinking people who were able to use the knowledge already accumulated as a base upon which to build a knowledge influential almost beyond belief. It was not the practical sort known to the earlier peoples, but instead a learning built upon a pure love for explanation, a comprehension which attempted to go beyond the question of "to what use can I put this" to "why should this be so."

In the Greek states existed a people who were largely economically independent, who lived on a beautiful land covered by friendly skies and worked by slaves, thus allowing a freedom for thought, and a leisure to enjoy that freedom. This very thought was unhampered by religious interference and the astrology which so dominated the civilizations coming before them. The religion was a friendly one, built upon myths, and thus

allowed a personal interpretation and freedom of thought impossible der many other circumstances. 

The very 7 personal nature of their religion invited speculation and thought concerning their surroundings. Nature smiled on the Greeks, and they believed the world surrounding them to be one which was understandable, whose secrets could be pried out with a wedge of speculation. 

All these factors, coupled with a desire to know and an intelligence which was able to find ways to answer their questions resulted in a period abundant in knowledge and understanding.

HELLENISTIC PERIOD 

The Greek democracy, so conducive to independent thought and individual effort, became weakened by internal dissension which resulted in a number of independent city states which were easily subjugated by the 

Macedonian King Philip. Alexander, the son of Philip, united all the Greek cities and spread their culture throughout the Orient, but when he died his great kingdom fell apart and became the property of his stronger generals. Ptolemy, one of the shrewdest of the dead conqueror's leaders, established himself as the leader of Egypt, and proceeded to make Alexandria the center of knowledge. Here in Alexandria a great library was collected, the Alexandrian Museum (place dedicated to the Muses) established, all the sciences were encouraged, and scholars from the entire civilized world gathered there to pursue their studies. Thus did the centers of learning of the Greeks pass on to Alexandria at the other side of the Mediterranean.

The period when the knowledge of the world resided in Alexandria is known as the Hellenistic period, and comprised two phases; the first an expansive and creative one in all fields of endeavor, the second showing a decline in the creative impulse due to Roman influence. 10 10

ROMAN PERIOD 

Alexandria flourished at the head of the intellectual world for about three hundred years before Caesar and the Romans burned part of the Alexandrian library and conquered Egypt, and by doing this, moved the capitol of the world to Rome. There the scientific spirit of the Greeks of Alexandria, (which had already been on the down grade), became submerged by the more practical pursuits of war, conquest, and government. 

The Romans were interested in Greek science, but they were so busy with the other phases of life that they could not spend time for the careful experimentation and study so essential to scientific knowledge. True, they studied the principles of Greek science and applied them where possible, but science cannot develop by the application of its principles. 11

THE DARK AGES, 476-1000 A.D. 

Lessons 12 and 13

Although the Dark Ages may be thought of as beginning roughly with the fall of Rome and ending about 

1000 A.D., it must be remembered that these dates are so approximate as to be almost meaningless. The only reason for assigning dates to this period is to give the student some basis for correlating history with science. One of the main thoughts in any science course is the teaching that science represents a gradual accumulation of knowledge which takes place over a period of time rather than being non-existent one moment, (or one year), and springing to life the next. The progess of scientific knowledge may be likened to the course of a long river beginning at the mountains and going toward the sea. Sometimes it moves very swiftly, at others it 12

THE DARK AGES 

moves so slowly that its motion is scarcely perceptible, but, it moves ever onward, constantly getting larger from the endless streams. that feed it on its course to the sea.

The transition from the swift-moving phase to its almost stagnant one is not instantaneous, but instead is a gradual slowing up as its momentum is slowly lost.

In the same way, the transition from the intellectually-rich age of Greece to the almost barren Dark 

Ages was not instantaneous, but took place over many years. Here the stream of knowledge was almost stopped, dammed by the thought of the period. Eventually, however, it overflowed its barrier, trickled, then rushed on toward the sea. Here the analogy ends, for the river will, at some time, reach the sea, while the progress of science, typified by the increase of knowledge, will never end, but will continue on as long as there is a living being to carry on its advance.

Except for the fact that the fall of Rome offers a convenient date, we could say that the beginning of the Dark Ages occurred during the decline of Rome, or even in the Hellenistic Period when the minds turned away from the attempt to find new knowledge, and became absorbed in repeating the Grecian. It was in Rome that the love for knowledge became lost--that the people turned from the theoretical question of "why" to the more practical but less scientific question of "to what use can I put this." Roman law, architecture, engineering, lend proof that the Roman mind was more occupied by social and worldly knowledge than theoretical knowledge for the good of knowledge. Rome absorbed the culture of Greece, used it where possible, but failed to advance it. Therefore, let us not think of the Dark

Ages as being bound by any definite dates such as 476 and 1000, but rather of these dates as opportune ones to unite history with our study of the progress of science.

We must think of the lack of intellectual advancement in Europe during the Dark Ages as being the result of a number of unfavorable circumstances. The barbarian invasions and subsequent conquest, no doubt, played a large part in the general decline of knowledge, but it must be remembered that the barbarian conquest 13

THE DARK AGES 

came as a result of a weakening of the civilization of the Romans. Historians seem to agree on three important factors which so weakened the Roman Empire that it was an easy prey to the barbarians: first, the social order was top heavy with too few people having the political power, and too many people having nothing; second, a financial and economic system which was very weak; third, the influence of Christianity in teaching the lower classes not to believe in the divinity of the Emperor. These factors paved the way for the success of the barbarian invasions which destroyed the culture and civilization of Rome. Knowledge is dependent upon the state of civilization and general culture of a people, and as the conquest destroyed these, it must have surely also destroyed the knowledge of that people.

The contribution which the early church made in stifling the quest for knowledge was a great one. The fact that the intellectual advance continued, though slowly, in the non-Christian countries is fair evidence of this. The church must not be judged too harshly for its part here, though. The position of the church, at its beginnings, was so perilous that it was necessary to quench all evidences of the pagan life. It was necessary for the ecclesiastical heads to reconcile the new religion with some of the older aspects of religion coming from pagan and Roman sources, but the church heads recognized the danger of too large a stream of pagan knowledge and took steps to prevent its influence. 

They could waste no effort in sorting out that knowledge which they felt would be harmful, from that which might have been useful, so most of it was forgotten and all initiative and effort was turned toward making Christianity a successful religion. To allow Greek thought to creep into Europe may have undermined the church structure, so the church fathers banned Greek knowledge, using ecclesiastic knowledge, monastic teaching, and a religious philosophy calculated to keep the minds of the people above the worldly quest for knowledge on earth. 

Such was the success of these methods that intellectual advance in Christiandom was stifled, and the Dark Ages was the result. 14

THE DARK AGES 

In the East, no such restrictions were put on knowledge. The conquests of the Arabians brought them into contact with the knowledge of other lands, and they began to eagerly absorb it after the initial momentum of their conquest had been slowed down. In this absorption, the Arabs were valuable as conservers of science rather than contributors. Most of the few advances which they made were in alchemy, medicine, and numerals, for they were not originators or research workers. However in their task of conserving knowledge, they were admirable, for they kept the knowledge of the past secure until the Western World was in a position to use it.

There came a time, immediately following the

Dark Ages, when the slight forward movement of the 15

stream of knowledge shifted from the Asiatic countries to the European. This embraced the Medieval Period, a period when the knowledge of the Greeks which had been held for a period by the Arabs, was partially recovered by the West. This recovery, however, meant not a complete acceptance of the earlier knowledge, but rather a shaping of it to fit the medieval mind. 

The medieval mind was a product of the circumstances of the period. During the Dark Ages the interests of the people of the Western World had been centered around building from chaos a civilization in which they could live comfortably. As a result of this building came feudalism, which was largely responsible for a people held down by poverty and illiterate beyond belief, for wars and continual strife, for a Europe consisting largely of armed estates.

At the same time during the Dark Ages, the church was uniting itself into a strong organization by weeding out inconsistencies and building a faith which might be able to withstand any controversy which might be brought against it. Such was the ecclesiastical success that although Europe was divided politically, it was united religiously under one head situated at Rome. 

These two forces, Christianity and feudalism were responsible for the medieval mind. Most of the population was bound to labor in serfdom, the people were illiterate for they had nothing to read, and they knew little of the world, for their only travels were in the armies of the nobles. Further, their education was not encouraged, for it was the belief of the church that learning was not for the masses, but only for a cultured few, while the few books available were written in Latin, the language of the educated. Even the Bible itself was not translated into the vernacular until the fourteenth century. What little education allowed the common man came by way of the village priest or occasional travellers. The first source was bound up with faith, and for the most part consisted of the teachings of the Bible. The second source, a poor one at best, used an information colored with imagination and 16

fanciful tales which fell upon ears already tuned to credulous happenings by the miracles recited to him from the first source.

Man at this stage was intensely religious. To him the earth was but a middle ground where he was to spend a lifetime preparing for his hereafter--a future which might be spent joyously in a heaven above if he were good, or a future spent burning in the clutches of a Satin if his behavior on earth had not been in accord with the concepts set down in the scriptures. On every hand he was taught to see evidences of the good life or the bad; in his every action he fancied himself tempted by the evil or tested by the good. He felt that he was subjected to such a life because he was man, scion of the world fashioned by an all-seeing Almighty in His image. The earth was made the center of the universe especially for his existence, the sun rose every morning in the east and passed around the earth that he might have warmth and light, the stars and the moon lit up the skies at night that he might not have to go in darkness, while every plant, tree, and animal were fashioned especially for man, who was so favored by God that God created him in His own image. His universe was indeed an homocentric universe.

Gradually the feudal system was overturned, and the lower classes began to rise. Towns began to grow by virtue of the craftsmen there and the attendant commerce and wealth, and as they grew more powerful they became more independent of the exudal lords, and there arose a middle class with wealth and a leisure sufficient to study and make an attempt to learn something of the world about them.

At the same time the church became powerful enough that it no longer needed to devote all of its time to perfecting ecclesiastical organization and it began to serve as a haven for many intellectual minds who were drawn into the monasteries by the leisure and comfort which allowed them to carry on their learning. 

Many of the original Greek manuscripts which had lain forgotten and lost were recovered, while many others found their way to Europe through trade and commerce 17

with the East. Thus did two groups of scholars come into being; one an independent group, the other an ecclesiastical group. The latter, by virtue of their access to Greek learning and language, had the advantage of an early start. 

Unfortunately this same Greek learning which helped the ecclesiastical students acted as a hindrance as well. The Greek science which was first recovered was dominated by Aristotle, who was unable to sever his science from moral and spiritual values. This type of learning, of course, fitted in well with ecclesiastical ideas, and the works of Aristotle were carefully translated and eagerly studied, while his knowledge and methods found their way into church writings. Plato was also well received, for here again there were ideas readily acceptable to religious thought. The fundamental elements of Platonism, the contrast between objects of the senses and objects of the mind, were construed to mean the difference between the worldly and the spiritual, while the Platonian concept of the world of ideas being superior to the world of the senses was interpreted as meaning the act of contemplating truth being the ideal and hence on a higher plane than the search for truth. God was an all-wise God, and man, by contemplating on this wisdom, could approach closer to God and the saintly ideal.

Aristotle was, however, the greater influence. 

Churchmen found in the logic of Aristotle an ideal and powerful tool for welding the truths of the church together, and the outgrowth of this was scholasticism-that curious union of Aristotelian reasoning and scriptural writings so well used to combat heretical arguments. Here, revealed truths were considered to be the ultimate wisdom, but they showed that these truths could also be reached by reasoning, while facts contrary to accepted authority could be refuted by the use of the logic of Aristotle. Nothing could shake the divinely revealed truths, for they were the ultimate, and in the cases where they were beyond reason, it could be shown that they were not contrary to that reason. In this way was the church the guardian of knowledge, while all knowledge had to be approved by the church leaders. 18

Here "approved" is hardly the word to be used, for the church leaders not only took the right to approve, but also to interpret. Some rebelled and found facts in opposition to the established beliefs, but these few were quickly quelled.

Thus the church led the way in the search for knowledge, while the greatest value of the scholastic was in finding the possibility of logic as a tool for testing and clarifying truth. True, logic could not seek new truths, for such truths were already revealed in the Scriptures, and thus it was known in advance where the seeker would end, but the scholastic was preparing the way for the renaissance, as he was attempting to find a way to understand the universe by reason.

Lessons 15-29 In contrast to the Dark Ages, which we have said were due to an accumulation of unfavorable circumstances, the Renaissance was due to an accumulation of favorable circumstances. We have seen that the Medieval Period came as a result of a particular type of thinking advocated by the church, which led to the scholastic outlook. It was the downfall of this scholasticism accompanied by the favorable circumstances which caused the western world to embark on a period of discovery and invention which resulted in a great change in the life and outlook of man.

Let us not think, however, that the change from a scientifically barren age to a scientific one occurred overnight. On the contrary, the "favorable circumstances" had to accumulate, one by one, slowly gaining strength by their combined presence, until they were strong enough to overthrow the barrier to science. the beginning of the Renaissance took place in the thirteenth century, and by the seventeenth and eighteenth, the new spirit was strong enough to make itself felt by starting a new machine age. Thus,

Let us list these circumstances which caused so profound a change. 

1. The decline of scholasticism 

In Aristotle, man became acquainted with a tool which was new to him--logic; a sharp-edged tool which he was able to use in scholasticism with good effect in reasoning toward an unshakable truth. Logic was, however, not only a means of arriving at answers already known, but it could be used to obtain new answers. 

When man learned to use it, he was unwilling to confine his reasoning to theology, but used it on the world about him--in short, he began to think. Scholasticism had also taught him that the world was understandable, and he began to throw off the restrictions laid down by the church fathers and think, not toward the ultimate 20

THE RENAISSANCE 

truths as he had been taught, but toward new truths of this understandable world. Thus, scholasticism had become the Frankenstein created by the thought of the church which became too powerful for his master to control. 

2. Humanism 

The very acceptance of Aristotle by the church caused people to turn to the other classical Greek authors when their works had found their way back to Europe, while a knowledge of these works led to an appreciation of the classical style, language, and thought, and culminated in Humanism. Humanism taught man to think of his life on earth not as a trial to determine what his after-life should be, but instead to think of it as something to enjoy while he had the chance. Man, through Humanism, looked at the world with its sunshine and flowers and enjoyed himself, forgetting about the saintly ideal which had bound him to a life of sober religious effort for so long. 

3. Geographical discoveries 

The fifteenth and sixteenth centuries were rich in geographical discoveries familiar to all of us, starting a chain of circumstances which had far-reaching effects. Exploitation of these discoveries brought increased growth of towns and wealth, a wealth which helped develop a new class of people with leisure and opportunity to exchange thoughts which led to an increased knowledge impossible in rural communities. With the congregation in towns came the opportunity of societies and clubs dedicated to research and constructive ideas, societies that stimulated thought, that welcomed controversy supported by evidence, societies that gave birth and encouragement to young men with new ideas, that later published works to have momentous consequences. Societies, towns, wealth, were all conducive to knowledge, and played their part in the rebirth of knowledge. 

4. A growing national spirit

With towns and a growing bourgeoisie population came a strong national feeling banding people together 21

Unscrupulous nobles took advantage of its weakness and usurped its lands and wealth in many communities. Thus the church lost much of its power to dictate and interpret knowledge through faith when it came into conflict with political rule.

More, it put into the hands of the common man a Bible which he might read and interpret for himself, and thus made his religious life more independent of the church.

ADVANCES IN ASTRONOMY 

Lessons 28 and 29

We have seen how the astronomies of Aristotle and Ptolemy; the first worked out deductively from his theory of God, the latter based on the all-importance of the earth and observation, had been challenged by the more mathematical astronomy of Copernicus. Up until the time of the seventeenth century, the new astronomy based on a heliocentric interpretation had been only a challenge to the earlier systems because of the lack of definite information concerning the heavens, for Copernican astronomy had agreed with observation little better than Ptolemaic. Information in astronomy consists of accurate and endless observations to give essential data which, so far, had been lacking. The Arabian and Medieval minds were far too interested in the allegorical and mystical conception of planetary influence on the lives of men to bother with the intricacies of accurate observation, but were more concerned with increasing their ability to cast spells and foretell the future from stars than they were to chart the positions of the stars which they supposed had such a predominant influence on the world. Their ideas coincided well with the superstitions of the age, and were accepted blindly by the people, just as the system of Ptolemy, sanctioned by the church. The task of turning the thoughts of the people away from the mysticism of astrology and the dogmatism of Ptolemy was too great for one man. Even Copernicus, failed to overturn the stubborn and tenacious hold on the past. 31

THE RENAISSANCE 

Before a newer, more correct astronomy could displace the older, it was necessary for more data to be collected, more observations to be made, and more refined instruments for the collection of such information perfected. It was necessary to pile up such a mass of unquestionable data to support any one theory that its sheer weight would overcome any opposition. 

It was

Tycho Brahe who spent a lifetime in perfecting instruments and making observations upon which the mass of data depended. Tycho Brahe was born but two years before the martyr Bruno, and died forty years before the Newton who was to put to such good use Tycho's information. Modern astrònomy owes a great debt to Tycho, for even with his predominantly Ptolemaic ideas, he was one of the first to stray from the fold of the astrologer to become interested in knowledge for its own sake. 

The observations of Tycho were put to work immediately by Kepler, who, by hit-or-miss methods, worked out laws from the heliocentric ideas of Copernicus which agreed with the observations. Here we have the beginning of an interpretation of the solar system established by precise observations. Kepler, blindly searching for a god of order in the universe found, to his surprise, that the circular orbits advocated by Copernicus were not consistent with Tycho's observations, so he discarded circular orbits in favor of elliptical ones which agreed with observations. Then using these same observations he found two new empirical laws. 

Even the data of Tycho and the laws of Kepler were not enough to insure the recognition of the heliocentric system of astronomy, and indeed, it was not until the efforts of Galileo brought forth a telescope with which one could see evidence of the heliocentric nature of the solar system that any doubt was cast on the time-worn systems advocating the earth as the center of the universe.

In the work of these three men, the world was given proof of the Copernican idea which upset all of the authorities, mysticism, and superstitions of the past set up by Aristotle, Ptolemy, and the astrologers; and which threatened to upset even the religious 32

interpretations of the heavens. ADVANCES IN PHYSICS 

The renaissance in physics began a few years after that in astronomy and biology, and took the nature of a trend from a philosophical study to a study based on real experimental evidence. In the development of any science there is a period devoted to clearing up misconceptions and disproving past superstitious beliefs, and this period in physics began with the work of Gilbert in electricity and continued with that of 

Galileo, Torricelli, Von Guericke, and Pascal, who were contemporary with the Herbalists, Bacon, Harvey, and 

Descartes. 

After the classic experiments of Gilbert, who successfully disproved many misconceptions concerning electricity and magnetism, came the work of those men interested mainly in the atmosphere, and culminated by the great quantitative work of Robert Boyle, Galilei, and Huygens. We find that with these latter three men, the type of work changes from a qualitative nature to a quantitative one. They were interested in relationships rather than causes, and their experiments were designed to find these exact relations: Galileo with falling bodies and pendulums, Boyle with air pressure and its relationship with volume, and Huygens with cen-. trifugal force. This marked a great step in physics, for it showed that these men were independent of the past masters who had stressed the reasons and ignored the relationships which are the true basis of science. 35

THE NEWTONIAN EPOCH, 1660-1859 

Closely following the Renaissance came a period which we might call the Newtonian Epoch after that great mind that straddled astronomy and physics and showed that the same mathematical laws governing the fall of a leaf guided the revolutions of the planets. The accomplishments of the Renaissance had been made: Astronomy and physics had their start in becoming sciences, authority in the past was on the wane, the scientific methods of Bacon and Descartes were known, the place of mathematics in knowledge had been realized, superstitions in all branches of knowledge were being wiped out, and men were turning their thoughts toward the future,, toward a more complete utilization of this newly-found method of learning.

The Newtonian Epoch is characterized not by a rebirth of knowledge, as in the past era, but in the whole-hearted application of knowledge already known to 38

life and to the world. This knowledge was applied to alchemy and the history of the earth, and the sciences of chemistry and geology came into being; it was applied to biology and a new world was opened, while sickness ceased to be a curse inflicted on man by a vengeful God and became a natural result of certain causes often known. The knowledge, applied to the universe in general changed it from a vast mystery to an orderly universe governed by simple mathematical laws which were obtainable and comprehensible.

Such was the contribution of the Newtonian Epoch in paving the way for the present world.

THE RISE OF BIOLOGY 

As physics and astronomy were being advanced by the works of Newton and other lesser figures, accomplishments in the knowledge of biology were rapidly being made. A large part of these advances were directly due to the use of the microscope. There is some controversy as to who is the inventor of the microscope, but this is a relatively unimportant detail when we remember that its greatest use in biology is in allowing one to see the appearance of objects too small to be viewed by the naked eye, and that the earlier microscopes needed much development before this was possible. Once such a microscope was practical, it opened a new field for the biologist, a new romantic field almost inexhaustible, for as new techniques were developed and more powerful microscopes were found, the limits of the field seemed to stretch farther and farther into the future. The hobby of Leeuwenhoek in finding curious and strange things in the microscope field of a crude instrument little more than a magnifying glass, is far different from the skilled techniques now used by trained men with precision lenses and instruments, but still, after all this advancement, there is yet important work to be done with the microscope.

Very often in the so-called "popular" science books we are reminded of the fact that our civilization is dependent upon the advances of science. We are asked to let our imagination run riot to picture our world when electric power or some other product of science is suddenly stopped. We are told that the lack of electric power for even a day would make our cities unbearable and unliveable. However, at most, a sudden shortage of the important products of science would only tend to send the world back of the eighteenth century. Without electricity our homes would be lighted by lanterns or gas, our street cars pulled by horses, our water hand-drawn from wells until steam power could be used. All in all, our world would, in all probability, go on--a less luxurious one more dependent upon animal muscles. 

In drawing such pictures of the necessity of the products of science, we sometimes forget the part played in our civilization by medical science. The reason why we are so likely to forget its importance is that medical science plays a negative rôle instead of a positive one.

It does not furnish us with anything so material as transportation or light, and instead of giving us such apparent benefits, it guards us against those sicknesses and diseases which in the past were so feared by man. 

The horror and power of uncontrolled disease are so remote in our minds that we forget that medical science is our great protective barrier, standing between us and the dreadful diseases which, if allowed to come among us, would utterly destroy our civilization, as they have nearly done in the past. The importance of machines and machine power have led to the growth of cities, and these very cities, with their crowded streets and houses, have increased the potential death-dealing power of disease. 

The fast transportation of today also makes disease a far greater menace than it was in the past. In the fifteenth century, it took fifteen months for a certain disease to spread from Constantinople to London. Today, with airplanes making runs between these two cities, infection could be spread from one to the other in less than a week if it were not for the precautions taken by medical men. Imagine what would happen in any of our larger cities were the drinking water not freed of the 52

harmful bacteria. Suppose modern medical attention and hospital facilities were denied expectant mothers of today, and the dread puerperal fever were again allowed to take its toll of lives. It is only when we think of these possibilities, that we realize the important place of medical science in our civilization of today. Thus, because of their importance, we take up a brief study of the men who made the civilization of today possible through their researches which made conceivable the control of disease.

Just what the building blocks of the universe must consist of has long been a subject for speculation and study. In general, the theories which resulted from such speculations and studies may be divided into two sets. Prior to the nineteenth century such theories suggested that elements were the ultimate building blocks, and that the world thus was composed of a number of different substances, very simple, which were united in some way so as to give a variety of more complex substances. The Greek idea of four elements, the three elements of the early alchemists, the seven noble elements of the later alchemist, the atomic theory of Dalton, and the theory of Prout are all examples of this attempt to find the mother elements. All of these theories might be listed under the caption, a chemical attempt at matter, for besides pure speculation, the only evidence leading to such theories was of a chemical nature. This type of work and the theories which resulted were quite different from the assault made on matter through a period beginning the last few years of the nineteenth century and continuing until today. This latter investigation took the nature of a physical attempt on matter, for it was during this time that the possibility of the electrical nature of matter was realized, and the physicists began to take over the work. 64

ARTICLE I 

We have already become acquainted with the idea that science is a knowledge which has been found by the method which insures the greatest possible truth. More specifically, science entails a particular kind of knowledge seeking general laws connecting a number of individual facts. The method used to connect the individual facts and force them to yield laws is relatively new, but in the comparatively short time in which it has been used, it has been a powerful force changing our lives more than any other factor with the possible exception of religion. There has been a greater change wrought by the use of the scientific method in the past three hundred or so years than was made in the world during the whole space of time man has occupied our earth up to the seventeenth century. Science will continue to make such profound changes in the future, and we have every reason to believe that the rate of change will be constantly increased.

The source of the scientific method is an obscure one. Probably some of the earlier primitive tribes had one or two individuals who made unconscious use, in a very crude manner, of some of the concepts of the scientific method. Thus, as an individual effort we do not know how old it can be, but as a collective effort to produce knowledge, we know that it has only been used since the seventeenth century. Even today we have a civilization in which there are many who are unfamiliar with the concepts of the scientific method, and we have many who apply this same method to their everyday problems without knowing the name or the finer points of the method, but use it only as common sense. 11 11 Man has an inherent curiosity concerning the world around him. It generally takes the nature of an attempt at an explanation of the things he is familiar with. A young child has this same curiosity, and he attempts to satisfy this curiosity by the explanations of adults around him, for he thinks of adults as authorities in knowledge just as they are his authority in behavior. Primitive man had no such authority, and consequently had to attempt to make explanations for himself. 90

ARTICLE I 

The explanations which resulted were not logical, for the primitive had not yet learned to think in a logical manner, but instead they took the form of fanciful tales conceived in the imagination and warped by the will to believe what he wanted to believe. The scientist of today is limited in his explanations by the data which he has at his disposal and he purposely refrains from giving explanations of certain phenomena because of the lack of data to support his contentions. The primitive suffered no such handicap, and he allowed his imagination to run riot in producing explanations of the apparent movements of the heavenly bodies, the wind, rain, clouds, or anything he happened to wish to explain. 

The methods used in their explanations were crude, but they believed in them because they had none better to accept. 

The explanations of the world have changed much since the crude efforts of the savages, and now they are products of observation, experiment, and logical thought. 

In short, they are scientific. The common ground on which our explanations and the explanations of the primitive meet is in the motive which instigated them. It is the same curiosity for a reasonable understanding of the world which prompts the present-day scientist to spend his life in research, that prompted the earlier man to attempt an explanation of his universe. The same yearning of the primitive to know something of the sun causes the astronomer of today to spend years peering through telescopes or weeks worrying over calculations, or months carrying apparatus to some distant part of the world that he might take pictures of an eclipse which might last only a few moments. The method of answering questions has changed, yes, but it is the same impulse which is the guiding spirit. 

The primitive was different from the animals about him by virtue of his ability to think, his language, and later, his writing. We have seen that his thoughts gave him knowledge, let us see what part the other two essential achievements, the importance of which cannot be overemphasized, played in his life.

Language provided the means of developing man still farther above the animals by establishing traditions of knowledge. When one person of a tribe happened on a better and, hence, more successful way to fish or hunt, 91

ARTICLE I 

he had the desire to pass that method on to his friends and relatives by oral communication, leading to oral traditions. This method of oral tradition gradually led to an improvement in the knowledge of hunting and fishing, as the less successful methods were automatically eliminated. Later, when man became able to write, these better methods were preserved for future generations. In the same way knowledge concerning nature was passed on, and when man was able to think logically, the more reasonable explanations survived. Thus it was that man very slowly built up for himself a tradition of knowledge.

Gradually, over a course of ages, knowledge accumulated and became voluminous, and the need for a classification became apparent. The information concerning animals was finally grouped, the facts known of the heavenly bodies placed in another group, and although many errors were made, this crude earlier classification constituted a distinct advance over the former haphazard accumulation of facts. Step by step, points of similarity and dissimilarity were found in the classifications and the recognition of these led to generalities or statements of these similarities or dissimilarities. 

Such statements are called laws. This type of formulation of laws is the scientific method of finding knowledge.

After man has accumulated a large number of laws, he is able to use them in gaining for himself additional information, for laws allow prediction in the future. If we have a certain set of circumstances which are covered by a law, we may look into the future and predict just what will happen under similar circumstances, without resorting to an actual trial. Let us use a simple illustration. A motorist may become familiar with his own car, and unconsciously make a set of rules governing its behavior; a push on one pedal decreases the speed, while a push on another increases the speed.

When the motorist drives another car he can predict those same actions. In the second car he does not have to try the pedals, for the rule, if true, will allow him to predict correctly just what will happen. This prediction by the use of rules is an important use of science, and allows the application of scientific knowledge to industry and life. 92

ARTICLE I 

The human knowledge embraced by the word science should be thought of, not as a set of distinct sciences, but rather as an accumulation of knowledge which has evolved in such a manner that all the divisions are so closely entwined and interlocked that they present one continuous advance from a common origin. This common origin is mathematics, which began, not as a system of calculation, but as a system of counting. Mathematics is, thus, the oldest of the sciences as well as a base from which the others evolved. The primitive counting process gradually widened into an arithmetical and geometrical process involving space and time. From such an advancement, it was but a step farther to the picking out of astronomical relationships, and, thus, astronomy came into being. At the same time, the space and time relationships of bodies on the surface of the earth were worked out, and the science of physics was born. Chemistry came next with the establishment of quantitative relationships to bring it out of the class of alchemy. 

These sciences of astronomy, physics, and chemistry were concerned with the properties of bodies. Next came the sciences concerning life. Biology, the study of the origins, structure, and functions of plant and animal organisms was the first, and it developed independently of the other sciences in the initial stages. Later, however, chemistry and even mathematics were found to be so closely associated with biology that some authors think of the science as being almost a continuation of organic chemistry. Certainly the materials with which biology is built are but a step higher than the organic chemistry of today, and the researches of the presentday organic chemists are continually lowering the height of this step. The various branches of biology which have evolved later seem to be only more specialized, or unions of chemistry and biology.

Thus, in conclusion, we see that the various branches of science are so interlocked that they may be studied as a whole, and that the divisions are divisions of convenience rather than of subject. As an example of this thought we have only to look into the subject matter of such sciences as astrophysics, pharmacology, physical chemistry, geophysics, and biochemistry. 93

During the fifteenth century, Leonardo da Vinci and a few scholars in Italy began to realize the importance of experimentation and observation in reaching rules. The type of thought was simple. Leonardo, in his formulation of the laws of the lever, first used experiment to furnish him with a common property of the lever. From this common property he arrived at his rule. 

This type of process by which he arrived at his rule is called induction. Induction is the type of reasoning which proceeds from the particular to the general. Thus 

Leonardo had started with a property found in particular experiments which became a general property of levers. 

This induction is the most common way of formulating rules. The process is essentially simple; many instances are carefully examined to find a common characteristic leading to a certain conclusion, and if the instances are numerous enough, and if the common characteristic is found in every instance, the conclusion is probably true and becomes a rule. 

This method of reasoning is in contrast with that of the scholastics, who used the knowledge of Aristotle as rules to deduce new truths, and is called deduction, as it proceeds from the general to the particular. In deduction one starts with a general principle assumed to be true, and arrives at the fact that every instance which falls under the heading of the general principle is true. Let us take illustrations of these from actual life. Suppose that after a careful observation of all the dogs we have seen, we realize that we have never seen a dog without a tail. Then from this observation we can formulate a rule that all dogs have tails. This rule has been arrived at in an inductive manner. Now let us suppose that we hear someone say that all girls with red hair have violent tempers, and we accept this statement without question. Then the next time we see a girl with red hair, we say to ourselves, "All girls with red hair have violent tempers, so as that girl has red hair, she also must have a violent temper." Then the method by which we arrived at this conclusion was a deductive one. Even in the more enlightened Medieval Period, the scholastics had used 94

ARTICLE II 

the concepts of Aristotle as a basis for further deduction. The fallacy of this method as a basis of knowledge can easily be seen. The truth about the particular instance is dependent upon whether or not the general principle is true. Thus, in arriving at the conclusion that the particular red-headed girl has a violent temper, we have to assume that our friend was right. In other words, the truth of our conclusion rests upon the truth of our friend. It was Leonardo and his contemporaries who saw the weak points in Aristotle's method and who applied the inductive method in its place. 

Of course the inductive method is not infallible. 

Merely because we have never seen a dog without a tail is no sure criteria that they do not exist. We may not have enough information at hand to draw conclusions.

But, however, until one sees a dog without a tail, we can be reasonably certain that our general rule is true. 

The word "reasonably" which is inserted is very important, for, as we shall see as we continue the study of science, many rules which can be formulated through perfect induction are proven to be wrong with the discovery of additional data.

Both induction and deduction are important to science. We arrive at our rules by the inductive method, and from these rules we apply deductive reasoning to particular cases. To get back to our old illustration; we have already used inductive reasoning to get our rule that all dogs have tails, now if we see only the head of a dog, and still recognize that it is a dog, we can deduce that the dog has a tail. Here we have reasoned from the general to the particular after we have satisfied ourselves that our particular instance comes under the general consideration. The entire method of reasoning here is perfectly sound, in that we have first induced a rule, and then applied it deductively. 

In reality, induction and deduction are much more complex, and a close examination of their form is quite beyond the scope of this course. Science makes use of induction mostly in the earlier stages in the development of knowledge, while deduction and the more complex forms of the union of induction and deduction are used in the higher stages of development. 95

So far science has been shown to be the gradual accumulation of classified knowledge. The advance of science is really this, but in a different sense it is more a classification of facts on which rules are based. 

These facts are sometimes true, but sometimes also they may be found to be false. The rules which are induced from facts later shown to be false must, of necessity, also be false. The dogs which we saw and upon which we based our rule may not have all been dogs, but instead animals closely resembling dogs; the tails which we saw on some of our dogs might have been artificial; there is a possibility that there are, some place in the world, dogs without tails which we have never seen. these possibilities weaken the facts on which our rule was based, and consequently throw the rule in doubt. 

However, as long as there are no exceptions to this rule which have been found, we are safe to call the rule a true one.

All This same line of reasoning is applied to science. As long as all the facts point to a general rule, we can accept the rule as true. We must, of course, in formulating our rule, find as many facts as we are able. 

As soon, however, as one fact is found which does not agree with our rule, we must amend or discard the rule if we are to have the true scientific spirit. If we do not amend or discard the rule, we must point out the exceptions. Very few of the rules which have been carefully and correctly worked out by competent workers have stood the test of time--most of them have been shown to be false with the discovery of additional evidence, and, hence, have to be discarded, and new ones formulated. 

The question naturally arises, then, what is the use of making rules when the chances are overwhelmingly in favor of the rule being wrong and hence having to be discarded? What is the value to science of a rule that is wrong? The answers to these questions are very simple.

A rule which is wrong is of more value to science than none at all, if the rule agrees with the facts known. A rule may sometimes be in the form of an explanation, and an explanation which agrees with the 96

ARTICLE III 

known facts is better than no explanation at all. Any rule, even a false one, is a stimulus to more research. 

Immediately when a rule is put forward by one person, others test it by deduction to see whether or not it is true. Still others look for applications. From this it can be easily seen that formulation of rules acts as a stimulus for more research, and the research uncovers more facts which, in turn, make us more familiar with our world. If the additional facts found do no agree with the rule, it is either discarded and a new rule put in its place which is based on all the facts, or it is modified to fit the evidence. The second rule may still not be true, but science has benefited from the knowledge which resulted from the additional research, and the second rule is, in all probability, closer to the truth than the original one. Further, a rule or law may be useful for practical purposes even though it may be false. We know now that Copernican astronomy was false, but it not only fitted all the facts of that time, but it also allowed the calculation of events to come. 

These calculations agreed with the observations of that time very closely, but the rule postulating circular orbits of the planets was still false, for the orbits of the planets are not circular, but elliptical, but observations more precise than those known during the time of Copernicus were necessary. Thus, we see why it is that rules and explanations, even though false, may benefit science.

Someone may point out here that the Ptolemaic system of the universe agreed with the facts known at the time it was formulated, and that later it held back the advance of astronomy, and thus was detrimental to science. It was not the rule itself which held back science during the Medieval Period, but the minds of the time who would not admit that later observations had supplied additional information which did not agree with the rule. They were putting the data secondary to the rule, discarding the facts when they did not agree with the rule, instead of discarding the rule. A scientist 

Must have an open mind subject to change in the face of sufficient evidence, and the Medieval Ages did not produce a sufficient number of outstanding minds with this characteristic. 97

To return to our analogy and rule, suppose that after we had formulated our rule, we found that on a certain island there were dogs without tails. To be scientific, we would have to discard our rule or modify it to fit the new facts. The medievalist would not have discarded the rule, but instead would have insisted that the dogs must have tails, for the rule-states that all dogs have tails. In this case, they held the facts secondary to the rule. The fallacy of such a procedure is obvious.

In many cases, the new facts which are contrary to the rule may be of questionable nature, and may start controversies. Such controversies are of great value to science, for they immediately start a hunt for additional evidence to support one or the other side. 

This, of course, always results in additional knowledge. 

In the foregoing paragraphs we have stressed the importance of facts in the making of rules, and we have shown how the medievalist insisted on the primary importance of the rules over the facts. Here we have two contrasting methods of thought; the one based on induction and deduction and, hence, logic, the other on authority, tradition, or superstition. The pitfalls and discrepancies of a knowledge based on the latter are quite obvious.

ARTICLE V 

Evolution has become so great a factor in scientific progress and human life that any course of this type ignoring it would be incomplete. Still, its growth and influence has only been comparatively recent. At the beginning of the nineteenth century, those who believed in evolution were thought of as Godless cranks.

At the end of this period, the theory of evolution had become essential to the naturalists and had proven its usefulness and general truth. It is not meant that the theory has been entirely worked out and is complete, for there is still controversy concerning it, but the general idea has been widely accepted. 

The idea of evolution is not a new one, for Lucretius in the first century B.C. had definite ideas concerning it. It remained for Darwin to bring the essential ideas to light, and prove them by a vast accumulation of observation and reasonings.

There are two general catagories in which ideas of creation of the world have fallen. In primitive times, the tillers of the soil, after seeing the growth of life in spring and its death in winter, naturally had the idea that everything sprang from the soil. The hunters and herdsmen, after being aware of the internal and external resemblances between man and the animals, must have thought in terms of a blood tie between them. 

The facts of heredity must have been known, for breeding was done only with the best animals. However, early man must have known something of the distinction between species in noticing that a union between some animals 101

ARTICLE V 

could not be made. The idea of a close tie between the animals may have been thought more probable because of the comparatively few species of animals known at that time.

The ancients did not, as we know now, know the complexity of the problem they were trying to solve, for there has been a gradual increase in the number of known forms of animals until now we have over six million. Still, both of these ideas were creation ideas designed to explain the presence of various forms of creatures on the earth. In one, we have the thought of 

God creating the earth and the animals on it in the form we now see them, while Darwin believed God had created a few simple forms which had changed during the earth's history.

In the non-living or inorganic world we have two theories of origin which closely parallel those of the organic world. The people living in the territories surrounding the Aegean Sea and on the Italian Peninsula . were familiar with earthquakes and volcanic eruptions which destroyed life and changed the contours of the earth, and which were followed by the creation of new stocks of plants and animals. These people formed an idea that the earth and the animals on it were successively destroyed and created, and the idea developed into Catastrophism.

On the other hand, the people of Egypt, watching the gentle and periodic floods of the Nile bring its heritage of new soil every year formed an idea of creation due to the slow and constant action of water--an evolutionary creation.

Down through the ages, these two theories have been in competition with each other, and it was not until the nineteenth century that it was realized that the theory of evolution harmonized with science. It was then that an attempt was made by Lyell and Scrope, Wallace and Darwin, to put the theory of evolution on the sound basis of exact reasoning and careful observation. 

We have seen that in geology Werner had championed a catastrophic theory which had been readily accepted. Hutton, another geologist, took the opposite side.

He had observed that rivers carried loose material from the valleys, and he consequently expanded the denudation idea of De Saussure that natural forces tend to 102

wear down mountains. He added to this theory the idea that the center of the earth had, at one time, been composed of melted rocks which were thrown up. This completed theory, sometimes called the Hot Earth Theory, or Uniformitarianism, he presented in a book, The Theory of the Earth. The volume was dull and technical, and consequently had no effect in changing the people from believing in the catastrophic theory. 

The reasons why the catastrophic theory was predominant up until the nineteenth century are many, and we will take up only the most important. The most prominent one was that it agreed with the religious views of the time. The Jewish, Arabian, and Christian sacred books all advocated ideas of sudden changes, and consequently evolutionary ideas were considered unorthodox and banned. Furthermore, Hutton had left out statements to the effect that his views were based on the Scriptures, and his words were misconstrued, while he was attacked by the clergy. Another grave mistake which he made was to allow his theoretical conclusions to precede his observations. The Geographical Society came out openly for the catastrophic theory and ridiculed Hutton. This was, no doubt, due to the fact that so many of the influential geologists of the society were clergymen. Thus it was that catastrophism enjoyed popularity.

The tables were turned, however, on the coming of two men who were to champion the doctrine of gradual change. Scrope and Lyell, both Englishmen, after extensive observations collected in their wanderings over the globe, independently put out publications opposing catastrophism. Scrope's work was less important than 

Lyell's Principles of Geology, which gave extensive reasoning and numerous observations to support the author's theory. Lyell went so far as to show conclusively that the forces of denudation were still at their work of changing the earth's surface. The book was generally well received, and a second edition published. Some critics felt, however, that any subject should be an attempt to harmonize nature with the Sacred Books, and although the publication was well read by Lyell's contemporaries, at first it did little to change the tide of thought toward uniformitarianism. Later, the striking 103

Another factor in the overthrow of the catastrophic theory, was the work of Agassiz. One of the strong arguments of the catastrophic theory was the presence of "erratics" in the Alps. High on the slopes were found boulders of a type of rock foreign to the immediate surroundings. These were called "erratics," and the catastrophist explained their presence there by postulating that they had been carried there by a great flood. For a long time the upholders of the evolutionary doctrine were at a loss for an explanation of their presence, but at last came Agassiz, who presented exhaustive proof of the fact that they had been left by receding glaciers which once had covered the entire continent. 

This blow marked the end of catastrophism. 

Thus we see that reasoning based on extensive observation had at last been able to untie the bonds which had bound religion and geology. 

ARTICLE VI 

The problem of light occupied the attentions of physicists during the seventeenth, eighteenth, and nineteenth centuries in that they were trying to find a theory explaining its nature in accordance with its properties. Hooke had advanced a wave theory which had been elaborated upon by Huygens, but it had few supporters, for this was the Newtonian Era, and Newton had advanced a corpuscular theory of light, while his dominance in the field of physics gave authority to his theory. Further, the wave theory could explain refraction and dispersion, but it needed a medium to explain the transmission of light, a medium which Huygens was content to call ether. On the other hand, the corpuscular theory also offered an explanation of refraction and dispersion, and needed no such mythical medium. 

Thus it was that, up until the time of Young, the corpuscular theory enjoyed the favor of the physicists. 

With Young, more evidence was found to support the wave theory. He not only found the interference when light was allowed to pass through two very small 104

ARTICLE VII 

holes onto a screen, but was able to explain this phenomenon by means of the wave theory, and he calculated the wave lengths of light. With such evidence supporting the wave theory which could not be explained by the corpuscular, public favor swung to the other side.

There remained, however, two phenomena still to be explained; polarization, and why light waves did not bend around corners as do waves in water.

Fresnel, besides developing the wave theory mathematically, cleverly explained polarization by suggesting that the waves of light were transverse, (moved across the direction of propagation). This explanation of polarization sounded the death knell of the corpuscular theory for the time being, but Fresnel went farther and explained that light waves were so small compared to the size of obstacles that their bending could not possibly be expected to be seen. This removed the last barrier to the acceptance of the wave theory, and it became accepted as a general truth.

Contemporary to the researches done to prove or disprove the wave nature of light, were experiments to lengthen the knowledge of the spectrum. The question which arose after the dispersion of light was whether or not there were present in sunlight other waves of light which were invisible to the eye. It was known that a thermometer placed in the sunlight became warm, and yet this portion of the spectrum had not yet been isolated. Sir William Herschel turned his efforts away from astronomy and began to experiment with the spectrum by putting small thermometers in the different colors. In this way he found that the red end gave the greatest heating while the end toward the ultra violet was the coolest. The next logical step was, of course, to place thermometers in the portion of the spectrum just beyond the visible red, and Herschel was surprised to find that the heating effect increased in this region now called the infra red, meaning below the red. 

Thus Herschel had discovered a new type of radiation invisible to the eye, but having a great heating effect, whose wave length was longer than the visible spectrum. 105

Faraday believed that all forms of energy in nature had a common origin, and consequently should show some interrelationship. He began to work with polarized light. He passed a strong beam of this between two poles of a powerful electromagnet, and when he turned on the current which produced the magnetism, the beam of light was twisted. When the current was broken, the light regained its former position. This was an important experiment, for it showed that light must have magnetic and electrical properties, and it bound together heat, light, and electricity. On the basis of these experiments Faraday predicted in 1846 that some day light and electromagnetic vibrations in the ether would be found to be related. He was predicting radio waves!

James Clerk Maxwell took the data which had been collected by Faraday and submitted it to a careful mathematical analysis. The numerous equations which resulted seemed to show some connection between light and electricity, while a constant approximately equal to the speed of light consistently appeared. From his results he believed that just as light was a wave disturbance in the ether, electrical waves in the ether formed from spark discharges were possible, and these electrical waves, when found, would move with the speed of light.

Development of the Sciences 

From time to time in the essays, various analogies in the advancements of the different sciences have been drawn. We have seen from them that each science seems to go through periods which are characteristic of all the sciences at some particular age. When we enumerate and describe these stages, we have a representation of the method in which the world has arrived at the knowledge it now possesses. 

1. A base of empirical thinking and superstition warped by overbeliefs.

In this stage we have the primitive man making his explanations of the world about him. In these explanations he draws upon his own experiences, and is quite biased toward the ideas which he would like to believe. All of his explanations are colored by his desires, and are an essential part of them. He sees his 109

ARTICLE VIII 

God, a superbeing, planting crops every spring just as he does, and he sees his God killing an animal with lightning bolts similar to the arrow he himself uses.

He builds up his image of God similar to himself because he wants to believe it. In the same way he explains and describes the universe around him. Even Aristotle was not above this. He believed the earth to be the center of the universe because he wanted to believe that the home of man was, next to man himself, the most important object in the universe. He pictured the sun as being a sphere and moving in circles because he knew God had made it, and he believed his God could make only perfect things. The medieval mind accepted the homocentric interpretation because it put man, whom he wanted to believe the most important object in the universe, at the center. Thus we can see the effect of one's wish on the picture of the universe which he builds. 

The primitive also drew upon his own experience, and in associating different acts in the past, made up an empirical knowledge which later was passed on from generation to generation and in many cases eventually became superstition.

Thus the initial stage in the advancement of knowledge is one where nearly all knowledge is wrong because it is based on conceptions which, in turn, are based on empirical knowledge or are biased. 

2. A period of disproving false ideas and proving true ones.

In every science there is a period when a few gifted people begin to realize that all that is generally accepted as knowledge may not be true. Gilbert, Galileo, Copernicus, Boyle, and many others belonged to this group.

In many cases they were just skeptical individuals, in other cases they were men who had a new world opened to them through a new method of reasoning or a new instrument. These men set themselves to the task of disproving all knowledge of the past that could be disproven, and proving all that could be proven. In this stage we have a sorting out of beliefs, the true is separated from the false, while more true data is added.

In the history of science, accurate measurement of temperature was the last of the familiar measurements to be made. This was probably because it was a little more complex than the other measurements. Man cannot trust his senses as much in measuring temperature as in other measurements. This is because the degree of hot or cold which one experiences depends upon his past experience. A simple experiment can prove this. Set three large pans on the table. In the first to the left put hot water, in the middle one put water which has been allowed to come to the temperature of the room, and in the one to the right put ice water. Now plunge the left hand into the left-hand pan, and the right hand into the pan of ice water. After immersion for about one minute, plunge both hands into the middle pan. The water in the middle pan will feel cold to the left hand and hot to the right. Thus, we see that we cannot depend upon our senses to give us an idea of temperature.

On the above scale, we could not find a spot on the surface of the earth remote enough to represent the nearest star. It would be 10.2 x 10 miles away, or a distance equal to about 412 times around the earth. Thus we can see that the solar system is very small compared to stellar distances.

The one great idea that a study of astronomy can give us is well illustrated by the above conception of the solar system; that man is but a speck of dust on a tennis ball lost in an immensity of space containing millions of bodies many times larger than the earth which is his abode. This is the idea which medieval man, with all of his conceit, was not able to grasp. 

It is indeed very hard for us to visualize today. 116

In medicine it is difficult to prove conclusively the value of a particular treatment or dosage. It is different from the more exact sciences in this respect. In Chemistry one can predict that under the same circumstances an acid will always neutralize a base, and in physics we always expect the angle of incidence to equal the angle of reflection. In these sciences it is possible to control the conditions so that every repetition of an experiment will give the same results. 

Such conditions in medicine are infinitely more complex and cannot be controlled so accurately. Consequently, the results obtained are more likely to vary each time the experiment is repeated. Some of the difficulty is due to the fact that the doctor is dealing with animate subjects of great complexity having a great chance of variation. We are all familiar with the fact that medicines in the same dosage may produce different results in different individuals, and different results in the same individual at different times. Some can stomach and even relish cod-liver oil while others are upset by even a smell of it; some people are violently reactive to aspirin, the new sulfa drugs, cold medicines, strawberries, fatty foods, and a host of other substances. 

These instances bear witness to the fact that conditions in various individuals are not the same even under the best of circumstances. The doctor is thus forced to rely upon a complex method of proving the worth of a certain treatment.

The method most frequently used is the control experiment. An illustration should suffice to explain this method. Let us suppose that a doctor-experimenter wishes to find the effect of a new medicine (let us call it medicine Y) on people suffering from smallpox. In other words, suppose he wishes to prove or disprove the worth of medicine Y treatment for smallpox. He will take a hundred or so victims who have contracted the disease at about the same time and separate them into two equal groups of fifty. Each group will be fed the same food and put in identical rooms kept at the same temperature. Great care will be taken that the 117

ARTICLE XI 

treatment of each group will be identical with one exception the medicine. Equal dosage of Y will be administered to every member of one group, while it will be withheld from the other group called the control group. Careful individual case histories of each person in both groups will be kept. A comparison of the number of deaths in each group or the average number of days of sickness in each group will enable the experimenter to get an idea of the value of the medicine. 

If more people in the control group die than in the group given medicine Y, the medicine is probably beneficial in the treatment of smallpox. If more people in the medicine group die, the medicine is definitely harmful, and if both groups respond to the same extent, there is no value to the treatment.

The above would constitute a more ideal experiment than is usually possible, but it serves to illustrate the essential points of the control experiment. 

The numbers in each group were large to minimize the effect of any one individual; the conditions were controlled so that the only difference in the groups was the use of the medicine. Obviously then, any difference in the results of the two groups could come logically only from the medicine. 118
