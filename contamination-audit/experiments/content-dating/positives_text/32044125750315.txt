The following Manual for Coast Guard Courts-Martial is prescribed, to be effective from date of receipt, with respect to all court-martial processes taken under the authority of Title 14, U.S. Code, approved August 4, 1949.

It is directed that all persons belonging to the Coast Guard whose duties are concerned with Coast Guard courts-martial acquaint themselves with and observe the provisions contained in this manual. It should be noted that the forms and examples are illustrative rather than mandatory, and that the rules of procedure, evidence, and substantive law set forth are to be interpreted and applied in conformity with the principle of reasonableness.

CHAPTER I PROCEEDINGS BEFORE TRIAL IS ORDERED 1. AGENCIES OF PUNISHMENT.-The law provides four, and only four, processes of punishment in the Coast Guard. 

These are: the commanding officer's mast, the deck court, the summary court-martial, and the general court-martial. The first is not a court but it is regulated by statute. The last three are courts whose existence is provided for by 

Act of Congress. No punishment may be imposed in the Coast Guard except by one of these four processes. 

2. PRELIMINARY DETERMINATION OF REASONABLE CAUSE.-Fundamental to our system of penal justice in the United States is the principle that before a person is subjected to a judicial trial, it must have been determined by responsible authority that reasonable cause exists to believe that the person may have committed an offense. In the Coast 

Guard this determination is ordinarily made at mast by the commanding officer of the person accused. As an aid to the exercise of his responsibility at mast, the commanding officer will usually require a mast information report to be furnished him, in accordance with current rules and instructions of the Commandant. (As of November 1, 1949 these are contained in Commandant's Circular 13-47.)

The mast information report must be appended to all general and summary court-martial records, but it is not a jurisdictional prerequisite; that is, it is not a condition to the legal validity of a court-martial trial that a mast information report shall have been furnished before the trial was ordered. Where only a minor offense is involved and it appears likely to the commanding officer that it may be disposed of by mast punishment or by a deck court trial, no particular form of premast investigation is required. However, the commanding officer must fully acquaint himself with the facts before he imposes punishment upon a person brought to mast for an alleged offense, and in order to learn the facts he will interview informally persons having knowledge of the offense. He may also permit the accused to be present when the other persons having knowledge of the offense åre interviewed, if in his discretion it appears desirable to do so. 

3. COMMANDING OFFICER'S (MAST.) PUNISHMENT.-If the commanding officer determines that the alleged offender before him at mast should not be tried by a court-martial, and should not be excused or let off with a warning, he is empowered by law to mete out formal commanding officer's punishment. Such punishment must accord strictly with the provisions of 

Title 14, United States Code, Section 561, reading as follows: 

"No commanding officer shall inflict upon a commissioned or warrant officer under his command any other punishment than private reprimand, suspension from duty, arrest, or confinement; and such suspension, arrest, or confinement shall not continue longer than ten days, unless a further period is necessary to bring the offender to trial; nor shall he inflict or cause to be inflicted upon any other person under his command for a single offense, or at any one time, any other punishment than one of the following punishments, namely:

(a) reduction of any rating established by himself; (b) confinement, not exceeding ten days, unless further confinement be necessary in the case of a prisoner to be tried by a court-martial; (c) solitary confinement on bread and water not exceeding five days; (d) solitary confinement not exceeding seven days; (e) deprivation of liberty on shore for a period not exceeding thirty days; (f) forfeiture of two days' pay for each day of unauthorized absence not in excess of five days; and (g) extra police duties.

No other punishment shall be imposed except by sentence of a court-martial. All punishments inflicted by a commanding officer or by his order, except reprimand, shall be entered upon the log of the vessel or unit concerned." 

4. LIMITATIONS UPON MAST PUNISHMENTS.-Under the law quoted above governing mast punishments, an officer exercising command, and only an officer exercising command, may impose a mast punishment. This is the only authority for the imposition of any punishment in the Coast Guard imposed otherwise than by a court. 

The punishment of reduction in rating cannot be imposed unless the rating was established at the same command. Reduction in rating under the authority of this statute is a punishment for an offense and not for general incompetence; the reduction may be to the next inferior rating only.

The punishment of solitary confinement on bread and water is not to exceed five days; a full ration must be allowed at least on the third day; and the confinement must be solitary.

Extra police duties adjudged as a mast punishment shall not extend over a longer period than sixty days; shall not exceed two hours per day; shall not be performed on Sundays. 

5. RIGHTS OF ACCUSED AT MAST.-An accused at mast shall be given an opportunity to explain his side of the case, but he has the right to remain silent and cannot be required to offer an explanation or make a statement. The accused has the right to appeal from a mast punishment. 

If a mast punishment is awarded, a log entry must be made of it, and if the accused believes the punishment to be unjust or out of proportion to the offense, he may address an appeal through the chain of command to the next superior authority, but no farther. His commanding officer's next superior may direct a reduction or remission of the punishment awarded, but may not change the nature of the punishment. -2-

6. 

MAST IS NOT TRIAL.-While commanding officer's mast is semijudicial in nature, it is not a court trial, nor is the assignment of a punishment tantamount to conviction and sentence by a court. A record of mast punishment does not constitute a record of a conviction. The direct punishing power of a commanding officer is an executive power which belongs to him alone and cannot be delegated to or exercised by any subordinate. Although mast should be held prior to trial whenever practicable, it is not a legal prerequisite to trial. 7. 

REGULATION OF MAST PUNISHMENTS.-The Commandant of the Coast Guard may place limitations on the punishing powers of commanding officers with respect to the kind and amount of punishment authorized, and the categories of commanding officers authorized to exercise such powers. 

8. ARREST OR CONFINEMENT BEFORE TRIAL.-A person subject to Coast 

Guard discipline may be ordered into arrest or confinement as circumstances may require upon reasonable belief that an offense has been committed and that the person has committed it. Arrest is the restraint of a person by an order directing him to remain within certain specified limits, or otherwise by the lawful statement to a person that he is under arrest. Confinement is the physical restraint of a person. All confined persons are in arrest. The taking into custody of an apparent or alleged offender constitutes an arrest. Arrest or confinement may, in the interests of the Government, be deferred until arraignment; failure to arrest does not affect the jurisdiction of the court. The character and duration of the restraint imposed before and during trial will be the minimum necessary under the circumstances. When a court adjourns at the conclusion of a trial, the commanding officer of the accused is to be notified immediately of the findings and sentence; depending on the circumstances he will thereupon either order the immediate release of the accused from any restraint, or the imposition of any restraint necessary pending final action on the case. 

9. RIGHTS OF ARRESTED PERSON.When a person is placed in arrest or confinement prior to any trial being had, immediate steps shall be taken to inform him of the specific wrong of which he is accused, and to try him promptly, or to dismiss the charges and release him. -3-

CHAPTER II THE DECK COURT 20. BASIC LAW.-The Act of Congress governing deck courts in the Coast Guard is contained in Title 14, United States Code, Section 562, reading as follows: 

"(a) Deck courts, consisting of one commissioned officer, may be ordered upon enlisted persons in the service under his command by the commanding officer of any vessel or unit, if the commanding officer is a commissioned officer, for the trial of minor offenses; and said courts shall, under regulations approved by the Secretary of the Treasury, be governed in their organization and procedure substantially in accordance with deck courts of the Navy. (b) Deck courts may sentence enlisted persons to either a part or the whole, as may be appropriate, of any one of the following punishments, namely:

GEC (1) (2) (3) (4) (5) (6) confinement not exceeding twenty days; solitary confinement not exceeding twenty days; solitary confinement not exceeding twenty days, on bread and water, or on diminished rations; reduction to next inferior rating; deprivation of liberty on shore for a period not exceeding two months; and extra police duties not to exceed three months and loss of pay not to exceed twenty days.

Extra police duties and loss of pay within the limits prescribed may be added to any one of the first five punishments enumerated above. (c) No sentence of a deck court shall be carried into effect until approved by the officer ordering the court or his successor in office." 

21. NATURE AND FUNCTION OF A DECK COURT.-The deck court is a judicial tribunal for the swift and simple disposition of relatively minor offenses. 

Offenses such as theft, issuing a bad check, escape from confinement and deliberately missing ship should not be regarded as minor nor should any other offense the circumstances of which may call for greater punishment than a deck court is authorized to impose. Since it is the function of a deck court to dispose speedily of offenses, a delay of over twenty days in disposing of such offenses as unauthorized absence and breaking arrest may be an abuse of the deck court process as well as an impairment of the Constitutional right to a speedy trial, and, accordingly, unless satisfactorily accounted for, may require disapproval of the court. 22.

CONVENING AUTHORITY; JURISDICTION.-A commanding officer, if commissioned, may order a deck court; this legal power or jurisdiction extends only over "enlisted persons in the service under his command." 

To insure that this restriction upon the jurisdiction of a deck court is -4-

observed, the deck court card should have printed upon it in the space above the commanding officer's signature on the face of the card, a statement certifying that the accused is under his command. However, the fact that a commanding officer orders an enlisted person to be tried is presumptive evidence that the person so ordered is under his command and subject to his order for a deck court trial. Jurisdiction of the person is thus implicit in the order for his trial. It is always open to the accused to object nevertheless to the jurisdiction of the deck court on the ground that he was not under the command of the convening authority on the date that the deck court card ordering his trial was signed. 

Objection to the jurisdiction is never waived and lack of jurisdiction may even be asserted for the first time on appeal. Jurisdiction may exist as a fact without formal attachment; for example, a man AWOL from a ship to which he is attached, upon turning in at a shore station, is thereupon subject to the command of that station. As in the case of any court if lack of jurisdiction is established, the proceedings are a nullity. 

23. CONSENT TO TRIAL.-Unless the enlisted person ordered to be tried by deck court consents to be so tried, the deck court may not function, but such person shall be tried instead by summary court-martial. 

The deck court card form contains a space for the signature of the accused signifying his consent to trial by deck court. 

24. THE DECK COURT OFFICER.-Only a commissioned officer may act as deck court officer. The officer ordering the deck court may designate himself as deck court officer, when there are no other qualified officers available. One who was personally affected by the offense, as where the offense charges that it was his direct order that the accused disobeyed, may not act as deck court officer. (This does not apply to violations of standing internal orders.) The deck court officer is subject to challenge on the same grounds as those on which a member of a formal court-martial may be challenged.

If solitary confinement on bread and water or diminished rations is adjudged, the record must specify that a full ration every third day or sooner will be allowed. If such punishment is in excess of ten days, there must be a doctor's statement upon the reverse side of the deck court card certifying that such punishment will not endanger the health of the accused.

If the sentence involves loss of pay, the rate and total amount of such loss of pay shall be specified. The pay loss per month should not exceed one-half of the actual pay. Thus, a sentence adjudging the maximum 20 days' loss of pay should be spread over at least two months. 26.

SPECIFICATIONS; GENERAL REQUIREMENTS.-A specification is a written statement of an offense punishable under the law. It is a prerequisite to trial and punishment under American law, that an offense be specified against the accused. There may be more than one specification against the same accused. Each specification must be complete in itself and allege but one offense. Each specification must contain the name and rate of the accused and facts sufficient to describe a punishable offense with enough particularity to apprise the accused of what he must defend himself against and to enable him to plead double jeopardy if he is ever brought to trial again for the same acts. Harmless errors of form in the specification which nevertheless leave the specification still describing a punishable offense will not be grounds for disapproval. 

The law looks to the substance and not to the form of a statement of offense. 

27. THE DECK COURT RECORD.-The record in all deck court cases will be kept on a printed form 32"x8" in size, furnished by Headquarters. 

The current form is CG-2512 (Rev. 9-47). In contested cases, the substance of the testimony as typed on separate sheets shall be appended to the deck court card and constitute a part. of the record. 

28. DUTIES OF DECK COURT OFFICER BEFORE TRIAL.-When the deck court card is received containing the order for trial and the specifications referred to him for trial, the deck court officer examines the specifications to ascertain the elements of the offense which he must prepare to prove.

He makes certain also that the specification does not allege present attachment of the accused to a different command from that of the officer ordering the trial. He sets the time and place for trial and notifies the accused and witnesses. 

He enters in the appropriate spaces on the form the date of birth of the accused, the date of current enlistment, and any extension thereof, the pay of his present and next inferior ratings, and previous conviction data. PROCEDURE DURING TRIAL.-In general the procedural steps follow those of the summary court-martial and are considered at greater length in the chapter dealing with that court. Steps in the deck court trial outlined in the paragraphs following: are (a) The formality of opening the court consists merely of the deck court officer announcing "the court is open." (b) The accused is asked if he desires counsel, unless he has counsel accompanying him. (c) The deck court officer reads aloud that part of the deck court card which sets forth that he has been designated as deck court officer to try the accused. (d) The accused is asked if he desires to challenge the deck court officer. If challenged, the deck court officer records the challenge, recesses the court, and communicates the challenge to the convening authority for a decision. If the convening authority and deck court officer are one and the same, the inquiry as to challenge may be omitted: the accused should refuse to consent to trial by deck court if he believes the deck court officer in such circumstances to be subject to challenge, and in any event he may raise the objection on appeal. -7-

(e) (f) The recorder, if any, is sworn. 

The accused is asked if he objects to trial by deck court. 

If not, he signs the record in the appropriate place indicating his consent. If he does object, the court is adjourned and the convening authority notified, whereupon trial will be ordered by summary court-martial. The accused may at this point if he consents to trial, state the particulars of any objection he may have to the specification. (g) The accused is asked if he is ready for trial. If he is not, the deck court officer may grant a 24-hour adjournment; a request for a longer adjournment must be addressed to the convening authority. (h) Arraignment: The deck court officer and the accused stand, the deck court officer reads the specification aloud, and asks the accused, "To the specification which I have just read, how do you plead, guilty of not guilty?" Entry is thereupon made on the reverse side of the deck court of the plea(s). (i) If the accused pleads "guilty," he is warned by the deck court officer that he thereby precludes himself from the benefits of a regular defense as to the specification admitted by so pleading; he is asked if he persists in his plea of guilty. (j) If the accused pleads not guilty," the deck court officer proceeds as follows: He calls the first witness for the prosecution, administers an oath to him, and questions him. 

He tells the accused that he may cross-examine the witness; if desired, redirect and recross examination may follow. All remaining witnesses for the prosecution are examined in the same manner. The deck court officer then announces that the prosecution rests its case, and that the accused is now to call his first witness. The direct examination of the defense witnesses is performed by the accused (or counsel); the deck court officer may cross-examine, if he so desires. The accused may testify for himself, but he can testify only at his own request. 

He is then subject to cross-examination. The deck court officer himself may not be a witness. (k) The deck court officer asks the accused if he desires to make a statement. The statement may be oral or written. 

It cannot be acted upon as evidence. If inconsistent with a plea of guilty, the deck court officer should change the accused's plea to not guilty and proceed with the trial on that basis. The statement or substance of it if oral, should be appended to the record for the information of the convening authority. (1) The deck court officer, if the case has been contested, at this point affords the accused an opportunity to present an argument. -8-

(m) The findings. Lxcept where the accused has pleaded guilty to all specifications, the deck court officer now clears the court and after due consideration makes his finding as to each specification. The finding is to be made solely on the basis of the evidence in the case. In this connection, the deck court officer should study articles 120 and 123 in the next chapter. The findings are recorded in the indicated space on the deck court card. A separate finding is recorded as to each specification. If the accused pleaded guilty to the first specification, the proper wording is "the first specification proved by plea." If he pleaded "not guilty" to the second specification, the proper wording might be "the second specification proved" or "the second specification not proved." (n) When the findings have been arrived at and recorded, the accused is recalled and the findings are read to him. Except in the case of an acquittal, the accused is then asked if he desires to offer anything in mitigation or extenuation. Wide latitude is permitted in the reception of such evidence, (0) The court is cleared and a sentence is determined upon, Great care should be taken in writing the sentence since the use of a word other than one used by the statute itself to describe a punishment may constitute an illegal sentence. For example, "restriction" instead of "deprivation of liberty" is illegal. Likewise certain combinations of punishments are illegal. Consult articles 20 and 25 of this chapter before writing the sentence. The deck court officer should assign as the sentence such punishment as in the circumstances of the particular case he deems to be fair, just and adequate to the nature of the offense, and merited by the accused. Due regard is to be had for the Commandant's policies relating to punishments. Matters of policy pertaining to summary courts martial noted under Article 131 of this Manual apply also to deck courts, Thus, a combination of reduction in rating and loss of pay should not be adjudged; a man should not be sentenced to reduction to the lowest rating of his branch after having completed recruit training; and punishments imposed upon petty officers should not be such as would injure morale. (p) After passing sentence, the deck court officer may recommend clemency to the convening authority by writing upon the record after the sentence, "The court recommends clemency."

Such a recommendation should never be based upon a doubt as to the guilt of the accused. (a) The accused is not informed of the sentence until it has been acted upon by the convening authority. In most deck court cases, the presence of the accused is no longer required after the findings have been determined and he has indicated that he does not desire to offer matter in mitigation or extenuation. -9-

540 COMPOSITION OF SUMMARY COURT-MARTIAL. -A summary court-martial consists of three commissioned officers, no more or no less, as members, and of a recorder. The recorder performs the function of prosecutor and is not a member. One of the three members appointed, and no more than one, may be a commissioned officer of the Public Health Service on active duty with the Coast Guard. Otherwise, only Coast Guard officers may serve. A commissioned warrant officer is eligible to be a member or recorder. So also are Coast Guard Reserve officers an active duty. A warrant officer may serve as recorder, but not as a member. 

55. COMPOSITION OF GENERAL COURT-MARTIAL. -A general court-martial must be composed of not less than five nor more than nine commissioned officers as members, and of a judge advocate. The judge advocate is the prosecutor, and he too should be a commissioned officer. 

No more than one-third of the membership of a general court-martial may be commissioned officers of the Public Health Service on active duty with the Coast Guard; otherwise the provisions as to the composition of a summary court-martial apply also to the general court-martial. 

56. WHO MAY BE TRIED BY SUMMARY COURT-MARTIAL. -A summary court martial, like a deck court, may try only such enlisted persons as may be under the command of the officer who signed the order for trial. 

A warrant officer may not be tried by summary court-martial. The ability of the convening authority to produce in court the enlisted person of the Coast Guard ordered by him to be tried by summary courtmartial is ordinarily sufficient to show that such accused was a person in the service under his command at the time that he was ordered to be tried. It is always open, however, to the accused to show the contrary. 

A contrary indication in the pleadings, as for example, where the specification is so drawn as to allege the present attachment of the accused to a command other than that of the officer who ordered him tried by summary court-martial, may invalidate the proceedings, unless the record of the trial contains satisfactory evidence that the accused was under the command of the officer who ordered his trial at the date of such order. By "order for trial" is meant the referral order which follows the specifications and refers the case to a particular court to be tried. Whether or not an accused was under the command of the officer who ordered him tried is a question of fact which becomes an issue in the case only if the accused raises the issue; this fact question, if raised for the first time only after the court-room trial has been concluded, will be resolved against the accused unless it can be conclusively demonstrated that the convening -16-

authority was unauthorized to direct the trial of the particular accused.

Coast Guard is subject to trial by Coast Guard general court-martial. 

Jurisdiction is not conditioned upon the person being under the command of the convening authority. Commissioned officers, warrant officers, cadets, and enlisted persons are subject to trial by general courtmartial, but a court to try a commissioned officer may be convened, and the order to try such officer may be signed, aly by the Commandant or the Secretary of the Treasury. While retired personnel who are entitled to receive pay are still "persons in the Coast Guard, an order to try a retired person is not to issue except when the most compelling circumstances require it and a civil court cannot do adequate justice.

61. WHAT ACTS ARE PUNISHABLE BY COURT-MARTIAL. -In the chapter dealing with charges and specifications, the specific offenses which 

Section 567 of Title 14, U. S. Code, lists as the offenses which Coast 

Guard courts have the power to punish, are defined. Certain of the offenses listed in Section 567 of Title 14 are, however, general offenses which are not capable of exhaustive definition. For example, all the acts which may be punishable as constituting the offense of conduct to the prejudice of good order and discipline, cannot be listed in advance or foreseen. While it is not possible to foresee in advance every form that punishable conduct may take, still it is necessary to distinguish between conduct which is penal in nature and punishable by a court, and conduct which is not so punishable. There must be some warrant in law to hold that any particular act or omission is susceptible of punishment by the Government acting through the -19-

medium of a Coast Guard court-martial under authority of Congress. The offense specified must be a violation of a law, regulation, or custom of the service. In general, an act is punishable if declared to be punishable by this manual, or shown to be punishable by a sample specification contained herein, or declared punishable by a court-martial decision of the Secretary of the Treasury, or recognized by acceptable legal authority as punishable. Court-martial decisions of other services, or criminal law textbooks or civil court decisions may furnish the basis for a ruling by the Secretary that a specified act is susceptible of punishment by a Coast Guard court-martial. 

Not every irregular or improper act constitutes a punishable offense; nor does the use in a specification of such words as "wilfully," "wrongfully," "knowingly, " and "without proper authority" in and of themselves suffice to convert an act into a court-martial offense. These words are no more than conclusions of the pleader when used otherwise than in conjunction with a prohibited act. Law Bulletin 92 of January, 1944, contains a compilation of acts which appeared to commanding officers. to be punishable, but which were held by the Judge Advocate General of the Navy not to constitute court-martial offenses. In the last analysis, the Secretary of the Treasury sits in judgment upon the legal sufficiency of every specification of an alleged offense for which a Coast Guard court-martial undertakes to pronounce a sentence; every specification on which a Coast Guard person stands trial, whether objected to at the trial or not, is tested for legal sufficiency upon review and is subject to being declared insufficient in law by the Secretary, (and in the case of deck courts, by the Commandant). 

62. CHARGES AND SPECIFICATIONS.-Only the general court-martial sets forth both a "Charge" and a specification or specifications under the charge. The essential pleading is the specification. There can be no legal trial without a specification of an offense; there can be a legal trial without designating a charge name for the specificatim. The charge is merely the name or title for what is to be specified below it as the punishable conduct. In the chapter dealing with charges and specifications in this manual, the names given to the charges are those names used in Title 14, U. S. Code, Section 567. 

As noted above, the essential pleading is the specification. A specification is a written statement of the essential elements alleged by the convening authority to constitute a punishable offense committed by the accused. As in the Navy, the Coast Guard summary court-martial and deck court set forth only the specification or specifications upon which the accused is to stand trial, without any charge name being pri rinted above the specification.

While charge names are invariably used in general court-martial pleadings, in keeping with the formality appropriate to these courts, the charge names are no more than formalities: the failure to place a specification under the appropriate charge, or even the misnomer of a charge, is harmless error, so long as the specification itself alleges an offense. The important thing is that the specification itself states -20-

facts sufficient in law to constitute an offense punishable by a Coast Guard court-martial. As to the general requirements in drawing a valid specification, the draftsman should consult Article 26 of this manual; as to the specific requirements for the particular offense at hand, the draftsman should consult the sample specifications and discussion in the pertinent articles of the chapter on charges and specifications of this manual. Since the law considers the substance and not the form of what is alleged, no particular or precise phraseology need be followed in drafting a specification; however, the sample specifications set forth in this manual for the guidance of the pleader costitute legally acceptable forms of pleading, and unnecessary departure from the approved sample may sometimes result in legally defective specifications. 63 

CONVEN ING AUTHORITY'S DISCRETION IN PREFERRING SPECIFICATIONS.- 

The convening authority "prefers" specifications against an accused when he signs the referral order directing the accused to stand trial before the designated court-martial on the particular specifications therewith set forth. It is within his discretion to say what specified offenses are to be brought to trial. Thus, in the exercise of a sound discretion, he may decide that certain of the specifications submitted to him for referral to a court-martial should not go to trial, either because they are trivial, or because the prosecutor will be unable to produce necessary evidence, or because some other good reason for not trying the accused with respect to the particular specification exists. 

While the convening authority is authorized to prefer multiple specifications against an accused to cover various contingencies of proof, good judgment should be exercised to avoid inequity and unreasonable multiplication of specifications. Thus, what is substantially one transaction or course of conduct by the accused should not be made the basis for an unreasonable number of specifications. The purpose of the court-martial is to accomplish substantial penal justice; the interests of the Government do not require that every possible punishable aspect of an incident be ferreted out and made the subject of a separate allegation against the accused.

67. JOINT OFFENSES.-A joint offense is one committed by two or more persons acting together in pursuance of a common design. It is a case of a single offense which it took two or more persons to commit. 

The joint offenders are named in a single specification containing the allegation that the offenders acted "in pursuance of a "in pursuance of a common design' or some other words signifying concert of action. When trial in joinder is directed, the offenders appear at one and the same time before the same court for trial together. Each pleads separately and separate findings and sentences are recorded as to each. An accused jointly charged with another may ask the convening authority for a severance, that is, for a separate trial, usually on the ground that possible unfairness may result if the witnesses are inconsistent or antagonistic. Likewise the prosecution may ask for separate trials, usually on the ground that the testimony of one accused is wanted against the other. An accused in a joint trial cannot be called as a witness for the prosecution without his consent to testify against the other accused.

Desertion, AOL and AWOL offenses should not be alleged as joinder offenses, since in the absence of a conspiracy these are individual offenses. 

68. DELAY; SPEEDY PROSECUTION.-The U. S. Constitution guarantees to the accused in all criminal prosecutions the right to a speedy trial and accordingly, a court-martial accused is entitled to a reasonably quick trial. The right is a relative one since justice, not speed, is the fundamental consideration; a reasonable delay, therefore, is not unconstitutional. Where inordinate delay occurs -23-

B at any point in the disciplinary process an explanation for the delay should appear of record so that it may be determined whether or not the delay was reasonable. Ordinarily, the convening authority, in his action on the record will state briefly the reasons for any unusual delay. In offenses of unauthorized absence (desertion excepted) and breaking arrest, twenty days is considered to be a reasonable time between the return of the accused to the jurisdiction of the service and the completion of his courtroom trial; in any such case if more than twenty days elapses, the convening authority should offer an explanation for the delay. In any other case, although no definite time limit is set, inordinate delay should be explained. The accumulation of offenses is an improper practice: that is, the commanding officer should not allow an offense to pass without taking disciplinary action and then later prefer a specification for such offense when another offense is committed. If an offense warrants punishment at all, punishment should be imposed at once. As to delay in deck court cases, see article 21, this manual. 69.

CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS OF ACCUSED.-Coast Guard courts derive their authority from Congress under the Constitution, which is the supreme law of the land. These courts must abide by the Constitution; persons tried by them must be accorded the rights guaranteed and protected by the Constitution. Otherwise their proceedings may be declared null and void.

The Constitutional rights of an accused before a Coast Guard courtmartial include: protection against double jeopardy, against selfincrimination and against unreasonable searches and seizures; the right to be tried according to due process of law; the right to a speedy and public trial (although not to a jury trial, which the 

Constitution itself makes inapplicable to courts-martial); the right to be informed as to the nature and cause of the accusation against him; the right to be confronted with the witnesses against him; the right to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor; and the right to assistance of counsel.

Many of the foregoing rights are relative, and not absolute; others of these rights are much broader in content than first appears, and their extent and application remain the subject of current United States Supreme Court decisions. In addition to what follows below, a brief discussion of the application of Constitutional rights to the accused at a Coast Guard trial is contained in Law Bulletin 149. 

700 SAME: DUE PROCESS OF LAW. -Law includes much more than a statute of Congress.

For example, nothing in the recently enacted 

Coast Guard Law, Title 14 of the U. S. Code, requires a court-martial to be sworn or the witnesses before it to testify under oath or that the accused be allowed to cross-examine the witnesses against him. 

But due process of law requires it; and it is in obedience to due process of law that this manual prescribes forms of oaths for courtsmartial use. -24-

The U. S. Supreme Court has interpreted the Constitution's undefined guarantee of due process of law as a guarantee of procedural standards adequate and appropriate to protecting an accused charged or suspected of crime from those who hold power over him. Only if the procedural safeguards of Due Process have been obeyed, may a court conviction be upheld and its sentence carried out.

The phrase "due process of law" as used in the Constitution has a meaning in Anglo-American law dating back to Magna Carta. It guarantees the application of standards of fairness and justice very broadly conceived and expresses a demand for civilized standards other than those covered by the specifically enumerated guarantees of the Bill of Rights. 

Judges are not free to apply their own idea of what is fair and just, but only the impersonal standards of the law. Due process of law is so fundamentally a part of our democracy, that a court which violates it loses the jurisdiction which it had at the start of the trial. process requires, among other things, that the court be legally constituted and sworn; that witnesses testify under oath or affirmation; that the court decide contested issues solely on the basis of legal evidence; that the prosecution have the burden of establishing guilt beyond a reasonable doubt and that no burden of proving innocence be laid upon the accused; that the accused have a reasonable time to prepare his defense, and that he be accorded at the trial the rights to challenge for cause, to cross-examine witnesses against him, and, in general, to have a fair trial and one that is free from intimidation or illegal coercion.

A Federal District Court in a habeas corpus proceeding may consider that the circumstances of a court-martial proceeding and the manner in which it was conducted ran afoul of the basic standard of fairness which is involved in the Constitutional concept of due process of law and, if it so finds, is empowered to declare that the accused has been deprived of his liberty in violation of the fifth amendment to the Constitution, and to discharge him from custody, or otherwise to overcome the conviction. 

71. ADVISING ACCUSED OF HIS RIGHTS; DUTIES OF PROSECUTOR.-The 

Constitutional right of the accused to be informed of the nature of the accusation against him requires affirmative actim if he is confined at any time before specifications of his offenses have been shown to him and, in any event, when he is brought to mast. In these instances the accused is told orally the reason for his confinement or his appearance at mast. If he is to be tried by court-martial, as promptly as possible after the paper containing the specifications and the referral order has been signed, a copy is served upon him by the recorder of the summary court-martial or the Judge Advocate of the general court-martial. At the time of such service, the prosecutor will explain to the accused in plain ordinary language what it is that he will have to defend himself against. At the same time he will inform the accused that he may have a reasonable time to prepare his defense. At the time of serving the specifications, it is good practice to take a receipt therefor. -25-

1. 

Right to counsel.-The prosecutor will advise the accused that he is entitled to retain a civilian lawyer or to request that a suitable officer be detailed to act as his counsel. Military counsel of his own selection, if reasonably available, may be assigned for him. 

He will further inform the accused that he ought to ask for counsel unless he intends to stand up in open court and state that he does not desire counsel. In a desertim case he will inform the accused that he will not be permitted to appear without representation and that if he does not select counsel of his own, one will be provided for him. 

Effect of plea. -The prosecutor will further advise the accused that if he pleads guilty to any one specification, no proof need be produced by the prosecution as to that specification and the court will find him guilty of it; that as to any specification to which he pleads not guilty, the prosecution must produce the type of proof acceptable in a court of law; that the burden of proving guilt will be on the proseoutim; that the accused may cross-examine witnesses against him and may introduce witnesses in his own behalf; that the Government will summon witnesses requested by the accused if such witnesses are within the jurisdiction of the court.

If it appears that the accused may have a defense, discussion of the case should terminate at ance with the advice that the accused plead not guilty and secure counsel. The prosecutor must never suggest to an accused that he plead guilty to anything. 

Testimony or statement by accused.-The prosecutor will further inform the accused that he has the right to take the stand and testify as a witness in his own behalf; but that he cannot be called as a witness by the prosecution, and has the right to remain silent, and that the court may not draw any unfavorable inference against the accused from his failure to testify that if he testifies, he may be cross-examined. Further, that just before the court retires to make its findings, it will ask the accused if he desires to make a statement; that he may thereupon read a prepared statement or make an oral statement without being sworn and without being subject to cross-examination; that such a statement cannot and will not be acted upon by the court as legal proof in the case, but is intended only as a vehicle for the accused to plead for the clemency of the Secretary of the Treasury without whose action no sentence may be executed; that if, following a plea of guilty, the accused says anything in the statement which can be construed as contrary to his plea of guilty, the court will thereupon strike out his plea of guilty, enter a plea of not guilty and proceed with the trial on that basis; that he must therefore avoid any statement of fact inconsistent with his plea of guilty that a statement made by the accused cannot prove anything, but if he desires to offer proof of any facts in mitigation of an offense, he can do so in the regular process of the trial after the findings have been made. 

Matter in mitigation and extenuation. -The prosecutor will inform the accused that after the court has made its findings, following -26-

either a guilty or a not guilty plea, the accused may introduce (1) matter in mitigation of the punishment and (2) matter in extenuation of the offense. Such matter is not, strictly speaking, evidence, because it does not go to the issue of his guilt or innocence and therefore the widest latitude is allowed in its reception. Thus, matter in mitigation may include letters attesting good character as well as material from his service record or oral testimony in his favor. The accused may himself testify on this side of the case, and because such testimony is under oath, it will carry more weight so far as any facts are concerned than does his unsworn statement before the findings. The court and the reviewing authorities will give to matter in mitigation and extenuation such weight and effect as in their judgment it appears to be entitled to. Matter in extenuation may properly explain the circumstances surrounding the commission of the offense. However, if any such matter introduced after a guilty plea is inconsistent with such plea then the court will have to reject the plea, enter a not guilty plea and proceed to try the issue.

Right to repudiate confession.-The prosecutor will further inform the accused that if he has signed a confession, and believes that he was taken advantage of or that his free will was overcome in obtaining the confession from him, he has the right to repudiate the confession; that he should then by all means obtain counsel for himself; that he can object to the admission of the confession in evidence at the trial and can himself testify in court as to the circumstances under which he signed it without subjecting himself to cross-examination on the issue of guilt or innocence.

Final statement. -For a parting statement, the prosecutor will state to the accused that he is about to be tried by a court-martial created under the authority of an Act of Congress; that under Coast Guard procedure he is entitled to every right guaranteed and protected by the Federal Constitution, and that the court-martial will try him in accordance with due process of military law. 72.

DUTIES OF PROSECUTOR BEFORE TRIAL. -The recorder in the summary court-martial and the Judge Advocate in the general court-martial prosecute in the name of the United States and, under the direction of the court, prepare the record of trial and see that it is accurate and in proper form.

The prosecutor's chief responsibility is that of presenting to the court the legal evidence available to prove each and every allegation of the specifications. He must familiarize himself, therefore, with the legal nature and elements of the particular offense to be tried, so that he will know what is and is not material in order to constitute the offense. He must analyze the particular fact allegations of the specifications which he is to prosecute so that he can determine what he must prove to the court. He must study the rules of evidence so that he will know how to prove his case. 

He will correct and initial slight errors or obvious mistakes in the specifications but will not without authority make any substantial -27-

changes therein.

He will report any substantial irregularity in the precept or specifications to the convening authority. 

He is entitled to the Mast Information Report and any other record of investigation or inquiry had before trial; he will examine any such for fact material and to learn who the witnesses may be. 

If it appears that any officer named as a member of the court is needed as a witness in the case, or is liable to challenge, he will notify the convening authority so that the precept may be amended to provide for a different member.

He may join in written stipulations with the defense counsel as to unimportant and uncontested matters of fact alleged in the specification, which would otherwise have to be proved, with a view to saving time, labor and expense.

Prior to trial he will notify and arrange to have present at the trial, the witnesses who are to testify in person, including those desired by the accused, and the reporter and interpreter, if any. 

He should arrange with the president of the court as to the time of trial, giving due consideration to the right of the accused and his counsel to have adequate time to prepare the defense, and see that all concerned are notified of the date, hour, and exact place of meeting of the court.

CHAPTER III SUMMARY AND GENERAL COURTS-MARTIAL PART 2. Matters relating to the actual trial. 

100. ASSEMBLING THE COURT.-A general or summary court-martial as sembles in accordance with the order convening it and thereafter as directed in the order of adjournment. The presiding member of the court ordinarily fixes the hour and date for the first and subsequent meetings. 

When the court is ready to proceed, he calls it to order. The members sit to the right and left alternately of the President according to rank. 

The prosecution usually sits on the right side of the room, facing the court, and the defense on the left. The witness chair faces the president and the reporter's table is placed near the witness chair. A copy of the charges and specifications should be furnished to each member of the court, if each has not earlier been supplied with the same. 

101. SECURITY OF THE ACCUSED.-The officer in whose custody or com mand the accused is at the time of trial is responsible for his attendance before the court. The presence of the accused throughout the trial is an indispensable requirement of due process of law, except where he has escaped or where proceedings in revision without new testimony are had. The court does not control the arrest or other status of restraint of an accused. 

102. INTRODUCING ACCUSED.-The Judge Advocate (recorder) announces the name of the accused and of his counsel. If the accused has no counsel, the president informs him that he still has a right to consult counsel before proceeding and that counsel will be assigned unless he states in open court that he does not desire counsel. In a desertion case, counsel must be provided for the accused. The president may himself adjourn the court to provide counsel for the accused, and for so many days thereafter as may be necessary to allow counsel to prepare. 

After introduction of the accused and his counsel the reporter is sworn. 

103. CONTINUANCES.-For reasonable cause, the president of the court martial may grant a continuance to either party for such time and as often as may appear necessary to the end that a full and fair trial may be had. A continuance is the adjournment or postponement of the trial to a subsequent day. Usual grounds for granting a continuance are the absence of an important witness; sickness; insufficient time to prepare. 104. 

CHALLENGES.-Before swearing the court, each member is subject. to challenge by the accused and by the Judge Advocate (recorder). The grounds for challenge are as follows: (a) That the member sat on a board which investigated the offense charged or that he conducted an investigation of the offense before trial; if this is a new trial, that he sat on the former trial. TIA -30-

(b) That he has formed or expressed a positive and definite opinion as to the guilt or innocence of the accused, (c) That he will be a witness for the prosecution.

(a) The general ground of bias or prejudice. Upon this ground, any facts which may indicate that the member cannot try the accused with complete impartiality may urnish support to the challenge. Examples of such facts tending to support this ground are: that the member sal on a court which tried a case so closely related to this one as to bring into serious question the member's ability to try the pending issue with an open mind; that he is directly concerned in the offense charged; or that he initiated the accusation against the accused; or that he personally recommended to the convening authority the present trial of the accused. The court should be liberal in sustaining challenges predicated on the ground of bias or prejudice. 

105. CHALLENGE PROCEDURE.-The court will not receive a challenge to more than one member at a time. If any of the grounds of challenge are admitted the member is excused forthwith. Otherwise the challenged member may be examined under oath, and both sides may introduce evidence on the question of his competency and make an argument. The Judge Advocate (recorder) administers the following oath to a member challenged and about to be examined: "You swear (or affirm) that you will answer truthfully to questions touching your competency as a member of the court in this case, so help you God?"

A belief of the accused that a member is personally biased or prejudiced against him is not enough: it must be supported by reasons and facts which give fair support to the belief that the member's bent of mind may prevent or impede an impartial judgment. 

The challenge is decided by a majority vote from among the other members. In case of a tie vote, the challenge fails, If the court is reduced below a legal quorum by reasons of challenges sustained, it adjourns to await the appointment of new members by the convening authority. 106. 

DUTY OF MEMBER TO DISCLOSE HIS DISQUALIFICATION.-If a member, although not challenged, believes that he cannot decide the issue with complete indifference and impartiality or that an indisputable ground of challenge exists as to him, it is his duty to make a disclosure to the court and he will be excused forthwith. 

107. MEMBER AS WITNESS.-If, during the course of the trial, the prosecution or the court calls a member as a witness, he must thereupon cease to act as a member of the court. The record should contain an appropriate entry to the effect that the witness did not continue as a member of the court. -31-

108. OATHS.-After the proceedings as to challenges, the members of the court and the Judge Advocate (recorder) are sworn. This completes the organization of the court. An oath or affirmation also must be administered to each and every witness called before the court to testify. 

The forms of oath appear in Chapter VI of this manual. 109.

ARRAIGNMENT.-The court being organized, the Judge Advocate reads the charge(s), and the specification(s), and asks the accused how he pleads to each specification, and then to the charge under which the specification appears. In a summary court-martial, there being no designated charges, the recorder arraigns the accused only as to specifications. 110.

PLEAS.-Except for special pleas in bar of trial, the only pleas to be made are guilty or not guilty. The court will not accept a plea of guilty to a desertion charge; if it does so, a reviewing authority will return the record for trial of the issue. If the accused stands mute, a plea of not guilty must be entered for him. In a general court-martial a plea of guilty to any one of several specifications under a charge requires a plea of guilty to the charge. This is automatic. A plea of guilty to a specification admits what the specification alleges and dispenses with proof. The court may refuse to accept a plea of guilty where it appears to the court that it is made improvidently or without an understanding of the nature of the charge. A plea of guilty admits nothing as to the jurisdiction of the court or as to the legal sufficiency of the specification. 111.

NOLLE PROSEQUI.-A nolle prosequi is a declaration of record by the prosecution that by direction of the convening authority the prosecution withdraws a certain specification and will not prosecute it at the instant trial. The convening authority may direct the nolle prosequi on his own initiative or upon application of the prosecutor. It may be directed by reason of the unavailability of evidence, or for any other reason which appears to the convening authority sufficient. 

112. SPECIAL PLEA IN BAR OF TRIAL.-The accused may, before his regular plea, enter a special plea in bar of trial on the ground of the statute of limitations or on the ground of double jeopardy. Both of these defenses are also available after the accused has pleaded to the specifications. The burden is on the accused to prove these special defenses. 

If the court sustains a plea in bar of trial, the procedure is to notify the convening authority and to adjourn to await his further instructions. 

The convening authority may either accept the ruling of the court and discontinue the case, or he may return the record to the court with directions to reconsider its ruling upon the special plea in bar of trial, accompanying such direction with a statement of the reasons prompting it. 

113. STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS.-Section 568 of Title 14 is a statute of limitations which forbids trial for an offense committed more than two years before the issuance of the order for such trial. For desertion in time of peace the two-year period does not begin to run until the end of the enlistment term. A statute of limitations is an act of grace on the part of the Government and its application may be withheld and the running of the statute tolled for good by the simple act of signing an order -32-

for trial before the limitation period has expired, even though the accused is absent in desertion at the time. If a manifest impediment exists to bringing the accused to trial (for example, if the accused was in prison under sentence of a civil court), such period is excluded in computing the 2-year limitations period. If a good defense on the ground of the statute of limitations is available to the accused, it is the duty of the prosecutor, whether the accused has counsel or not, to bring it to the attention of the convening authority before trial and ask a nolle prosequi, or after trial commences, to bring it to the attention of the court so that the court may direct the entry of a plea in bar of trial on the ground of the statute. 

114. DOUBLE JEOPARDY.-An accused before a Coast Guard court who has been tried and acquitted or convicted and punished by a civil court, state or Federal, for substantially the same conduct which is made the subject of court-martial specification(s) has a good defense in bar of trial by the court-martial. This rule, first announced by the Secretary of the Treasury in general court orders quoted in Law Bulletins 131, page 2, and 152, page 16, is an extension of the spirit of the Constitutional protection against double jeopardy. Even if the defense is not raised at the court-martial, the Secretary of the Treasury will recognize and consider it upon review.

An accused may not be tried twice by a Coast Guard court for the same offense, but no proceeding in which an accused has been found guilty by a court-martial upon any specification shall be held to be a trial within this rule unless and until the Secretary of the Treasury has taken final action upon the case if a summary or general court-martial, or until the convening authority has acted if a deck court. In general, a person convicted of an offense cannot thereafter be tried for another offense based on the same conduct if either offense is necessarily included in the other and different from the other only in degree. 

115. INSANITY OF ACCUSED.-When we speak of insanity in connection. with Coast Guard courts-martial, we mean "not mentally responsible for an offense" or "lacking the mental capacity to stand trial." The legal andard of mental accountability for Coast Guard courts is: "A person is not mentally responsible in a criminal sense for an offense unless he was, at the time, so far free from mental defect, disease, or derangement as to he able concerning the particular act charged both to distinguish right from wrong and also to be able to adhere to the right." 

116. SAME; CAPACITY AT TIME OF TRIAL; PRELIMINARY INQUIRY.-Even as suming that an accused was mentally responsible at the time of commission of an offense, he will not be brought to trial unless he possesses sufficient mental capacity to understand the nature of the proceedings against him and to conduct or cooperate in his defense. If, before trial commences, the commanding officer or any investigating officer, or the Judge Advocate, or defense counsel form a well-founded suspicion of the insanity of the accused, a preliminary inquiry into his mental condition should be directed. The psychiatrists or medical board examining the accused should be asked to make separate and distinct findings upon each of -33-

the three following questions: (a) Was the accused at the time of the alleged offense so far free from mental defect, disease, or derangement as to be able concerning the particular acts charged to distinguish right from wrong? (b) Was the accused at the time of the alleged offense so far free from mental defect, disease or derangement as to be able concerning the particular acts charged to adhere to the right? (c) Does the accused possess sufficient mental capacity to understand the nature of the proceedings against him and intelligently to conduct or cooperate in his defense?

On the basis of the medical report received, further action in the case may be suspended and proceedings taken to discharge or retire the accused from the Service on grounds of mental disability (or otherwise as may be administratively provided) or, if affirmative answers are received to all three questions, the charges and specifications may be referred for trial. 

117. INSANITY OF ACCUSED AS DEFENSE AT TRIAL.-Even though an ac cused is found mentally responsible and able to stand trial by the medical report resulting from an inquiry before trial, the accused may still dispute his mental responsibility for the offense charged at the trial. The issue is raised under the plea of "Not Guilty"; it is not the subject of a special plea.

An accused is presumed sane, but once evidence to disturb the presumption is produced in court (usually by the defense) the burden of proving him mentally responsible for the offense charged rests on the prosecution. In order to disturb the presumption of sanity, however, some evidence must be produced; the mere assertion in court by the accused or his counsel that the accused is insane, is not sufficient. 

If, in the light of all the evidence, including that supplied by the presumption, a reasonable doubt as to the mental responsibility of the accused at the time of the offense remains, the court must find him not guilty.

If a reasonable doubt exists as to the mental capacity of the accused at the time of trial, the court will adjourn and transmit to the convening authority the record as far as had with a statement of its finding as to the present mental capacity of the accused. If the convening authority disagrees with the court in its finding that the accused lacks present mental capacity or where reviewing officials determine that the disability was temporary and that the accused has recovered his mental capacity, he or they may return the case to the court with instructions to reconsider its findings and, if appropriate, to proceed with the trial. -34-

118. INSANITY, ACCEPTABLE EVIDENCE-Although the testimony of an expert on mental disorders as to his observations and opinion with respect to the mental condition of the accused may be given greater weight than the testimony of a lay witness, anyone acquainted with the accused who has observed his behavior may testify as to what he observed and thereupon express a lay opinion as to the accused's "sanity." A lay witness may not be asked for his opinion based upon a hypothetical question; only an expert may be asked to state an opinion based upon a hypothetical question.

Evidence on this issue should be presented by the testimony of witnesses in open court, by deposition, or by documentary evidence. An official medical report of examination containing the opinion of an expert on mental disorders as to the mental responsibility of the accused at the time certain acts were committed by him or as to his mental capacity to stand trial is admissible in evidence. The weight to be accorded such evidence, however, is for the court to decide.

Counsel for the accused may similarly make an opening statement. When all the evidence is in, and before the findings, both sides may present arguments. The Judge Advocate argues first, then the defense, and then the 

Judge Advocate may make a closing argument. The argument is not a vehicle of evidence and it is improper to refer in it to any facts which are not in evidence or which are not matters of judicial notice. 

120. DUTY OF COURT IN RESPECT TO FINDINGS.-The duty of the court members is to see to it that the accused is fairly tried according to the American Constitutional conception of what constitutes a fair trial. (See Articles 69-70, this manual), In respect to making findings, it is their duty to decide whether the accused did the things that the specification says he did; they will decide on the basis of nothing but the legal evidence in the case. It is a prerequisite to an adequate discharge of their duty, that the members of a court-martial familiarize themselves with the general rules of evidence set forth in this manual. "Evidence" means the kind of proof that a court of law may credit. 

Evidence is ordinarily submitted on both sides of a disputed issue of fact. When the court retires to make its findings it "weighs" the evidence; that is, a mental balancing of the probabilities based solely upon the proof produced in court takes place. If the members find that the evidence leaves a reasonable doubt in their minds as to the guilt of the accused they will acquit; or, if there is a lesser included offense as to which there is no such doubt, they will find him guilty in the lesser degree,

Every court member is expected to know that under our law an accused must be presumed innocent until his guilt is established by legal evidence -35-

FINDINGS: WHAT IS MEANT BY REASONABLE DOUBT.-If the evi dence adduced in a contested case is sufficient to satisfy the court of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt it will convict. By "reasonable doubt" is intended not fanciful doubt or pure conjecture, but substantial, honest, consciencious doubt generated by the evidence or the lack of it in the case, and not merely by the ingenuity of counsel. The rule is not that there must be an acquittal in all cases of doubt - it may be that there are no cases without doubt. It must be a doubt solemn and substantial enough to produce in the minds of the members of the court grave uncertainty as to the verdict to be given. 

In other words, to convict the accused, the judges, who are the triers of the facts, must find that the facts proved, by virtue of their probative force, establish his guilt. 

122. FINDINGS; EXCEPTIONS AND SUBSTITUTIONS TO SPECIFICATIONS.- 

The court may, in writing its findings, except words or figures from the specification and, where necessary, substitute others; provided, however, that a legal specification is left remaining. Thus, the court must be careful not to eliminate words expressive of an essential element of the offense; nor is the court authorized to change the nature or identify of the offense so as to find the accused guilty of an offense for which the convening authority did not order him to stand trial, unless such offense is a lesser included offense within that charged against him.

For example, the court may not by its findings change a specification alleging disobedience of a lawful order into a specification alleging neglect of duty. Nor may the court find the accused guilty of an accusation of which he was not informed by the specification: thus, one charged with stealing a watch may not be found guilty instead of stealing a ten-dollar bill. A specification charging AWOL terminated by delivery may, however, by appropriate exception and substitution, be changed to read that the AWOL was terminated by surrender. Here, a word which does not affect the essence of the offense is changed. 

In making exceptions and substitutions, therefore, the court must make certain (1) that the specification still states facts sufficient to constitute an offense, and (2) that the offense now stated is not different in its essential legal quality from that with which the accused was originally charged and which the court was directed to try. 

123. FINDINGS; LESSER INCLUDED OFFENSES.-The court may find the accused guilty of a lesser offense which is included within the offense specified, and of the essence of the offense specified. An attempt is usually a lesser included offense. The test of what properly may be considered a lesser included offense is: In proving the offense specified, is it necessary to prove all the elements of the lesser offense found? Thus, AWOL may be found under desertion; or negligent operation of a vehicle under manslaughter; or misappropriating property of the -36-

United States under theft of such property; or an attempt to strike another (assault) under a specification alleging the actual striking. 

124. VOTING ON FINDINGS.-Except where the accused has pleaded guilty to all specifications, the court will sit in closed session in deliberating and voting on the findings. Either the courtroom is cleared, or the members withdraw to another room. Voting is by secret ballot, signed by the member, and is obligatory. A majority rules. 

Each specification is voted on separately; the vote is tallied and the result announced by the president or senior member to the other members before the next specification is voted upon. Each ballot slip is read aloud by the president without disclosing who signed it. The finding on a charge, in a general court-martial, is controlled automatically by the findings on the specifications. Accordingly, a finding of "proved" on only one of several specifications under a charge would require entry of a finding of "guilty" as to the charge.

The findings may be typewritten or they may be entered in the handwriting of the Judge Advocate or recorder. It is essential that the findings be worded clearly so that there may be no mistake as to what the verdict of the court was.

127. ACCUSED'S STATEMENT BEFORE FINDINGS.-Just before the court re tires to make its findings, and after the arguments involving the law and the evidence have been made, the accused personally may make a statement, oral or in writing, in the nature of a plea for clemency. This statement is not made under oath and has no value as evidence in favor of the accused.

If the accused, after having pleaded guilty to a specification, adverts to matters of fact in his statement which may tend to cast doubt upon his guilt, the court will reject the guilty plea, enter a plea of not guilty, and proceed to try the issue. If an accused who has pleaded not guilty but has failed to testify as a witness in his own behalf, adverts to matters of fact in his statement, the president of the court should promptly stop him, remind him that his statement in the circumstances may not go beyond a plea for clemency, and offer him the opportunity of then and there requesting that he be sworn as a witness if he desires to make statements of fact bearing upon the issue; and the court may further remind him that he will be privileged to testify as to fact matters in mitigation or extenuation after the findings have been made. 

If it should appear from the statement of the accused that any plea of guilty made by him was improvidently entered or made without adequate understanding of the nature of the charge against him or the effect of the plea, the court should reject the plea, enter a plea of not guilty and try the issue. The convening authority or the Commandant or the Secretary of the Treasury may remand the case to the court with directions to enter a plea of not guilty and try the issue in any case where from the record it appears that the court erred in not doing so of its own motion.

Upon the request of the accused and upon proper assurances being made to the court, the president of the court may in his discretion permit the accused to defer the making of a clemency statement until after the findings have been recorded.

Since matter of this nature is introduced after the findings have been recorded, it cannot affect the issue of guilt or innocence, and accordingly there is no need to apply strict rules of evidence to its reception. -38-

The latter includes the rate of pay in the present and next inferior ratings of the accused, the date and the term of his current enlistment, the date of birth given by the accused upon enlistment, and information as to allotments from his pay. 130. VOTING ON THE SENTENCE.-The sentence is determined by a major ity of votes in both the general and the summary court-martial. When the court has been closed for the purpose of determining the sentence, each member shall write down and subscribe the measure of punishment which he may think the accused ought to receive and hand his vote to the senior member, who shall, after receiving all the votes, read them aloud.

If the requisite number do not agree upon the nature and degree of the punishment to be inflicted, the senior member proceeds in the following manner to obtain a decision: He shall begin with the mildest punishment that has been proposed, and after reading it aloud, shall ask the members successively, beginning with the junior in rank, Shall this be the sentence of the court? And every member shall vote orally, and the senior member shall note the votes. Should there be no decision, the senior member shall, in the same manner as before, obtain a vote on the next mildest punishment, and shall so continue until a sentence is decided upon. The court is to pronounce a fair and just sentence, adequate to the nature and circumstances of the offense(s) of which the accused is guilty. After the sentence is recorded the individual members of the court may make and sign a recommendation for clemency if they so desire. -39-

131. SUMMARY COURT-MARTIAL PUNISHMENTS.-A Coast Guard summary court martial now has the same sentencing power which Navy summary courts-martial have. Section 563(b) of Title 14 provides: 

"(b) Summary courts-martial may sentence enlisted persons to either a part or the whole, as may be appropriate, of any one of the following punishments, namely: (1) discharge from the service with a bad conduct discharge, but the sentence shall not be carried into effect in a foreign country; (2) confinement not exceeding two months; (3) solitary confinement not exceeding thirty days; (4) solitary confinement, not exceeding thirty days, on bread and water or on diminished rations; reduction to the next inferior rating; deprivation of liberty on shore for a period not exceeding three months; and (7) extra police duty and loss of pay not to exceed three months.

Extra police duties and loss of pay within the limitations prescribed may be added to any one of the first six punishments enumerated above. (c) No sentence of a summary court-martial shall be carried into execution until the proceedings and sentence are approved by the Secretary.'

Combined punishments limited. -The court must be careful to adjudge only one of the first six above enumerated punishments. Number 7 may be adjudged in whole or in part as the entire sentence of the court; or Number 7 in whole or in part may be combined with any of the other six. Solitary confinement on bread and water or diminished rations.-If this punishment is adjudged, the court must add these words: with a full ration every 3rd (or 2nd) day." A medical statement to the effect that execution of such punishment will not produce serious injury to the health of the accused must accompany the record. The punishment may not be adjudged in combination with simple confinement. For example, it is illegal to sentence a man to "Two (2) months' confinement, the first twenty (20) days of which shall be solitary confinement on bread and water, etc.". The punishment should not be adjudged unless facilities for solitary confinement are available. The convening authority is not authorized to extend solitary confinement to the limits of the station, in executing a sentence, but he is authorized under 14 USC 566 to remit, in his action on the record, so much of a sentence as requires the confinement to be solitary or on bread and water. It is considered detrimental to morale to adjudge and enforce this punishment upon a petty officer If the punishment is not over 10 days, the medical certificate is not required.

Confinement not exceeding two months.-A sentence of sixty days may sometimes exceed two months and therefore be contrary to law. -40-

Reduction to next inferior rating. This punishment should not ordinarily be imposed in combination with loss of pay, as a matter of policy, since the accused would thereby be penalized with both a fixed and a continuing loss of pay. Under exceptional circumstances a departure from this policy may be warranted. A summary court-martial should not sentence a man to reduction to the lowest rating of his branch after he has completed his recruit training. 

Bad conduct discharge.-A summary court-martial cannot adjudge a "dishonorable discharge" but the statute does authorize a "bad conduct discharge". When a bad conduct discharge is imposed, the court should ordinarily add loss of pay and extra police duties to it. Then in the event that the bad conduct discharge is subsequently remitted outright or on probation, the accused will not altogether escape penalty. If execution of the bad conduct discharge is authorized by the Secretary, the loss of pay and extra police duties will be remitted (in accordance with 

Navy practice).

Loss of pay.-A sentence of loss of pay shall state the rate and total amount of pay to be lost and the period of time over which it shall extend. Loss of pay shall be stated in dollars and cents and not in days' pay.

Loss of pay per month should not exceed one-half of the man's base plus longevity pay. A sentence of loss of pay operates only upon such pay as accrues to the accused on and after the date of approval of such sentence by the Secretary of the Treasury; a sentence of loss of pay cannot affect any pay which accrued to the accused before such date. (This rule accords with the Navy Department's long-standing interpretation of the legal operation of loss of pay pronounced by a summary courtmartial).

Extra police duties.-Since the phraseology of a penal statute must be followed in writing a legal court sentence, the summary court-martial should be careful to write "extra police duties" and not "extra duties, the latter not being provided by the statute and therefore illegal. Extra police duties should be stated in terms of days or months, not to exceed three months. In executing the punishment, no more than two hours of extra police duties may be required to be performed per day and such duties shall be discontinued on Sundays.

IMMEDIATE SUPERIOR IN COMMAND.-Section 566(a) of Title 14, pro vides: "The officer ordering a court, and his immediate superior in command, if any, in the case of a summary court-martial, or his successor in office, may set aside the proceedings or remit or mitigate, in whole or in part, but not commute, the sentence of such court."

This provision applies only to summary courts-martial. Unlike the Navy provisions (contained in Article 32 of the Articles for the Government of the Navy) our law does not make action by an Immediate Superior in Command, if such there be, a prerequisite to execution of the sentence. Also essentially different from Navy requirements of law, is the requirement of our law that no sentence of a summary court-martial can be carried out until the Secretary of the Treasury approves it. Accordingly, and in order to avoid delay in carrying out the sentence of a summary court-martial, the convening authority should forward the record directly to the Commandant (L) for action by the Secretary of the Treasury except only in those cases where the officer next superior to him in line of command has, with the approval of the Commandant, expressly directed that summary court-martial -42-

records be first forwarded to him for his action as Immediate Superior in Command. Otherwise, the merely permissive provision relating to Immediate Superior in Command contained in the above-quoted statute need not concern the convening authority.

While the convening authority possesses the legal power under the above statute to set aside the proceedings in the case of a summary court-martial, such power is not to be exercised arbitrarily, but only upon substantial legal grounds, Where legal doubt exists, a recommendation to the Secretary as to the legality of the proceedings would appear preferable to irretrievable action by the convening authority setting them aside. 

133. CONVENING AUTHORITY'S ACTION; MITIGATION OF SUMMARY COURT- 

MARTIAL SENTENCE.-The convening authority will in all cases attach a forwarding indorsement to the summary court-martial record indicating that he has examined it, If unusual delay has occurred in the proceedings, the convening authority will explain such delay. The convening authority is empowered to take positive action remitting in whole or reducing in part the sentence of the summary court-martial, or he may content himself with merely recommending such action. Once positive action reducing the sentence has been taken by the convening authority, neither the Secretary of the Treasury nor any other authority has the legal power to reinstate the original sentence, and can affect only what remains of the sentence. Specimen forms for positive action by the convening authority on summary court-martial sentences are shown at pages 364 to 369 of the publication "Naval Justice".

No convening authority may place upon the record an action purporting to disapprove a finding of not guilty. Such action can have no legal effect. Moreover an accused is entitled in law to the full benefit of a finding of not proved or of an acquittal.

135. EFFECT OF EXPIRATION OF ENLISTMENT; SEPARATION OF ACCUSED FROM 

SERVICE.-An enlisted man may be detained in the Coast Guard beyond the term of his enlistment while awaiting disciplinary action or trial and disposition of his case. However, an accused may not be detained in the 

Coast Guard beyond the expiration of his enlistment for the sole purpose of executing a summary court-martial sentence which does not require confinement; nor may he be detained for the sole purpose of serving out a period of probation. Any probationary term is considered automatically ended (if it has not theretofore expired) when the accused becomes otherwise entitled to a discharge from the service. 

It is contemplated that both summary and general court-martial cases will arise where, after the record of proceedings has been forwarded to Headquarters, it is decided to separate the accused from the Coast Guard by administrative action independent of and apart from the disciplinary proceedings taken. In such cases the sentence of the court-martial, although approved by the Secretary, is not to be regarded as barring necessary and proper administrative action, even though such action may result in the non-execution of the court sentence. 

136. EFFECTIVE DATE OF SUMMARY COURT-MARTIAL PUNISHMENTS. -No sen tence of a summary court-martial may be carried out until approved by the Secretary of the Treasury. The punishment of reduction to the next inferior rating is effective as of the date of approval by the Secretary. 

The punishment of loss of pay operates upon pay accruing on and after, but not before, the date of approval by the Secretary, and is to be effectuated in accordance with the pertinent Pay and Supply Instructions. 

A bad conduct discharge takes effect following approval by the Secretary when executed, but shall not be executed in a foreign country, and shall be executed as promptly as possible after notification of approval is received, provided that there is no necessity for retention of the accused, Execution of a bad conduct discharge may be deferred, however, by the commanding officer if further disciplinary involvements concerning the accused have intervened. 

Any other punishment adjudged by a summary court-martial shall be computed beginning with the date that official information by letter or dispatch of the action of the Secretary of the Treasury upon the sentence is actually received by an officer having the immediate command and custody of the accused. However, an approved sentence of confinement adjudged by one court while the accused is serving a sentence of confinement under a previous court, does not begin to run until the former period of confinement has been served, unless the Secretary's action in the later court provides otherwise. A commanding officer may carry out a sentence of confinement not involving solitary confinement by extending the confinement to the limits of the ship or station, during working hours or -44-

otherwise, as may appear expedient. Ordinarily the official notification of the Secretary's action upon a summary court-martial will be contained in a letter signed by the Chief Counsel of the Coast Guard and addressed to the convening authority, who will promptly take the appropriate steps necessary to carry the sentence into execution. If the accused has been transferred to another command meanwhile, the convening authority will forward the notification letter to the new command for execution or take such steps as may be necessary to see that the sentence is carried out. 

137. GENERAL COURT-MARTIAL PUNISHMENTS.-The legal punishments now authorized for a Coast Guard general court-martial are contained in sub paragraphs (b) and (c) of Section 564, Title 14, U. S. Code, reading as follows: 

"(b) General courts-martial may sentence a commissioned or warrant officer to any of the following punishments, namely: (c) (1) dismissal from the service; (2) suspension from duty for a period of two years or any part thereof upon reduced pay which shall in no case be less than one-half nor more than three-fourths of the duty pay of such officer; and (3) reduction of grade; (4) loss of numbers; (5) imprisonment for a period not to exceed 5 years; (6) official reprimand.

General courts-martial may sentence any person in the Coast Guard other than a commissioned or warrant officer either to a part or the whole, as may be appropriate, of any one of the following punishments, namely: (1) dishonorable discharge; (2) imprisonment for a period not to exceed five years and forfeiture of any pay that may accrue during the period of imprisonment, and any sentence of imprisonment shall include reduction to the rating of apprentice seaman; and (3) any sentence which a summary court-martial is authorized to inflict,"

Under the law as now written, the general court-martial must pronounce a sentence which is wholly embraced within one and only one of the above categories of punishments numbered by the statute. Thus, a general court-martial cannot sentence an enlisted man to both a dishonorable discharge and imprisonment. 

138. IMPRISONMENT AND "ACCESSORIES" .-A general court-martial sentence which adjudges imprisonment, shall contain the words " .. and to suffer all the other accessories of the said sentence." For example: "The court, therefore, sentences him, Walter T. Hatch, boatswain's mate second class, -45-

U. S. Coast Guard, to be imprisoned for a period of eighteen (18) months, and to suffer all the other accessories of the said sentence as prescribed by Article 138, Coast Guard Court-Martial Manual." 

The words "other accessories" when so used in the sentence of the general court-martial shall be understood to include the following: (a) the forfeiture of any pay (but not allowances) that may accrue during the period of imprisonment, except such amounts as the pertinent Pay and Supply Instructions may declare to be exempt from forfeiture (the cost of transportation of discharged prisoners, an allowance for necessary prison expenses including health and comfort items, the furnishing of civilian clothing on discharge and the payment of a discharge gratuity, etc., may thus be made chargeable against pay to accrue to the prisoner, so much of the pay being thus exempted from outright forfeiture) and (b) automatic reduction of the accused to seaman recruit or its equivalent. 

Any unliquidated portions of loss of pay under prior sentence of a deck court or summary court-martial are considered to be automatically remitted upon the approval date of a sentence of a general court-martial involving imprisonment with accessories.

When the word "confinement" is used in a general court-martial sentence, it is pursuant to the power of the general court-martial to adjudge any sentence authorized for a summary court-martial; accordingly it is never appropriate or regular for a court to use the word "confinement" when it means "imprisonment", nor to adjudge "confinement" for a period exceeding two months, nor to use the word "accessories" in connection with the punishment of "confinement".

Designation of place of imprisonment.. In cases where a man is sentenced to imprisonment as distinguished from confinement, the general courtmartial order signed by the Secretary of the Treasury usually will designate a Naval Disciplinary Barracks or Retraining Command as the place of imprisonment. The court in its sentence should make no statement concerning the designation of a place of imprisonment. The designation of a place of imprisonment in a general court-martial order constitutes only an administrative direction and is not an essential part of the sentence; it is subject to change at the request or direction of the appropriate Naval authorities or the Commandant. Similarly the direction in a general court-martial order that the commander of a certain district is to execute the sentence is not a condition to the execution of the sentence but is intended only. as a notification to the officer in whose district the accused stood trial, to take the initial steps necessary to execute the sentence. 

A sentence of imprisonment being served at a place under the control of the U. S. Navy or of Federal civil authorities is subject to the administrative regulations in force at such place, including the regulations concerning reduction in the period of imprisonment by one-third as and for a good conduct allowance, procedures pertaining to restoration to duty, and so forth. 139.

DISPOSITION OF GENERAL COURT-MARTIAL PRISONERS.-The law does not authorize a general court-martial to adjudge a dishonorable or a bad conduct -460

discharge in combination with a sentence of imprisonment. An accused convicted by a general court-martial and sentenced to be imprisoned presumptively should be separated from the service with an undesirable discharge if he has not earned the right to be restored to duty after serving the required part of his imprisonment. In accordance with current policies in effect at disciplinary barracks and retraining commands to which Coast Guard general court-martial prisoners are sent, such prisoners ordinarily are not to be discharged from. the service at the outset of the imprisonment. Where an accused is sentenced to imprisonment, and is not thereafter restored to duty following a favorable action upon recommendation of the prison clemency board or other authority, an undesirable discharge, under directions to be issued by the Commandant, ordinarily will be issued to him upon completion of the term of imprisonment which he is required to serve. 

One convicted by a general court-martial and sentenced to imprisonment, who, before delivery to prison authorities, is found to be mentally incompetent or otherwise in need of hospitalization, is subject to such directions as in the judgment of the Commandant are proper and necessary in the circumstances of the particular case, in lieu of being delivered to the place of imprisonment designated in the general courtmartial order. 

140. CONVENING AUTHORITY'S REVIEW OF GENERAL COURT-MARTIAL PROCEED- 

INGS.-The convening authority, upon receipt of the trial record, will examine it without delay and either forward it to the Commandant (L) with an appropriate recommendation or return it to the court for revisory proceedings. No provision of the law requires the approval of the convening authority before a general court-martial sentence becomes final, but Section 564(d) of Title 14 makes approval by the Secretary of the Treasury a prerequisite to carrying out the sentence and, when read with Section 566, makes it clear that the Secretary is the sole reviewing authority for a general court-martial (except that the President of the United States is to act upon the proceedings before a commissioned officer can be dismissed).

While not having the direct power to affect the findings and sentence, the convening authority of a general court-martial will nevertheless examine the record of proceedings and place an indorsement thereon before forwarding it. He may make such recommendation in relation to the findings and sentence as may be appropriate, except that no convening authority shall place upon the record any remarks censuring the court with respect to its findings or sentence or the exercise of any judicial responsibility, or disapproving an acquittal or finding of not guilty. An accused is entitled in law to the full benefit of any finding of not guilty. 

If the members of the court perform their duties in. an improper, capricious or arbitrary manner, this may be made the subject of a separate disciplinary proceeding.

The convening authority in his indorsement upon the record should explain briefly any inordinate delay which may have occurred in the disciplinary proceedings. As to his powers in regard to revisory proceedings, see Article 144 below. -47-

141. PROBATION: GENERAL COURT-MARTIAL.-The Secretary alone has the power to remit a general court-martial sentence, in whole or in part, on probation. The general court-martial order made by him upon the case will state the terms of the probation if any. A probationary period will end automatically before its declared termination if the party on probation becomes entitled to his discharge. No person will be detained in the service beyond the expiration of his enlistment solely for the purpose of completing a probationary period. Probation as to a dishonorable discharge, a bad conduct discharge, or a sentence of imprisonment, may not be revoked, nor such punishment carried into effect, until approval by the Secretary of a duly submitted recommendation for revoking probation.

When a person convicted by a general court-martial is placed on probation, any misconduct from the moment he leaves the presence of the court may furnish the grounds for a recommendation to revoke his probation. 

142. LEGALITY OF TRIAL RECORD; HARMLESS ERROR.-Upon review for legality of a court-martial record, any harmless error, defect, irregularity or variance which does not affect substantial rights will not be considered grounds for disapproving the proceedings, findings or sentence. On the other hand, any substantial legal defect in the court-martial is subject to notice by the Secretary, whether objected to at the trial or not, and will result either in setting aside or in such other action as the interests of justice may require. if the court in fact lacked jurisdiction, or if the specification failed to state facts sufficient in law to constitute a punishable offense, or if a constitutional right of the accused was violated, or if any of those things which the principle of due process of law holds to be indispensable to a judicial trial was omitted, the defect is substantial. Thus, 143. DETERMINING SUFFICIENCY OF EVIDENCE.-Every court-martial record will be reviewed on behalf of the Secretary in order to determine whether there is any competent and substantial evidence fairly tending to support findings of guilty. While it is the province of the members of the court to try the facts, and while the Secretary will not substitute his judgment for the judgment of the triers of the facts, it is still necessary, in order to uphold a conviction, that the record contain legal evidence from which the court could properly find or infer guilt. Thus, if proof was lacking as to a single essential element of an offense, then, as a matter of law, a finding of guilty would have to be disapproved. It is not necessary in order to uphold a conviction that the Secretary concur in every fact which must necessarily have been found by the court, but it is necessary that legal evidence in support of each and every essential allegation of the contested specification shall have been adduced. 

The Secretary is not restricted, however, in reviewing the findings of a court-martial to the functions of an appeal court: Congress, having made his approval a prerequisite to the execution of the sentence of a Coast Guard court-martial, thus constituting his action a part of the judgment of the court, such approval will be given or withheld as law and conscience may dictate, in accordance with the responsibility reposed in him.

144. 

REVISORY PROCEEDINGS.-Occasionally it becomes necessary to direct the court to reconvene in order to correct the record or to reconsider its findings and sentence. A court-martial is prohibited, however, from reconsidering an acquittal or a finding of not guilty or from increasing the severity of a legal punishment. The convening authority, the Commandant, and the Secretary of the Treasury are the only authorities who may direct a court-martial to reconvene for revisory proceedings.

The convening authority may properly direct reconsideration and revision where the court has sustained a special plea in bar without having heard proof upon the guilt or innocence of the accused; or where an apparent error or omission in the record requires revisory proceedings; or where the record shows improper action by the court with respect to a finding or sentence which can be rectified without material prejudice to the rights of the accused.

Errors or omissions in the record may sometimes be corrected without requiring revisory proceedings, where a certificate of correction signed by the Judge Advocate and the President of the court-martial would suffice. This might be done, for example, where the record omitted an entry that a certain person was sworn as reporter, when in fact such person had been sworn.

The convening authority may also return a record to the court for revisory proceedings in two instances requiring the court to hear further testimony: (1) where the court accepted a plea of guilty to a charge of desertion and (2) where it appears that the court erred in failing to reject a plea of guilty, as where an accused makes a statement to the court inconsistent with guilt. An order to reconvene in the latter two instances is a "remand" of the case to the same court for trial. 

The Secretary, or the Commandant as representative of the Secretary, may either recommend to the convening authority or directly order such revisory proceedings as the interests of justice may require, or may direct a remand of the case, or may in a single action set aside the proceedings and at the same time direct a new trial. 

145. NEW TRIAL.-The Secretary may direct a new trial after conviction of the accused in a case where a substantial error adverse to the accused had been committed upon the original trial; but not if there was a lack of sufficient evidence in the record to support a conviction, in which event the accused is entitled to a disapproval of the conviction with the same force and effect as though he had been acquitted. 

When a new trial is ordered, it must be before a court composed entirely of different members, although the same Judge Advocate (recorder) may prosecute.

No accused may be retried for any offense of which he was found not guilty upon the first trial nor may the court upon retrial adjudge a sentence more severe than that imposed by the first court. -49-

Instances where a new trial may properly be granted include: where an involuntary confession was received in evidence except for which the record might have been sufficient for conviction; where some other Constitutional right of the accused was violated; where the Judge Advocate overstepped the bounds of fairness; misconduct affecting a member of the court; newly discovered evidence; where the specifications upon which the accused stood trial were insufficient in law to state offenses. A new trial may not be granted as to an accused who is no longer in the service.

Documents relating to occurrences during the procedure are appended immediately following the record of the trial in the order in which they occur. Exhibits are appended after these latter documents in the order in which they were introduced into evidence.

CHAPTER IV CHARGES AND SPECIFICATIONS 

CHARGES.

-A charge is a title under which a specification (or specifications) is alleged. Section 567 of Title 14, U. S. Code, lists by name the offenses over which the jurisdiction of Coast Guard courts shall extend. These names are the names of the charges. Only the general court-martial pleading sets forth the charge name. In a summary court-martial or deck court, the specification appears without the charge name. 201. 

SPECIFICATIONS.-The specification is the essential allegation against an accused which informs him what it is that he is accused of. 

The specification must state facts sufficient to constitute an offense, For our purposes, the word "offense" means that which is recognized in law as punishable by a court-martial. A Coast Guard court-martial is more limited in its power to try offenses than are Army and Navy general courts-martial; therefore, the offense specified must be one which a Coast 

Guard court is empowered to try. As to the general requirements of a specification, see Articles 26, 61 and 62; for trial in joinder, Article 

67; for jurisdiction of civil authorities, Article 354. 202. 

PRINCIPALS.-A Coast Guard person who commits an offense punishable by a Coast Guard court or who aids, abets, counsels, commands or procures its commission, or who causes an act to be done which, if directly performed by him, would be so punishable, is a principal, or offenderin-chief. 

203, ATTEMPTS-An act done with specific intent to commit an offense and amounting to more than mere preparation, and tending but failing to accomplish its commission, is an attempt to commit that offense, and is punishable by a Coast Guard court. Ordinarily, an attempt to commit an offense is to be considered as itself constituting an offense of" conduct to the prejudice of good order and discipline". 

204. LESSER INCLUDED OFFENSES.-An accused may be found guilty of an offense necessarily included in the offen se specified or of an attempt to commit either. He may not be held guilty of committing an offense not alleged against him and not necessarily included in the offense specified against him; to do so would violate his constitutional right to be informed before trial of the nature of the accusation against him. 205, ABSENCE WITHOUT OR OVER LEAVE.-To constitute the charge of either 

"absence without leave" or of "absence over leave", there must be an absence from the service and such absence must have been without authorization. The word "leave" includes liberty. One who receives permission to leave his ship or station for a limited or specific purpose has only a conditional authorization to be away, and if he breaks the condition he may be held to be AWOL from the time he departed; if he is en route under oral or written orders, he is AWOL from the time he abandoned his journey. 52.

Where a man on leave is arrested and detained by the civil authorities and he is tried and acquitted in the civil court, he may not thereafter be held guilty of absence over leave; but anything short of an acquittal by the civil court will not avail him as a defense to the AOL, except where he can establish that he was wholly without fault in being detained. If the accused was AWOL when first detained, he is AWOL for the entire period, irrespective of his guilt or innocence of the civil charge which led to his detention. Similar rules are to be applied to cases of illness preventing the return of the accused.

The duration of the absence goes to the gravity and not to the essence of the offense. A man is AWOL if he exceeds a limited permission, or a conditional permission, or if he lacked the permission of one competent to authorize the leave or liberty which he claims to have. He is AOL if, when his leave or liberty has expired, he is not present in the control of competent authority which authority is apprised of his presence and true status. 

Where an accused was absent from his ship or station without authority, but not absent from the service, the offense constitutes conduct to the prejudice of good order and discipline. Absence offenses should not be made the basis of joinder specifications, even though committed by two men at the same time, but each offender should be charged and tried separately. For court-martial purposes, a period of unauthorized absence is considered terminated when the offender comes into custody of any U. S. Military authority.

Conduct to the prejudice of good order and discipline An assault is defined as an attempt or offer with unlawful force or violence to do bodily harm to another. It is the apprehension of hurt, not the real design of the offender, that constitutes the assault. An attempt to strike is an assault.

Under the first four charges listed above, attacks on persons of the Coast Guard only may be alleged. If a person in another service, or a civilian, is the victim of an assault or of a striking, the offense constitutes the fifth charge, conduct to the prejudice of good order and discipline. Thus, a commissioned officer of the Navy is not the superior officer of a Coast Guard man, but a petty officer of the Coast Guard senior in rating to the accused is his superior officer, for the purposes of the above charges. Cases of aggravated assaults or strikings, such as an assault with a dangerous weapon, or an assault with intent to commit a felony, are covered by the foregoing charges. That the victim was assaulted or struck while in the execution of duties is a matter to be alleged in aggravation. Charges 1 or 2 may be found as lesser included offenses under Charges 3 or 4.

In assaults and strikings no wrongful intent other than that inferable from the act itself is required. Accordingly, evidence of the drunkenness of the accused is not relevant in a simple assault or striking case, although such evidence may be relevant where a constituent element of the -56-

offense is that the victim was a superior officer, since it may then show that the accused could not have known that the person assaulted was his superior officer: Evidence of drunkenness is also relevant to show that the accused could not have entertained the specific intent alleged where an assault with an intent to commit a particular felony is charged.

That a striking was justified is a matter of defense, but when the issue is raised by the accused, the prosecution has the burden of proof on the whole case that the striking was without justifiable cause. If the court finds that an assault or battery (striking) was with justifiable cause the accused is entitled to acquittal. The following rules relating to provocation, self-defense and justifiable cause apply: (1) No words of reproach, no matter how grievous, and no epithets, no matter how coprobrious, will in and of themselves justify an assault. Such words may constitute an offense against military discipline; but words do not of themselves constitute either an assault or provocation sufficient to justify an assault. (2) An offer or attempt with force or violence to injure another, or the apprehension of such injury, may be resisted under the right of self-defense. The extent of resistance that may be used in selfdefense differs with the circumstances of the particular case; it is governed by the violence and nature of the attack. If the resistance to an aggressor exceeds what appears to be proper self-defense, so as to become vindictive, then the court should find such excessive resistance to be without justifiable cause and punishable. (3) One may not use any means of self-defense likely to cause injury in excess of that reasonably believed to be necessary for one's own protection. to believe (4) The court must decide what it was reasonable for the man Ordinarily, a person is not justified in using a dangerous weapon in self-defense where the attacking party is not armed, but does injury by means of his fists, or in some other manner not essentially dangerous to life or limb. (An exception might be found, for example, in a case where a physically helpless person is being beaten by one who is overpoweringly his superior). (f) The right of self-defense exists only so long as the danger exists; when the attack is over and done with, there can be no further need of defense If the victim then pursues his assailant, after the latter has given up the attack, and inflicts injury on him, he does so without legal justification There is an exception to this rule where one who is severely beaten strikes his assailant immediately, in the heat of passion, upon escaping from the attack.

CONDUCT TO THE PREJUDICE OF GOOD ORDER AND DISCIPLINE.- 

Conduct to the prejudice of good order and discipline covers a group of offenses not otherwise specifically provided for or chargeable under one of the other general charges. It includes violations of customs and usages of the service, violations of published local ship and station orders, attempted offenses, breaches of local laws in a country, state, 60

or territory, and such other acts, disorders and neglects as have been or may be pronounced culpable and subject to punitive measures. 

The possible punishable acts comprehended by this charge cannot be exhaustively listed in advance, but, as recognized by the U. S. Supreme 

Court in Carter v. McCloughry (1901), it is "peculiarly for the courtmartial to determine whether the crime charged was to the prejudice of good order and discipline."

The acts charged must be prejudicial to good order and discipline in a real sense and not in a remote or indirect sense, Not every irregular, mischievous or improper act which interferes with good order, will be held to constitute this court-martial offense. It must be the kind of an act which deserves to be pronounced a court-martial offense. 

It must be recognizable as such an offense. The decisions contained in 

Naval Court-Martial Orders are persuasive, but not binding, precedents for the Coast Guard as to what conduct constitutes the offense. An informative compilation of these cases is contained in Law Bulletin 92. 

Instances of offenses under this charge are: disobeying an order by one not the superior officer; failing to comply with standing orders; negligence in carrying out orders; neglect of orders; unauthorized absence from station without being absent from the service; improper uniform; dishonorable neglect to pay debts, etc.

Disobeying the orders not of a superior officer involves the same elements of direct defiance as in the offense of disobeying the lawful order of a superior officer; it covers orders given by personnel of other services, by non-superiors in the Coast Guard or by an authorized civil officer of the Government. It differs from the offense of negligence in carrying out orders, which latter is characterized by carelessness or forgetfulness rather than by intention or wilfulness.

CONDUCT UNBECOMING AN OFFICER AND A GENTLEMAN.- 

Conduct in violation of this charge is action or behavior in an official capacity which, in dishonoring or disgracing the individual as an officer, seriously compromises his character and his standing as a gentleman; or action or behavior in an unofficial or private capacity which, in dishonoring or disgracing the individual personally, seriously compromises his position as an officer and exhibits him as morally unworthy to remain an officer. 

There are certain moral attributes common to the officer and the gentleman, lack of which is indicated by acts of dishonesty or unfair dealing, of indecency or indecorum, or of lawlessness, injustice, or cruelty. Not every one can be expected to meet ideal standards, but there is a limit of tolerance below which the individual's standards cannot fall without his being morally unfit to be an officer or to be considered a gentleman. 3. charge contemplates such conduct by an officer or cadet which, taking all the circumstances into consideration, satisfactorily shows such moral unfitness.

This charge includes acts made punishable by any other charge, provided such acts amount to conduct unbecoming an officer and a gentleman; thus, as to an officer who commits theft. 

Instances of violations of this charge are: knowingly making a false official statement; dishonorable neglect to pay a debt; opening and reading the letters of another without authority; passing bad checks; being grossly drunk and conspicuously disorderly in a public place; using insulting or slanderous language to or about another officer; cruel treatment of enlisted persons; acts involving moral turpitude; unjustifiable failure to support his family.

If an officer by his conduct in incurring private indebtedness or by his attitude toward it or his creditor thereafter reflects discredit upon the service, he may be brought to trial for his misconduct. He should not be brought to trial unless the facts and law are undisputed, and unless there appears to be no legal or equitable counterclaim. The service will not discipline officers for failure to pay disputed private indebtedness or claims where the dispute is genuine.

CONSPIRACY.- 

Any person who conspires with another to commit an offense punishable by a Coast Guard court is also triable for the separate offense of conspiracy. A corrupt agreement must be proved. If the object of the agreement is to defraud the United States by obtaining or assisting other persons to obtain the payment of a false or fraudulent claim, or in any other manner to defraud the United States, or to steal, embezzle or misappropriate property of the United States, then the mere entry into such agreement constitutes the offense of conspiracy, and it is not necessary to prove the commission of any overt act toward carrying out the object of the conspiracy. 215. CULPABLE INEFFICIENCY IN THE PERFORMANCE OF DUTY.- 

Culpable inefficiency means a failure to exercise the judgment or the care and caution demanded by the service of an officer or enlisted person of ordinary prudence under like circumstances. Culpable inefficiency is synonymous with negligence. A slight error of judgment, such as may befall an ordinary prudent man, is not punishable; but all the circumstances of the case, including what the accused should reasonably have foreseen, must be considered; and it is for the court to say, considering the facts and circumstances of the particular case before it, what efficiency and care was reasonably demanded of a person in the situation of the accused, and at what point inefficiency or the want of due care became culpable. 

The charge is much more serious than Neglect of Duty, which is ordinarily restricted to failures to perform routine duties or to neglects which do not have grave consequences. The charge may be based on omissions as well as commissions. If the alleged culpable inefficiency was without serious consequence or result, then the lesser charge of Neglect of Duty should be preferred.

DESERTION.- 

The essence of the military court-martial offense of desertion is unauthorized absence of the accused from the service plus an intention permanently to abandon the service. In order to convict of desertion, the court must find that the accused was actuated by such intention either in commencing or in continuing his absence. 

This special intent is the characteristic feature of the offense which distinguishes it from ordinary AWOL or AOL. The question of the existence of this intent is one for the court-martial to determine from all the facts and circumstances in evidence. A man's intent is not a concrete thing which can be viewed in the court room; it must be inferred. The court may probe into everything which may show the accused's state of mind in order to decide the question of intent, and anything which may have actuated him in commencing or continuing his absence is relevant.

The offense is triable by general court-martial only. A plea of not guilty must be entered and the accused should in all cases be represented by counsel. Counsel for the accused should never forego the opportunity to make an argument in behalf of the accused, whether he has presented any affirmative defense or not, since there is no case in which the question of the existence of the intent is not arguable. 

A record entry made in accordance with Personnel Instructions declaring an absentee to be a "deserter", is in no sense evidence that the accused had the particular intention to desert, but constitutes only an administrative determination that the accused has been absent of record for a period of days necessitating a certain administrative entry, often called the mark of desertion, in his record. It is prejudicial and illogical for a judge advocate to argue to a court that it should infer that an accused had the intent to desert on the basis of his having been administratively declared a "deserter."

The intention to desert, or never to return to the service, may be inferred by the court solely on the basis of an unauthorized absence of long duration established by legal evidence, if the court is satisfied that the accused had formed in his mind a settled intention to abandon the service permanently. In any particular desertion case where the court makes the inference that the accused intended to desert, such finding is subject to the concurrence of the Commandant and of the Secretary. As a matter of law in the Coast Guard an unauthorized absence of five days or less is of insufficient duration to constitute desertion, except in cases of overwhelming cogency. -69-

AQUIME TAM VAR 

A man convicted of desertion while a state of war subsists may lose his nationality under Title 8, U.S. Code, 801(g); a state of war still existed for the purposes of this statute as late as October 1949. A person discharged from the service as a convicted deserter is likely to be ever after hindered in his efforts to earn a livelihood and redeem himself. Accordingly, before it finds a man guilty of desertion, the court should ask itself whether the prosecution has presented proof cogent enough to justify it in inferring that the accused intended to abandon the service permanently, rather than find him guilty of a lesser included offense. DISOBEYING THE LAWFUL ORDER OF A SUPERIOR OFFICER.- 

This offense is restricted to cases of a wilful disobedience to a direct order given by a superior officer of the Coast Guard, the disobedience being manifested either verbally or by conduct indicating immediate defiance or a deliberate refusal. FALSEHOOD.- 

The elements of the offense of falsehood are: (1) a false representation as to a matter of fact, (2) which the accused knew or should have known to be false or which was made in disregard of the true facts, or without ascertaining the truth, (3) made with intent to deceive and (4) having reasonable tendency to deceive. The falsehood may be oral or in writing, but it must amount to more than an expression of mere opinion. The charge may not be preferred against a person for denying his guilt as to some other offense; a man accused of an offense must not be placed in a position where he either has to confess his guilt, or by denying it, be made to stand trial for both the originally charged offense and falsely denying it.

Under this charge also a person may be prosecuted for making, signing, or certifying to be true a false official statement or document provided that the constituent elements of falsehood are present.

MANSLAUGHTER.- 

Manslaughter is of two kinds, voluntary and involuntary. One who with an intent to kill or inflict great bodily harm, unlawfully kills a human being in the heat of sudden passion caused by adequate provocation, is guilty of voluntary manslaughter. 

One who, without intent to kill or inflict great bodily harm, unlawfully kills a human being, either by culpable negligence or while -83-

engaged in a criminal act not amounting to a felony, is guilty of involuntary manslaughter. (A killing while engaged in committing a felony may be murder).

The offense of manslaughter is triable by general court-martial only. 

No Coast Guard court should try a man for other than a negligence killing, except in a case where a Federal district attorney or a State prosecuting attorney having jurisdiction has first been consulted and has refused to prosecute. The existence of a provocation so great as reasonably to excite passion in an ordinary man and cause him to act rashly and without reflection reduces a killing from murder to manslaughter. 

The most usual cases of involuntary manslaughter arise from the negligent operation of vehicles or the negligent handling of firearms. An essential element of manslaughter in such cases is that the death be the proximate result of the negligence of the accused. What is culpable in such cases depends on the facts and circumstances of the particular case; and it is for the court to say, as the triers of the facts, whether the death resulted from a blamable lack of the prudence and care which an ordinary reasonable man in the same situation would have exercised. The culpable negligence charged must be alleged with sufficient particularity to inform the accused of what acts or omissions the negligence consists. See further the discussion of negligence in connection with the charge of negligence in handling firearms or vehicles in Article 239, infra.

NEGLECT OF DUTY.- 

Neglect of duty means an omission or failure to do or perform some work or act which the accused had a duty to do or perform. 

The duty may be imposed by a law, regulation, order, or custom of the service, in force at the time of the offense. 

If the duty was one imposed irrespective of special circumstances and having a general application throughout the service, then the accused may be held chargeable with knowledge of the existence of the duty, and it will be no defense for him to say that he did not know that such a duty existed.

A duty may be breached in whole or in part. The offense ordinarily consists of nonperformance of a subordinate or routine duty, such as failing to indent a time detector dial, failing to muster, failing to instruct his relief, failing to stand a watch. The term neglect does not have to imply carelessness or imprudence, but it is sufficient if an omission to do or perform an act or duty is involved. Unlike negligence, it may even be wilful; that is, it is possible for a person intentionally and deliberately to omit to do an act or perform a duty. Where wilfulness is alleged, however, actual knowledge of the duty must be proved. The offense of neglect of duty is punishable irrespective of whether the breach of duty resulted in some damage or injury. -87-

NEGLIGENCE IN HANDLING FIREARMS OR VEHICLES.- 

Charges: 1. Endangering the lives of others through carelessness 

2. Negligent handling of firearms 

3. Negligent operation of a vehicle

Where, as a proximate result of the careless handling of a firearm or of the negligent operation of a vehicle, a person is killed, the offense should be charged as Manslaughter. The difference between the charge of "endangering the lives of others through carelessness" and the other charges listed above, is that in the former charge there must be an allegation and proof that the life of one or more other persons was endangered. Negligence in handling a firearm or in operating a vehicle constitutes an offense whether someone else was actually endangered or not, the gist of these offenses being blameworthy negligence in handling a potentially dangerous instrumentality. An unintentional injury to the accused himself or an injury to another person as a result of negligence in handling a firearm or a vehicle are properly chargeable under one of the foregoing charges. It is not an essential element of the offense, however, that a personal injury Occur. 

Negligence, as distinguished from intent or wilfulness, is a constituent element of these offenses. The accused is punishable not for an intentional wrong, but for a wrong constituted by carelessness and want of ordinary prudence. Punitive measures cannot be predicated upon pure -89-

accident without fault, however. Carelessness, to be deserving of punishment, must be something more than a mere error of judgment: it must be a failure to act as an ordinary prudent man was reasonably required to act in the particular circumstances. Once the convening authority has found that reasonable cause exists to refer the case to a court for trial, a finding of whether or not negligence deserving of punishment exists rests with the court. 

The mere act of handling a firearm imposes a duty to exercise due caution and circumspection even upon a man who has not been indoctrinated in the use of firearms, or who is carrying the firearm not of his own volition but as a matter of duty, because the man is nevertheless chargeable with knowledge of its dangerous potentialities. The finding of negligence in any given case will depend upon the facts and circumstances of the particular case; it is for the court to decide what measure of care was required and at what point the failure to exercise due care became culpable. 

In alleging negligence in the operation of a vehicle, it is necessary that the specification set forth with reasonable particularity the acts or omissions relied on to show the negligence of the accused. 

It is not sufficient for the specification to allege negligence generally. For example, the operator of a Coast Guard truck involved in an accident caused by a latent defect in the truck and wholly without fault on his part commits no offense; a specification could not properly allege negligence on the part of the driver in such a case. 

If the pre-trial investigation of an accident fails to show facts upon which a conclusion of negligence might be predicated, it is apparent that it will not be possible to draw a legally sufficient specification, and further that there would be a failure of proof upon a trial,

246. THEFT.- 

en Theft, or stealing, is the unlawful appropriation of personal property which the thief knows to belong either generally or specially to another, with intent to deprive the owner permanently of his property therein. Unlawful appropriation may be by trespass or by conversion. 

Once a theft is committed, a return of the property or payment for it is no defense. Personal property only is the subject of theft. Property includes not only things possessing value in and of themselves, but also documents and writings which may represent value, including commercial paper, incompleted checks and honorable discharge certificates. The appropriation by the thief must be without the consent of the owner and must be complete. As a general rule, however, any movement of the property or any exercise of dominion over it with the requisite intent is sufficient. The appropriation need not be by the thief with his own hand. For example, if a person with intent to steal, procures a railroad company to deliver to him the trunk of another by changing the check on it, or has the funds of another transferred to his own bank account, he is guilty of theft.

If general ownership is in one person and special ownership in another, such as a borrower or lessee, it is optional to charge the ownership as in the general owner or in the special owner. The general owner is the person who has title to the property, whereas the special owner, such as a borrower or lessee, does not have the title but does have legal possession.

When theft of several articles is committed at substantially the same time and place, it is a single larceny, even though the articles belong to different persons. Thus, if a thief goes into a room and takes property belonging to various persons, there is but one larceny, which should be alleged in but one specification. 

There must be an intent to deprive the owner permanently of his property. It may exist at or after the time of the appropriation. 

The existence of the intent must in most cases be inferred from the circumstances. If a person secretly takes property, hides it, and denies that he knows anything about it, the intent to steal may well be inferred; but if he takes it openly, and returns it, this would tend to negative an intent to steal. Proof of a subsequent sale of property is strong evidence of an intent to steal. A person may be guilty of larceny even though he intends to return the property ultimately, if that intent depends upon a future condition or contingency which may never happen. One who pawns the property of another without authority, intending to redeem it at a future date and return it to the owner, may be held guilty of larceny. Lost or mislaid property is the subject of larceny; and a finder who, when reasonable means to ascertain the owner exists, fails to pursue such means, may be guilty of theft. 

Theft is a serious offense. 

It should not be tried by deck court. -98-

The conditions of service in the Coast Guard make intolerable the retention of a thief, and the best interests of the service otherwise demand that, whatever the mitigating circumstances, a thief should be separated from the service. This is not to say, however, that in all cases where a man is convicted upon a theft specification, he must be discharged from the service. Where a court-martial convicts a man of theft, if the members or any of them believe that he is not a thief at heart, they should so state in the recommendation for clemency; in a summary court-martial, the convening authority may state such a belief as grounds for remitting outright or on probation, a sentence of bad conduct discharge. So-called petty pilfering should not be made the subject of a theft specification against a man, Thus, where an enlisted man stole a pie from the galley or, on a single occasion, abstracted a sweatshirt from the laundry and wore it openly aboard ship, a warning or mast punishment would ordinarily suffice. If a local ship or station order is promulgated forbidding the use or possession of personal property of others, it will often be more appropriate to try the offender by deck court for a violation of such an order rather than for theft; this is a matter resting within the sound and judicious discretion of the commanding officer. In cases involving the taking of automobiles (joyriding) the specification should be drawn as shown under the charge of Unauthorized Use of a Vehicle, unless the circumstances of the offense indicate that the accused had the intention to steal, Where Government supplies or equipment are used on board ship or station without authority and for private purposes, the charge should ordinarily not be theft, but Applying to his own use etc., (Article 237, supra). Examples of the operation of presumptions in proving theft are given in Article 302, infra. Lesser included offenses are: Scandalous Conduct (attempted theft), Misappropriating or Applying to His Own Use Property of the United States, and violation of a regulation or local order forbidding possession of property of another

CHAPTER V RULES OF EVIDENCE 

GENERAL RULES-Evidence, for our purposes, is defined as that means sanctioned by law by which alone a court-martial decides a contested issue of guilt. Evidence is proof admitted at a trial. The rules of evidence are designed to exclude from the consideration of the court untrustworthy offers of proof. In determining a contested issue, a court may consider nothing as proof unless it has the quality of evidence under the law. Proof must be (1) competent and (2) relevant. To be relevant the proof offered must have a reasonable probative value. To be competent, the proof offered must not fall within any of the exclusionary rules of evidence,

All facts having probative value are "competent", unless there is some rule of evidence excluding their reception For example, the socalled Hearsay Rule, discussed hereafter, is an exclusionary rule of evidence, which itself has important exceptions: all documentary evidence is hearsay, but not necessarily incompetent.

The rules stated in this chapter are applicable in Coast Guard courts, So far as not otherwise prescribed herein or in the courtmartial decisions on behalf of the Secretary of the Treasury, the rules of evidence recognized in the district courts of the United States are applicable in Coast Guard trials. 

302. PRESUMPTIONS-Presumptions or inferences may be considered as falling into two classes: first, those which arise without the introduction of any evidence and second, those which cannot arise until after some evidence has been introduced. In the first class are those presumptions which relate to facts the existence of which courts are bound to presume in the absence of proof to the contrary. An accused is presumed to be innocent until his guilt is proved beyond a reasonable doubt; an accused is presumed to have been sane at the time of the offense charged until a reasonable doubt of his sanity at the time appears from the evidence- In the second class are those presumtpions which relate to facts that a court may infer, if it deems the inference warranted by all the circumstances from the existence of other facts which must be first established. For example:

A sane person is presumed to have intended the natural and probable consequences of acts which he is shown to have committed. 

Persons shown to be acting as public officers are presumed to be legally in office and to perform their duties properly. 

Proof that a letter correctly addressed and properly stamped or franked was deposited in the mail raises a presumption of delivery -103-

to the addressee, and a similar presumption arises with regard to telegrams regularly filed with a telegraph company for transmission. Proof that a person was in possession of recently stolen property may raise a presumption that such person stole it, and, if it is shown that the property was stolen from a certain place, at a certain time and under certain circumstances, that the person stole it at the particular place and time and under the circumstances shown. 

It may be presumed that one who has assumed the custodianship of the property of another has stolen such property if he does not or cannot account for or deliver it at the time an accounting or delivery is required of him.

The weight to be given presumptions of the second class necessarily depends upon all the circumstances attending the proved facts which give rise to the presumptions. For this reason the making and weighing of such presumptions and the consideration of evidence tending to overcome them call for the application by members of courts of their common sense and general knowledge of human nature and the ordinary affairs of life. 303 

DIRECT AND CIRCUMSTANTIAL EVIDENCE-If a statement made by a witness or contained in a document is such that if true it would directly prove or disprove a fact in issue, the statement is called direct evidence. If the statement would, if true, tend to establish a fact from which the fact in issue can be inferred, the statement is circumstantial evidence. Thus, on a charge of stealing a purse, testimony of a witness that he saw the accused take the purse from the coat of the owner, is direct evidence, and testimony of a witness that he found the purse hidden in the locker of the accused is circumstantial evidence of the taking « Circumstantial evidence is admissible even when there is direct evidence, There is no general rule for contrasting the values of circumstantial and direct evidence. It is frequently unassailable. 

As a classic example, what better evidence would Robinson Crusoe need that he was not alone on the island, than the footprint in the sand? is Witness testimony.-A witness, in the primary sense of the word, a person who has knowledge of an event acquired by seeing it. Such a person is qualified to tell the court under oath that which he observed through his own senses.

Opinion evidence; Expert witnesses.-It is a general rule that a witness must state facts and not his opinion or conclusions. However, a witness may express an opinion on matters within the common observation and experience of men, such as to the speed of an automobile, or as to whether a certain person was drunk or not, or as to whether a voice heard was that of a man, woman or child. 104

An expert witness, that is one who is skilled in some art, trade, profession or science, may express an opinion on a state of facts which is within his specialty. Prior to expressing his opinion, his qualification as an expert must be brought out. 

Expert testimony may be adduced in several ways An expert witness may be asked to state an opinion based on his personal observation, examination or study, without first specifying hypothetically in the question the data on which the opinion is to be based- He may be required on direct or cross-examina ti on to specify the data upon which his opinion is based. An expert witness may also be asked to express his opinion upon a hypothetical question (a question supposing a certain state of facts to exist) if the question is based on facts already adduced in evidence at the time the question is asked, or, if the court in its discretion so permits, on facts which are later to be received in evidence. If evidence of such facts is not later adduced, the opinion based on those facts should be disregarded. 

Character evidence.-The basic rule is that the doing of an act may not be evidenced by showing the bad moral character of the accused or his former misdeeds as a basis for an inference of guilt. Exceptions to this rule include the following:

In order to show the probability of his innocence, the accused may introduce evidence of his own good character, including evidence of his military record, and of his general reputation as a moral well-conducted person and law-abiding citizen, However, if the accused desires to introduce evidence as to some specific trait of character, such evidence must have a reasonable tendency to show that it was unlikely that he committed the particular offense charged. For example, evidence of reputation for peacefulness would be admissible in a prosecution for creating a disorder; but it would not be admissible in a prosecution for embezzlement. Once the accused introduces evidence of his own good character, the prosecution may introduce evidence in rebuttal. 

The rebutting evidence is limited by the nature of the character evidence introduced by the accused, Thus, in a theft case, if the only character evidence introduced by the accused is evidence as to his reputation for honesty, the prosecution is limited to offering proof of his reputation for dishonesty, and may not introduce evidence as to his general morality, Once the accused takes the stand as a witness, his reputation for truth and veracity may be attacked as in the case of any other witness: 

304. THE HEARSAY RULE.-The hearsay rule is an exclusionary rule of evidence declaring certain matter incompetent as evidence; the rule has exceptions of the utmost importance. In short, the hearsay rule is that a court of law cannot accept and consider as tending to prove a fact something that someone not in court vouches for. In other words, a fact cannot be proved by showing that someone not in court stated it to be a fact. All documents are hearsay, but documents are often admissible in evidence under one of the important exceptions to 

105

the hearsay rule. Underlying the hearsay rule is the principle that the testimony of witnesses, to be of value, must be taken in court so that witnesses may be sworn, cross-examined, confronted by the accused, and observed by the court. Where an exception to the hearsay rule exists, it is because the experience of mankind recognized the circumstances of the particular exception as providing an adequate substitute for the conditions that make witness testimony acceptable. 

Illustrations.-Ensign A conducted the pre-mast investigation of the accused. The testimony of Ensign A at the trial as to what 

Seaman X told him that he saw the accused do is inadmissible because such testimony from Ensign A is hearsay. Ensign A could, however, testify as to an admission made to him by the accused himself, since an admission of an accused is recognized to be trustworthy evidence and constitutes an exception to the hearsay rule. 

A seaman is being tried for stealing clothes from a shipmate's locker. X is able to testify that Y told him that he, Y, saw the accused have the ship with a bundle of clothes about the time of the theft. Such testimony from X is hearsay and inadmissible. Y himself must be called as a witness. 

A seaman is being tried for disobeying a lawful order given him orally by Commander C. X is able to testify that he heard Commander 

C give the order to the accused. Such testimony is not hearsay; X heard the order and therefore can testify of his own knowledge on the fact in issue he is not testifying as to a fact learned secondhand. 

305. EXCEPTIONS TO THE HEARSAY RULE.-One of the most important exceptions to the hearsay rule for Coast Guard purposes, is the socalled "due course of business" exception, under which entries in service records, log books, Form 2599s, enlistment contracts, travel orders, etc., become acceptable in evidence. This exception to the hearsay rule is now expressed in a Federal Statute, 28 U.S. Code 1732, and is applicable in Coast Guard courts. The statute reads: 

"Any writing or record, whether in the form of an entry in a book or otherwise, made as a memorandum or record of any act, transaction, occurrence, or event, shall be admissible as evidence of said act, transaction, occurrence, or event, if it shall appear that it was made in the regular course of any business and that it was the regular course of such business to make such memorandum or record at the time of such a ct, transaction, occurrence, or event or within a reasonable time thereafter. All other circumstances of the making of such writing or record, including lack of personal knowledge by the entrant or maker, may be shown to affect its weight, but they shall not affect its admissibility. The 

106

term 'business' shall include business, profession. occupation, and calling of every kind. 

This statutory rule is controlling on all questions involving writings. 

In testing whether a writing is admissible in evidence to prove a fact recorded therein, the most significant factors are those stated in the statute itself, i.e.: (1) Was the writing made as a memorandum or record of an act or event? (2) Was it made in the regular course of Coast Guard business? (3) Is it the regular course of Coast Guard business to make such record at or within a reasonable time after the event occurs? 

If the answer is "yes" to all three questions, then the writing is admissible even though the maker of the entry may not have had personal knowledge of the fact recorded. Applying this rule we can see why most letters or dispatches are incompetent. They are incidental communications and not records required to be made regularly. In a case where an accused was charged with breaking arrest, the prosecutor attempted to prove that the accused had been under arrest, by offering in evidence a letter written to the prosecutor by the accused's commanding officer. 

Such a letter is not competent evidence: It is not a writing made as a record of an act, nor is it the regular course of Coast Guard business to record arrests in such a manner. 

It is the regular course of Coast Guard business to record an AWOL at the command from which the man absented himself: an entry made at another command not having the duty to make it is inadmissible.

Narrative writings such as may be contained in shore patrol and police reports of disturbances or accidents are not considered due course of business fact entries and are inadmissible. The same is true of mast information reports.

Entries in service records, log books, and Form 2599's recording AWOL dates, breaking arrest, transfers, etc., are clearly "due course of business" entries. Such entries must be signed; the office or rank of the entrant ought to appear in order for the court to see that the entry was recorded by a person whose duty it was to do so, Likewise either the date of the entry must appear or there must be other evidence to show that it was made within a reasonable time, Note that the statute itself declares that all other circumstances of the making of the writing or record, including lack of personal knowledge by the entrant, may be shown to affect its weight as evidence, but shall not affect its admissibility. Thus, it is not a 

107 -

condition to the admissibility of a Form 2599 reporting an AWOL, that the entrant did not see the man go AWOL The existence of an official duty to make entries relative to absences cannot be doubted "1 In one case the court admitted in evidence an official memorandum of an investigating officer containing a synopsis of statements and conclusions of persons interviewed by the officer. 

The fact that the officer followed the practice, or that it was his duty, to submit reports of his investigation did not make the memorandum one which could be held "regular course of business," What writings are made in "the regular course of business" depends upon their character as well as the circumstances under which they are made. The writing must constitute a record of an "act, transaction, occurrence, or event." In this case the writing was not such a record. It was a record of a series of hearsay statements of persons interviewed during an investigation. 

Furthermore, the writing must have been made in those circumstances under which business organizations and public agencies rely on its trustworthiness and act on such reliance. An official medical report of examina ti on made in the regular course of business by a Public Health Service or other Service doctor and containing a professional medical opinion and conclusion as to matters within the orbit of medical science and knowledge is admissible in evidence under the "due course of business" rule, and an opinion of this nature has the standing of a statement of fact. 306.

EXCEPTION TO HEARSAY RULE - CONFESSIONS AND ADMISSIONS.- 

Another exception to the hearsay rule with which the court in the trial of a penal offense must be familiar, is the exception which allows a confession or an admiss ion made by an accused out of court to be received in evidence. A confession is an acknowledgment of guilt- 

An admission is something short of a confession. While a confession. of the accused is competent as evidence, it is competent only on condition that the confession is freely and voluntarily made without having been induced by the expectation of any promised benefit or by fear of threatened injury or the exertion of duress. The Constitution stands as a bar against the conviction of any individual in an American court by means of a coerced confession; if such a confession is received in evidence, the conviction will be set aside even though there may be sufficient other evidence in the record to support a finding of guilty. A conviction upon the uncorroborated confession of the accused cannot be approved as legal. A court may not consider the confession of an accused as evidence against him, unless there is other evidence, either direct or circumstantial, that the offense charged has probably been committed. In other words, there must be evidence of an offense apart from the confession. This other evidence need not be sufficient of itself to convince beyond a reasonable doubt that the offense has been committed as charged, or to cover every element of the offense specified, or to connect the accused with the offense. 108 -

The fact that a confession or admission was made to an investigator during an investigation of a charge does not of itself make the confession or admission inadmissible. An accused has the right to testify concerning the involuntary nature of his confession or admission without subjecting himself to cross-examination upon other issues in the case or upon the truth or falsity of the confession or admission. He may so testify upon the offer, and before the receipt, of a purported confession in evidence. 307,

DOCUMENTS; PROVING CONTENTS; AUTHENTICATION.-A writing is the best evidence of its own contents. If it is desired to prove the contents of a paper such as a voucher, a claim against the Government, an enlistment contract, a written confession, or a service record entry, the formal method of doing so is to call a witness who can identify as genuine the document, and then to introduce the original in evidence. However, a carbon copy of a document, as complete as the original in all essential respects, including signatures, if any, or an identical copy made by photograph or other process, is considered to be a duplicate original and, as such, is equally admissible as an original, Moreover, if the document is a Coast Guard document (such as a log or service record entry, a Form 2599, and so forth) a copy certified as a true copy by an officer or civilian official and bearing an indication of the place where the original is kept, is admissible in evidence to the extent that the original would be. 

A copy of an official record of the National Military Establishment, or of any unit thereunder, is admissible to the extent that the original would be, if such copy is duly authenticated by the seal, inked stamp, or other identification mark of the office or unit from which it emanates, or if an attesting certificate is affixed to it. Thus, "A true copy: (sgd) John Doe, CDR., U. S. Navy, Office of the Judge Advocate General" would be sufficient, on its face, to authenticate a document as a copy of an original record in the office of the Judge Advocate General, Navy Department, Washington, D. C.

A copy of an official record of the United States or of any state or political subdivision or court thereof is as acceptable in evidence as the original would be provided only that upon examination by the court it bears good and sufficient indication on its face that it is a true copy of a genuine documentary government record. 

In any case in which the identity of the accused as a member of the military service is in issue, his identity may be established by an official certificate that a duly qualified fingerprint expert has compared the fingerprints submitted as those of the accused with the fingerprints of a person in the military service, described by name and serial number, and that such fingerprints have been found to be those of one and the same person.

A duly authenticated certificate or statement signed by an officer 

109

having the custody of an official record, or by his deputy, that after diligent search no record or entry of a specified tenor is found to exist in the records of his office, is admissible as evidence that the records of his office contain no such record or entry Whenever the genuineness of the handwriting of any person is in question, as when it is desired to introduce in evidence against annoy accused a pay voucher or photostatic copy thereof purportedly signed by him, any admitted or proved handwriting of such person is competent evidence as a basis for comparison by a witness or by the court to prove or disprove genuineness; but before admitting such specimens of handwriting, evidence should be offered respecting a reasonable inference as to the genuineness of the specimens. 

Maps, photographs, sketches, and similar items relating to localities, wounds, persons, and like matters are admissible when properly verified either by the person who took or made them, or by some one personally acquainted with the locality, object, or person thereby represented or pictured, and able to testify from his own personal knowledge or observation that they are correct, or that they represent the actual appearance of the subject matter in question. 

308. DEPOSITIONS.-As an exception to the hearsay rule, the testimony of a person not in court given in answer to written questions (interrogatories and cross-interrogatories) may be received by the court, under certain conditions. This is testimony by deposition- 

The prosecution or the defense may arrange to take a deposition either on consent of the opposite party or by application to the convening authority or by the direction of the court. 

The party at whose instance a deposition is to be taken submits to the opposite party the questions he desires the witness to answer, affording the other party a reasonable opportunity to submit cross-interrogatories. The person whose testimony is desired answers the interrogatories under oath or affirmation before an authorized officer who writes down or has recorded the answers of the witness, and who observes and attests the signing of the deposition by the witness. According to circumstances, the interrogatories, when drawn, are sent to the commanding officer of the station nearest the witness, or to the witness himself, or to some responsible person, with an appropriate request, and a letter containing instructions, if necessary.

Depositions may be taken before and authenticated by a designated commissioned or warrant officer of the Coast Guard, or any military or civil officer, including notaries public, authorized by the laws of the United States or by the laws of the place where the deposition is taken to administer oaths.

A deposition signed by the witness and properly certified by the 110 -

officer taking the deposition, when taken upon reasonable notice to the other party, may be read in evidence in any Coast Guard courtmartial case, so far as otherwise admissible under the rules of evidence, if it appears (1) aletes 

That the witness resides or is beyond the state or territory (or District of Columbia) in which the court is ordered to sit, or beyond the distance of 100 miles from the place of trial; or (2) That the witness by reason of age, sickness, infirmity, imprisonment, military necessity, nonamenability to process, or other reasonable cause, is unable or refuses to appear and testify in person at the trial. 

Answers in a deposition are subject to the same objections when offered at the trial as would be the testimony of a witness on the stand. 

Before trial, the Judge Advocate (recorder) retains custody of the deposition, but must permit the defense to examine it at the earliest opportunity.

Limitation on punishment. -Introduction in evidence of a deposition by the judge advocate shall disable the court from imposing imprisonment for more than one year, and upon the trial of a commissioned or warrant officer, from pronouncing a sentence of dismissal from the service. 309.

FORMER TESTIMONY.-When, at any trial by a court-martial, in cluding a rehearing or new trial, a witness who has testified at a former trial of the same accused in which the issues were the same as in the case on trial, is dead, insane, or too old or infirm to attend the trial, or is beyond the reach of process or more than 100 miles from the place where the trial is had, or cannot be found, his testimony at the former trial may be received by the court if otherwise admissible. The testimony of a witness at a former trial is evidenced by the record of the former trial or by a duly certified copy of so much of that record as contains the desired testimony. 310.

312. COMPETENCY OF WITNESSES.-The general capacity of an adult witness is always presumed. One who asserts a specific ground of incapacity in a witness, must prove it to the court. Any known objection 112 1

to the competency of a witness should be made before he is sworn. 

If his incompetency should later appear, however, his testimony is to be disregarded (stricken out). A child is competent as a witness if the court is satisfied that the child has an understanding of the difference between truth and falsehood and has some moral belief in the obligation to tell the truth. 

The accused is, at his own request, but not otherwise, a competent witness. One of two or more accomplices is competent to testify, whether tried jointly or separately, and whether called for the prosecution or for the defense; except that he cannot, if he is an accused in the same trial, be called except upon his own request; and if not himself an accused in the trial he may assert his privilege against self-incrimination, unless already tried, The fact that an accomplice testifies for the prosecution does not make him afterwards immune from trial except to the extent that immunity may have been promised him by the convening authority or the prosecutor personally. j- 

313, EXAMINATION OF WITNESSES.-All witnesses testify under oath or affirmation. Subject to the discretion of the court, a witness before completing his testimony is not ordinarily permitted to be present in court during the introduction of other evidence or during the opening statements. 

Witnesses are usually examined in the following order: Witnesses for the prosecution, witnesses for the defense, witnesses for the prosecution in rebuttal, witnesses for the defense in rebuttal, witnesses for the court. The order of examining each witness is: direct examination by the party calling the witness, cross-examination by the opposite party, redirect, recross, then examination by the court,

The court may permit the recall of witnesses, including an accused, at any stage; it may permit testimony to be introduced by either party out of its regular order and place; it may permit a case once closed by either or both sides to be reopened for the introduction of testimony previously omitted.

The court should not excuse a witness until satisfied that neither party has any further questions to ask him. 

A witness should be required to limit his answer to the question asked. He cannot, however, be required to answer categorically by a simple "yes" or "no" unless it is clear that such an answer will be a complete response to the question. A witness may always be permitted at some time before completing his testimony to explain any of his testimony. 314. 

CROSS-EXAMINATION.-Cross-examination of a witness is a matter of right, Leading questions may be used freely on cross-examination. 

A greater latitude may properly be allowed in the cross-examination of the accused than that of any other witness: when the accused testifies in denial of an offense. the cross-examination may cover the whole subject of his guilt or innocence of that offense. As to other witnesses, 113

the cross-examinati on should in general be limited to the issue concerning which the witness testified on direct examination and to testify his credibility. On the question of credibility. witnesses may be cross-examined on matters of personal interest, motives, means of obtaining correct knowledge of the facts, and any other matter affecting credibility

When an accused is on trial for a number of offenses and on direct examinati on has testified about only one or some of them, his cross-examination must be confined to matters having a bearing on the offense or offenses about which he has testified and upon his credibility

The court and its members may ask a witness any questions that either side might properly ask the witness, If new matter. not. properly the subject of cross-examination of the witness on his previous testimony, be elicited by questions of the court, both parties will be permitted to cross-examine the witness upon the new matter. Questions by the court are subject to objection on proper grounds by either side, 315. OBJECTIONABLE QUESTIONS - -Leading questions are questions which either suggest the answer it is desired the witness shall make, or which embodying a material fact, are susceptible of being answered by a simple yes or no. They are objectionable because the questioner rather than the witness is giving the substance of the testimony.

There are many points at a trial at which leading questions will be unobjectionable, Thus the witness may be led through that rart of the examination which is purely introductory. Also when from the nature of the case, the mind of the witness cannot be directed to the subject of the inquiry without a particular specification of it, a leading question may be asked for that purpose. The court has wide discretion in allowing leading questions.

Double questions, or those embodying two or more separate elements are objectionable. Thus the question "Did you see the accused leave the ship with a bundle under his arm?" really contains four separate questions.

Also objectionable are questions which assume a fact to which the witness has not previously testified. Thus the question "when you saw the accused was anyone with him?" would be improper if the witness has not previously testified that he had seen the accused. 316 

PROTECTION AGAINST SELF-IN CRIMINATION.-The fifth amendment to the Constitution provides that in a criminal case no person shall be compelled to be a witness against himself. The principle embodied in this provision applies to trials by courts-martial and is not limited to the person on trial, but extends to any person who may be called as 114.

a witress, Although an answer to a question apparently would incriminate or tend to incriminate a witness, he cannot refuse to answer, if, for instance, with respect to the offense as to which the privilege is asserted, he is in a position successfully to plead the statute of limitations or double jeopardy. 

A witness may, of course, waive his privilege of refusing to answer a question which may incriminate him. 

317. THE ANGLO-SAXON RULE.-In a 1949 case (Watts v. Indiana). 

the United States Supreme Court said: "Under our system society carries the burden of proving its charge against the accused not out of his own mouth. It must establish its case, not by interrogation of the accused even under judicial safeguards, but by evidence independently secured through skillful investigation. 'The law will not suffer a prisoner to be made the deluded instrument of his own conviction.' 2 Hawkins, Pleas of the Crown c. 46, Sec. 34 (8th ed., 1824)."

This rule applies to the accused alone. It forbids a prosecutor from calling the accused to the witness stand to establish the case against him.

Of course, an accused can plead guilty to most offenses (desertion is an exception). But where the accused pleads not guilty, he thereupon calls upon the prosecution to establish each and every element of the offense specified against him; the prosecutor may not seek to establish the offense against the accused by the testimony. of the accused himself, Where the prosecutor fails to adduce proof in support of each material element of the offense specified against the accused, the testimony of the accused himself may not be looked to, to supply the material defect in the prosecutor's case. The entire record in a court-martial trial will always be examined to see that the burden of proving his innocence has not been thrust upon the accused nor the spirit of the Constitutional protection against compulsory self-incrimination violated, In a deck court case, after a plea of not guilty, if the name of the accused is listed as a witness for the prosecution, the proceedings will be set aside as a patent violation of the Anglo-Saxon rule. 

318. PRIVILEGED COMMUNICATIONS.-Certain communications are privileged from disclosure in court where public policy demands the protection of the confidential nature of the communication. State secrets are privileged; the deliberations of courts are privileged; confidential communications between husband and wife and between an accused and his counsel are privileged. Private communications by telegram are not privileged. Communications between service medical officer or civilian physician and patient are not privileged, 319. ILLEGAL SEARCH AND SEIZURE.-Evidence obtained as a result of an unlawful search of the property of an accused conducted by persons acting under authority of the United States is not admissible in a trial by court-martial. A search of property owned or controlled by the United States, which search has been authorized by the commanding 115

officer having jurisdiction over the locality where the property is situated or, if the property is situated in a foreign country, over the personnel of the Coast Guard at such locality, is lawful. A private dwelling searched without a warrant, or without the request or invitation or consent of the occupant of the premises, is not lawfully searched. It is likewise unlawful if, without reasonable cause any other building or property is searched without a warrant or without consent. 320. 

IMPEACHMENT OF WITNESSES.-When we speak of the impeachment of a witness, we refer only to the processes allowed in a court of law whereby the opposing lawyer may undertake to attack the credibility of witnesses for the other side. In other words, impeachment signifies the process of attempting to diminish credibility, For example, the veracity of the witness may be impeached, When attack of a witness on this ground is undertaken, the impeaching evidence must be limited to evidence of his general reputation for truth in the community in which he lives or pursues his ordinary business. 

A witness may, after testifying that he knows the reputation of the person in question as to truth and veracity in the community in which he resides or pursues his business and that such reputation is bad be further asked whether or not from his knowledge of such reputation he would believe the person in question under oath, After such impeaching evidence, evidence that the general reputation of the witness for truth and veracity in such community is good may be used in rebuttali For our purposes, "community" includes the entire Coast Guard, Evidence of conviction of any crime, whether by a civil or military court, is admissible for the purpose of impeachment if the crime involves moral turpitude or is such as to affect the worthiness of belief of the witness. The witness himself must first be questioned with reference to the conviction sought to be shown.. in order that he may have an opportunity of denying or admitting and explaining it. If the witness admits the conviction, other proof is unnecessary For the purpose of impeachment it may be shown by cross-examination or otherwise that the witness is presently in custody and that his testimony was affected by fear or favor growing out of his detention: 

Inconsistent statements, at some prior time made by the witness, are allowed to be shown in evidence to impeach the witness. By inconsistent statements we mean statements inconsistent with his present testimony. INTENT-We know that it is the duty of the prosecution to offer proof of each and every essential element of the offense specified against the accused. We also know that in many offenses "intention" is a material element. Intention, of course, exists in the mind and it is not given to the members of the court to look into the mind of an accused. It follows that. intention is provable only by a process of inference. Frequently, unintended acts such as negligence in operating a vehicle or handling a firearm or negligence in performing a duty or obeying orders. or in suffering a vessel to be hazarded, constitute offenses. 

In such cases the quality of negligence substitutes for the quality of intention in other offenses. In a third class of offenses we find that conduct is mede punishable irrespective of the existence of any element of either intention or negligence. (For example where a city ordinance makes driving through a red light punishable.) Traditionally, words in the specification like "willfully" or "knowingly" indicate that intent is an element of the offense. Intent is an essential element in other offenses by virtue of the historical under standing of the crime, even though no express words indicate it. We can classify offenses requiring criminal intent in two major groups: those in which the act is the principal feature, the existence of the wrongful intent being simply inferable from the nature of the acts; and those in which a special kind of intent is looked upon as a separate element of the offense wholly apart from the act itself. In the former class are: assault striking another, breaking arrest AWOL, sleeping on

watch, drunkenness and so forth.

In the latter class are desertion, theft. bribery, burglary, deliberately missing ship, fraud, forgery, extortion, perjury, and many others. In the former class of cases the law will infer the intent from proof of the unlawful act, invoking the presumption that a man intends. the ordinary consequences of his acts; in the latter class of cases, however, the intention is something which the members of the court must infer from all the evidence tending to show that the particular kind of intention existed in the mind of the accused The practical effect of the distinction is seen by comparing proof in an assault case with proof in a desertion case. Thus in an assault, a man may show that he was drunk and testify that he has no recollection of the striking of another; yet if the court is satisfied that he did strike the other and without provocati on. he is guilty. The law by construction supplies the intent, But in desertion the law does not by construction supply the special intention to abandon the service permanently from the physical fact of a long AWOL It is up to the court. as triers of the facts, to make the inference of the existence. of this special intention from the fact of the AWOL and from all the other relevant facts in evidence; but the law will not by construction supply this intent.

As above noted, intent is found only by inference: it is not a concrete physical thing which can be produced in the view of the court. The question of intent must necessarily be one for the court to determine from all the facts and circumstances in evidence. 

Judges cannot look into a man's mind and read its purposes. 

But they may probe into his words, his knowledge his experience, his surroundings, his acts and doings and their results, in order to decide the question of intent.

322. VOLUNTARY DRUNKENNESS AND INTENT Voluntary drunkenness. 

whether caused by liquor or drugs, is not an excuse for crime. It does not refute intention where the law by construction supplies the intention, as in simple assault But where a special kind of intention or of conscious knowledge or of deliberation is necessary to constitute the crime evidence of drunkenness is admissible on the issue of whether or not the accused was capable of entertaining the specific intent. Thus it may be shown in a charge of disrespect to a superior officer that the accused was incapable of knowing that a superior officer was being addressed. Since no special intent other than that which is inferrable from the act itself is required in assault cases, using obscene language, sleeping on watch, leaving station before being relieved AWOL, and so forth, evidence of drunkenness of the accused is not relevant since the state of mind of the accused is not in issue- Evidence of drunkenness of the accused may, however, be admitted as evidence in extenuation 323. 325. JUDICIAL NOTICE.-Courts-martial, like other courts, may take judicial notice of the existence and truth of certain facts. 

When the court takes judicial notice of a fact, it dispenses with the need of producing evidence to prove such fact. The type of facts of which the court may take judicial notice includes facts forming part of the common knowledge of every person of ordinary understanding and intelligence.

Also included within the scope of judicial notice are facts which the court is bound to know as a part of its own special duty and function, such as the United States Constitution, Federal statutes, the statutes of the state within which the court is sitting; Coast Guard Regulations and circulars, Law Bulletins and court-martial decisions of the 

Secretary of the Treasury; the organization of the Coast Guard -119-

and the Register of its officers. Examples of other facts of which the court may take judicial notice are: historical and geographical facts; time. days and dates; the time of sunrise on a given day from the Nautical Almanac; the names of high government officials etc.

361. POWER OF GENERAL COURTS-MARTIAL AND COURTS OF INQUIRY TO SUMMON WITNESSES.-By virtue of Title 14, United States Code, section 

574, Coast Guard general courts-martial and courts of inquiry may subpoena witnesses in the same manner as United States courts of criminal jurisdiction within the State, Territory, or District (of Columbia) in which the general court-martial or court of inquiry is ordered to sit. This subpoena power includes the power to compel witnesses to appear and testify, or to produce documentary evidence, as ordered. It runs throughout the United States and its territories. 

The statute provides that anyone who, having been legally subpoenaed by such a general court-martial or court of inquiry, wilfully neglects or refuses to obey, shall be guilty of a misdemeanor, punishable by a fine of not more than $500 or imprisonment not to exceed six months, or both.

However, such punishment shall not apply to persons residing. beyond the State, Territory, or District (of Columbia) where the general court-martial or court of inquiry is held. In other words, though the subpoena power is general, disobedience is an offense under the law only where the witness resides within the local limits specified. 

Civilian witnesses who are legally subpoenaed and appear in obedience thereto are entitled to be paid witness foes, mileage, and subsistence in the manner set forth in Article 367. 

362. POWER OF SUM ONS DOES NOT EXTEND TO LESSER COURTS.-The subpoena power given by Title 14, United States Code, section 574, extends only to Coast Guard general courts-martial and courts of inquiry. There is no authority to subpoena witnesses to compel their attendance before summary courts-martial or deck courts. The attendance of civilian witnesses before such courts may be requested, but compliance is purely voluntary. There is no authority to pay witness fees, mileage, or subsistence to civilian witnesses in attendance before summary or deck courts. 363.
