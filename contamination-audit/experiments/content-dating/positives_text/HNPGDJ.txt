PREFACE 

The period in which this little tale is laid is many, many years ago. The characters are imaginary. The writer has allowed himself some poetical license in his descriptions. Some of his readers may agree with him that both reality and romance are forever interwoven into the texture of one's life. We see visions and dream dreams in our childhood. Happy are we if we remain faithful to these visions, and when old age comes on, we still dream dreams. "We are such stuff as dreams are made on." 

Thomas C. Thacher 

Yarmouth, Massachusetts, October, 1944.

THE EVERGREEN PINE

The Evergreen Pine 

A merry band of children played on a mossy bank of a gentle hill near a tall pine, which raised its erect head into the sky while the warm sunshine of a July afternoon made fragrant its evergreen branches. From one of the top limbs a robin sang a serenade, it may have been to the children below, or perhaps for very joy because the day was so lovely and the view so glorious. 

The little bird from its lofty perch, looked over a stretch of old pasture carpeted with moss of various shades like beautiful marble or agate, here and there white daisies, yellow indigo blossoms, red fireweed, wild cranberry and blackberry vines, and many a tangle of wild-roses, clethra, sweet-fern and bayberry. Then came small cedars, white birches, a grove of oaks, a swamp of alders, high-bush blueberries, wild grape and honeysuckle vines and the swamppink or wild azalea with its exquisite perfume. 3

Beyond, hidden away in a woody nook, nestled 

Miller's Pond, the home of the shy pink sabbatia, while in another direction sparkled the blue waters of Dennis Pond, with pond lilies sleeping on its bosom and countless gulls diving for perch and pickerel. A gentle breeze from the 

South Sea, five miles away across the Cape, fragrant with the perfume of the oak, pine and swamp-pink, tempered the heat of the hot summer day.

To the north there were green meadows and low hills, then a peaceful village through which ran an elm-arched street, with old gray houses, many the homes of sea captains, and here and there a few church spires. Then came more meadows divided by stone walls and rail fences, and a mile of salt marsh, intersected by creeks which ran into the sea. To the eastward ran the silvery strand of Gray's Beach and the 

Dennis Dunes, while toward the west across

Barnstable Bay, stood up under a canopy of fleecy clouds, the glistening white hills of

Sandy Neck, surrounded by water and extending for miles, always beautiful, and never more

glorious in their mystic splendor than when resplendent under the dying rays of the sun as it sank to sleep in the waters of Cape Cod Bay.

These young children used to love to wander from their nearby homes, across the fields, to pick mayflowers in the woods, or flowers and berries in the fields, and then they would all gather beneath the pine, which they called their tree, and tell stories and play. To-day the prettiest one of all the girls, named Rose, light-haired with deep blue eyes, was making a wreath of wild roses. It was a fitting decoration, for she herself was as beautiful as a rose. 

Susan, with black hair and eyes ("Black-eyed 

Susan," they called her), and two other girls,

Jane and Lucy, were busy helping Rose with her wreath. The oldest boy, John, a ruddycheeked blue-eyed youngster, was building a house of pine cones, fallen from the tree, for his brothers David and Henry, freckled-faced, curly-haired tots. Three other boys, Fred, whose tiny hands grasped firmly a small boat, Herbert and Nat, clinging to a few precious marbles,

were watching some squirrels playing in the pines and listening to the plaintive call of the marsh quail. 

At last the girls finished the wreath and put it on Rose. She so loved roses and had so much childish imagination that sometimes she would kiss the wild rose bush and call it her sister or even whisper her childish secrets to the tree. To simple-minded little children, flowers and trees have more meaning than to wise, prosaic adults. 

Then the children, with Rose leading, joined hands and danced around the tree just as if they had all been fairies. Their merry voices were so gleeful that the little bird on the tree stopped singing, and a little brown rabbit scampered up from an oak grove to see what was going on. Who knows if a deer from the nearby woods may not have done the same? 

The birds and animals all seemed to know that no harm could come to them from these happy children playing beneath the evergreen pine.

Now the children all sat down and told stories about fairies who lived in the woods, and

Rose said, "If only one of these fairies would come out now and dance with us."

They all made wishes of what they would like to be when they grew up. Rose wished that she might have flowers always near her and to remain ever young and beautiful; Susan wished to be very rich so that she could buy everything she wanted; Jane and Lucy to be always as happy as they were then; John longed to be a poet; Herbert a soldier; Nat a merchant; and 

Fred a sailor, so that he could see the world.

Three months passed. Summer had flown. 

Autumn had come. Late October had arrived, bringing all the rich wealth of coloring which at this season, the pageant of the year, nature displays. The children basked in the warm sunshine as they sat near the old pine and admired the delicate silvery and yellow grass waving in the gentle breeze, the rich garnet-colored oaks, ruby red sumachs, yellow and scarlet maples, crimson huckleberry bushes, and the brilliant wild cranberry and blackberry vines.

Summer had lingered late that year before saying good-bye.

Just as the last strains of a beautiful symphony, or the farewell words from a dear friend are the most cherished of all, so it seems that 

Mother Nature before saying good-bye to her children, with a promise to return in the spring, keeps her best treasures until the last.

John had found as he climbed over a stone wall a few sweet roses growing out of a wild rose bush clinging to the sheltered side of the wall. These he gave to Rose, who fastened them to her waist. She and the other girls made garlands of rich red oak and yellow maple leaves, which they tied about themselves; thus festooned, these autumnal fairies danced with the boys about the tree. A soft mystic light filled the air. The fleecy clouds hung low, bathing the earth with a gentle, kindly embrace. The golden rays of the sun rested on the children. 

Across the salt meadows the high fall tide reached to the stone walls and fences. Now and then huge flocks of black ducks were flying southward. 

Years went by. John was a young men, a long time out of college, and now, alas! not a

poet, as his childish fancy had wished, but a merchant. But all these years he had loved poetry and often he had gone to read poetry under the friendly pine, and sometimes he had gone there for peace and comfort when in trial or sorrow. His two brothers, David and Henry, whom he loved dearly, had died in their boyhood. But now a great happiness had come to him. A new day was dawning. A new life was opening before him. He and Rose were to be married. As John sat down on the mossy bank beneath the pine, he thought of his past life, far from perfect, with many wild moments. 

All this was over. He felt that now he would walk hand in hand with Rose, who like a good angel would lead him into a happier and better land. He knelt down beneath the old pine and vowed that he would be true to his sweetheart, and to the best that was in him. 

Two-score years had passed. John, a grayhaired man, stood on a dark December day once more beneath the pine, and listened sadly to the sound of the surf on the outer bar beyond Sandy Neck, and the deep solemn tone

sounded like a requiem for sailors lost at sea, or for some saint departed. Two grievous losses had come to him. Rose, after their many years of happiness together, had died, and his only son Bob, in whom his hopes were centered, had been killed in the Great War. He felt indeed alone. Of his childhood friends, Herbert and

Nat, whom he loved so much, had both died, and Fred had been lost at sea. But he felt thankful that he had two daughters left to comfort him.

John thought of his forty years of happiness with his wife. How she had loved beauty as expressed in music, flowers, art, good books and good people! Her life had been indeed a harmony with no false notes. What a help she had been to him in his career, which had combined business, politics, welfare work and extensive travels! Many a trial and disappointment had come to him, but through it all he had learned that a life of service was the true life. He had tried to see the best that was in his neighbors and in his modest way to help make the world a little better. So many blessings 10

had come to him that out of gratitude he felt he should do all he could for others.

Perhaps the unassuming work he was doing might in some slight way help to benefit someone who should come after him.

He had now in his old age a far better understanding of himself, and appreciation of the world and people than ever before. He had more sympathy with the young and old, and a deeper insight into their hearts. He had always loved the best books of all ages, and he seemed to have absorbed some of the beauty which he had read about and seen, a beauty evidenced not only in the works and lives of people of the past, but in this everyday world and in the people about him. As he reviewed his long life, with all its varied wanderings and experiences, he felt that like Ulysses he was a part of all he had seen. 

And now Rose was gone. He felt all alone, and yet his heart must not break. He must go on. John knelt beneath the old tree and asked for renewed strength to carry on and to help others, as he had tried to do when Rose had 11

been by him to encourage and inspire him. Then a soft breeze touched the branches of the pine, as though they were the chords of an Eolian harp. 

John said, "Surely it must be the murmur of the wind, and not the pine whispering to me."

CONCLUSION 

John, an old man of four-score years, lay on his bed in his last illness. His frame was frail, and his face pale and wan, but when he opened his eyes, one could catch traces of their youthful lustre, and a noble spirit within. A deep sleep had fallen upon him. His two daughters and his nurse watched anxiously by his bedside as he lay apparently unconscious. Suddenly, the nurse saw a happy smile light up his face, and leaning low, she heard him murmur, "I see the tree." "Hush," she said, "he dreams." 12

The Dream 

John was once more back in his childhood days, seated on the mossy bank near the old pine, which seemed to glisten with a soft light in its branches. The same gentle breeze from the South Sea, laden with scent of oak, pine, fern, and swamp-pink, fragrant like sweetsmelling incense, cooled his brow. 

Yes, there were his playmates of long ago.

There were his little brothers David and Henry, with their blond, curly heads and blue eyes, and there too was Rose, more radiant than ever, with open arms to welcome him. There stood by the tree his father and mother, with outstretched arms, and his son Bob, and the same tender glow from the tree seemed to rest like a halo on his brothers, wife, son, father and mother.

Then out of the tree, as in a vision, appeared an old blind man and a crippled woman, both of whom John had helped, a boy saved from drowning, a sailor lost at sea, whose fam- 13

ily he had cared for,-all these and others appeared, all shining with the same mystic light. 

John gazed, lost in wonder. 

Suddenly the tree blazed with light. John saw gleaming candles in its branches and without counting, it flashed upon him that there were eighty in all, just his age. On the top was the same Santa Claus of his childhood, with the

Christ Child, and the tree was all aglow with the same mystic beauty and tender light which he had seen on the faces of his brothers, wife and all others, and now the light from the tree shone upon him, illuminating him. "What does it all mean?" he said. "Who will explain this mystery?"

A deep silence, and then a voice like the voice of many waters, which stirred his very soul. "John, my son, have I been so long with you and yet thou dost not know Me? Verily I say unto thee because thou hast both known Me and hast known My tree, which is The Tree of Life, and hast eaten of its fruit and hast made 14

it a part of thy life, thou shalt never die but live forever. Enter now into thy Father's

Heavenly Home."
