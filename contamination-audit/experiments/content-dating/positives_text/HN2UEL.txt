Preface 

SUNDRY friends suggested, a long time ago, that I write a book. They professed to find in my old shooting log the material necessary to so ambitious a venture, and volunteered their services in editing the same.

The writing of a book, they assured me, was quite an easy matter. All that one required was some brains and a capacity for work. They intimated that it would be their part to supply the needed brains. 

How, I often wonder, should I have done without them! 

They have come around nights, smoked my tobacco, drunk the liquor prescribed for me by an over-worked yet kindly physician, and ravaged my old diary from cover to dusty cover. 

One after another, these flatterers have egged me on till out of the mists of old yesterdays, memories come a'flocking. But there are, I take it, hard and fast rules which govern the writer's art. Alas, I know nothing of them.

I am piecing together the memory of old days; [i]

living them over again, and setting them down in black and white just as they really happened, and I shall wander far, no doubt, from the path of nice composition. Deal kindly with these little yarns and grant me a sportsman's indulgence for all that they may lack. 

ROLAND CLARK

I. 

A Sportsman in the Bud

ICLEARLY remember my first introduction to a shotgun and the fascination it instantly had for me. I think I must have been about twelve years old—certainly no more—when an older brother took me one day to the stables, and, swearing me to secrecy, produced from the darkest corner of the harness room a sawed-off musket. "See what you think of that," he said with manifest pride. "I bought it this afternoon." "You bought it?" I asked in a whisper. "You -you mean it's yours?” "Yes," he answered. "And what's more, I've got a pound of powder and a salt bag full o' shot." 

I gazed in silent admiration on this magnificent weapon. How wonderful it must be to possess so fine a thing!

Then A. L. told me that it was his intention to kill ducks-ducks and rabbits, perhaps a few wild geese and lesser game in profusion. Winter [1I

My interest in the slaughter to come was enormous. On the strength of that interest, no doubt, I was accepted by A. L. as a sort of henchman, with certain prescribed privileges, and thereafter, whenever he went afield, I was sure to be trailing along. 

I know I experienced some disappointment in finding, as time went on, that there had been a grave miscalculation as to the rabbits and ducks we should bag. The rabbits we encountered were few and nimble. They usually escaped unscathed. 

Our one and only method of hunting ducks was to stalk those occasional trustful birds which had approached too close to shore. If a duck were killed, which sometimes happened, the problem of retrieving it was quite a special art. When stones enough were handy, we would hurl a succession of missiles to hit the water beyond our duck, and so, by slow stages, waft the prize within reach.

We were successful in only a minor degree. 

Our ducks, for the most part, had a tantalizing way of resting just outside of gun range while we [2]

STRAY SHOTS 

sat by the hour behind a rock and waited for one to swim in.

Snow and ice came down on us early that winter. Long Island Sound froze solid with only an "air hole" here and there where a tide rip kept the water open. Ducks had pretty well left, though wherever there remained a spot unfrozen a few birds still lingered on.

One such area of open water there was just outside of Davenport's Rocks. I think A. L. and

I were the first to discover it—and it was literally full of ducks!

We glimpsed the spectacle from a distance, then stared at one another in blank dismay. Here were ducks in glorious plenty within a stone's throw of shore. A "sneak" of a few short rods would put us close to them. The rocks would conceal us--but we'd left the gun at home! 

I am sure we covered the two miles back, through heavy snow and a biting wind, in something like twenty minutes. It is my belief that the return trip was shortened by four or five minutes at least. Snow drifts barred our way, but we plowed through them. We slipped and slid, stumbled and fell, but at last we were back at the shore once more. The ducks were still there. If [3]

STRAY SHOTS 

anything, there were even more ducks than we had noticed in the beginning.

We crouched low in a crevice of the shelving rocks. A. L. reached in his pocket for a cap and placed it carefully over the gun nipple. "Never carry a gun on this sort of trip with a cap set," he took time to warn me. "It's mighty dangerous."

The ducks were diving and feeding, all unconscious of the dreadful menace that lurked for them there on the shore. "When most of them go down again, we'll make a race for that biggest rock," my brother whispered, "and for gracious' sake, keep your head down; they're sure to get up if they see us."

The first rock was reached without mishap, and another at the water's edge as once more the ducks went under. When next they came to the surface the musket was ready, levelled over our rugged breastwork. "Canvasbacks!" A. L. breathed hoarsely. "Watch out, you're going to see something happen." 

Then-WANG!-The earth trembled. I distinctly felt the boulder behind which I crouched move from its solid base. A. L. had somersaulted [4]

STRAY SHOTS 

backwards into a bank of snow. As I turned, he was gripping his shoulder and cursing profoundly for a boy of his tender years. And the gun-our gun!-the pride of my eye! the joy of our idle hours! Where there had been a sawedoff musket just a few short minutes ago there now remained but one-third of a musket-a stock alone with eight or ten inches of twisted barrel. The balance had disappeared. 

A. L. had got to his feet and was dismally wiping blood from his nose. "Something must have been in the barrel. It might have been snow." He pointed to a long line of ducks stretching away in the sunset glow. "Look at 'em," he groaned; “and canvasbacks. Yes, I guess you got snow in the barrel."

Now I am morally certain that those ducks were not canvasbacks. The enthusiasm of youth was quite apt in those days to blind us in the matter of genus and species.

The ubiquitous old squaw was, in point of fact, our special quarry, and while the Disaster of 

Davenport's Rocks put a check on our sporting activities, it was only temporary. The musket, to be sure, was gone for all time, but the urge remained. A gun we must have-two guns, for my [5]

STRAY SHOTS 

education had advanced rapidly and I yearned for a gun gun of my own. 

How I eventually came to get one, I cannot recall. There is a hazy recollection of making the deal with a neighboring fisherman whereby I acquired a time-worn relic and suffered financial embarrassment for many months thereafter. 

A. L. also had secured, by some stroke of genius, a superior specimen of the mail-order type, with plenty of glitter and flash. In the course of time we added to our equipment a bunch of nondescript "stool," and awful they were, caricatures of a living duck, but needless to say they filled the bill for us. Before winter had far advanced we were properly organized, and with the breaking up of the ice our duck-shooting dreams began to be realized. Where earlier in the game we had depended on the ducks coming to us, we now fared boldly forth to the feeding grounds of the ducks. mere 

Looking back over forty years of duck shooting I realize that my happiest memories are of those early days when an old squaw sold for fifteen cents and a "coot" brought twenty-five. I don't recall that there was at the time any open market for old squaws and coots, but I soon estab- [6]

STRAY SHOTS 

lished a family trade on somewhat unusual lines. 

I shot the ducks, sold them to an indulgent parent, and the money procured in the deal was allowed for the purchase of more ammunition.

Literally and figuratively, I cut my duckshooting teeth on old squaws and coots, for it was provided in the bargain that if I received money for my ducks I must eat them myself, which no doubt was fair enough. 

Duck shooting as practised by me at this period was an exceptionally hazardous pursuit. 

Never, I venture to say, was there a more decrepit or unreliable weapon than the rusty wreck

I termed my fowling piece. I know now that I took my life in my hands whenever I picked it up. Temperamental to a degree was that ancient arm. Sometimes as mild as a summer's mornagain resembling nothing so much as a cantankerous army mule. To this day its destructive wallop is indelibly fixed in my mind. 

In my minor capacity of helpmate to A. L. opportunity was not given me at this time to distinguish myself to any marked degree. My job, or one of them, was to chase down and finish the cripples that had fallen to A. L.'s gun. 

If you've ever chased a wing-tipped old squaw . [7]

STRAY SHOTS 

in a choppy sea, when the mercury stood at, say ten degrees, and your old skiff leaked and your gun misfired from time to time, and your fingers stuck to the frosty barrels-well, you've tackled a troublesome job. One humiliating experience in this connection stands out clearly in my memory. It had to do with a large and lusty coot that A. L. had dropped in the stool. 

Our coot, as is the nature of all healthy coots, had made a clean hole in the water and disappeared. I straightway prepared for action, unlimbered my murderous fowling piece, and took to the oars. I was quickly on the spot. I waited patiently, gun raised, to give the coup de grace. Time passed. At length, some half mile away, I saw my agile prey bob to the surface. "Get after him, and hurry up," my brother shouted. "He's going to be a hard one." And he was. For the first five miles I think that coot gained steadily despite my most strenuous efforts. 

He was headed for Montauk and the open sea. 

Then gradually, by slow stages, I shortened the distance between us. I was almost on him. One more dive would see the finish. I marked time, the gun ready for instant action. I scanned the unruffled waters. No coot. Yes; about a mile [8]

STRAY SHOTS 

back, in the direction from which we had just come, I spied him. He seemed to be quite at ease. 

I seized the oars and pulled away in pursuit. 

Late that same evening I got within what I supposed to be reasonable shooting distance. Exhausted in body, I still preserved a lively desire for revenge. At last we were at close grips. Taking careful aim, I cut loose a hail of No. 2's, and counted the battle won. But something went wrong. When the smoke cleared away I saw him still, so to speak, top side up. He was shaking his head. It seemed to me there was a suggestion of tolerance—perhaps amusement-in the action; and then, while I gazed in full bewilderment, that coot arose on leisurely wings and flew a straight course down the Sound.

Many another duck must, of course, have escaped me, but I believe my general average of captures was fairly high. I had extensive practice in that particular branch of ducking and I confess at this late date to considering myself pretty bad medicine for the craftiest diver that lived.

Long Island Sound was, at its best, a roughweather ducking ground; your coot and old squaw hardy specimens of the family, favoring broad and unsheltered waters and apparently [9]

STRAY SHOTS 

indifferent to the depth at which they fed. Thirty or forty-foot decoy lines,-perhaps fifteen or twenty of them-with a half brick for an anchor, are cold playthings of a winter morning. 

Taking up decoys,—and that too was part of my business-constituted, as I should view it today, about as mean and blood-chilling an amusement as one might care to choose.

Taking it all and all, we boys exerted enough effort and suffered a degree of bodily discomfort in the name of Sport sufficient to have earned us much greater rewards.

I remember that we regarded a factory-loaded' shell as far inferior to the home-made product. 

We entertained very precise ideas as to just what load of powder, how many wads, what size shot, etc., our particular gun required, and we swore by this or that combination in the fullest possible faith, Sitting up far into the night after school work was finished and loading shells for the forthcoming Saturday was a regular procedure throughout the winter months. It occurs to me that the heaviest load the gun would stand up to without flying out of one's hands was considered about right. This, no doubt, accounts for the fact that my gun was wrapped here and there with [ 10 ]

STRAY SHOTS 

wire and strengthened by sundry straps of brass where the stock had given away. 

But for me the memory of those old winter mornings can never die. The hurry and bustle to scrape up a breakfast over the kitchen stove; the general mêlée of boots and cartridge boxes, everyone diving into the pile for slicker coat or 

leather vest-then down the hill to our boats anchored in the creek below, and with the first pale light of dawn we were off for the ducking grounds. It might be a bunch of old squaws feeding off Table Rock that interested us today, or we'd made up our minds to "line" for coots, and that meant a six or eight mile row to Scotch Caps or Milton Point. Distance never seemed to be any [11]

STRAY SHOTS 

particular bar where there promised to be a day's shooting. Cold we soon learned to discount. Only heavy winds, with the Sound so rough that our boats could not live, kept the ducking squadron on shore of a Saturday morning.

Eventually came the ambition to rise above these plebeian members of the duck family. 

Coots and old squaws were well enough in their way, but there was better game to be had. Sundry fishermen in our neighborhood spent much of their time shooting during the winter months, but they "rigged" for broadbills and whistlersan occasional black duck, even, came to their bag. 

We were stirred to the depths whenever we chanced to see one of these men come in with a deck-load of decent ducks. If they could get broadbills, we in time could do the same. A start was the principal thing.

Old squaw decoys changed their spots over night at this period and became broadbills of a sort. Coots miraculously shed their sombre plumand blossomed forth as whistlers and black age ducks.

We constructed a sink box too, but, if I remember correctly, it was immediately condemned as a "death trap" and we were forbidden [12]

STRAY SHOTS 

ever to use it. Nevertheless, we were successful after some experimentation and study, in discovering the habits and haunts of our broadbill and in getting our share of shooting. 

The disease had spread. Three or four of us were shooting ducks-and at ducks now; the entire juvenile half of the family had fallen into line. It was just at this time that we fell heir to a small cabin sloop. The unbounded joy that this modest craft brought to us is hard to imagine. A sail boat, of course, opened up unlimited possibilities for bettering our shooting, allowing us the opportunity to travel much further and to spend the night on our ducking grounds. 

I can think of many glorious cruises in that little sloop, some pretty blustery experiences, as well, when we had to beat home in the teeth of a slashing nor' wester, duck-boats pounding and diving astern and ice making every moment where the seas broke over the side. No trouble was too great, no distance too far, if, at the end of it all, there lay the chance of a day's shooting.

Black ducks had for us-as they have for me to this day-a fascination all their own. We would have gone to almost any lengths for a shot or two at black ducks, and I am reminded as I [13]

STRAY SHOTS 

write, of one occasion when this excessive urge came near to begetting us trouble. 

We lay at anchor off the Thimble Islands, in preparation for the next day's sport. It was that charmed hour between sunset and dark; the water all calm about us and reflecting the shadows of a tiny island close aboard and the shore line further on. The combined odor of coffee, beans, and frying bacon stole out to me through the open companionway. Supper was about to be served. And then-straight across the rosy sunset glow sped a pair of black ducks. I watched them set their wings above the island and quickly fade from sight in the shadowy distance. A faint whisper of wings, and, following close in their wake, three more sailed past to dip over the nearby trees.

Now I should state here, for the benefit of the novice, that to pursue a bunch of black ducks under the given circumstances is something worse than foolish. The black duck is a singularly wary customer. Every condition must be in the stalker's favor, and even then, it is seldom that he meets with any great success. I don't think, however, that this ever deterred us. A black duck was always worth trying for, and our [14]

STRAY SHOTS 

supper that night was quickly disposed of while we planned an immediate sortie in pursuit of those ducks.

We paddled ashore, a party of four of us, with the stealth of Apache Indians. Before us a narrow creek, high banked on either side, wound into the darkness beyond. A typical black duck haven, it seemed to be beckoning us on to the fulfillment of all our hopes. Somewhere in this little creek were black ducks, many black ducks, perhaps. If we divided forces and crept cautiously up on either bank it seemed to our untutored minds that the chance of getting a black duck or two was pretty well assured. There ensued a whispered conference. The plan met with general approval. Two and two, we took opposite sides of the creek and began to make our stealthy way along the bank. It was a slow and painful progress. Everywhere were bushes and stumps to meet us out of the dark. Briars tore at our faces; stones continually barked our shins and cut our hands but we crawled on despite discomfort. The distance seemed endless; our advances more difficult every moment. At length from somewhere in the near distance, a dog barked. My partner in crime came to an instant [15]

STRAY SHOTS 

halt. "What d'you make of that?" he asked in a whisper. "It doesn't sound good to me." "I don't know," I answered. "Let's wait a while and see if anything happens."

No other sound reaching us, we pushed on, our eyes continually strained on the dark waters below us for some sign of those elusive ducks. 

How far we may have wriggled and crawled I hardly dare to say. We were scratched and bruised in a dozen places. I believe we were gradually losing heart when from somewhere close at hand came the splash of ruffled waters, and the unmistakable sound of feeding ducks. 

We were almost directly above them, the bank at this point sloping gently down to the water's edge. A better approach could hardly be imagined. Gauging distance from the sound of their movements, we knew that once we gained the foot of the bank our ducks would be in range. 

Abandoning caution now we stumbled down to the water as fast as the going would let us.

Straining our eyes we made out a large flock of ducks at some forty yards distance. Dimly we glimpsed them, closely huddled together, and presenting despite the darkness a magnificent shot. [16]

STRAY SHOTS 

With one accord we raised our guns, pouring four barrels into the mass before us. Not a bird rose from the water. Apparently we had, at one volley, killed the entire flock. But no; two or three ducks were splashing away toward the opposite side of the creek. Simultaneously a dog, evidently the dog of an earlier moment, started an awful row somewhere higher up the creek. 

From the blood curdling yaps and howls that reached us, I realized that he was rapidly moving in our direction. And not alone. A loud voice was urging him on. There was the crackle of breaking wood and thrashing of underbrush as some heavy body made toward us through the night. 

Ducks were drifting down to us in the current. 

Our black ducks at close range showed for the most part white as the driven snow! The moment had come for rapid decision and still more rapid movement. Bill had already decided on his course. He was half way across the little stream, up to his waist in the icy water but making magnificent speed. Respecting his good judgment, I followed with a silent prayer that the creek might not prove too deep to wade, and that the dog would either lose track of us or stop at the water's edge. [17]

STRAY SHOTS 

Death from cold and exposure is, doubtless, quite terrible to contemplate, but to us it seemed infinitely preferable to taking a chance with that man and his dog. And so we lay close and chattered and shook till the hullabaloo died off in the distance and we felt it safe to crawl up on higher ground. Dismally, and in all possible silence, we started back towards our landing place. One of our boats should be on this side of the creek. Had the other been discovered by our irate pursuer I was sure that it would be turned adrift or damaged beyond all use. 

My hair literally stood on end as figures suddenly loomed out of the darkness before me. 

Then I knew. It was the other and more fortunate half of our raiding party. "Jimmy," I croaked, "is that you?" "Ye-ye-yes-we're here," came back weakly. "What'd we better do?"

My teeth were fast set-the first stage, I was sure, of a death from freezing. “D-d-d-do what we're d-d-doing," I stuttered, and we hurried forward, our only thought now being to reach our boat and get back to the cabin stove.

That we made it, finding both of our boats as [18]

STRAY SHOTS 

we had left them at the mouth of the creek; that neither Bill nor I froze to solid blocks on the return trip, I consider one of the miracles of those early hazardous days. Hazardous? Yesand glorious too, those old days on Long Island 

Sound! That we youngsters were just a bit difficult, I don't pretend to deny. Certain it is that our parents kept vigil on many a night, wondering what had happened this time and why we didn't come home.

It has been my good fortune to enjoy some wonderful shooting since those early days on

Long Island Sound. 

The de luxe side of the game has a decided appeal as we travel down the years. With it all I have always been glad my duck shooting started precisely as it did. The leaky skiff, the patchedup gun-each in its way fulfilled a mission. Situations arise; unexpected difficulties of one kind or another have to be met in the best regulated duck shooting, and those early lessons help a lot. [19]

II. 

The Log of a South Bay Cruise. 

IF my shooting had, for the first few years, been confined to the waters of Long Island Sound, a desire to extend the field of operations was never far from my thoughts. There were other places to shoot ducks-more ducks and better ducks than fell to our usual lot. Great South

Bay was not so distant but that tales of its legions of water-fowl had reached my eager ears, and there came a time when the urge to prove these wondrous tales was no longer to be denied.

Finally, our plans took shape one winter night as Peter and I sat around the fire and talked of the morrow's "line." "We'll hook up the 'Noah'," said I, "new sails and all that, put a coal stove aboard and build a new sink box, load up with grub, and take a whack at Great South Bay!" 

From being merely a sporting proposition our scheme quickly grew in magnitude. New possibilities presented themselves as we talked the [21]

STRAY SHOTS 

matter over. What would wo do with all our ducks? Eat 'em? We couldn't. Ship 'em! Send a crate every day to market! Easiest thing in the world! 

We were neither of us on the "line" next morning when the coots came down past Scotch 

Caps. I went home at an early hour, wondering if

I could induce a lenient parent to countenance our splendid plan. Somehow I managed it. The wherewithal to finance my end of the partnership was promised and preparations began that very day toward outfitting the good ship Noah. I distinctly recall a trip to New York when we secured a large bundle of shipping tags and arranged for the sale of our game.

It was the middle of January, I think, before our affairs were fully in shape and the sharpie ready for sea. The weather had been turning colder from day to day, ice fringing the creek banks each morning and threatening to lock our craft fast at her moorings. It was high time we were getting away. Ice had formed pretty generally throughout the Great South Bay. "Pete!" 

I said, "we can't give it up without a try. Tumble your duffle over the rail. This hooker goes to 

Great South Bay if she has to go on wheels." [22]

STRAY SHOTS 

This was neither jest nor idle boast on my part, much as it may have sounded. With our departure delayed from one cause or other and the ice making thicker each day the idea of transporting our outfit across Long Island had gradually taken shape. What was the sharpie but an over-sized skiff, flat and narrow, but thirty feet long, and weighing, perhaps, a couple of tons with all our impedimenta? Once we had crossed Long Island

Sound I was certain someone could be found to haul us over the land.

We got an early start from New Rochelle one morning with the mercury registering five degrees and sleet flying spitefully at our backs. I doubt if we would have tackled it had the wind been anywhere else. It meant a straight run into 

Hempstead Harbor with very little handling of icy sheets, a consideration when the thermometer stands at five. Slipping across the Sound, we dropped anchor off the town of Roslyn in the early afternoon. It was now up to me to locate a man who would pack our rig over the island. 

I hate to think of the weary miles we covered that day. It still amuses me to recall the incredulity of the Long Island native as he listened to our preposterous plan. But at last we struck "Bill" [23]

STRAY SHOTS 

Soles. Bill was quite human, and possessed four sturdy "team." He said he would take the job, and he was on hand in the cold gray dawn next morning. Picking out a strip of hard beach the wagon was backed in nearly to the wheel tops, our gunning skiff hauled across decks and the sharpie floated over the trucks without the slightest trouble. A crack of the whip, a derisive jeer from the town youth assembled, and we were off on the second leg of our cruise. 

The journey from Roslyn to Freeport-for it was for Freeport we set our course-was something of a triumphal progress under Bill's capable guidance. Bill Soles, it transpired, had many friends along the line of march. At one weatherbeaten farmhouse where we stopped to water the horses Bill casually mentioned cider and we purchased a large supply. Later, Mr. Soles pulled up without comment and pointed to a tiny cabin some distance back from the road. "Gal in there 

I know middlin' well; she kin cook t' beat th' band. Come on. Dinner won't cost you nothin'." 

He was right; I don't doubt they divided on 

Bill's back trip, for Bill was a canny man.

A gradual failing of the sombre light and a marked increase in the cold came with sunset. [24]

STRAY SHOTS 

Freeport seemed but a chimera of Bill's imagination, despite his constant assurances that it lay "just through that next neck o' pines." It was only the smell of the marsh that persuaded me Bill hadn't lied from the first, and wasn't carting us back to Hempstead Bay by some circuitous route. At last we made out a ribbon of water. "The Sea! The Sea!" I hailed from aloft. "That ain't no sea," Bill scoffed. "That there is Freeport Creek."

We spent the night snugged away in the lea of the marsh. The wind, which had risen to half a gale, shrilled eerily through our rigging to the incessant accompaniment of whipping halyards and the creak of our restless rudder. Inside the 

Noah's cabin, however, we made light of wind and weather. We were warm and cheerful, and this was prime duck-shooting weather. Visions of black ducks, and mallards, and high-flying widgeon danced through my head that night as 

I lay between sleeping and waking and listened to the wind. We had at last arrived at the very gates of Duckdom. Within sight of our cabin lights-in reach, perhaps, of our good ten bores, -ducks nestled close in those teeming marshes; ducks that were worth a fellow's trouble; ducks, [25]

STRAY SHOTS 

by Gad! that would pay expenses! Only one thing disturbed my drowsy reflections: we had failed to provide for shipping crates for the ducks we were going to kill. 

Next morning, to our disappointment, the wind had hauled due north, and while not blowing as it had on the preceding day, I was not in the least deceived.

We had figured on a run to Amityville the first day, where we meant to purchase some overlooked stores and add to our supply of coal. In view of the long portage, we had sailed light of the latter important commodity. At Amityville we could fill our lockers, and from there strike across to Cedar Island where we hoped to kill our ducks. We tucked in two reefs in anticipation of the worst that old norther could do, and poled out to the mouth of the creek. Ice barred our way in some places, but it was not of a serious thickness, and we managed by the combined use of pole and oar to break our way through in time. 

Already the clouds were scudding across the blue above us and a heavy swell making in from the broader water. Peter, a youth wise in his generation, was for temporizing awhile with the wind before thrusting ourselves in its path. But we [26]

STRAY SHOTS 

didn't. We ran up our two shortened sails and slipped jauntily out to face it. 

A dead whack to windward, eight or ten miles of it, lay before us, and I knew in my heart we need look for no favors from those swiftly moving clouds. And yet so fickle is the North Wind —we sailed out in a comparative calm; there was just enough weight in the frequent flaws to keep us on the alert. Even my suspicions were somewhat allayed. Sand bars we met and cleared with good fortune; bearing off for some, shooting up for others, with the hum of the breeze in our halyards and the waves slapping noisily along the sharpie's sides. And then—“z-z-z-zing!”

It caught us a point or two off the wind and walloped the poor old Noah down to her cabin ports. 

Sinister rattlings and crashes from the sharpie's internals warned us that, despite much careful stowing, various articles had slipped their moorings and were rolling about at random. We were not in a position, however, to investigate the wreck. White squalls were tumbling down on us, burying us in an avalanche of icy water. 

How we pulled the Noah out of it I don't know. At last, with a wicked final puff, the wind lulled. We were able to beat our numbed hands [27]

STRAY SHOTS 

back to life and shake out that portion of the Great South Bay which had lodged about our persons. This was the moment when, holding discretion far above valour, we should have put about and run back to our sheltered creek. Instead, we expressed the cheering belief that we'd seen the worst of the blow. With sheets flattened again to retrieve our lost windward position, we pointed the Noah's nose in the wind, relashed the tender across decks, and plunged gayly on our way.

For the better part of the morning we battled against the wind. I still think we would have made our intended port if we hadn't encountered ice. A great field of it lay directly to windward of us, and we ran close up to its jagged edge in the hope of finding an opening; but in this we were disappointed. III. 

Marsh Magic 

TALK to any old guide or ex-market-hunter along the reaches of Currituck and the Carolina 

Sounds and he'll tell you tales of the beach and marsh that must thrill the most blasé of duck shooters. Visions of dusky dawns will arise; marshes awake to the whir of wings; companies, squads and legions of ducks, roar up from their feeding-grounds to darken the amber sky.

Speak of the gunners of those far days and, man by man, he'll count them off. Famous they stand in the annals of shanty and cabin. The market-hunter has gone his way but the memory of his prowess lingers throughout the passing years.

Many a master shot was bred in that roughweather school. Straight shooting was often a family inheritance, descending from father to son and to son again through several generations.

V. 

Gun Shy 

WINTER had slunk beyond the hills—a surly, grudging winter that gave way reluctantly before the advance of spring. You might catch the scent of fresh-turned earth, hear faintly the roll of thunder. Down in the swamp the peepers held high revelry; a red-wing conned the nearby reeds and made plans for a summer home. 

Only yesterday you might have seen the geese go sailing north, belated travelers, following fast on winter's heels. Perhaps you had watched those geese with more than passing interest. For some of us, I know, the spring migration was always a pleasing sight. Yearly we scanned the sky-line for a first glimpse of the hurrying lines. Here was sign and portent.

When the geese began their northerly flight we took it as infallible proof that the snipe had already come. It was time to overhaul winterworn rubber boots, load our shells with number nines and get out for a look around. [89]

No doubt our snipe shooting was quite outrageous as viewed by present-day standards. We shot snipe in the spring, for it was then only that snipe dropped into our low grounds, and we were oppressed by no sense of guilt that I can recall at this late date. "Conservation" was a little-used word thirty odd years ago, and the shooting conscience not developed to the point that it is today. 

Be that as it may, we did shoot snipe in the spring; but I don't pretend to say we always found them on these early season hunts. He's a mysterious mite, is little Jack-o'-the-bog; here today and gone again with just a few scattered borings, or a spat or two on the muddy ground to mark his hurried visit. Silhouetted against the gray water astern, the dense canopy spread above us and the banks showed black and eerie. I might almost have imagined Sam some ghoulish boatman transporting me over the river Styx to a forest inferno beyond. [115]

We were bound for no inferno, however. "Salt Point" lay at the end of our journey, and

Salt Point, from all reports, left nothing to be desired from a duck shooter's point of view. 

Leaving the main stream, a small branch of the Cooper River, we entered a ditch so narrow and cluttered with snags and sunken logs that we had difficulty in forcing a passage. A vista of broad marsh, however, soon opened before us. Pulsestirring sounds were borne to me over the grassy bank. A lusty old mallard was warning his companions of my approach. He told them, I know, that trouble was brewing for the Salt Point ducks that day. Then, to show them that he, at least, had no idea of furnishing any of the amusement, he rose with a noisy clatter of wings and left for parts unknown.

We hauled over the bank a little further on and pushed out through the shallow water. Sam, accustomed, of course, to the sight of many ducks, seemed unmoved by the picture before us. 

I might exaggerate if I said there were a thousand ducks in that little pond; again I might be falling short of the number. At all events, there was a downright heap of ducks. Our blind lay on the opposite bank, easily reached from the check [116]

It had been a long paddle and we were somewhat late. Many of the ducks we had moved from our pond were still sailing about in detached bunches. I could, I am sure, have made a very decent bag on my walk around to the blind. The flocks were for the most part too large to shoot into, however, and I steeled myself to patience, waiting for Sam and the decoys. 

Would he never come? Was I to tramp up and down that bank indefinitely, with the banging of other men's guns in my ears and myriads of ducks over my head? At last! the swish of the paddle, the crackle of crushed saw-grass and Sam came into sight around the bend. 

Out went the decoys; first the wooden ones, then a half dozen live mallards, tugging fiercely at their anchors as Sam dropped them overboard. 

I noticed that Sam kept one eye on me as he hurried with the decoys, and I reassured him somewhat by standing my gun in the corner of the blind while he finished setting out. 

I early found that Salt Point was, for that day at least, distinctly a mallard rendezvous. Other varieties of duck, sprig, gadwall, now and then a [117]

spoonbill or blue-wing, passed by the edge of my blind; but invariably they had business elsewhere and merely gave my layout a good look. 

But the mallards! How they poured in! By the time our stage was set and ready, a great number of ducks had already settled at the farther end of the open water. These I made Sam rout out at the start. They swept over me, past me, almost through the top of my blind, and I will always recall, with a certain satisfaction, that I let that avalanche pass over me without raising gun to shoulder. "Don't shoot into the big bunches," the unwritten law of the Mulberry marsh, must have stopped me, had I been stupid enough to want to. My reward came quickly. In my "duck diary" I have written the epitaph of three mallards in these terse terms: "3 Mallards. Right and left — loading and dropping third with choke bl. at 60 yards."

The way of it was this: I turned my gaze from the great flock, now far across the marsh, to find three big mallard drakes directly over the decoys. Before I could reach my gun one of the trio had lit almost at the foot of the blind. As I rose, the other two swerved up and off at a most disconcerting angle. Remembering that one [118]

The frightened mallard was perilously near the limit of range when I drew down on his white tail feathers. I'm vain enough to wish some appreciative sportsman could have seen that bit of marksmanship. After all, it wasn't so hard to get 'em if you knew how. 

Oh pride, pride, that goeth before a fall! I was crouched in the front of the blind again.

There were ducks to the right of me, ducks to the left of me. They were slanting down to the decoys from either side. A pair on the left were over first, and were my best chance. I had already counted them in with the bag. Practically speaking, they were mine. . . . But I pause to explain that there is a certain peculiar shifting motion which a duck will sometimes make. Strangely enough, my mallards had both learned it, and naturally-Well, I missed them! "Scaipe! Scaipe!"—alluring note, soft voice of the marsh at break of day. "Scaipe!"—that brave little challenge. Straightway a tiny whisp of brown, diving, dipping, lost in the shrouding willows. I was tempted to try a long shot and glad the next moment I hadn't, for he appeared to herald the return flight of ducks to Salt Point -a flight that I shall remember for many a day.

A stiff breeze had sprung up and the bulk of my ducks, coming in from the windward end of the pond, would sail past the decoys on the far side, swing into the wind again and come floating up to me on almost stationary wings. I don't want to give the impression that I took only the easy shots. Now and then there would come a chance at ducks passing down wind and any duck with a thirty-mile wind behind him and the sight of a leveled gun to urge him onward, is far from an easy mark. I believe some of those ducks, killed dead in the air, sailed eighty yards before they struck water. [120]

But mallards, mallards, mallards! It passed my understanding to see only mallards, with just an occasional black duck or two, when I knew the marsh was alive with ducks of other kinds. 

I had learned my lesson on the first day in the old rice field. To shoot is only half the fun of shooting, and I found enough pleasure in just watching many of the ducks as they dropped in to my decoys or swept over on the wings of the gale.

It blew harder and harder as the morning wore on. A heavy bank of cloud was rolling up from the eastward, promising rain before we could pick up and start for home. I thought of 

Sam and the long stretch of water between us and Mulberry. Getting in the decoys should be short work, so I fondly thought, and we would get under way, perhaps, before the heaviest of the rain.

I have a notion that Sam, despite my bombardment, had spent the morning in uninterrupted slumber. At all events, I nearly shouted my lungs out before I raised him from the bank, and a steady downpour had set in by the time he had hauled across and begun to take in the decoys.

Done bus' her string an' gone!" And she had. Ten feet from Sam's outstretched hand, and fast increasing the distance, she was swiftly paddling toward the further end of the pond. "Lor' ! how 

I gwine ketch her now?" I looked at Sam and earnestly yearned to tell him. What we did do was to chase that duck for something like one hour 'round and 'round the pond. A dozen times we were near to having her cornered in a pocket of the marsh. Sam, armed with a forked stick, would creep up within reaching distance, then attempt with a sudden lunge to pin her down in the shoal water. Flip!—she had dodged adroitly, leaving Sam with a boot full of muddy marsh and murder in his heart.

Art and subterfuge alike failed. We were by this time drenched to the skin, discourageddesperate. Sam waded ashore. He was "gwine cut him a sure 'nough willer," something in the nature of a flail, I gathered, with numerous branches that would spread over a whole flock of ducks. [122]

He had scarcely reached the line of willows when Miss Mallard edged gingerly up to the boat, cocked her eye at me hunched up in the after-end, and with a hop and a flop tumbled over the gunwale and settled comfortably down by her coop. Stealthily I crawled forward and reaching under the seat seized her by the neck. "Sam," I yelled, "I've got her! Never mind your tree." Only once on the long pull home did 

Sam make reference to our weary chase. “Sure did ac' mean." "Who?" I asked innocently. "Dat ol' fool duck." Then silence, and the steady dip of our paddles as we made for Mulberry. 

There was a day at "Big Broughton," where sprig, teal, black duck and gadwall, as well as mallard, went to the making of my bag. A wonderful day when my host and I doubled up in one of the house field-blinds and took our toll of sprig. How they flew that morning! How clearly they stood out against the morning sky! I see them yet, swerving down at sight of the decoys; circling, breasting up, at length to meet our hail of lead. Did we miss an easy one, we turned about the next moment and pulled a clean right and left out of the very clouds.

In time we'd go over it all again, sitting before [123]
