After the required study was initiated, the 84th Congress of the United States in its Second Session amended the Social Security Act by enactment of Public 

Law 880. One part of this amendment relates to medical care for recipients of old age assistance, aid to the blind, aid to the permanently and totally disabled, and aid to dependent children. Previous amendments to the Social 

Security Act had provided for federal participation in payments to medical vendors for medical care of assistance recipients. Federal participation in these vendor payments was covered by the same formula and maximums which applied to money payments to the individual. Under the 1956 amendment, vendor payments for medical care of recipients will receive separate participation limited by an average amount per recipient. In other words, this average amount times the number of recipients gives the total amount of federal money which would be available for matching of medical care vendor payments. The present formula of federal participation, with its limitation on the amount of payment to the individual case in which the federal government will participate, will apply only to money payments to the recipient. 

If Michigan is to benefit fully from this change in federal participation, it will be necessary for the State Department of Social Welfare in Michigan to alter its current policies and procedures concerning money payments to the individual versus vendor payments for medical care. 

Having studied the problem of hospitalization in Michigan as required by House Resolution No. 36, and having studied the relationship of this problem to the newly amended Social Security Act, the Michigan Social Welfare Commission presents the following as its findings and recommendations in respect to hospitalization in Michigan:

STATE PARTICIPATION IN AFFLICTED ADULT HOSPITALIZATION 

The House of Representatives, Michigan Legislature, in its 1956 Session, placed itself on record as being aware of the increasing and excessive burden of afflicted adult hospitalization upon the counties. It recommended that the 1957 Legislature ease this burden by enacting some provision for state participation in this cost. 

The excessive burden upon the counties is evidenced by expression of the counties themselves; by the fact that some counties have large unpaid obligations for hospitalization; by the fact that some county departments have found it necessary to restrict hospitalization either to persons already in receipt of some other form of assistance or to persons whose conditions are a matter of life and death; and by the fact that the gross statewide cost of this program has risen to $9.8 million.

Through old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the disabled, the state (together with the federal government) has established itself as assuming liability for assistance to these classes of needy within certain specified limits. The state has also established itself as liable, within the limitations of $90 per recipient monthly, for hospitalization of these persons. 

The extent of this liability has been increased from time to time as costs of assistance have risen or as additional areas of assistance have been deemed essential. It would seem logical to expand an already existing program rather than to initiate a new one, if the desired ends can be obtained by such expansion. If the entire cost of hospitalization for these recipients and for adult recipients of aid to dependent children were met by the state, county cost for hospitalization would be reduced by 34 percent (outside of 

Wayne county it would be reduced by 52 percent).

The county departments of social welfare have had experience and have the facilities for administering afflicted adult hospitalization. If the state assumed the cost of hospitalization for old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the disabled, and for adult recipients of aid to dependent children, the county departments would still be obliged to provide hospitalization for all other afflicted adults; therefore, the county departments would still be involved in the task of administering hospitalization. County departments in many instances have established contract arrangements with local hospitals and doctors, or are in a position to do so, for more economical administration of hospitalization. If the State Department entered into direct administration of any phase of hospitalization it would involve a duplication of administrative machinery. 

Administrative action without fiscal risk is open to abuse. An expenditure of local funds for each expenditure by the state would help retain a local interest in economy of administration. 

The Commission recommends that the Social Welfare Act be amended to provide for 90 percent state reimbursement to the county departments of social welfare for all afflicted adult hospitalization expenditures (exclusive of expenditures for convalescent home care) in respect to recipients of old age assistance, aid to the blind, aid to the disabled, and adult recipients of aid to dependent children under Act 267, P.A. 

1915, as amended, without any limitation of maximum allowable monthly payments per recipient.

UNIFORM ELIGIBILITY FOR HOSPITALIZATION OF OLD AGE ASSIST- ANCE, AID TO THE BLIND, AID TO THE DISABLED, AND ADULT AID TO DEPENDENT CHILDREN RECIPIENTS 

One of the basic requirements for federal approval of public assistance plans is that they be "in effect in all political subdivisions of the state." This

means, in part, that there must be uniform provisions relating to eligibility and amount of assistance in effect in all parts of the state. In respect to hospitalization of old age assistance, aid to the blind, aid to the disabled, and adult aid to dependent children recipients, this would require that any of these recipients would be eligible for and could receive hospitalization on an equal basis and according to the same defined standards in every county of the state. Under the present plan this requirement is met by inclusion of hospitalization in the money payment to the individual at the determination of the county bureau of social aid if it cannot be provided under contract with the county department. It is improbable that vendor payment and money payment can substitute for each other under the new formula for federal participation.

Factors of eligibility for hospitalization vary among counties, particularly amount of income and amount of property holdings. A person eligible for old age assistance might not be eligible for hospitalization because of the amount of his property holdings.

The Social Welfare Act provides that the county boards of social welfare "shall comply with and be governed by the rules and regulations of the state department of social welfare only as to those forms of relief which are wholly or in part financed by federal funds" (Section 52, Act 280, P.A. 1939, as amended). This provision might be construed to apply to hospitalization of recipients of old age assistance, aid to the blind, aid to the disabled, and adult recipients of aid to dependent children, if financed jointly from federal, state, and local funds. Specific reference would leave no doubt regarding interpretation. 

The Commission recommends that Section 52, Act 280, P.A. 1939 be further amended by specific reference to hospitalization under Act 267, P.A. 1915 when provided to recipients of old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the disabled, and adult recipients of aid to dependent children.

LEGAL SETTLEMENT UNDER AFFLICTED ADULT HOSPITALIZATION 

Act 267, P.A. 1915 provides that the county in which the person possesses legal settlement is responsible for the cost of his hospitalization. One-half of the hospitalized persons with legal settlement in another county in Michigan in 1955 were recipients of old age assistance, aid to the blind, or aid to the disabled. If the Social Welfare Act is amended to provide that state and federal funds be expended to pay 90 percent of the cost of hospitalization for these persons, collection of any part of this expenditure from another county in which the person possessed legal settlement would cause administrative confusion and might endanger receipt of federal funds.

When recipients of old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the disabled are removed from this group, only an insignificant proportion of total hospitalization expenditures (2.6 percent) is in respect to persons with legal settlement in another county. PAYMENTS TO COUNTIES IN RELATION TO OPERATING COSTS OF COUNTY MEDICAL CARE FACILITIES 

Medical care facility costs are part of the county cost for hospitalization or of the cost for direct relief. If a medical care facility is not available, the person requiring such care may be either hospitalized or placed in a private convalescent home. If he were placed in the convalescent home, the expenditure would be made from direct relief funds if this home could be certified as his usual domicile. Such direct relief expenditures receive 

50 percent reimbursement from the state. If the person were hospitalized, the expenditure would be chargeable against afflicted adult hospitalization. 

The Commission is recommending 90 percent state participation in hospital costs relative to these recipients. 

The Commission proposes to continue its present practice of payment to the county departments within the $90 maximum allowable payment. This will provide the county department with a basic three dollar ($3.00) payment for each day's care for recipients of old age assistance, aid to the blind, aid to the disabled, and adult recipients of aid to dependent children requiring continuous care in county medical care facilities (three-fourths of the cases). 

Payment for persons receiving short time care would be upward to entire cost of service.

These payments in regard to recipients in county medical care facilities are on the same basis as payments for care of such recipients in private convalescent homes. If the payments for convalescent home care, together with other resources of the recipients, are not adequate to cover the cost of such care, the county departments of social welfare usually may pay the additional costs out of direct relief.

ESTABLISHMENT OF A POOLED FUND 

One of the various procedures for payment to vendors for medical care under which federal participation is available is use of a "pooled" fund. There are three distinct advantages to such a fund. One is that a minimum payment may be made for each recipient, in the manner of an insurance premium, rather than large payments up to several thousand dollars for a selected few recipients provided care. A second is that payments are not limited to categorical eligibility at the time of payment; if the recipient was eligible and a monthly "premium" paid into the fund during the time of hospitalization, the bill may be paid whenever presented. This advantage of the pooled fund would minimize loss to the county by death of recipient prior to payment. 

The third is that payments into the fund remain at a constant average amount per case monthly subject to a constant proportion and amount of federal participation. If the fund is established at a payment of six dollars ($6.00) or more per recipient the federal government will contribute three dollars ($3.00) per recipient, under an approved plan. 

Payments to the fund must be irrevocable. The fund cannot be used for any purpose other than that for which it is specified. Amounts are to be considered as expended upon entry into the fund. Balances in the fund cannot revert to the general fund of the state at the end of a fiscal period. Regular monthly payments to the fund in respect to recipients covered by the fund must be adjusted to experience with expenditures from the fund. 

The amount considered as assistance to the individual is limited to the fixed money amount paid into the fund (plus any money payment to the individual). Payments from the fund may not be identified as assistance to the individual in obtaining refunds, making collections, or in recovery from the estate of the recipient. Such refunds, collections, and recoveries are limited to the amount of the regular monthly payments into the fund for the recipient.

ADDITIONAL APPROPRIATION FOR PUBLIC ASSISTANCE GRANTS 

Effective July 1, 1957, federal participation in vendor medical payments for recipients of old age assistance, aid to dependent children, aid to the blind, and aid to the disabled will be determined by a new formula separate from and in addition to federal participation in money payments to the recipient. In respect to old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the disabled, federal participation will be one-half of total vendor medical payments, not counting so much of such expenditures for any month as exceeds the product of $6 multiplied by the total number of persons receiving assistance under each program considered separately. In respect to aid to dependent children, federal participation will be one-half of total vendor medical payments, not counting so much of such expenditures for any month as exceeds the product of $3 multiplied by the total number of children plus the product of $6 multiplied by the total number of adults receiving aid to dependent children. This means that out of total vendor medical payments of $8,620,000 in respect to all programs in 1957-58, federal participation would be $4,310,000 and state cost would be $4,310,000.

Under the Commission recommendations in this report, federal participation in vendor medical payments will be available in respect to state payments for afflicted adult hospitalization of recipients of old age assistance, aid to the blind, aid to the disabled, and adult recipients of aid to dependent children. It is also available in respect to the vendor medical payments for service provided these recipients in county medical care facilities. 

The greater part of the recommended payments in respect to afflicted adult hospitalization is an additional cost of assistance not previously provided by the state and therefore additional to the estimated costs of assistance included in the State Department of Social Welfare 1957-58 request for funds.

GENERAL SOCIAL WELFARE BACKGROUND 

During calendar year 1955, the various county departments of social welfare (including the Detroit Department of Public Welfare) expended a total of $29,564,180 in their various assistance programs, including afflicted adult hospitalization. These programs include general public relief (otherwise known as direct relief in Michigan), the institutional programs of county infirmaries and county medical care facilities, and afflicted adult hospitalization. Direct relief includes medical care of the recipient in his own home (and convalescent home care if the home has become his normal domicile) in addition to his normal maintenance in food, clothing, shelter, utilities, etc. 

In discussion of afflicted adult hospitalization, it is necessary to consider the institutional program, the direct relief medical care program, and the direct relief convalescent home program as well, because an individual may receive varied treatment for his medical needs according to the facilities available in each county. Afflicted adult hospitalization itself cost $6.8 million. The institutional program cost an additional $7.5 million. Direct relief medical care and direct relief convalescent home care each cost somewhat more than one million dollars. (Table I.)

HOSPITALIZATION DEFINED 

Afflicted adult hospitalization is administered by the county departments of social welfare under Act 267, P.A. 1915, as amended (being Sections 404.101 to 404.112 inclusive, C.L. 1948). The determinations of the county department under this program are final, not being subject to control by rule and regulation of the Michigan Social Welfare Commission. Hospitalization provided under this Act may be for any medical requirements of a person who is unable to pay for such hospitalization as determined by the county department, in relation to both medical and surgical treatment required by disease or physical injury, either to preserve life, to ease suffering, or to restore to the person his ability for self-support. Afflicted adult hospitalization is limited to persons 21 years of age or over; except that the county department is “authorized in its discretion to provide necessary hospital care for afflicted minors who are unable to secure such care under Act 283 of the Public Acts of 1939 as amended." Hospitalization may be provided either at the University of Michigan Hospital or at a local public or private hospital as decided by the county department of social welfare. 

The Act requires that an agreement to repay be obtained from the person hospitalized or from a legally responsible relative or guardian. It also provides that if the legal settlement of the person hospitalized is in another

10. 

county of the State, the county in which the person is legally settled is liable for the cost of such care. The Act places no restriction upon hospitalization of individuals without legal settlement in any county. 

The $6.8 million expenditures for afflicted adult hospitalization, remaining after all refunds, collections, recoveries, and state payments in 1955, represented 23.2 percent of the state-wide total of all county department assistance expenditures.

COUNTY INSTITUTIONS 

Some conditions of disease or chronic ailment do not require the facilities of a public or private general hospital. Such cases had been provided for in county infirmaries under the Social Welfare Act. With the enactment of federal legislation in respect to old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the permanently and totally disabled, county infirmaries fell into disuse because it was impossible for a person receiving care in a county infirmary to receive, at the same time, assistance under one of these federally sponsored programs. 

Many counties, therefore, disposed of their county infirmaries and provided care in private convalescent homes for persons requiring an institutional environment. 

When the 1950 amendment to the Social Security Act provided for federal participation in the cost of care provided in public medical institutions, a new development grew out of the county infirmary. Portions of, or entire institutions were rehabilitated to meet the standards established by the State Department of Social Welfare, permitting persons resident in these institutions as medical patients to receive assistance under the federal-state programs. This movement was given legal sanction and impetus by a 1954 

amendment to the Social Welfare Act (Sections 400.58, 400.58a, and 400.58b, 

C.L. 1948, as amended).

These approved medical care facilities, operated by the county departments of social welfare, must be considered in relation to county programs of afflicted adult hospitalization. If the county department operates an approved medical care facility, it provides hospitalization in the medical care facility for those persons who do not require major surgery. It also transfers patients from public or private general hospitals to the county medical care facility for convalescence as soon as they may be moved with safety. 

The $7.5 million county institutional costs represent the gross costs, less capital expenditures, of operating county infirmaries and medical care facilities, and net cost of farm operations, in 52 counties, including Wayne county. In 35 of these counties a county medical care facility was either part of or the entire operation. In Wayne county, infirmary and medical care

11. facility are operated as part of a general institutional program, under a separate Board of County Institutions, independent of the Wayne County Department of Social Welfare. Therefore costs of those operations are not usually included in the total assistance costs of the county departments of social welfare. These costs have been added, however, from special study data. 

DIRECT RELIEF CONVALESCENT HOME CARE 

Convalescent and chronic care of needy persons is provided in private convalescent homes and institutions also. If the county department can obtain a statement that this home or institution has become the normal domicile of the individual, then cost of this care may be paid as direct relief and receive state participation. Therefore, the county reduces its cost of afflicted adult hospitalization by providing necessary care in convalescent homes under direct relief whenever possible. Counties not operating an approved medical care facility possess no resource other than convalescent homes by which to reduce the cost of afflicted adult hospitalization. Even in counties operating approved medical care facilities, private convalescent homes are used if the need for care exceeds the capacity of the medical care facility. Old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the permanently and totally disabled may be provided to or for a person otherwise eligible while he is in a private convalescent home approved by the State as well as while he is in an approved medical care facility as a patient in that facility. If the convalescent home cannot be considered normal domicile during convalescence, the cost is paid out of afflicted adult hospitalization. 

The $1.2 million direct relief expenditure for convalescent home care included supplementation to the $80 monthly money payments to recipients of old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the disabled who were receiving convalescent home care. To this extent the direct relief payments. represented only partial cost of this form of care. As direct relief these expenditures were subject to state participation; only one-half of the expenditure was an actual county cost. The total expenditures represented only 3.9 percent of county department assistance expenditures. 

DIRECT RELIEF MEDICAL CARE 

Direct relief medical care, other than care in a convalescent home, involves home and office care by a physician, drugs as provided by the physician or purchased from a drug store, limited dental care and nursing service, and prosthetic appliances. There was a time in the history of welfare medical programs that hospitalization in a county might vary according to the extent of the direct relief medical program. There seems very little likelihood that this would occur today, since it is prevailing practice to hospitalize a person whenever extended care is required.

Direct relief medical care in 1955 cost $1.1 million. Like convalescent home care under direct relief, this expenditure was subject to at least 50 percent state participation. It represented only 3.7 percent of county department assistance expenditures.

VARIATIONS IN COSTS 

Variations among the counties in costs for one program are related to differences in total amounts spent for assistance and in proportions of use of one program as against the other types of care. The measures of cost used here are 

1. percentage relationship between total assistance expenditures and expenditures for the specific program; 

2. per capita cost; i.e., cost per person in the county's population for these expenditures; 

3. mills per dollar of the state equalized property valuation spent on these assistance programs.

Each of these measures has a different significance as an indicator of the county's assistance efforts. The first percentage relationships among the types of programs (Table II), illustrates the extreme variation among the counties in relative use of the several programs available. In relation to afflicted adult hospitalization and care in county institutions, costs for the former ranged from 2.7 percent of county department assistance expenditures in Houghton county (in which the proportion of non-medical direct relief cost was the highest in the state) to 59.4 percent in Ogemaw county (which operated no institutional program), with a state average of 23.2 percent. The $7.5 million institutional cost represented 25.4 percent of all county department assistance costs, ranging from 7.2 percent in St. Clair county to 78.4 percent in Shiawassee county.

The second measure, per capita cost (Table III), shows how much the care cost in relation to county population, or how much the care would cost each man, woman and child in the county if distributed equally among them. 

The third measure, mills per dollar state equalized valuation (Table IV), shows how much the care cost in relation to the total value of taxable property in the county as determined by the State Board of Equalization.

SPECIAL STUDY OF AFFLICTED ADULT HOSPITALIZATION 

The above references have been to data obtained from monthly summary reports submitted by the counties to the State Department of Social Welfare during calendar year 1955. Detailed analysis of afflicted adult hospitalization is based upon an individual case study made for the purpose of this report during the summer and fall of 1956, but relating to afflicted adult hospitalization expenditures during 1955. Data differ between the summary reports and the study in several respects. In the summary reports, gross expenditures for hospitalization duplicate expenditures for persons with legal settlement in another county: first, as an expenditure of the county hospitalizing the person and second, as an expenditure of the county in which the person possessed legal settlement. In the special study, the counties were instructed to report only on hospitalization for which they arranged, and not to report on payments made to other counties. The special study also differs from the summary reports in that receipts were reported only in respect to those persons for whom an expenditure was made during the year. Receipts included might be in repayment for care provided in another year, not related to the expenditures reported; no 1955 refunds or collections from persons hospitalized before but not during 1955 were reported. 

HOSPITALIZATION BY SOURCE OF OTHER ASSISTANCE 

In the classification of other assistance, as defined in the special study, old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the disabled indicate that the recipient of hospitalization was also a recipient of assistance under one of these programs sometime during his period of hospitalization. The classes, aid to dependent children, direct relief, county infirmary, and medical care facility, indicate that the person hospitalized was previously included in an aid to dependent children or direct relief case, or had been provided care immediately before hospitalization in the county infirmary or county medical care facility. The absence of data in the last two of these classes in Wayne county is not a positive indication that none of the hospitalization cases in Wayne county were previously institutionalized in a county institution. The Wayne county classification is based upon the Wayne 

County Department of Social Welfare Annual Report. That report did not take into consideration previous residence in the infirmary or medical care facility sections of Wayne County General Hospital, operated under a separate board of county institutions.

Total expenditures for hospitalization, as estimated from the special study, were $9.8 million,---$5.5 million in Wayne county and $4.3 million in other counties of the state. Of these totals, $2.6 million were expended for persons who received old age assistance at some time during the period of their

15. hospitalization. The expenditures in respect to old age assistance recipients amounted to only a little more than one-fourth of total expenditures for hospitalization; however, in counties other than Wayne expenditures for old age assistance recipients amounted to 43.5 percent of the total. In Wayne county, nearly three-fourths of the expenditures for hospitalization were in respect to persons not otherwise receiving assistance (Cf. Tables 1 and 2). 

The proportion of expenditures for hospitalization provided to old age assistance cases (26.8 percent) is greater than the proportion of such assistance cases provided hospitalization (24.4 percent), but is less than the proportion of days care hospitalization provided to old age assistance recipients (28.8 percent). This relationship is equally true in respect to aid to the disabled recipients hospitalized. These persons were provided with more extended hospitalization (21.6 days per recipient of old age assistance, and 35.1 days per recipient of aid to the disabled) than other persons hospitalized (Cf. 

Table 3). As a result hospitalization expenditure per person was $379 for old age assistance recipients and $568 for aid to disabled recipients, compared to $344 for all persons hospitalized by the county departments of social welfare in 1955. Expenditures may be proportionately smaller than days care for these persons because of less out-patient care, which is in cost but not in days care figures.

The four programs, old age assistance, aid to the blind, aid to the disabled, and aid to dependent children, are state administered assistance programs in which there is federal participation. All four are subject to the new method of federal participation in vendor medical payments. Under the first three, current Michigan policy provides for monthly payments to or for recipients upward to $90 for hospitalization. In total, 9,368 recipients of these four programs were hospitalized by the county departments in 1955. This was 

33.0 percent of all persons hospitalized. Hospitalization of these persons cost the counties (before collections, recoveries, current payments from the state) a total of $3.3 million. This accounted for 34.2 percent of county expenditures for hospitalization,---20.1 percent in Wayne county, and 52.2 percent in all other counties of Michigan.

In counties other than Wayne, the study showed receipts totaling $575,706 in relation to total expenditures of $4,293,660. These receipts exclude $260,369 from other counties, on nonsettled cases.

Receipts are of three general types (Cf. Tables 4, 5, and 6): 

1. Those which lower, at all levels of government, the cost to the public of this type of care. 

2. Those which do not lower the over-all public expense, but do distribute what is otherwise a cost of local government to state and federal levels as well. 

3. Those which neither lower the cost to government, nor spread the cost to different levels of government. 

Included in the first type of receipt are payments by the person hospitalized from his own resources (including old age and survivors insurance), payments by responsible relatives, collections from estates of persons hospitalized, etc. Receipts of this class may legitimately be considered reductions of public expenditures.

A total of $237,435 in this type of receipts was reported. If the report had included receipts on cases for which there were no expenditures this total would have approximated $500,000. There were refunds and collections of this type in respect to all classes of cases, but the greatest proportion were in respect to persons not in receipt of any other assistance (9.8 percent). 

It is probable that the largest amount of unreported receipts related to nonassistance cases. Even if all classes of cases were equal in proportion of unreported receipts, doubling the reported receipts in respect to old age assistance, aid to the blind, aid to the disabled, and aid to dependent children cases would not have resulted in refunds and collections equal to 10 percent of gross expenditures for those cases.

The second general type of receipt for the county includes state assistance payments for hospitalization. Receipts of this class may not be considered as reducing public expenditures, but as redistributing a portion of the cost to the state and federal governments. These receipts were of two kinds: direct payments which the county received as vendor, and indirect payments made by the recipient out of his money payment. In combination of these two kinds of payments, the state paid the counties for 8.3 percent of all hospitalization expenditures. The reimbursement from the state was made up of reimbursement for 17.9 percent of the cost of hospitalization of old age assistance recipients, for 14.7 percent of this cost for recipients of aid to the blind, and 12.9 percent of this cost for recipients of aid to the disabled. Considering unreported as well as reported receipts, the counties were left with 86. 0 percent of hospitalization costs, after refunds, collections and state payments.

17. Third, payments from other counties are an unproductive general type of receipt. These receipts neither lower the cost of assistance to government, nor spread the costs to different levels of government. Each county pays out and receives with relatively little net gain, as evidenced for Wayne county. 

These receipts, understated by as much as a third because of the method of reporting in the study, equalled 6.4 percent of the expenditures. Out of $260,369 receipts from this source, $131,418 were in relation to recipients of old age assistance.

LEGAL SETTLEMENT, RESIDENCE, AND HOSPITALIZATION 

Three facts stand out in relation to the legal settlement and residence of persons who received afflicted adult hospitalization in 1955. Nearly onehalf of the hospitalization expenditures for persons with legal settlement in a Michigan county other than the one arranging hospitalization were in respect to recipients of old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the disabled (Cf. Tables 7 and 8). Persons without legal settlement in Michigan accounted for only 4.6 percent of all persons hospitalized; cost of their hospitalization accounted for only 3.8 percent of expenditures (Cf. Tables 9, 10, 11, and 12). Transient persons not settled in Michigan accounted for only 0.5 percent of the cases hospitalized, and only 0.3 percent of hospitalization expenditures.

Of the $9.8 million expenditures for hospitalization in Michigan, $8.8 million were expended in care of persons possessing legal settlement in the county which arranged for the hospitalization. In this group $2.5 were expended for recipients of old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the disabled. 

In respect to this group, payment of part or all of the cost of hospitalization from combined state and federal funds raises two problems. The hospitalization must be available to all recipients of the categorical programs on an equal basis throughout the state, if a comprehensive vendor payment program is developed under the federal-state assistance programs. County agreements to repay and collections would have direct bearing upon federal-state expenditures. County collections would be limited to the county portion of the expenditure. All collections or refunds in relation to the state portion would be claimed by the state and federal governments. If payments were from a "pooled" fund, the maximum collectable would be an amount equal to the sum of the monthly payments into the fund for the individual case. 

Expenditures for hospitalization of persons possessing legal settlement in another county of the state amounted to $495,801 or 5.1 percent of total expenditures. This amount, and the resulting proportion may be understated because of sampling in Wayne county. The sample provided an estimate of $92,804 in expenditures for this type of case in Wayne county as compared to $122,558 received by Wayne county from other counties in respect to this type of case. These receipts may be in respect to previous year expenditures; current year expenditures of this type may also be underestimated.

18. 

With recipients of old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the disabled removed, the proportion of expenditures for hospitalization of persons with legal settlement in another county is reduced to 2.6 percent. Nearly one-half of the expenditures were for these categorical cases. If all or a major part of the hospitalization expenditures for categorical cases is paid from federal-state sources, the expenditures cannot be charged back to the county of settlement. The federal-state expenditures replace the local expenditures; so that the local county cannot properly collect on them elsewhere.

An often discussed group of hospitalization recipients consists of the persons who do not possess legal settlement in any county in Michigan. It has been suggested that the state should take over these cases as state-at-large cases--that then the counties would be able to bear the remaining cost of hospitalization. Whenever such comments are made, special emphasis is laid upon the burden of the transient case, the person who neither has legal settlement in the community, nor normally resides in the community.. During 

1955, there were 1,170 persons hospitalized who were living in the county which arranged for the hospitalization but who did not possess legal settlement in any county of Michigan. A total of $338,826 was expended by the counties in their hospitalization. Old age assistance, aid to the blind, or aid to the disabled had been received by 111 of these persons, for whom the cost of hospitalization was $50,634. Also reported were 133 persons hospitalized who did not possess legal settlement in the state nor reside in the county. Cost of their hospitalization was $33,333. The 273 persons hospitalized at a cost of $91,965, whose legal settlement is unknown, may also belong in one or other of these two groups. For most of the cases in this class "settlement unknown" merely means that it was not reported by the county.

AGE OF PERSONS RECEIVING AFFLICTED ADULT HOSPITALIZATION 

The Afflicted Adult Hospitalization Act permits the county in its discretion to provide hospitalization for minors if they cannot be provided care under the Crippled and Afflicted Child Act. Out of the 28,376 persons hospitalized during 1955, only 326 or slightly over 1 percent were under 21 years of age. On an expenditure basis the significance of this group was considerably less, accounting for only 0.5 percent of the expenditures (Cf. Tables 

13, 14, and 15). It was more common for minors to receive hospitalization in counties other than Wayne. Excluding Wayne county, the proportion of minors hospitalized accounted for 2.2 percent of all hospitalizations and 

0.9 percent of all expenditures.

More significant is the number of persons and amount of expenditures for persons who were age 65 years and over, but not receiving old age assistance, aid to the blind, or aid to the disabled. This group of persons who either had not been determined eligible for old age assistance, or were ineli-

19. gible for reasons such as length of residence, accounted for 2,916 persons out of the total persons hospitalized, or slightly over 10 percent of all persons hospitalized. The non-assistance persons age 65 years and over received more than average number of days care per case and accounted for more than average expenditures per case. Average expenditures per person for old age assistance recipients were $379, while average expenditures per non-public assistance person age 65 years and over were $489. 

MEDICAL CARE FACILITY 

Estimated expenditures in operation of medical care facilities in 1955 were $6.3 million (Cf. Tables 16, 17, and 18). Expenditures at medical care facilities relative to recipients of old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the disabled amounted to 76.2 percent of the total. Medical care facilities provided proportionately more care for recipients of public assistance than afflicted adult hospitalization did (Cf. Tables 19, 20, and 21). Only 26.8 percent of afflicted adult hospitalization expenditures related to recipients. of old age assistance, but 41.6 percent of expenditures for both medical care facilities and afflicted adult hospitalization were in respect to old age assistance recipients. The proportion of expenditures for aid to the blind recipients provided care under afflicted adult hospitalization and under medical care facilities combined was identical, at 0.6 percent. The proportion of expenditures related to aid to disabled recipients under afflicted adult hospitalization was only 1.5 percent, but under hospitalization and medical care facilities combined was 5.3 percent. Combined afflicted adult hospitalization and medical care facility expenditures chargeable to the special public assistance programs of old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the disabled was $7.6 million, or 47.5 percent of the total expenditures for both programs combined. 

COUNTY RECEIPTS DERIVED FROM STATE PUBLIC ASSISTANCE PAYMENTS 

In respect to total county expenditures of $16,080,282 for afflicted adult hospitalization and operation of county medical care facilities combined, the counties received reimbursement from state public assistance funds to a total amount of $2,533,974 in 1955. Of this total, $2,081,482.00 was paid directly to the counties under contract arrangement with them to provide care to these recipients (Cf. Table 22).

By 1955 a total of 56 counties had signed contractual agreements with the State Department of Social Welfare under which they provided hospitalization or medical care facility care under certain specified conditions for old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the disabled recipients, and in return received the payment for the recipient directly. The amount of the payment was limited to the $80.00 maximum for these recipients in hospitals or convalescent homes. (Since 1955 this maximum has been increased to $90.00 per month for recipients hospitalized or receiving care in convalescent homes.) 

In exchange for those amounts in direct payment, the county obligated it-

20. 

self to make cash payments out of direct relief for continuing non-hospital needs of the recipient, as determined by the county bureaus of social aid. 

This obligation of the county department was limited by the state maximum payment for a person not receiving hospital care, or $70.00 in 1955. These direct relief cash payments to the recipients were reimbursement by the state to at least 50 percent of their total, according to the direct relief formula of state participation. The amount of these cash payments from direct relief to hospitalized recipients is not known, but this amount did in effect reduce the amount of county reimbursements for hospitalization. 

Under federal regulation, and according to current law, and policies of the Michigan Social Welfare Commission, public assistance payments to the counties for hospitalization of old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the disabled recipients, relate only to an equivalent of the number of months during which the person was hospitalized. The month of payment may not be the month of hospitalization, because of the time-lag in the payment process. As long as the recipient remains eligible this delay makes no difference in the amount of the payment received by the county. If, however, the recipient enters the hospital during a terminal illness and dies before the payment to the county can be authorized, no payment is possible under this plan.

Vendor payments were made in respect to an average of 2,509 recipients monthly under the three programs during 1955. Average monthly payment to the county per recipient was $69.13. When the total vendor expenditure is divided by the 1955 average caseload of the three programs combined, the resulting annual average expenditure per recipient of old age assistance, aid to the blind, and aid to the disabled is $26.44. In other words, the amount expended in vendor payments could have been provided' by setting aside $26.44 during the year, or $2.20 per recipient monthly.

If a similar fund had been established in 1955 for afflicted adult hospitalization and county medical care facilities combined, a monthly payment of $7.46 for each old age assistance recipient in the total load, $4.67 for each aid to the blind recipient, and $30.85 for each aid to the disabled recipient would have paid for total expenditures in respect to those recipients hospitalized or provided care in medical facilities. Under afflicted adult hospitalization alone, the required monthly payment would have been $2.93 for each old age assistance case, $2.57 for each aid to the blind case, and $5.33 for each aid to the disabled case. This "pooling" of funds for all recipients to provide for the needs of those hospitalized is a limited form of insurance. 

If established in respect to federal participation in vendor payments, under the new formula, federal contribution would be $3 monthly per case in respect to which "pooled" fund payment was $6 monthly or more. Amount of care provided through such a fund may be limited to any degree, as long as equal extent of care is available to each recipient who needs it.

DEVIATION IN COST OF CARE PER PERSON 

The alternative to an insurance type of payment such as the "pooled" fund, is direct payment of actual cost per person hospitalized. Certain restrictions in federal and state law would make it impossible to provide direct payment of bills for all persons hospitalized. Variations in total expenditures from month to month, so that in one the cost was greater and in another less than $6 multiplied by the total number of assistance recipients, would cause the state to lose federal money. Direct payment of individual bills would introduce the additional problem of extreme variation in individual payments. 

Cost of hospitalization for an individual recipient of old age assistance in counties other than Wayne were as high as $4,000 for ten months of care. 

Only one-fourth of the recipients received care costing $100 or less. Onehalf of the recipients received care costing $230 or more. Average days care provided these "midpoint" recipients was 10.9 days for payments from $230 to $240. One-fourth of the recipients received care for 20.1 days and upward for $460 or more.

In Wayne county the deviation in cost was not as great. The highest cost reported for an old age assistance recipient was $3,000. Averages were higher than in other counties. The lowest quarter of the payments per recipient were $200 or less. One half of the recipients received care costing $360 or more, with an average of 19.0 days care provided to the "midpoint" group of cases for payments from $360 to $370. One-fourth of the recipients received 29.0 days care or more at a cost of $640 or more.

1/ The amounts of expenditures in operation of medical care facilities could not be determined from direct accounts. In many instances the county operated an infirmary and a medical care facility jointly without segregated costs. Therefore, medical care facility costs were estimated by using a days care unit of cost calculated for both medical care facility and infirmary operation. 

This days care unit of cost was then multiplied by the number of days care provided in the medical care facilities to estimate the amount of medical care facility expenditures. This method results in an underestimate, inasmuch as care for medical patients is certain to be more expensive than care for ambulatory patients in a county infirmary. Such expenditures as medical care, special diets, and the cost of laundry services are greater for bed patients than for ambulatory inmates. The total expenditures for medical care facilities were distributed according to the proportion of days care provided to the various types of medical patients. In the special study, data were collected for each patient during 1955 to determine whether he was a recipient of old age assistance, aid to the blind, or aid to the disabled at any time during his period of residence at the county medical facility. All persons not recipients of one of these three types of public assistance were grouped together. 

Days care throughout the year were reported for each patient. The summary of days care obtained by program was used to distribute expenditures on an individual county basis. In state total this results in a slight deviation in the proportion of expenditure to each program from the proportion of days care, but the difference is so slight that no distinction is necessary. 

No separate data for medical care facility. Average cost is derived from County Infirmary, Farm, and Medical Care Facility combined costs.

Separate data is available for medical care facility, but since the average derived from that source was less than that derived from the combined institutional costs, the latter source was chosen.
