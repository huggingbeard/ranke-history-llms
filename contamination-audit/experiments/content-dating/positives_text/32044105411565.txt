INTRODUCTION 

The Rime Machine is the latest in the series of Thinking Machines, and is presented in a form different from the preceding Epigram Machine and Story Plot Machine, which attracted international interest, and which have been in use in Hollywood studios and among professional writers generally, for the mechanical construction of dialogue and plots for motion picture scenarios, plays and stories. (See Page 19).

The Rime Machine is designed to make rimes, by a simple mechanical method never before presented to the public. It does the work of a riming dictionary, with greater facility and convenience. It is intended for use by writers of serious poetry, greeting card verses, newspaper-headline writers, advertising-copy writers, and for those who enjoy cross-word puzzles. It is useful to teachers of English and to school pupils, because it gives at a glance the variations in spelling and pronunciation of word endings. The preferred pronunciation is used throughout. Using the Rime Machine should increase one's vocabulary.

DIRECTIONS 

RIME LIST. This gives all consonants that are the beginnings of words. Riming words of one syllable are found by placing at the right of each letter (or letters) in the Rime List, any of the syllables contained in the Syllable List. 

For this purpose a small block of paper is convenient. 

Write the syllable on a piece of paper and place it opposite letter B. Then go down the list. The riming words will thus appear. Use this method with each syllable in the Syllable 

List that rimes with the last syllable of the word you have chosen. 1

To find perfect rimes for a word of more than one syllable, write the word phonetically on a piece of paper, leaving off the first consonant or consonants. Thus, BEAUTIFUL would be written UTIFUL. Place this at the right of letter B in the Rime List, and go through the List, as with a single syl lable. This produces DUTIFUL. 

Or each syllable may be rimed to a separate word, producing, for example: (to) DUTY COOL; (a) LUTEY MEWL; (the) PRAIRIE GHOUL; (that) SOOTY FOOL; (his) 

SNOOTY SCHOOL. 

Further, any syllable of a word, except the last, may be altered at will, producing BOUNTIFUL, PEACEFUL, POW- ERFUL, TUNEFUL, etc. 

SYLLABLE LIST. Only the syllables that are printed in capital letters should be used with the Rime List. Syllables in small type, preceded by a hyphen, are endings of words of more than one syllable. Foreign words, and a few 

English words of exceptional pronunciation, are given in small type.

The syllables are grouped, according to perfect rime, singular and plural, and present and past tense. Under ADE, for example, perfectly riming words with different spelling are indicated; such as PAID, SPRAYED, WEIGHED, dissuade and SUEDE. Under the syllable AD, however, the syllables ADE and AID indicate the riming words BADE 

and PLAID.

And under AB, the reference to OB serves to find SWAB and SQUAB. 

An asterisk (*) means refer to the syllable indicated.

Syllables that do not vary either in spelling or pronunciation are omitted from the Syllable List; such as ABE, ADGE, ESH, EMPT, etc. These may be used, however, in the same manner as the printed syllables. 2
