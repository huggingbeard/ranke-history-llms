"ETHICS" CHARGES 

A balanced view of Judge Haynsworth's attitude toward ethical problems cannot come exclusively from a reiteration and refutation of the charges against him. His whole record of conduct during his 12 years as a circuit judge must be examined. 

At the time he was appointed to the Federal bench in 1957, Judge Haynsworth resigned as an officer and director of a number of publicly held corporations. He did so because the positions were public knowledge, and he felt a judge should not be held out to the public in such an exposed position. His own honesty was unquestioned, but he was concerned about the appearance of things. His action in resigning was taken at a time when a substantial number of Federal judges were serving on the boards of corporations. It was not until 6 years later that the Judicial Conference passed a resolution objecting to service on boards of directors.

Another action taken by Judge Haynsworth is useful in illuminating his view of proper conduct. He sold his stockholdings in Carolina 

Vend-A-Matic Corp. in 1964 although he was under no compulsion to do so. The issue of his ownership of the stock had been raised by the Textile Workers Union of America, and he has been exonerated from any wrongdoing by Chief Judge Sobeloff and the Attorney

General. Nonetheless, he took steps to dispose of the stock because of his sensitivity to the position of the bench. 

His reputation, as mentioned previously, was of the highest. He was held in esteem by those who knew him and worked with him as a man of honor and integrity.

Opponents of Judge Haynsworth's confirmation have made a series of attacks variously couched in terms of "ethics," "appearances," and "appearance of impropriety." These objections may be summarized as follows: 

(a) Judge Haynsworth should have disqualified himself from sitting in five cases which he heard as a judge of the Court of 

Appeals for the Fourth Circuit; 

(b) Judge Haynsworth should have sold stock which he owned in J. P. Stevens & Co.;

(c) Judge Haynsworth was untruthful when testifying before the Tydings subcommittee of this committee on June 2, 1969. 

These objections will be discussed in order. 

A. Disqualification for interest 

Any justice or judge of the United States shall disqualify himself in any case in which he has a substantial interest, has been of counsel, is or has been a material witness, or is so related to or connected with any party or his attorney as to render it improper, in his opinion, for him to sit on the trial, appeal, or other proceeding therein. 

Canon 29, American Bar Association Canons of Judicial Ethics, provides in pertinent part as follows:

A judge should abstain from performing or taking part in any judicial act in which his personal interests are involved. 

Evaluation of the opponents' attacks requires the interpretation and application of these provisions to the facts involved in the various cases where disqualification is said to have been required. (1) Darlington Manufacturing Company v. NLRB, 325 F. 2d 682, was orally argued before the Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit on June 13, 1963, and was decided by that court on November 15, 1963. Deering-Milliken, Inc., was a party to that action, in which the court of appeals ruled by a divided vote against the contentions of the National Labor Relations Board and the Textile Workers 

Union of America. Deering-Milliken owned or controlled approximately 27 textile plants in the southeastern part of the United States. 

It granted space in these various plants to vending machine companies on the basis of competitive bidding for such rights. Carolina Vend- 

A-Matic Co. was one such company; during 1963 Carolina Vend-A- 

Matic obtained slightly more than 3 percent of its gross sales from vending machines located in three plants in the Deering-Milliken combine. Approximately 700 out of 19,000 Deering-Milliken employees were employed in these plants. 

In 1950, seven years before he was appointed to the court of appeals, Judge Haynsworth and several of his law partners were original incorporators of Carolina Vend-A-Matic. Prior to his appointment to the court of appeals in 1957, the judge was both a director and one of the vice presidents of the company. At the time he took the bench, he orally resigned as vice president, but continued to serve as a director until October 1963. At that time, he resigned his directorship in 

Carolina Vend-A-Matic, as well as another directorship in a nonpublic corporation which he had retained, in compliance with a resolution of the U.S. Judicial Conference adopted the previous month. During his tenure on the court of appeals, Judge Haynsworth served for several years as an uncompensated trustee of Carolina Vend-A-Matic's pension and profitsharing fund, and his wife served for 2 years as secretary of the corporation handling routine office matters. 

Judge Haynsworth took no active part in the day-to-day conduct of the company's business after his appointment to the circuit court of appeals. He played no part at all in obtaining locations for the company's vending machines and was largely unfamilar with their

location. As a director, he did attend luncheon meetings of the board, and he did assist in obtaining some financing from a local bank. 

During 1963, Judge Haynsworth owned one-seventh of the stock of Carolina Vend-A-Matic, which he sold the following year because the criticism by the Textile Workers Union of his participation in the Darlington case raised the possibility that his interest would become general knowledge. During 1963, Carolina Vend-A-Matic had competed for three location sites in the Deering-Milliken plants, but had obtained only one. None of the Deering-Milliken officials who awarded vending machine rights knew that Judge Haynsworth was associated with Carolina Vend-A-Matic.

On the basis of these facts, it is charged that Judge Haynsworth should have disqualified himself in Darlington because he had an "interest," within the language of either the canons or the statute, in Deering-Milliken.

This contention is contrary to all of the decided cases involving judicial disqualification, and was squarely rejected by two professionally qualified, objective witnesses who testified before the committee.

Efforts to require disqualification for interest in cases where a judge holds stock, not in a party litigant itself, but in a corporation which has some sort of dealings with the party litigant, have been uniformly rebuffed by the courts. At least two cases have passed on the question arising when a judge owns stock in a creditor of one of the parties to the litigation before him, and his disqualification is sought on the theory that the creditor necessarily has a pecuniary interest in seeing its debtor prevail in a lawsuit with a third party. 

Both of these cases have unqualifiedly rejected the argument for disqualification (Webb v. Town of Eutaw, 63 So. 687 (Ala. 1913); 

In re Farber, 260 Mich. 652, 245 N.W. 793 (1932)). Similarly, where a judge owned stock in a title insurance company which had insured the title of many trust beneficiaries whose interests would fail in the event that the plaintiff in litigation before the judge was successful, he was held not to be disqualified from deciding the litigation (Central 

Savings Bank of Oakland v. Lake, 257 Pac. 521 (Calif. 1927)).

The State cases are made more clearly relevant and helpful in Federal litigation because of the well established rule that in the Federal system "there is as much obligation upon a judge not to recuse himself when there is no occasion as there is for him to do so when there is." In re Union Leader Corporation, 292 F. 2d 381, 391 (1st Cir. 1961), quoted with approval in Wolfson v. Palmieri, 396 F. 

2d 121 (2d Cir. 1968). The applicability of this rule was emphasized by Judge Lawrence E. Walsh, chairman of the American Bar Association Committee on Judicial Selection, in the following testimony before the committee:

We believe that there was no conflict of interest in the Darlington case which would have barred Judge Haynsworth from sitting and we also concluded that it was his duty to sit.

Likewise John P. Frank, a leading authority on the subject of judicial disqualification, testified:

It follows that under the standard Federal rule Judge Haynsworth had no alternative whatsoever. He was bound by

the principle of the cases. It is the judge's duty to refuse to sit when he is disqualified,but it is equally his duty to sit when there is no valid reason not to * ** I do think that it is perfectly clear under the authority that there was virtually no choice whatsoever for Judge Haynsworth except to participate in that case and to do his job as well as he could. 

Dicta in a recent decision of the Supreme Court of the United 

States, Commonwealth Coatings Corp. v. Continental Casualty Co., 

393 U.S. 145 (1968), has been cited by opponents as requiring a different result. However, the arbitrator who was held presumptively disqualified in that case had conducted a large business in which he served as an engineering consultant; one of his regular customers was a party to the arbitration, and the relationship actually included the rendering of services on the very project involved in the arbitration. Thus, in Commonwealth Coatings, the arbitrator was the controlling shareholder in a consulting business and had dealt directly with one of the parties to the dispute, even to the point of rendering service on a project involved in the dispute; in Darlington, Judge 

Haynsworth, while owning a one-seventh interest in Carolina Vend- A-Matic, had not himself dealt personally with any of the parties to the litigation, and most assuredly had not rendered any service to anyone of them in connection with the litigation in question. The fact that the Court in Commonwealth Coatings was concerned primarily about the disqualification for personal relations, rather than for financial interest, is evident from the fact that the one canon of judicial ethics cited in the opinion is canon 33, and not canon 29. Canon 33 

provides as follows: 

It is not necessary to the proper performance of judicial duty that a judge should live in retirement or seclusion; it is desirable that, so far as reasonable attention to the completion of his work will permit, he continue to mingle in social intercourse, and that he should not discontinue his interest in or appearance at meetings of members of the bar. He should, however, in pending or prospective litigation before him be particularly careful to avoid such action as may reasonably tend to awaken the suspicion that his social or business relations or friendships, constitute an element in influencing his judicial conduct.

There is not the remotest basis for suggesting that Judge Hayns worth at any time violated this canon. Nor can it fairly be argued that either the holding or the dicta in Commonwealth_Coatings required Judge Haynsworth to disqualify himself in the Darlington case. 

The suggestion has been made that Judge Haynsworth should have disposed of his stock in Carolina Vend-A-Matic at the time he ascended the bench, because it was "apt to be involved in litigation" as that term is used in canon 26, proscribing personal investments by a judge "in enterprises which are apt to be involved in litigation in the court." 

The interpretation of this canon is discussed hereafter; it is a sufficient response to the charge with respect to Carolina Vend-A-Matic that the latter corporation not only was not "apt" to be involved in litigation in the fourth circuit, but it was not at any time in fact involved in such litigation.

8 There has also been at least implied criticism of Judge Haynsworth for having made a substantial profit out of his investment in Carolina Vend-A-Matic. It has been suggested that the continued rise in sales of Carolina Vend-A-Matic after he ascended the bench is somehow grounds for thinking that the prestige of his office was used to promote the company's business. Not a scintilla of competent evidence supporting this conclusion was offered at the hearings before the committee, and abundant evidence was offered to refute the suggestion. Alex 

Kiriakides, president of one of Vend-A-Matic's principal competitors, wrote a letter to Chairman Eastland containing the following paragraph:

Because of the growing recognition that vending services provide the most pleasant and most efficient means of providing food and refreshment for industrial employees, the industry throughout the United States has experienced phenomenal growth. In the Southeast general industrial expansion has made the growth of all vending companies even more spectacular. The experience of Carolina Vend-A-Matic was not in the least unique to it. My own business experienced comparable growth. A vending business in Spartanburg, just 30 miles to the west, had similar experiences. It is simply the case of having a service to offer at a time of rapidly rising demands for that service.

Kiriakides also stated, "This business was not developed on the basis of anyone using anyone's influence on anybody. I know that

Judge Haynsworth's name was never used in any attempt to influence anybody."

Judges are entitled to regulate their conduct by the rules applicable at the time the conduct occurs. At the time Judge Haynsworth ascended the bench, there was no prohibition whatsoever against a judge being either an officer or a director of a corporation organized for profit, so long as he adhered to the applicable statute and canons governing his conduct. When, in 1963, the Judicial Conference recommended that offices and directorships in even closely held corporations no longer be held by Federal judges, Judge Haynsworth quickly and willingly complied with this recommendation. Following charges by the union that he ought not to have sat in the Darlington case-charges which Judge Sobeloff found, after investigation, to be "completely unfounded"-Judge Haynsworth nonetheless took necessary steps to sell his stock in Carolina Vend-A-Matic so that the same unfounded suspicions would not again arise.

The committee concludes that Judge Haynsworth's sitting in the Darlington case was not merely proper, but was required by law, and that his sitting in cases involving other corporations which had granted vending machine sites to Carolina Vend-A-Matic was likewise both proper and required. 

2. Parent-subsidiary cases

Among the several thousand cases which Judge Hanysworth heard as a judge of the Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit during his 12-year tenure on that court were three in which he owned a small amount of stock in a corporation which in turn owned a controlling interest in one of the litigants before him.

Farrow v. Grace Lines, Inc., 381 F. 2d 380 (1967), involved a seaman's claim that his wrist was injured and this caused him to miss 60 hours of overtime work for which he should have been paid at the overtime rate. In his complaint, he prayed for judgment in the amount of $30,000. After trial, a jury returned a verdict in his favor in the amount of $50. He appealed, claiming that the jury should have awarded him a larger amount. The Court of Appeals for the Fourth 

Cicruit affirmed in a brief two-sentence per curiam opinion. 

At the time he sat and heard this case, Judge Haynsworth owned 

300 shares of stock in W. R. Grace & Co., which in turn owned 53 subsidiaries, one of which was Grace Lines, Inc., the party to the above-described appeal. The judge's interest in Grace Lines, Inc., was a minute one, and his interest in this particular litigation involving 

Grace Lines, Inc., was for all practical purposes nonexistent. Grace

Lines, Inc., contributed less than 7 percent of the parent company's 

1967 gross revenue of $1,576 million; the judge's 300 shares of stock in the parent company compared to 18,252,335 shares of common stock outstanding. Assuming that the plaintiff's entire claim of $30,000 against Grace Lines, Inc., had been awarded, and that the common stockholders of the parent corporation were solely liable for this amount, such a judgment would have reduced the value of Judge Haynsworth's 300 shares of stock by a total of 48 cents. 

Donohue v. Maryland Casualty Co. 363 F.2d 442, was argued in 1966 before a panel of the Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit which included Judge Haynsworth. The case was a civil action raising a question as to the remedy available to injured employees. 

Maryland Casualty Co. v. Baldwin, 357 F.2d 338 (1966), was a declaratory judgment action brought by Maryland Casualty to determine its liability under a policy issued to a garage keeper. The district judge held that Maryland Casualty would be liable under the policy for payment of any damage claims. Maryland Casualty appealed but 

Judges Haynsworth, Bell, and Hutcheson affirmed the district court's judgment against Maryland Casualty in a brief per curiam opinion. 

Judge Haynsworth had no direct interest in any of the litigants of these two cases, but he did own 67 shares of the common stock and 200 shares of the preferred stock in American General Insurance Co., a corporation which controlled Maryland Casualty Co. and at least 

11 other subsidiaries. American General had total consolidated assets of $888,857,336, total gross income of $356,602,892, and a consolidated net profit of $26,672,196. Judge Haynsworth's interest in this corporation amounted to .0059 percent of the 3,276,558 outstanding shares of preferred stock, and to .0015 percent of the 4%½ million shares of common stock. While the effect of a judgment involving coverage cannot be mathematically assessed, it is inconceivable that a decision one way or the other could have had any measurable effect on the value of 

Judge Haynsworth's holding in American General Insurance Co.

Opponents of confirmation claim that Judge Haynsworth was barred by the applicable statute and by canon 29 from hearing and deciding these cases. The relevant language of the statute is "substantial interest" in a case, while that of the canon is "personal interest" in a case.

Two decisions have construed the statute in connection with stockownership by a judge in a party litigant and have held that minimal stockholding even in a party before the court is not grounds for 

Ex. Rept. 91-12- -2

disqualification under 28 U.S.C. 455. Kinnear-Weed Corporation v. 

Humble Oil and Refining Company, 403 F. 2d 437 (5th Cir. 1968); 

Lampert v. Hollis Music, Inc., 105 F. Supp. 3 (1952). The language of the statute, speaking not of "substantial interest" in a litigant, but instead of substantial interest in a case, rather clearly requires that the impact of a possible decision on the litigants, as well as the extent of the judge's interest in a litigant, be taken into consideration. By no conceivable argument, in light of the facts involved in Farrow, Donohue, and Baldwin, can it be said that Judge Haynsworth had a “substantial interest" in the outcome of any of these cases. That being so, he was not disqualified under 28 U.S.C. 455.

Formal opinion No. 170 of the American Bar Association Committee on Ethics states that a judge who is a stockholder in a corporation which is a party to litigation pending in his court should not perform any act in relation to such litigation involving the exercise of judicial discretion. Since the decision of a case clearly involves the exercise of judicial discretion this canon may be taken to bar participation by a judge who himself has stock in a litigant. It does not however deal with the question involved in Farrow, Donohue, and Baldwin where the stockholding of the judge was not in the litigant itself but in a parent corporation. Thus the Supreme Court of California construing language similar to that of canon 29 in a disqualification statute of that State has held that ownership of stock in a parent corporation did not require disqualification in litigation involving a subsidiary. 

Central Pacific Railroad Company v. Superior Court 296 Pac. 883. 

This case is a valuable precedent in interpreting similar language in canon 29.

The committee believes, however, that neither of these precedentsformal opinion No. 170 and Central Pacific-can be read as laying down hard and fast opposing rules, so that disqualification is automatic in the case of stockownership in a party litigant, but is never required in the case of stockownership in a corporation which in turn controls the party litigant. The canons themselves in their preamble contain the following admonition, which is certainly not at odds with any other rule of construction: 

The association accordingly adopts the following canons, the spirit of which it suggests as a proper guide and reminder for judges, and as indicating what the people have a right to expect from them.

It may be that formal opinion No. 170 creates a presumption of disqualification in the case of stockownership in a party litigant, while Central Pacific creates a presumption against disqualification for stockownership in a parent corporation, unless additional circumstances are present which in fact demonstrate an actual "interest" in the litigation other than that which would arise from the holding of a small amount of stock in the parent corporation. But tested either by the language of the canon itself, or by the application of these precedents to the present fact situation, it is clear in the light of the judge's miniscule interest in the litigation involved in Farrow, Donohue, and Baldwin, his sitting in these cases did not violate canon 29.

It may be noted that should the canons be otherwise construed, so as to require disqualification in cases such as these, there would be presented a serious question of conflict between the canons, so con-

strued, and the Federal statute, which clearly does not require disqualification. 

The Federal rule requiring judges to sit when not disqualified under the statute suggests rather strongly that the statutory rule does not simply set a minimum standard, but instead sets an exclusive standard which determines both when a judge is disqualified, and when he must sit. The committee believes that the construction of the canons must be consistent with the Federal statute and the court interpretations of it. 

3. The Brunswick Case 

On November 10, 1967, a panel of the fourth circuit consisting of Judges Haynsworth, Winter, and Jones heard oral argument in 

Brunswick Corporation v. Long, 392 F. 2d 337. Immediately following oral argument, the three judges met in conference to decide the case. 

They unanimously voted to affirm the judgment of the district court in favor of Brunswick, and the opinion was assigned by the panel to 

Judge Winter for preparation.

On December 20, 1967, Arthur C. McCall, a stockbroker in Greenville, S.C., who handled Judge Haynsworth's account, placed an order for the purchase of 1,000 shares of Brunswick Corp. stock for the account of Judge Haynsworth at $16 per share. Judge Haynsworth was not aware at the time that the opinion and judgment in Brunswick had not been filed. Judge Haynsworth was the 45th account for whom 

McCall had purchased Brunswick stock over a period of 2 years, since in the latter's opinion it would be a good long-term investment for his customers. The order was executed on the afternoon of December 26, 1967.

Meanwhile, Judge Winter had prepared a draft opinion in the case and circulated it to the remaining judges on December 27. Judge 

Haynsworth noted his concurrence on January 3, 1968, and the written opinion was released by the clerk of the Court of Appeals for the 

Fourth Circuit in Richmond on February 2, 1968. Petitions for rehear ing, and a petition for certiorari to the Supreme Court of the United States, were subsequently denied. 

The issue of disqualification under the Federal statute is difficult to frame because the stock was not owned at the time the case was heard and decided. Substantively, it is clear from Judge Winter's testimony, as well as that of Judge Haynsworth, that the case was in fact decided on November 10. However, the judge's pecuniary interest in the case would by any interpretation of the Federal statute appear to fall far short of being "substantial."

The issues in Brunswick Corporation v. Long involved competing claims to the repossession of used bowling alley equipment. Had the plaintiff been awarded priority for the full amount of his claim, and had the sale of the security been sufficient to liquidate the claim he would have received $90,000. Assessing this figure against the judge's ownership of 1,000 out of almost 1812 million shares of stock outstanding the total pecuniary effect of such a decision on Judge Haynsworth's interest in Brunswick would have been less than $5. 

The committee is of the opinion that because the purchase was inadvertent and because the issues in the case had been decided prior to the purchase, neither the statute nor the canons were violated. 

Judge Haynsworth stood to make no financial gain by reason of the stock purchase and could not have been influenced in his judicial

actions. While a change in the court's opinion was technically possible prior to the issuance of the written opinion, as a practical matter the chances for such a reconsideration were nil. 

Under these circumstances and in view of the nature of the case Judge Haynsworth reasonably concluded he should not disqualify himself from the case. This would have required assigning a new panel to hear the case, rescheduling oral argument, deciding the case anew and rewriting the draft opinion. As chief judge, charged with the administration of the court, Judge Haynsworth recognized this procedure would have been unduly burdensome in this case where the merits were not in doubt. Judge Winter, author of the opinion, testified that Judge Haynsworth was not legally disqualified and that he did not violate canon 26 or canon 29.

It scarcely needs to be added that to regard an inadvertent error such as the purchase of the stock as a basis for refusing to confirm Judge Haynsworth would be to demand a degree of perfection seldom, if ever, achieved by those in either public or private life. This committee requires a nominee to be honest, honorable, and sensitive to ethical considerations. It does not require him to be infallible. 

B. Retention of stock in J. P. Stevens & Co., Inc. 

At the time he ascended the bench, Judge Haynsworth owned 550 shares of stock in J. P. Stevens & Co., Inc. It has been suggested that, because Stevens had been involved in litigation before the fourth circuit on several occasions, the retention by the judge of this stock violated canon 26, American Bar Association Canons of Judicial 

Ethics, which provides in pertinent part as follows:

A judge should abstain from making personal investments in enterprises which are apt to be involved in litigation in the court; and, after his accession to the bench, he should not retain such investments previously made, longer than a period sufficient to enable him to dispose of them without 

Even assuming that J. P. Stevens & Co. is an enterprise "apt to be involved in litigation in the court," it is clear that the canon was not violated. The evident purpose of this canon is to avoid the necessity of frequent disqualification on the part of a sitting judge by reason of the judge's interest in pending litigation. Another purpose of the canon may be to avoid the necessity of a judge personally testifying, or being the subject of testimony, in pending litigation. 

Neither of these purposes would have been served by Judge Haynsworth selling his stock in J. P. Stevens & Co., Inc. He testified before this committee that while he never had anything to do with the company's labor relations, the company was a close client of his while he was in private practice. Because of this relationship, he stated he would have disqualified himself in any case in which the company was involved, whether or not he had retained his stock in the company. In fact, Judge Haynsworth has never sat in a case involving Stevens. 

Since sale of his stock by Judge Haynsworth would not have permitted him to sit in cases involving Stevens, and since no Stevens litigation involved Judge Haynsworth personally, the committee finds that canon 26 was not violated by Judge Haynsworth's retention of stock in the company.

C. June 2 testimony

It has been charged that Judge Haynsworth "demonstrated a lack of candor" when he testified before the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Improvements in Judicial Machinery in early June of this year. 

The committee believes that this criticism is unjustified. 

In the wake of the controversy surrounding the resignation of Justice Fortas last spring, several bills were introduced in the Senate requiring various forms of financial disclosure by Federal judges. At the request of the subcommittee, Judge Haynsworth voluntarily appeared for the purpose of expressing his opinion on various provisions of a pending bill. The date of his appearance before the subcommittee was June 2, 1969, some two and a half months before the President nominated him for the Supreme Court. 

During the course of the questioning, the following exchange took place:

Senator TYDINGS. What about the disclosure of the name and address of each foundation, eleemosynary institution and each business or professional corporation, firm or enterprise in which the judge was an officer, director, proprietor or partner during the preceding year? 

Judge HAYNSWORTH. I certainly would have no objection to such a thing as that. I don't believe that most judges would. I think there is very little remnant of that. 

Of course when I went on the bench I resigned from all such business associations I had, directorships and things of that sort. The only one I retained is the trusteeship of this small foundation which I mentioned in my main statement, and I think that perhaps the best rule for a judge to go by now is to stop doing even that much. (Transcript, p. 95-96.) 

Judge Haynsworth had in fact resigned from seven corporate directorships at the time he ascended the bench, but he had retained until 1963 directorships in two smaller corporations-the Carolina Vend-A-Matic Co., and a small family corporation known as Main- 

Oak. In order to be absolutely accurate, Judge Haynsworth should have testified as follows: 

Of course, when I went on the bench I resigned from seven business associations that I had, directorships and things of that sort, and then in 1963 I resigned from two others. The only one I still retain is the trusteeship of this small foundation which I mentioned in my main statement, and I think the best rule for a judge to go by now is to stop doing even that much.

The committee is convinced by several factors that this inaccuracy was completely inadvertent. If Judge Haynsworth had lumped together his 1957 resignation with his 1963 resignation in response to a question directed to his connection with Carolina Vend-A-Matic during the recent hearings on his confirmation when his relationship with Carolina Vend-A-Matic was being scrutinized by this committee, he would be subject to the most severe censure. The wholly different context of the June 2 hearing becomes evident upon inquiry. The subcommittee's inquiry was in no way directed to the individual business affairs of Judge Haynsworth or any other judge. The subcommittee was only considering bills regulating the future conduct of

judges. Senator Tydings' question did not call for Judge Haynsworth to comment on his own directorships; it sought only his opinion on a provision in the bill. This opinion was given in the first part of the answer and was followed by the personal reminiscence in question. 

There was absolutely no motive for Judge Haynsworth to attempt to imply that he had resigned from the two directorships in 1957 instead of 1963. During the previous investigation by Chief Judge 

Sobeloff in 1963, Judge Haynsworth had freely disclosed the circumstances of his holding a corporate office in Carolina Vend-A- Matic. Since this information was made known to his fellow judges, the litigants in Darlington and to Attorney General Kennedy in 1963, there would be no reason to conceal any part of it in 1969. 

There was likewise no reason at all for the judge to conceal the fact of his retention until 1963 of a directorship in Main-Oak, another small closely held corporation which at no time has been subjected to any sort of criticism.

The committee finds not the slightest reason to believe that Judge Haynsworth deliberately misrepresented when he freely volunteered information which was not even asked for by the committee. The fact that Judge Haynsworth inadvertently failed to correctly itemize the dates of his resignations from business corporations, some of which had taken place 12 years earlier and some of which had taken place 6 years earlier, and none of which would have in any way been affected by the pending legislation, cannot fairly be said to evidence any lack of candor on his part. 

D. Conclusion 

For the foregoing reasons, the committee has concluded that the so-called "ethics" attacks against Judge Haynsworth have not been substantiated, and that nothing in his judicial conduct during the 12 years he has sat as a judge in the Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit would in any way justify recommending against his confirmation. Having dealt with the specific charges, however, the committee believes that two more general observations are appropriate. 

On more than one occasion those who oppose Judge Haynsworth's confirmation have couched their arguments in terms of canon 4 which requires that "a judge's official conduct should be free from impro priety and the appearance of impropriety ***" The formal opinions which have been issued by the American Bar Association Committee on Ethics indicate the properly broad scope of this canon in its application to numerous situations not directly dealt with by other, more specific canons.

The position of some opponents appears to be that if facts are adduced which are thought to show a violation of a canon, but full development of the facts requires the conclusion that no such violation has occurred there nonetheless may be an "appearance of impropriety" which violates canon 4. No such position has ever been taken by the 

American Bar Association Committee on Ethics, nor is such an interpretation warranted by the language of the canon itself. Particularly in the case of disqualification, where as previously noted a Federal judge is obligated to sit where not disqualified by statute, any notion that canon 4 would require him to bend over backwards and refrain from sitting even though not disqualified is contrary to the rule laid down in the cases previously discussed.

This is not to say, however, that at a hearing on confirmation a nominee for high judicial office does not have the obligation to fully cooperate with the Judiciary Committee in the development of any facts the committee believes to be relevant to its inquiry. Indeed, an "appearance of impropriety" with respect to which a nominee for high judicial office offered no explanation, and refused to cooperate in development of the facts necessary to reach a conclusion, might well be grounds for recommending against confirmation. No such situation is presented here. Judge Haynsworth has cooperated fully and painstakingly with the committee and has furnished personal records on a scale and in detail such as have never been required of any prior nominee. 

Finally, it has been suggested during the debates over Judge Haynsworth's confirmation that even though each of the various "ethical" attacks are shown to be insubstantiated, the very fact that they were made and elaborately argued is in itself a reason for refusing confirmation. Such a position is contrary both to traditional notions of fair play and to the lessons of history. 

To accede to such a view would be to place a nominee's fate, not in the hands of those charged by the Constitution with advising and consenting to his nomination, but in the hands of his accusers. The most basic concept of fairness demands vindication where a charge is found to be unsubstantiated, just as surely as it demands condemnation where the objection is found to have merit. The making of such a determination is placed in the hands first of this committee, and then of the Senate as a whole. Neither the committee nor the Senate may wash its hands of so vital an obligation by deciding that the mere making of attacks against a nominee casts a sufficient cloud upon his reputation to warrant rejection of his nomination. 

In taking this position, the committee adheres to one of its own precedents adopted 43 years ago at the time of the nomination of 

Justice Louis D. Brandeis as an Associate Justice of the Supreme 

Court. The minority report of the Subcommittee of the Judiciary

Committee to which that nomination was submitted contained this language:

A man to be appointed to the exalted and responsible position of Justice of the Supreme Court should be free from suspicion and above reproach. Whether suspicion rests upon him unjustly or not, his confirmation would be a mistake.

The majority of the subcommittee, and the majority of this committee, refused to follow this approach, and recommended confirmation of Justice Brandeis. The Senate confirmed him, and he survived the charges made during the course of debates on his confirmation to become an outstandingly able and respected Associate Justice of the Supreme Court. Having concluded, as we do, that the "ethical" attacks against Judge Haynsworth are not substantiated, the committee has no hesitation in deciding that the mere making of the attacks offers no reason whatever to withhold a favorable recommendation on the nomination.

A. Introduction JUDICIAL PHILOSOPHY 

None of us doubt that the President may properly consider, in determining whom he shall nominate to be a Justice of the Supreme

Court of the United States, the judicial philosophy of the proposed candidate. But differing views have been expressed about the extent to which the Judiciary Committee, and the Senate as a whole, may properly consider the judicial philosophy of a man nominated by the President. Reasoned arguments have been made supporting both the view that the committee and the Senate should consider only the professional qualifications of the nominee, and the contrary view that Senators may properly inquire into, and consider, his judicial philosophy. While the committee does not attempt to reconcile these differences, the following observations are appropriate:

Clearly, if each Senator sets up for himself a requirement that the nominee decide each case just as the Senator would if he were sitting as a judge, no vacancy on the Court would ever be filled. There are only nine Justices, among whose number representation has traditionally been found for differing geographic, professional, political, and philosophical backgrounds. In considering nominees for the highest Court, advocates of one particular judicial philosophy cannot be given an absolute veto power as to each nominee. 

Judge Haynsworth's views on labor and civil rights matters, discussed extensively during the committee's hearings, are dealt with in subsequent sections of this report. This separate treatment should not be permitted to obscure the fact that Judge Haynsworth has written opinions in literally hundreds of cases, all of which must be examined if his judicial record is to be accurately evaluated. Such an evaluation was made by Prof. Charles Wright who stated that "the most striking impression one gets from his writing is of a disciplined attempt to apply the law as he understands it, rather than yield to his own policy preferences."

Judge Haynsworth's evenhandedness, craftsmanship, and scholarship are evident, for example, in a group of excellent opinions he has written in the field of criminal law. In Rowe v. Peyton, 383 F. 2d 709 (1967), Judge Haynsworth broke new ground by allowing a convicted defendant to seek review, through habeas corpus, of a consecutive sentence he was not yet serving. In a scholarly opinion, he anticipated that the Supreme Court would no longer adhere to its earlier decision to the contrary, an anticipation which proved correct when his opinion was affirmed by the Supreme Court, 391 U.S. 54 (1968). In Hayden v. 

Warden, 363 F.2d 647, 657 (1966), his separate opinion urged abandonment of the doctrine that "mere evidence" may not be the object of a lawful search, noting that the doctrine unnecessarily restricted police in their use of scientific detection methods. Judge Haynsworth's position was accepted by the Supreme Court when it reversed the decision, 387 U.S. 294 (1967). Writing for the court in United States v. Chandler, 393 F. 2d 920 (1968), Judge Haynsworth concluded that old standards governing the insanity defense should be abandoned in favor of the criminal responsibility rules formulated by the American Law Institute. His opinion, characterized by Senator Tydings during the committee's hearings as "brilliant," makes a most significant contribution on a question of growing concern. 

We believe that Judge Haynsworth's confirmation will bring to the Supreme Court not a judge who will be known primarily as a "liberal" or as a "conservative", but rather one who will be known for his balanced judgement and his care in writing opinions-a jurist who will combine clarity of expression with a reticence to speculate beyond the

issues presented for decision. Judge Haynsworth has given ample evidence of such disposition and ability in his 12 years as a judge of the Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit, and there is every reason to believe that he will continue to display these attributes during his career as an Associate Justice of the Supreme Court.

C. Labor decisions

During the course of the hearings on the confirmation of Judge Haynsworth, the committee heard a number of representatives from organized labor, who uniformly opposed the confirmation of Judge Haynsworth.

The committee believes that the attack upon Judge Haynsworth's labor record is unjustified.

While it was arguably proper for George Meany, AFL-CIO president, to state frankly to the committee that he "would not approve of a decision against labor," this is not a position which the committee can in good conscience adopt. The fact that organized labor does not receive everything it demands from a judge in its hard-fought legal contests with management does not mean that the judge is antilabor, any more than management's dissatisfaction with a judge's decision would mean that he is antibusiness. This committee believes that 

Judge Haynsworth has satisfied the appropriate criterion: the evenhanded treatment of litigants according to law. 

Organized labor's evaluation of Judge Haynsworth's labor views relied upon several labor decisions which were reversed by the Supreme Court and others in which there was a division of opinion in the fourth circuit. The committee believes that this appraisal fails to present a complete and accurate analysis of Judge Haynsworth's labor record. 

Indeed, AFL-CIO counsel Thomas Harris candidly admitted to the committee that they had not even attempted to look at all of Judge Haynsworth's labor decisions. This omission cannot be justified in any serious attempt to reach an objective appraisal of Judge Haysnworth's labor views. His views cannot be evaluated merely by observing that several cases have outcomes unfavorable to labor. The committee believes that an analysis of all of his decisions indicates no bias against labor. In making their evaluation of Judge Haynsworth, organized labor totally ignored prolabor opinions written by Judge Haynsworth and many prolabor cases in which he participated.

INDIVIDUAL VIEWS OF MR. SCOTT 

I have consistently held throughout my Senate service that any person nominated by the President of the United States has the right to have his or her qualifications judged not alone in the closed sessions of a committee but by the entire membership of the Senate. I continue to hold this belief and adhere to my conviction that, after full debate, the Senate must reach its own decision. 

Moreover, a failure to report a Presidential nominee would unjustly deprive the full Senate, and all Senators, of the exercise of the constitutional prerogative to advise and consent under article II, section 2, of our Constitution.

Therefore, I concur in the decision reached by the majority of the committee to report the nomination of Judge Clement F. Haynsworth to be a Justice of the Supreme Court of the United States. (23)

INDIVIDUAL VIEWS OF MESSRS. BAYH, BURDICK, HART, KENNEDY AND TYDINGS 

No duty we perform as U.S. Senators is more awesome or important than our constitutional responsibility to provide "advice and consent" for the President's nominations to the Supreme Court. In the exercise of this function, we find ourselves at one of the few points where all three branches of our tripartite system meet. And the way we play our role determines whether the balance of powers among these branches is to remain in equilibrium. 

To vote for a nominee, a Senator need not conclude that he will agree with the nominee's decisions on the High Court. But each Senator must be secure in his own conscience that he is giving his fellow citizens and their progeny a Justice who will promote the ideals of the Constitution, contribute to the harmony and peace of the Nation, and provide the Court with insights and sensitivities that will make the whole Court even greater than its parts. That standard is a difficult one to meet, and it is not necessarily a personal reflection on a nominee's accomplishments or integrity, if we find that he fails to meet this standard.

For various reasons, we, as lawyers and as Senators, have concluded that the present nominee does not meet this test. Some of us see flaws in his extrajudicial activities. Some of us have doubts about his record on the appellate bench. And some of us are concerned about his presentations before committees of Congress. But we all share the feeling that our review of the nominee's record leads us to advise the President to withdraw it, and would require us to deny it our consent.

INDIVIDUAL VIEWS OF MR. BAYH 

It is a fundamental principle that in order to govern effectively; a government must have the confidence of the people. That confidence is quickly eroded when those in positions of public responsibility fail to meet the standards expected of them by the public. Thus, Government has long been concerned with insuring the even handed and disinterested administration of the functions and powers given it by the governed.

The judicial branches of our State and Federal Governments have been most diligent in safeguarding the integrity of their officers. 

Among public officers, judges occupy a unique position. Unlike legislative and executive officials who are constantly judged by their political choices and decisions and by the practical success of their proposals and programs, Supreme Court judges are lifetime appointees and are primarily appraised by a test of trust: Are their decisions impartial, just, and in accordance with the law? To meet this test, rules have been established in case law, statutes, and canons of ethics, calling for judges to disqualify themselves from cases in which their personal interests might knowingly or unknowingly influence their decisions. Though these rules may appear strict, they are especially important today. For the first time in history, the U.S. Senate is asked to confirm a nominee for a Supreme Court seat which was vacated by the resignation of a sitting justice accused of conduct involving the appearance of impropriety.

In exercising its constitutional power to advise and consent to the present nomination, the Senate thus has the added responsibility, at this time, of shoring up public confidence in the Federal judiciary in general, and, more specifically, in the Supreme Court itself. Supreme 

Court Justices truly must be beyond reproach and must be sensitive to the ethical standards which have been established in order to guarantee propriety, the appearance of propriety, and equal justice to all who come before them.

In light of the judicial rules which have been laid down over the years governing the conduct of judges and the pressing concern of the people to see these standards adhered to, it would be a mistake for the Senate to confirm the nomination of Clement F. Haynsworth, Jr., to the Supreme Court of the United States. It is true, as the majority of this committee has said, that Judge Haynsworth is basically an honest man. It is also true that the nominee has been a tremendously successful businessman and member of the bar in his home community. But these are not the sole standards on which the Senate must base its decision in determining whether or not to confirm the nomination of a Supreme Court Justice. The Senate must also consider the standards of ethical conduct which the nominee has established for himself while serving on the Federal bench. The sources of the law on judicial disqualification are in the common law, constitutional law, and statutory law. Each source indicates that a judge should not sit in cases where he holds stock in a litigant.

The canons of judicial ethics, though they do not have the force of law, have established accepted guidelines for the conduct of judges. 

Like the law on disqualification, the canons hold that a judge should not sit on cases where he has an interest. Canon 29 states: 

A Judge should abstain from performing or taking part in any judicial act in which his personal interests are involved. 

If he has personal litigation in the court of which he is judge, he need not resign his judgeship on that account, but he should, of course, refrain from any judicial act in such a controversy.

In interpreting Canon 29, the American Bar Association's Committee on Professional Ethics stated in Opinion 170:

A Judge should not perform a judicial act, involving the exercise of judicial discretion, in a cause in which one of the parties is a corporation in which the judge is a stockholder. 

Judge Winter recognized the significance of this Opinion in his testimony before the Judiciary Committee:

The American Bar Association committee at least has taken the position that if you own any stock, that is it. 

You ought not to sit at all. (Hearings, p. 248.)

Judge Haynsworth's financial interests were involved in the Brunswick, Grace Lines, and Maryland Casualty cases, yet he did not refrain from performing judicial acts in these controversies. To argue that 

Canon 29 does not apply in situations where the litigant is a subsidiary of a corporation in which a judge owns stock is unreasonable. 

The Canon states that a judge should not sit in a case "in which his personal interests are involved," and Opinion 170 further indicates that even one share of stock in a corporate litigant is an interest. 

Certainly direct interest in a litigant through ownership in the parent corporation should be treated no differently. 

Canons 4 and 34 also come into play when a judge sits on cases in which he has personal interests. They state:

Canon 4-Avoidance of Impropriety. A judge's official conduct should be free from impropriety and the appearance of impropriety; he should avoid infractions of law; and his personal behavior, not only upon the bench and in the performance of judicial duties, but also in his everyday life, should be beyond reproach.

Canon 34-A Summary of Judicial Obligation. In every particular his conduct should be above reproach. He should be conscientious, studious, thorough, courteous, patient, punctual, just, impartial, fearless of public clamor, regardless of public praise, and indifferent to private political or partisan influences; he should administer justice according to law, and deal with his appointments as a public trust; he should not allow other affairs or his private interests to interfere with the prompt and proper performance of his judicial duties, nor should he administer the office for the purpose of advancing his personal ambitions or increasing his popularity. 

Judge Haynsworth's conduct was not "beyond reproach." He disregarded the precedents on disqualification which have been so carefully established to avoid the appearances of impropriety. While not dishonest, he has callously ignored the ethical rules which the great majority of judges follow meticulously.

Although John Frank has testified that he believes Judge Haynsworth's interest in the litigation was too remote to require disqualification, Supreme Court cases indicate that the law of disqualification extends to cases of even relatively remote financial relationships. 

The basic standard a judge is required to follow in deciding whether or not to hear a case is set out in In Re Murchison 349 U.S. 133 (1955), where the Supreme Court reversed contempt convictions handed out by a Michigan State judge who had investigated the underlying offense as a one-man grand jury. The Court stated: * * * This Court has said, however, that "every procedure which would offer a possible temptation to the average man as a judge * not to hold the balance nice, clear, and true between the State and the accused, denies the latter due process of law. Tumey v. Ohio, 273 U.S. 510, 532. Such a stringent rule may sometimes bar trial by judges who have no actual bias and who would do their very best to weigh the scales of justice equally between contending parties. But to perform its high function in the best way "justice must satisfy the appearance of justice." Offutt v. United States, 348 U.S. 11, 14 (349 U.S. 133, 136).

This standard was clarified in Commonwealth Coatings Corp. v. 

Continental Casualty Co., 393 U.S. 145 (1968). In that case, one of the parties to an arbitration proceeding had done business with one of three arbitrators, a consulting engineer. The relationship between the party and the arbitrator had been sporadic over the years and amounted to less than 1 percent of the arbitrator's business. In fact, there had been no business dealings between the two for over a year. The financial relationships in Commonwealth Coatings, obviously, was far more remote than Carolina Vend-A-Matic's relationship with Darlington. There, the relationship was current, and the business amounted to 3 percent of Carolina Vend-A-Matic's sales. Yet, the Court set aside the judgment of the arbitrators and applied the constitutional rules of judicial disqualification. Justice Black stated:

It is true that petitioner does not charge before us that the third arbitrator was actually guilty of fraud or bias in deciding this case, and we have no reason, apart from the undisclosed business relationships, to suspect him of any improper motives. But neither this arbitrator nor the prime contractor gave to petitioner even an intimation of the close financial relations that had existed between them for a period of years. We have no doubt that if a litigant could show that a foreman of a jury or a judge in a court of justice had, unknown to the litigant, any such relationship, the judgment would be subject to challenge. This is shown beyond doubt by Tumey v. Ohio, 273 U.S. 510 (1947), where this Court held that a conviction could not stand because a small part of the judge's income consisted of court fee

collected from convicted defendants. Although in Tumey it appeared the amount of the judge's compensation actually depended on whether he decided for one side or the other, that is too small a distinction to allow this manifest violation of the strict morality and fairness Congress would have expected on the part of the arbitrator and the other party in this case. Nor should it be at all relevant, as the Court of Appeals apparently thought it was here, that "(t)he payments received were a very small part of (the arbitrator's) income * * * "For in Tumey the Court held that a decision should be set aside where there is "the slightest pecuniary interest" on the part of the judge, and specifically rejected the State's contention that the compensation involved there was "so small that it is not to be regarded as likely to influence improperly a judicial officer in the discharge of his duty 

The opinion concluded by noting the similarity in rule 18 of the American Arbitration Association and the pertinent section of the 33d 

Canon of Judicial Ethics which states: 

Canon 33-Social Relations.— * * * [A judge] should, however, in pending or prospective litigation before him be particularly careful to avoid such action as may reasonably tend to awaken the suspicion that his social or business relations or friendships, constitute an element in influencing his judicial conduct.

The Court suggested further that the standard required for ethical conduct rested on a broader and more fundamental constitutional concept. In the words of Justice Black:

This rule of arbitration and this canon of judicial ethics rest on the premise that any tribunal permitted by law to try cases and controversies must not only be unbiased, but must avoid even the appearance of bias. (393 U.S. 145, 150)

By acting as a director and vice president of Carolina Vend-A-Matic, Judge Haynsworth clearly accepted duties likely to "interfere or appear to interfere" with the proper administration of his official judicial functions. Shortly after investigating bribery charges in the 

Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals in 1963-64, Judge Simon Sobeloff, in an article for the Federal Bar Journal, observed:

One can readily see that if a judge serves as an officer or director of a commercial enterprise, not only is he disqualified in cases involving that enterprise, but his impartiality may also be consciously or unconsciously affected when persons having business relations with his company come before him. (Sobeloff, Striving for Impartiality in the Federal 

Courts, 24 Fed. Bar J. 286, 293 (1964)).

IV. Canon 25-Business Promotions and Solicitations for 

Charity. A Judge should avoid giving ground for any reasonable suspicion that he is utilizing the power or prestige of his office to persuade or coerce others to patronize or contribute, either to the success of private business ventures, or to charitable enterprises. He should, therefore, not enter into such private business, or pursue such a course of conduct,

as would justify such suspicion, nor use the power of his office or the influence of his name to promote the business interests of others; he should not solicit for charities, nor should he enter into any business relations which, in the normal course of events reasonably to be expected, might bring his personal interest into conflict with the impartial performance of his official duties.

Judge Haynsworth's continued financial interest and active participation in the affairs of Carolina Vend-A-Matic constituted a clear breach of this standard. 

V. Canon 29-Self-Interest.—A judge should abstain from performing or taking part in any judicial act in which his personal interests are involved. If he has personal litigation on the court of which he is judge, he need not resign his judgeship on that account, but he should, of course, refrain from any judicial act in such a controversy. 

By deciding cases involving customers of Carolina Vend-A-Matic on at least six occasions, he performed judicial acts which clearly violated this canon.

VI. Canon 33-Social Relations. It is not necessary to the proper performance of judicial duty that a judge should live in retirement or seclusion; it is desirable that, so far as reasonable attention to the completion of his work will permit, he continue to mingle in social intercourse, and that he should not discontinue his interest in or appearnace at meetings of members of the Bar. He should, however, in pending or prospective litigation before him, be particularly careful to avoid such action as may reasonably tend to awaken the suspicion that his social or business relations or friendships constitute an element in influencing his judicial conduct." [Emphasis added.]

By sitting in cases involving important customers of Carolina Vend-A-Matic, Judge Haynsworth gave grounds for the appearance that business relations influenced his conduct. 

VII. Canon 34—A Summary of Judicial Obligation.—In every particular his conduct should be above reproach. He should be conscientious, studious, thorough, courteous, patient, punctual, just, impartial, fearless, of public clamor, regardless of public praise, and indifferent to private political or partisan influences; he should administer justice according to law, and deal with his appointments as a public trust; he should not allow other affairs or his private interests to interfere with the prompt and proper performance of his judicial duties, nor should he administer the office for the purpose of advancing his personal ambitions or increasing his popularity. [Emphasis added.]

Judge Haynsworth, in view of the facts detailed above, has obviously not conducted himself in such a manner that his conduct is above reproach "in every particular.”

Violation of 29 U.S.C. 301-308 

Another matter deserves notice. Judge Haynsworth was a trustee. of the Carolina Vend-A-Matic Co. profit sharing and retirement plan from 1961 until 1964 and qualified as an administrator by law. The 

Welfare and Pension Plan Disclosure Act provides that an administrator of a pension fund must file with the Secretary of Labor an initial description of the plan and annual reports thereafter. Willful violation of the act can lead to 6 months imprisonment or a fine of $1,000 or both. On September 17, 1969, the director of the Office of 

Labor-Management and Welfare-Pension Reports of the U.S. De partment of Labor advised by letter, "Our records do not show that any reports have been received under the name of Carolina Vend-A- Matic Co., Inc., for a profit sharing and retirement plan." 

The omission by the judge was in all probability an oversight and not an intentional violation. However, the facts are cited to reinforce the obvious conclusion that complicated financial relationships and judicial responsibility can become a dangerous mixture. 

Lack of candor

The statements made by Judge Haynsworth to the Judiciary Committee have shown an amazing lack of candor. Senator Griffin has aptly pointed these out in his report, and there is no need to repeat these contradictions here.

Conclusion 

The central theme of the canons of judicial ethics and the law of disqualification is that judges must be extremely careful to avoid bias or even the appearance of bias in administering their judicial functions. Judge Haynsworth entered into and maintained numerous relationships which, in view of the fact that he continued to perform judicial acts affecting other parties to those relationships, give the appearances of bias and thus constitute breaches of the canons of ethics and violations of the disqualification law

He sat on cases involving litigants in which he had a financial interest; he purchased stock in corporations apt to appear before his court; he sat on cases involving customers of a corporation in which he was a major stockholder and for which he served as a director and vice president. Moreover, he failed to comply with Federal law in administering a profitsharing trust, and he displayed a lack of candor in testimony before this committee.

This is not acceptable conduct for a nominee to the Supreme Court. 

The Supreme Court is the final determinant of the standard of judicial conduct not only for itself but also for every court in the land. 

The Court requires men sensitive to the many ethical problems which often arise. The Senate must await such a nominee before exercising its power to consent.

IV. RESOLVING THE DOUBT 

Clearly, the record raises substantial and legitimate doubt concerning the nominee's sensitivity to the high ethical standards expected of those who are to sit on the Supreme Court. 

It may be argued that the nominee is entitled to a presumption of innocence; and that unless he is proven guilty, he should be confirmed. 

Such an argument misconstrues the constitutional role and ressponsibility of the Senate. For the question before the Senate is not the nominee's guilt or innocence, it is whether he should be promoted to a place on the Nation's highest court. 

The central issue was put in focus by a letter which came to me recently from a professor of law who teaches legal ethics to future lawyers and future judges. He wrote:

In the U.S. district court a jury awards an injured seaman $50 on a claim against Grace Lines-a claim which he thought was worth $30,000. Saddened, he takes his case to the U.S. 

Court of Appeals. It is not difficult to imagine the bitterness in the heart of this injured seaman when he learns that one of the judges to whom he appealed in vain, was even a small owner of the company that owns Grace Lines. 

By the standards of the marketplace perhaps Judge

Haynsworth's stock holding was trifling. But it looms large in the mind of the unhappy litigant searching to discover just what it was that tipped the scales of justice against him. 

To avoid such avoidable strains on the legal system, it has long been a maxim of the law that courts shall not only do justice but they shall seem to do justice. 

This ancient wisdom finds expression in the Canons of 

Judicial Ethics providing that a judge's conduct should not

only be "free from impropriety" but from the "appearance of impropriety." The importance of the appearance of things is stressed over and over again (canons 13, 24, 26, 33) culminating in the injunction that "in every particular his conduct should be above reproach." (Prof. David Mellinkoff, UCLA, Oct. 20, 1969.)

Although the Canons apply to all judges at every level, they should apply most stringently to those who are to grace the Nation's highest court.

Ironically perhaps, the nominee himself suggested the test which is appropriate in this case: *** while I am concerned about myself and my reputation, I much more am concerned about my country and the Supreme Court as an institution, and if there is substantial doubt about the propriety of what I did and my fitness to sit on the Supreme Court, then I hope the Senate will resolve the doubt against me. (Hearings, p. 105.)

Particularly at this point in history, it is essential that substantial doubt in a situation like this be resolved against the nominee—and in the interest of preserving public confidence in our system of justice.

INDIVIDUAL VIEWS OF MR. HART 

To the minorities in our society, especially, the Supreme Court is the symbol of assurance that justice will prevail, that inequities will be removed. Those who see the law as a redemptive instrument are in strong competition with other voices who insist that disregard of the law is the only course. Against this background, I believe that confirmation of the nominee, Clement F. Haynsworth, Jr., would be a symbol serving poorly the cause of moderation. We cannot ignore the fact that in the very recent past the nominee was writing and joining in decisions which would have further delayed reforms recognized as within reach of the law by some of his colleagues on his own court.

Opposition to a nominee should not be based on an assessment of his liberal or conservative leaning. Certainly it should not be by reason of his regional origin. But I reluctantly believe we should withhold our advice and consent to the confirmation of a nominee whose decisions reflect a hesitancy to eliminate discriminatory practices.

Criticism of Judge Haynsworth's decisions in civil rights matters does not rest solely on those decisions written early in his career but include the most recent.

The test of confirmation is not an examination of some of the nominee's opinions but rather a thorough study of them all. 

Disagreement even with a majority of a judge's opinions would not cause me to oppose his confirmation. But opposition is justified when his decisions indicate a consistent insensitivity to the rights of individuals recognized to be within the reach of the law. 

This insensitivity, I believe, characterizes Judge Haynsworth's opinions in civil rights matters. Therefore, I will not advise and consent to his confirmation to be an Associate Justice of the Supreme 

Court of the United States. (48)

INDIVIDUAL VIEWS OF MR. TYDINGS 

The Senate's duty to render "advice and consent" to the President's Supreme Court appointments is a constitutional responsibility of the first magnitude. In the matter of Judge Clement F. Haynsworth, the importance of this responsibility has been coupled with the difficulty of the task. Indeed, my vote on the confirmation of Judge 

Haynsworth represents one of the most difficult decisions I have been called upon to make in the Senate. 

At the time the President announced his nomination, I was ready to support Judge Haynsworth. However, after active participation in the 

Judiciary Committee's hearings on this confirmation and a careful examination of the hearing record and other relevant material, I now feel compelled to vote differently.

I long have considered Judge Haynsworth to be a fair man, a good judge and a fine judicial administrator. My decision to oppose his elevation to the Supreme Court is not intended in any way to discredit the valuable services he is rendering as a circuit chief judge. 

I have not predicated my decision upon Judge Haynsworth's views. on particular social or economic issues or his rulings in particular cases. 

In considering the nomination of Judge Haynsworth, as in considering previous nominees for the Supreme Court, I have adhered to the position that, barring some unusual situation, a man selected by the President for the Federal bench should be confirmed by the Senate if he has demonstrated a proper judicial temperament, an intellectual capacity equal to the task set for him, and a character beyond reproach. In addition, I believe a nominee to the Supreme Court should subscribe to a judicial philosphy which in general would contribute to the high court's critical role in our system of government. 

The requirement that a Supreme Court nominee possess a character beyond reproach contemplates not only an absence of actual wrongdoing but also an image of impeccable rectitude and a reputation which is not subject to reasonable doubt. This important requirement is predicated upon the proposition that the strength of the Supreme Court is inherently dependent upon public respect and confidence. 

We know that, unlike its sister branches of government, the high court commands neither money nor manpower to enforce its decrees and to guard its constitutional mandate against encroachment. Since its inception, the Court's foremost guardian has been its stature as an institution of unremitting integrity in which an amalgam of wisdom and thorough impartiality has been finely blended. Public trust can only be nurtured by such an image. Indeed, this image has enabled the Court to withstand the broadside attacks which, from time to time, have come from either side of the political spectrum. The perpetuation of this trust is of overriding importance in any matterrelating to the Court. (49)

The sanctity of the Court as an institution is in large part dependent upon the public's respect for the individual Justices. This fact influenced me to call for the resignation of the Justice whose seat is now open. (115 Cong. Rec. S5168-S5169, May 13, 1969.)

The Fortas affair cast over the entire Court a shadow of suspicion and mistrust from which the Court has not yet fully emerged. This aura hangs most noticeably over Mr. Fortas' vacant seat, the very one to which Judge Haynsworth has been nominated. We must recognize that the ultimate decision in the present matter cannot realistically be insulated from this specter. This is especially so because the ethical postulates which were drawn into question then are most conspicuously at issue now.

In calling for the resignation of Justice Fortas, I stated that a "judge's official conduct should be free not only from impropriety but even from the appearance of impropriety," and I observed that this appearance is "a violation not only of the spirit but of the letter of the Canons of Judicial Ethics." I felt that Justice Fortas' activities abridged this important teaching and thus cast a shadow upon the Court's integrity which it could ill afford. 

The command that a judge's conduct must remain free from improper appearances is indeed a cardinal rule of judicial ethics. It is specifically set forth in Canon 4 of the Canons of Judicial Ethics, and runs through the Canons' teachings. At one place or another, the 

Canons admonish a judge to avoid any conduct that would "justify the impression" (Canon 13), give "ground for any reasonable suspicion" (Canon 25) or "arouse the suspicion" (Canon 26) that he has acted improperly. According to Judge Simon E. Sobeloff, the respected former chief judge of the fourth circuit and Solicitor General of the United States, this obligation represents the central theme to the entire subject of judicial ethics. Sobeloff, Striving for Impartiality in the Federal Courts, 24 Fed. B. J. 286, 296 (1964).

The application of this obligation effected the unprecedented resignation of a justice already sitting on the Court. Clearly then, this ethical postulate deserves an even more demanding application when addressed to those suggested for the Court. Moreover, to ignore, circumvent or belittle now a standard thought so important then would further seed the clouds of public mistrust at a time when the need to remove their shadows and bulwark public confidence in the 

Court has never been greater.

Five decisions or activities of Judge Haynsworth have received the most prominent attention as evidence of his insensitivity to the strictures of the Canons of Ethics. These decisions or activities are: (1) Judge Haynsworth's purchase of Brunswick Corp. stock at a time when that corporation had a case pending before his court; (2)

Judge Haynsworth's substantial ownership of Carolina Vend-A-Matic, a company having the bulk of its business with the textile industry, while ruling in favor of an important Southern textile concern in 

NLRB v. Darlington Mfg., a landmark labor law case; (3) Judge 

Haynsworth's stock holding in W. R. Grace Co. at the time of a decision favorable to a subsidiary, Grace Lines; (4) Judge Haynsworth's failure to disclose to the litigants his interest in or connection with one of the parties in the Darlington, Brunswick, and Grace cases; and (5) Judge Haynsworth's failure to relinquish his stock in the litigious J. P. Stevens Co. after he became a Federal judge.

In assessing Judge Haynsworth's conduct in these five matters, one must be mindful of an array of judicial standards in addition to the vastly important requirement that a judge must avoid the appearance of wrongdoing.

Section 455 of title 28 of the United States Code provides that: 

Any justice or judge of the United States shall disqualify himself in any case in which he has a substantial interest *** or is so related to or connected with any part or his attorney or to render it improper, in his opinion, for him to sit on the * ** appeal. 

Canon 25 of the Canons of Judicial Ethics provides that:

* * A judge should avoid giving ground for any reasonable suspicion that he is utilizing the power or prestige of his office to persuade or coerce others to patronize or contribute * to the success of private business ventures. He should, therefore, not pursue such a course of conduct as would justify such suspicion. * * * 

Canon 26 provides that:

A judge should abstain from making personal investments in enterprises which are apt to be involved in litigation in the courts; and, after his accession to the bench, he should not retain such investments previously made, longer than a period sufficient to enable him to dispose of them without serious loss.

Canon 29 provides that, “A judge should abstain from performing or taking part in any judicial act in which his personal interests are involved." In this connection, the American Bar Association Committee on Professional Ethics has formally stated in Opinion 170 that "A judge who is a stockholder in a corporation which is a party to litigation pending in his court may not, with propriety, perform any act in relation to such litigation involving the exercise of judicial discretion." 

Finally, last year in Commonwealth Coating Corp., v. Continental 

Casualty Co., 393 U.S. 145 (1968) the Supreme Court intimated that a judge should disclose to litigants appearing before him "any dealings that might create an impression of possible bias." Id. at 148. In that case, an arbitrator, in a dispute between a subcontractor and the surety on the prime contractor's bond, failed to disclose to the parties that he had past business contacts with the contractor. The 

Supreme Court held that these business contacts must be disclosed and indicated that the same result would have obtained if the case involved a judge rather than an arbitrator. 

I recognize that the outlines of some of these admonishments are hazy and susceptible to different interpretations. However, I firmly believe that it behooves our judges not to be loose constructionists when construing their own ethical responsibilites. I found Judge

Haynsworth's alacrity to rationalize the propriety of his prior activities almost as disconcerting as these activities themselves. 

For instance, Judge Haynsworth, with regard to his purchase of Brunswick stock, testified that, "I do not think it [Canon 26] was violated at all, its letter or its spirit." (Hearings, p. 284.) A commonsense reading of Canon 26 dictates otherwise. Similarly, Judge Hayn-

worth's belief that his decision to remain on the Brunswick case was free from any ethical error (Hearings, p. 296), flies in the face of Canon 

29 and the plain meaning of ABA Formal Opinion 170. 

Moreover, Judge Haynsworth's present belief that he had no obligation to advise the parties of his relevant business interest or financial holdings in the Brunswick and Darlington cases, as well as in some other cases in which he had an interest in one of the litigants (Hearings pp. 

295, 297, 298), appears to be in cavalier disregard of the important teachings of Commonwealth Coating. 

Likewise, his inability to grasp the implications of his decision to hear the Darlington case (Hearings, p. 293) at a time when Carolina Vend- 

A-Matic, a corporation in which he had a major interest, was doing business with the principal party in this case, suggests a present obliviousness to the subtle requirements of both section 455 of title 

28 and Canons 25 and 29 of the Canons of Judicial Ethics.

In addition, it is reasonable to conclude that Canon 26 would require him to liquidate his holdings in such litigious companies as J. P. 

Stevens, but Judge Haynsworth construes this admonishment as nothing particularly relevant to appellate judges (Hearings p. 284). 

Judge Haynsworth has said that his financial interest did not influence his vote in these matters. As an attorney and a longtime acquaintance of Judge Haynsworth, I accept his explanation. It is not responsibly contended that Judge Haynsworth has either deliberately transgressed ethical standards, unwholesomely reaped financial benefit through his judicial duties, or in any way acted in a dishonest manner. 

In this regard, I do not believe that Judge Haynsworth became entrapped in a conflict of interest in the traditional sense. 

However, I cannot help observing that to the unsuccessful litigant in Judge Haynsworth's court the explanation would ring hollow. 

As Professor David Mellinkoff of UCLA Law School has observed:

At best losing a lawsuit is disheartening, at worst a crushing experience to anyone convinced rightly or wrongly of the justice of his cause. The disappointment is endurable only under a system of justice in which the loser knows that the process by which he lost was a fair one. 

In a grosser age, when the brilliant Francis Bacon was forced from office and forced to acknowledge that as Lord Chancellor of England he had been taking gifts from litigants, he was still able to assert, "* * * I am as innocent as any born upon St. Innocent's day: I never had a bribe or reward in my eye or thought when pronouncing sentence or order."

It may have been true, but it was hardly satisfying, least of all to the man who lost his case in the Lord Chancellor's court.

I do not believe that any one of the decisions or actions of Judge Haynsworth to which I have alluded is of a gravity which requires a denial of the seat to which he aspires. However, when all the pertinent matters are viewed in the aggregate, one clearly discerns a pattern of repeated judgments which unfortunately create the impression that Judge Haynsworth is insensitive or oblivious to the subtle requirements of judicial ethics, especially that cardinal rule which admonishes judges to avoid even the appearance of impropriety.

I have concluded that Judge Haynsworth's past actions and present approach to the principles of judicial conduct have cast a shadow over his ethical sensitivities. The incursion of this shadow upon the sanctity of the Supreme Court at a time when its general reputation is at low ebb is something this great institution can ill afford. Indeed, Judge Haynsworth himself clearly recognized the burden under which we labor when he stated: [W]hile I am concerned about myself and my reputation, I much more am concerned about my country and the Supreme Court as an institution, and if there is substantial doubt about the propriety of what I did and my fitness to sit on the Supreme Court, then I hope the Senate will resolve the doubt against me. (Hearings, p. 105.)

In this light, I regretfully have been forced to conclude that the standards of Supreme Court appointees which requires an image of rectitude and a reputation which is not subject to reasonable doubt has not been satisfactorily met in this case. Therefore, I must oppose confirmation.
