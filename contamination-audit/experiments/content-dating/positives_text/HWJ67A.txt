Just a Moment, If You Please WHEN 7HEN a price and a penalty is placed upon honesty in matters pertaining to the welfare of the public, it is time for change in those conditions which penalize such public honesty. Someone has to speak out against such conditions in order to bring about a change for the better.

The conditions which penalize honesty as described herein are conditions which arose in the first World war when, in order to avoid the profit limitations in government contracts, a system was devised that makes honest men unwilling partners in frauds against Uncle Sam that literally run into billions of dollars.

Because the benefactors from this system are often the most influential and respected business leaders of the country, few dare even to mention these doings in public for fear of reprisals. The government itself, being composed of men, sometimes frightened men, does not vigorously put its foot down on the practices described herein. 

I happened to know two men who have been penalized for their collaboration with the federal government in saving the taxpayers hundreds of thousands of dollars. For their assistance to the government they have been harrassed and put under pressure. Nevertheless they have rendered and they now are rendering the public a service. 

Now is the time for the public to join them in bringing to a stop the practice they encountered, and for that reason this story of theirs is published.

The Price of Honesty In Big Business The Case of A Corporation Doing Business With Uncle Sam N SATURDAY, July 24, 

ON 1943, Stellan Larsson, 3319 N. 3rd st., Milwaukee 12,

Wis., was laid off the job he had worked on for fourteen years: and with that lay-off the time came for the public to look into a matter that can at the very least be described as shenanigans at the expense of Uncle Sam. 

Larsson, a native of Sweden and a naturalized American, was an employee of the Falk 

Corporation, 3001 W. Canal st., Milwaukee 8, Wisconsin. As such an employee, Larsson had participated with a group of other employees of the corporation in an action which resulted in the corporation paying to the Treasury Department of the federal government a sum of approximately $500,- 000.

An official of the government says that this $500,000 back payment is for excess profits, but another former employee who first set the government on the trail insists that the whole case is one of fraudulent overcharging of the federal government. 

Stellan Larsson was not the only figure to gain prominence by being fired from the corporation after he had helped the federal government. Earlier-in fact, on the last day of 

1936-another employee, one 

Anthony Kinch, was fired, and around Kinch a strange and intricate story has woven itself. 

Anthony Kinch went to work for the corporation in 1929 and was employed there during the depression. The corporation for many years has made speed reducers for vessels of the 

United States navy on contracts that run into millions of dollars. Speed reducers are a gear arrangement by means of

According to Kinch, shortly after his employment he became aware that considerable time and machine operations put in on private jobs were charged to the Navy jobs; and that such a practice of cheating the Navy had been going on since the beginning of the last war, if other men in the plant were to be believed. This practice of falsifying the expenses on Navy jobs was costing the federal government thousands of dollars, and it was so flagrant that Kinch decided to do something about it. 1 

An Appeal to the President 

After making sure of his facts, he sent the following letter to the President of the 

United States, on February 28, 1936: 

Dear Mr. President:

Our government is being robbed right and left on cost plus 10% contracts, through the same old wartime tricks. Thousands upon thousands of hours are being charged against the government jobs that are never put on, and because of it over twelve hundred men are being fed with disrespect for their government daily in the plant where I work. What makes it still worse, all this is going on under the eyes of the inspectors sent here by the Department of the government through which the contract was let. 

This is the most difficult letter I ever wrote, but it is even harder to work with the feeling of being a partner in crime because crime it is, Mr. President. It got me to the point where I begin to wonder if honesty is constitutional. (Signed)

A. KINCH.

1. The undersigned started to work for the Falk Corporation of Milwaukee, Wis., as a mechanic in the assembling shop late in 1929, and soon thereafter fellow workers began to confide in me about the system through which the firm had been defrauding our Government out of enormous amounts of money ever since the World War No. 1,

2. As time went on I noticed the fraud upon our Government was becoming more and more prevalent. In fact, it was going on so openly it caused workers to grow increasingly disrespectful toward our Government, including the President. Something just had to be done about it, but at the time I did not feel that what others do was my business; however, it wasn't long before the management made it my business. 

3. On Saturday, August 24, 1935, I was working on a unit model, C. H. 

PON M. O. 25-535, for the Worthington Pump & Machinery Co., with several hours overtime, and was ordered to come down with my helpers on Sunday, August 25, 1935, to finish the job. The foreman, one George

Robinson, showed up about 11 a. m., and after he spent several minutes by the time clock, he came over to us and told us that he had fixed our time job cards and that all we had to do is to punch out when we go out. I checked the cards after he left and found that our time was charged to M. O. 19-930, which was a Navy job. 

4. On Monday, August 26, 1935, I called Mr. Robinson's attention to the erroneous charges. His excuse was that job M. O. 25-535 could not stand overtime. "How about if somebody squawks to the inspectors?" I asked Mr. Robinson. "He just would lose his job," he replied. "But what would happen if someone takes it up with the higher-ups in the Navy?" I asked. "Nothing would happen. It has been going on for 20 years that I know of, and nothing has happened yet." "I would not take the chances, I said. "They are not taking any chances. The Navy and the big shots are in cahoots," he answered "In other words," I said, "there is a thorough understanding between them?" "Right," he said, "this firm is a piker in comparison to others. Bids are formalities as far as the Navy contracts are concerned, and there is nothing to be done about it but keep your mouth shut." 

5. On the night of November 20, 1935, I called the erroneous charges to the attention of my boss, the head foreman of assembly shop, Mr. 

William Henderson. His answer was, "that the system of big business was responsible for it, and that if 

Falk didn't follow the system, he would not get the business, and that it would be suicide for anyone to step in their way." 

6. I told them that the management could spare itself from lots of headaches if they leave me as I was, with all the stealing that goes around here. And when I told them about it at his request, he just grinned and said, "It isn't as much of it going on as it looks sometimes." At any rate, I was elected to the works council about week later, and all of a sudden became popular with the big bosses; they waited for me to drive me home.

This letter to Senator Walsh is quite a story; but as the saying goes, "It ain't half." Sometime that journal will be printed and the public may read and wonder.

The average man's response to this story at the first hearing is, "What is his motive? 

Who paid for his investigation and who supported Kinch?"

Those same questions bothered federal officials to whom Kinch brought his story and facts; and those officials spent a good deal of time investigating Kinch and his financial support as well as his charges. 

The answer to those questions is simple: Kinch supported himself out of his own savings; and when those savings ran low, fellow employees in the shop supported him out of their meager earnings. 

One of the strangest things about this case is that these fellow employees stuck with 

Kinch through thick and thin and helped him out of their own pay checks to stop a fraud of which they were an unwilling part. Nevertheless, many officials did and do not believe that employees are so constituted.

XII How to Prevent Further Overcharges 

On this very subject of avoiding the penalty of falsely overcharging the government, Kinch wrote yet another letter to Senator Walsh, dated 

January 27, 1942. In it he said:

If there is to be war cost reduction to minimum via new legislation some means must be found to curb padding cost through erroneous charges of material and labor time. 

Under the present excess profit law, as I understand, it, the favorites of the plant management filling war orders can enter into pact with any of their workers or foremen and load their time cards with as much as 

20 hours per week overtime while absent from the plant. Members of the management can buy material and use shop labor to improve their own personal property or interest and charge same to government contract-if they get caught at it the blame is put on some long picked non-beneficiary of fraud who is always ready to take responsibility with some innocent excuse and make federal authority helpless to do anything about it. 

Making the beneficiaries of fraud through erroneous charges who are actively engaged in filling war orders in whatever capacity, jointly criminally liable with their medium, would save the government countless millions of dollars. Respectfully, 

(Signed) A. KINCH.

Not all the urgings and all the appeals can have much effect as long as employees feel that there is one kind of severe and harsh justice for them and another kind of easy-going, wink-the-eye variety for the employer.

XIII Kinch Describes What's Wrong 

A rather interesting summary of the attitude of a lot of people who are involved in this business of loading Uncle

Sam with phony charges is contained in a letter which the redoubtable Kinch sent to 

Senator Walsh under the date of May 18, 1942. Here it is, and it might just as well wind up this story: 

My Dear Senator Walsh: 

Since leaving Washington last

March I have visited several large

cities where war orders are being filled and have talked with many workers, formen, topcost men, engineers and plant maintainence men. 

In addition I also talked with business and labor leaders, lawyers, politicians and the heads of various 

Associations in each of the cities I visited for the express purpose of getting their reaction on the illegal profiteering and tax dodging by those who can best afford to pay, which, by the way I must admit, is most disgusting. 

From the shop-workers I learned, that they have done most everything for their big bosses and foreman on government time, but cut their hair. 

Their lack in keeping records of erroneous charges is due to the fear of losing their jobs, getting black-balled, or turned over to the Draft Board as a "non-essential." This is what keeps them from standing up to their charges. 

The shop foreman tells me: "What would it get me but lots of trouble and a discharge, with no protection from the N. L. R. B., if I were to tell what I know about the way the government is being defrauded in our plant." The "top cost man" who really knows about erroneous charges, confides in me, "sure there is a lot of fraud going on in our plant but my orders are given me confidentially and orally and I have no records of any kind; however, if the Government Agents that come to check the books would show me some real interest and assure me they knew what they were doing, I would find the way to help them without putting myself on the spot of disloyalty to the boss, but the 

Agents that come to our place are just the routine men and the bosses know that they are not running much chance in getting caught." 

The yarn of a plant engineer: "Of course we have quite a bit of chiseling going on at our plant. It takes about 2,000 tons of coal yearly to run the power house but the big boss buys 3,000 tons. He has a big house, his favored employees and friends live in houses too, get it? 

He clips six or seven thousand dollars yearly by doing his friends a favor, charging them a couple dollars a ton less than they could get it from the regular coal dealer. The boss' house needs remodeling, well, the shop men are sent to do it for him. All this increases the overhead and reduces the profits of the Company, but not the boss'. All this is between you and me of course," he concluded.

Now we come to the plant “maintenance" man. "Sure, we charge a lot of things to the plant's overhead and Government Contracts that are never used on either, but what can any of us do about it when the bosses have all the influence and we have none? Before the war orders began coming in and personal income taxes were doubled the company's president's salary was $50,000 yearly and the vice president's $30,000, which netted them, after they had paid their personal income taxes, about $36,000 and $24,000 respectively. Since then their salary has been raised to $85,000 and $45,000 which now nets them about $45,000 and $30,- 

000. Now does this look to you as though everyone is bearing their just share of the war burden as our

And now let's go over on the other side of the fence. I called on the president of a large Corporation, a member of the National Association of Manufacturers and past president of a State Association of Manufacturers, to tell him that some of th members of his organization had been defrauding our Government for many years, not out of millions of dollars, but billions, through erroneous charges and that I thought it was about time some influential member like himself, made some effort to purge his organization of these defrauders before the workers lost what little confidence they did have left in the employers that were still trying to be honest. I informed him who the defrauders were and that I was ready to face them, at his convenience, in order to pull them down from their high horse, but all I got in the way of satisfaction after talking to him for nearly an hour, was, "my business is to build machines and not to reform people."

The attitude of a dozen or more labor leaders that I contacted was pretty much the same. After telling them how they could help to put a stop to the form of fraud in question. their answers were practically alike, viz "You are right. We know that that form of fraud has been going on for years and that it could be stopped, but, right now we are having a hard time holding our own with the employers."

In talking to lawyers about it, one of them was honest enough to tell me that if it wasn't for the crooks they wouldn't have much of a practice. And, one of the high ranking politicians whom I know quite well, and whose advice I was seeking, pointed out to me, that the campaign contributors have to get their money back in some way.

The facts stated here clearly indicate that making an honest living is becoming more and more of a problem for an increasing number of employees, and, to ignore its evil effect much longer, may result in creating more trouble for our country than it would be able to cope with through democratic process.

XIV The End of the First Part 

This last part of this book might be well called "The End of Part One", for Kinch is still gathering material and from the little that I know of his present activity I think it is safe to say that he will have another interesting story for the proper congressional committees before very long. 

The material that I drew upon here has long been available to leading publishers, political leaders of all parties, leading union officials, business men and club leaders.

With tolerably few exceptions they have shied off in quaking fear. Their personal safety comes before public honesty. 

The material, of course, is not to be used for a political football as mentioned before, because it is Kinch's own opinion that the Falk Corporation in its practices is not worse than thousands of others doing business with Uncle Sam. 

A prime objective of those who would feel that a stop must be put to this sort of business should be a two fold one.

The first objective is the enactment of a law making the beneficiary and the prepetrator of false charges jointly liable; and the second is to remove the fear from those common citizens like Kinch and Larsson who are willing to help the government but who are penalized for doing so. 

No doubt there are others equally unwilling partners in loading Uncle Sam with heavy accounts who want to put a stop to it; and these people must realize that there are other people ready to help
