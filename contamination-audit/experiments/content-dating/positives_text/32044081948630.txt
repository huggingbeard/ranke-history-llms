HIS letter was not written for the trained musicians of the Tmonetary orchestra, but for an influential member of the audience who wished to know what the orchestra was trying to do or ought to be trying to do, and requested a simple explanation of the principles underlying the writer's letter of January 15, 1934, written at the request of Mr. Walter Lippmann, which contained a discussion of practical expedients for monetary policy. It must not be thought to follow from this limited aim that the discussion is superficial. If this short treatise on money has any merit, it is because of the attempt to go succinctly to the very heart of the subject, eliminating all chaff.

It is here printed as a pamphlet for private circulation among the writer's friends, and in the belief that in the long run it is intelligent opinion among the audience that will finally determine what the orchestra shall play and who shall conduct.

DEAR FRANK, You need not feel apologetic because you found my letter to Mr. Lippmann on monetary policy difficult to understand. The famous MacMillan Report refers to "the complexity, not to say mystery, in which the problems of finance are involved," and continues: "It is no exaggeration to say that to the vast majority even of intelligent people, the principles of finance and the theory of money are a closed book."

It is not possible for me to produce an explanation for you in words of one syllable, as you request. But I am glad to try my best to give you a concise exposition of the main principles underlying my letter; but I warn you, that even a simple statement will require some concentration on your part. So read slowly. Also, if I am to be school teacher, you must permit me the privilege of being arrogant occasionally.

I will begin with a fable. It is a commonplace story which has been going the rounds of ordinary circles, although I have embellished it considerably to suit my purpose. You must not be bored by its repetition, for it is sound economics and is capable of teaching simply a number of intricate things. 1

In a certain village, where all business was stagnant because there was no money in circulation, a traveler arrived at the inn. He overheard the innkeeper and the butcher complaining of their debts and the total lack of money with which to pay them. "Why don't you sell something?" interposed the traveler. "Sell what?" replied the innkeeper. "And who will buy?" "Why not that silver teapot?" asked the traveler. "I will give you a hundred dollar bill for it." "It belonged to my ancestors and I hate to part with it," regretfully replied the innkeeper. "But I think you are right. I will sell it." "But I," moaned the butcher, "have nothing that you will buy, and the interest on my mortgage is due." "I will take your note at sixty days for $100," said the genial stranger. "Interest at six per cent."

So it was all arranged, and the traveler departed to return in sixty days, while the innkeeper and the butcher discharged their obligations, and the receivers of this new money immediately spent it; and it was used again and again in a string of transactions, recounted in the common version with much detail and local color, which I will spare you here. Suffices it to say that much activity and business revival were stirred in the village. Wood was bought and sold, potatoes laid in, cows acquired, doctors and lawyers recompensed, labor employed, sheds built, and a dozen other things accomplished, until finally by a perfection of incident which occurs only in fables, the innkeeper received back the original hundred dollar bill in payment of an 2

old debt, and the butcher sold his horse and received the other bill, on the very morning of the traveler's return. "I am very glad to see you," said the innkeeper, "I have been worrying about parting with that old teapot. 

Won't you sell it back to me?" "Surely," said the traveler, "if you will give me back the hundred dollars I paid you.' 99 "And here's my money to pay my note,” said the butcher. "Good!" said the traveler. "But where's the interest?" "Alas!" said the butcher, "I haven't the money. I will have to give you a note for the interest." "What! I insist on gold or legal tender," said the traveler, pounding the table and throwing the butcher into a fearful stew.

The poor man pled tearfully that he had exhausted every effort to raise the money, that there was no gold or legal tender in the village except belonging to an old miser, and that he would have to sell all he had at a tenth of its value, and knock down in sympathy all the values in the village, inducing all creditors to insist upon immediate and impossible payment, before the old skinflint would be ready to part with a nickel and buy something from him. "That's no affair of mine," said the traveler virtuously. "Moreover, it will be only healthy for the village to face the facts and get down to bedrock; only so can it hope for a new and sound prosperity." "Prosperity for old skinflint," bitterly cried the butcher.

After much talk of this character back and forth, which gradually reduced the innkeeper and the butcher to a state 3

of panic, the traveler's countenance, which had been gray with an austere and Calvinistic joy, suddenly changed. "I was only fooling," he said gaily, "I will forego the interest."

So the teapot and the note were turned over in exchange for the bills.

The traveler took them to the window and examined them carefully; and seeing that they were the same as before, he turned,—and threw them into the fire. "Don't think 

I am crazy," he said. "THOSE BILLS ARE COUNTERFEIT."

At this his auditors were thrown into more terror than before. "The Lord help me," cried the butcher. "I have lost my horse and my mortgage interest isn't paid after all." "And the whole village will be in an uproar," shouted the innkeeper. "Dozens of honest deals will be upset and no one will know where he's at." "Don't be a fool," said the traveler. "YOU KNOW NOTH 

ING ABOUT MONEY." 

That night after they had somewhat recovered their composure, the butcher and the innkeeper pressed the traveler for an explanation of the enigma. "I am a simple man," said the traveler, "and cannot tell you much. But you can see for yourself that money is a thing about which you should not talk. It is a secret for bankers. It will never do to tell your neighbors about what has happened today. As the innkeeper has said, there will be an uproar, and trouble for everybody. True thoughts on money, moreover, should not be cast before swine, for they will certainly turn and rend you." "But," said the innkeeper, "that does not explain why it worked."

"It worked," said the traveler, "because money is merely a solvent into which things are dissolved in order to be crystallized out again into other things, and is not valuable for itself. It is when men's minds get twisted and they think of this mere solvent as a thing valuable for itself, that trouble begins. If money is legal (or is accepted as if such), its 'intrinsic value' is immaterial." "But," continued the innkeeper, "suppose you hadn't returned." "Oh ho!" said the traveler. "In that case I would have gained one silver teapot and the value of the note.

But you wouldn't have known the difference, for in this simple village no one would ever have discovered the counterfeit." "But, who then," persisted the innkeeper, "would have lost the value of my silver teapot which you gained?" "The whole village," said the traveler. "I would have successfully levied a capital tax of one teapot on the community. You see, that is what a government does when it creates fiat money, only it makes its counterfeit money legal tender and forces you to accept it. If the issue is moderate, the activity it induces, as in the case of this village, more than offsets the injury. But if the issue is too great, the results are serious. You have heard of Germany and Russia, haven't you?" "I see" said they both in chorus, greatly mystified but impressed. "But I returned," continued the traveler, "and gave back the assets for which I issued the money, so I was like a good central bank. By an 'expansion of credit' I created money to loan to you, Mr. Butcher, and I conducted an

'open market operation' by purchasing silver from you, Mr.

Innkeeper. But now that I have given back these assets and retired the money, there has been a deflation, and I think the village is going to have another slump. Citizens may have all possible 'confidence', but there being no fundamental basis for it, it will be only an excuse for further losses." "Can't you prevent that?" anxiously asked the innkeeper. "Everyone is so cheered up that no one will believe that we are about to have another depression. You shouldn't have destroyed the bills." "I can't help you," said the traveler, "because I have no more counterfeit bills; but if I were really a central bank I would manufacture some bills or bank deposits to loan to you, or to anyone else who by the use of them could make profits sufficient to pay the interest. Then the reissue would be justified; provided taxes were not increased so as to take away the profits." "But," said the butcher, "how is anyone to get money to pay interest any more than I was able to do, however much he may work." "Ah!" said the traveler. "Right you are. It is very sad," and he arose to go. "But wait, wait!" cried the butcher. "One more question. Why did I have so much trouble about my interest?” "Oh, that," replied the traveler, "is the working of the gold standard. You see, I, as central banker, had reached the limit of my reserves and couldn't properly loan anything more to enable you to pay the interest without having too much credit outstanding in relation to my gold holdings; so I had to force you to get some gold, even though

it meant bringing panic to the whole village, unless you could find some more gold in a mine, or from outside somewhere. This happens all the time to the whole world, so there is nothing to complain about. As central banker I wasn't able to allow credit to expand in accordance with the needs of the community." "It follows," continued the traveler, musing aloud "that mankind cannot possibly advance faster than the pace at which the miner of new gold walks. As often as man tries to produce faster, he is pulled back to gold standard reality after an interval by a cyclical depression; although man, by discovering steam and electricity and gas motors and other wonders, should be advancing at a tremendously faster pace. It's a funny thing, but world production as a whole has gone ahead only at the average rate of about three per cent. per annum since the Eighteenth Century,-which happens to be the average rate of central bank interest and the rate of gold production for that period." "Well, why do we have a gold standard then," asked the innkeeper. "Because we are used to it," said the traveler, "and we like it." "I don't understand you at all," said the butcher. "I didn't suppose you would," said the traveler, leaving the room. "He is a funny man," said the butcher. "But it is true that the village has been revived." "Just the same, I don't believe it's going to have another slump," said the innkeeper. "You didn't believe it before the last one," replied the butcher.

Most hearers think there is some catch in this story. 

There is none. It is real. Let us examine this realness. 

1. The first thing we see is that a whole community may be ready to pay their debts, deal with one another, do useful labor, and create real wealth, but are unable if there is no money, because the methods of barter are too clumsy. 

There were no excess supplies or overproduction in the village, though there were persons who wished to work and things people wished to sell. There was no lack of desire to employ labor and to buy. But there was an absence of demand in terms of money; the only demand which is effective except in a barter economy. That is, there was no "circulating medium" through which the unquestioned human demand could express itself as money demand. Mr. 

R. G. Hawtrey in his new book puts it well: "In the modern world 'demand' is a purely monetary phenomenon.

It is demand in this sense that governs economic activity. " Trade Depression (1933), p. 101.

What is true where money is completely absent as in the village, is equally true if money is absent only to a degree. If you have some money, but not enough, you will have poverty, low standards, and relative stagnation, perhaps (with short interruptions) for long periods, as for instance the 23 years from 1873 to 1896. While if there is enough money or a little more than enough, activity is strong, unemployment is absorbed, and general welfare is high. I do not say that there are not other factors of importance, but without a favorable money factor other favorable factors cannot operate.

Especially there seems to be a definite relation of the supply of money to the long-term trend of trade. Pains-

taking researches show that if the money expands with this trend, prices are stable, if it goes above this trend they rise, if below they fall. It is far below today. You will be greatly repaid in a most practical way if you study the facts as given in an article by Dr. Carl Snyder, of the New 

York Federal Reserve, with illuminating charts, in the Economic Forum, Vol. I, No. 3, p. 275 ff. I quote his summation: "If the quantity of circulating medium be increased at an even pace with the normal long-time increase of trade and industry, the price level, and therefore the long chain of other economic effects, will be approximately stable." (L. c., p. 282.)

This conclusion has very important implications, for the trend of trade has a definite relation to the increase of population. From 1830 to 1930, and probably for long before, "the rate of our industrial expansion per individual has not changed substantially through the entire period. 

The rate of increase so shown has been very close to 22 per cent per annum" (L. c., p. 288). These researches, therefore, indicate a definite MEASURE of the quantity of reflation necessary to absorb the normal capacity per individual, and thus really absorb unemployment, and at the same time restore prices to a proper relation to the existing structure of debts and fixed costs. THIS ABSORPTION 

IS THE TRUE TEST OF RECOVERY, RATHER THAN SOME ARBITRARY PRICE LEVEL (e. g. 1926). 

2. The second thing the fable tells us is that the usefulness of money does not depend upon "intrinsic" value, or upon its having gold or some special value "back" of it, but money can be completely valueless in itself. In barbarous times, or in the case of weak governments, it is

necessary for money to have some "intrinsic" value in order to be accepted. In such times people need to hoard metal to protect themselves against disorder and the disappearance or perfidy of governments. In other words, money, in addition to being money, must be also a precious commodity. But this has disappeared from our modern money. If anyone has any doubt of it, he has merely to consider what happened to the owners of gold dollars and United States gold certificates, which were supposed to be warehouse receipts for gold.

Except under barbarous conditions, one accepts money in trade, not because one wants it for itself, whether it be gold, silver or paper. One does not want to eat it. He takes it solely because it is, pro tanto, exchangeable by law, at convenience, into all purchasable things; and it is the whole bulk of these purchasable things, that is, all goods on offer, that gives value to the whole of the money that is "in circulation." When, therefore, you sell an article for a portion of this money, you are really bartering the article in exchange for a sight draft on a corresponding portion or fraction of all-goods-that-are-on-offer. 

The unit of money therefore is properly ALL MONEY, and the so-called monetary units we are familiar with, namely, dollars, are merely fractions of this great unit. 

The size of these fractions mathematically varies of course with their number; and one dollar consequently is merely the numerator of a fraction of which the number of all dollars that are out is the denominator. The value or purchasing power of one dollar is that fraction of the-totalof-all-goods-on-offer. Consequently the value of units of money vary with their quantity. 10

It follows that to speak of money as a "measure of value" is very misleading. A measure is something which does not vary, such as a yardstick, for instance. The number of yardsticks obviously does not interfere with the measuring power of each yardstick, whether there be one or a thousand million in use. But in the case of money this is exactly what makes all the difference. 

This has been known from the earliest times, long before paper money was known. Lord Desborough, in his remarkable pamphlet "On Money" gives a quotation (which I somewhat abridge) from the Jurisconsult Paulus, which was incorporated 300 years later into the Pandects of Justinian, and is as true now as when it was first written: "Primitively there was no money. Everyone bartered things useless to him, for those which would be useful to him. But as it would not always or easily happen that you had what I should wish for, and that conversely, I had what you wished to obtain, choice was made of a material, which being forever LEGAL value, would obviate the difficulties of barter by means of a quantitative equation; and this material, stamped in the corner by the State, circulates with a power which it derives not from the SUBSTANCE but from the QUANTITY."

The invention of this "material stamped by the State" was a tremendous step forward, for it enabled the barterer to keep "one side" of his barter "in suspense", readily divisible, and capable of being completed at a different time and a different place. Accordingly, one could barter a small cow for a large cow by putting a "price" on each in terms of this "material", or he might barter one cow in this village today, and the other cow or something else in another village year after next. In other words, this "material" became purchasing power in solution,-whether represented by tokens of metal, paper, or a mere credit entry in the books of a bank. 11

The results proceeding from this introduction of flexibility of time and place into barter were enormous. One of the most important was "hoarding", whether over night or for years. In other words, there is, in addition to the immediate demand for goods through money, a certain demand for money itself as a storehouse of future power to get goods.

This special demand for money slows up the velocity of turnover of money, which is the third thing to be noticed in our fable. 

3. It was the velocity of turnover of the $100 bills that permitted the revival in the village; for the usefulness of the bills was obviously multiplied by the number of times they were turned over; it being a principle that a dollar used ten times is the same as ten dollars used once. Accordingly, in setting up the monetary equation, money is multiplied by velocity. The usual simplified money equation is:

Quantity of Money x Velocity =Price Level x Quantity of Goods or "Trade".

It is obvious that if one of these factors varies, one or more of the other factors in the equation must change. It is also obvious that if two factors on opposite sides vary together, e.g., Velocity and Trade, they should be eliminated from both sides of the equation.

The item of velocity is the chief red herring used by the opponents of the quantity theory of money to throw all thought on it into confusion, usually ignoring that Trade and Velocity tend to vary together. In the village no one hoarded the money or it would have spoilt the story; but 12

everyone spent the money as quickly as he received it. If this happened in real life, velocity could be entirely ignored in computing the value of money, as it would vary exactly with the volume of Trade. But the practice of keeping cash balances, as well as other hoarding, slows up velocity, while violent distrust of the money, as in post-war Germany, for instance, speeds it up. Nevertheless, in normal practice velocity can be ignored, for the researches of Dr. Carl 

Snyder and others clearly indicate that normally velocity varies with "Trade", and therefore may be stricken out from both sides of the equation. This leaves the Price Level varying with the Quantity of Money.

The principle represented by this monetary equation is the famous Quantity Theory of Money. 

This Theory has had the support of the greatest economists. Ricardo, who was a man of affairs as well as a great economist, says of it: "The value of money in any country is determined by the amount existing. That commodities would rise or fall in price in proportion to the increase or diminution of money, I assume as a fact that is uncontrovertible."

The most well known and prophetic of our modern economists writes: 

"THIS THEORY IS FUNDAMENTAL. ITS CORRESPONDENCE WITH FACT 

IS NOT OPEN TO QUESTION. Nevertheless it is often misstated and misrepresented. Goschen's saying of sixty years ago that 'there are many persons who cannot hear the relation of the level of prices to the volume of currency affirmed without a feeling akin to irritation,' still holds good." Keynes, Monetary Reform, p. 81.

It is, in fact, so irritating to the owner of money to be told that its value can be lowered merely by increasing the quantity outstanding, that it is not surprising that many refuse to acknowledge the principle; and that an emo- 13

s tional complex has been built up which causes, especially in America, a valiant little group of economists and newspapers passionately to attack, and constantly try to prevent by innuendo, the general belief in this fundamental principle. Like the mariner's compass, the principle is not quite as absolute in all its details as its strongest adherents insist, and therefore is subject to "correction for compass error"; but like that compass it is the only reliable navigating instrument we have. It is therefore not surprising to find that those economists who, in the recent great storms, have passionately refused to look at this navigational aid, have been notoriously all at sea. 

The soundest criticism of the Quantity Theory is that it is a static principle of equilibrium, which, except in quiet weather, is constantly being disturbed by disequilibria (such, for instance, as those I refer to in my letter). The law that water seeks it level is a similar principle; and water's level, too, is often disturbed. Stormy winds cause waves and hollows; and subtractions and additions for the reservoirs of Nature and of man bring temporary changes of level. In like manner the winds of speculation or the demands of "saving" or hoarding, and other factors, disturb the price level; so that at one time goods seem more in demand and at another money. These are human and dynamic factors. They are most conveniently summed up in the Law of Supply and Demand, the principle of which we all understand. This fundamental law properly applies to money just as it does to any other article, and "quantity" is the chiefly controlling factor in its application to both goods and money. For short-term purposes the demand and supply point of view is perhaps the most satisfactory 14

way of looking at the money equation. But no hydraulic engineer or monetary economist while studying the dynamic flow of water or money, and estimating the extent or duration of the disequilibria, can afford to ignore the static principle which is the foundation of his science. And business men too will do well to take note that prices, like water, tend to seek their level,-a level which is roughly ascertainable and which is usually reached after an interval. 

I do not hesitate to say that without the Quantity Theory to guide him, one cannot see his way through the present monetary chaos, while with it he can proceed surely and with understanding.

A clear grasp of it, for instance, makes clear at once why the gold policy of Professor Warren is a fallacy and does not work.

If the gold content of a coin did not control its purchasing power even in the days of Justinian, when all money was metallic, what a waste of time it is to discuss the gold content of the dollar, which is not even a coin, but a piece of inconvertible paper in the hands of our citizens. 

How clear it is that if it is desired to lower the purchasing power of the dollar, one must create and put into circulation more dollars.

Of course the gold policy eventually will cause an increase of dollars as a secondary result; for by raising the official value of our gold reserve to almost eight billion dollars, the possibility has been created of an enormous future issue of new dollars on this enlarged base, if we cling to gold standard rules. But the new money will be of no help to the situation until it is in circulation. Also the gold policy results in a "gold profit" to the Government of just 15

under three billion dollars, which in effect is the creation of fiat money to this amount. But this new money also will be of no help until it is out,-by the Government spending the profit, which it seems to be in no hurry to do. Also to the extent that new gold flows into the country, new dollars are put out to a corresponding amount. But such slow secondary effects are not what the proponents of the policy have in mind.

The application of the Warren theory has immediately shown that it is not gold that gives value to money, but that it is the money that gives value to gold; for the establishing of a paper money price of $35. instead of $20.67 per ounce has had no recognizable effect on the internal purchasing power of the dollar, but has promptly raised the value of gold throughout the world. This is plainly seen by observing that present American prices in terms of old gold dollars are lower than they were when President Roosevelt took office,—a matter that should not disturb us in the least; except that our Treasury foolishly gives foreigners good American dollars in exchange for their gold at a high and artificial price. The "gold profit" of $14.33 per ounce from these transactions does not go into the 

United States Treasury, but is a bonus absurdly given away to the foreigner at the expense of our citizens. 

I can hear you saying, "The foregoing is all very well, but no one can be expected really to believe that money is good that is not backed by something."

The money I am discussing is backed by all-the-goodson-offer in the country; which ought to be something. But this is not what you mean. You refer to the deposit of gold, or government bonds, or of some other specific "security". 16

This theory of "backing" is one of the most misleading of all the misconceptions about money. Fundamentally it is the fallacy of the famous John Law, who believed that if money is issued for a valuable commodity, or is backed by a valuable commodity, all is well. In the time of John 

Law, real estate was the most valuable of all men's possessions. The entire social structure of France was built upon it. What more reasonable then than to propose, as 

John Law did, that real estate should be used as backing for the issue of money? This was done with disastrous results, because there was too much real estate, and the more money you issued against it, the more its value rose, and the greater the quantity of money. The good value behind the money did not protect its purchasing power. 

The same thing happened again in France of the revolutionary period. The famous assignats were not a baseless paper currency. They were backed by valuable real estate.

The same applied to the money proposed by Kellogg, the "Father of Greenbackism", whose money was to be secured by real estate mortgages. 

The same in principle was the money of Robert Owen in 1832 which represented day's labor, for which he issued currency notes. This idea of work as a basis for currency has been revived again in our day by the technocrats and others.

A similar fallacy is the idea that money is sufficiently protected if we issue it against "eligible paper" which represents actual short-term "self-liquidating" transactions. This is the "elastic money" defect in the Federal

Reserve Act, of which some of its founders are so proud. 

If these transactions tend to become too great on a rising 17

price level, there may be no sufficient limitation on "quantity". This has been partly our experience, and was emphatically the experience in Germany, where the then head of the Reichsbank innocently believed that all was well because he was issuing his floods of new paper only in accordance with the needs of expanding business and price levels; all duly needed for transactions and backed by government bonds.

Backing by gold is not different. Only it has so happened that until now gold has been limited in quantity, and, as the relation of the amount of paper to gold was restricted, the issue of too great a quantity of money has usually been prevented. But now that we have revalued gold, the same principle is again at work to make gold a dangerous backing, if we allow it to have its automatic effect, because, at present, the United States has too much gold.

We now have in this country almost eight billions of gold at the new valuation. If paper money and deposit currency are issued on this new base in accordance with the old rules, there will soon be a total issue in excess of 200 billions of dollars, or over five to six times the present amount. Some observers would figure much higher. In other words, our monetary system, if left to itself, will presently so expand as to cause the dollar to have a purchasing power of less than one-fifth of its present value. 

The reason is that our gold does not at the moment provide a sufficient "limitation" on the "quantity" of our money.

A conservative, therefore, must be for a management of our currency and against an automatic operation of the 18

gold standard. The old die-hards, who are shouting for a return to the automatic gold standard, are now the real inflationists, little as they realize it.

This brings out sharply what is the essence of the principle of money: Its QUANTITY is controlling, and its soundness depends upon a proper LIMITATION of that quantity.

The limitation should not be rigid, because we are dealing with a fluctuating and living organism,-in fact, the very life of the community. 

Heretofore money has been managed primarily to maintain its parity with everybody else's money; that is, in order to walk in lock-step with other nations. Thus the diseases of one nation, its follies and its mistakes, tended to pull down all other nations to its level; and deflation, once started in one place, tended to spread throughout the civilized world. But the managed currency for which I argue in my letter to Mr. Lippmann and which was the objective of Governor Strong, and of the others I mention, is money so managed as to keep activity of employment and price levels as stable as possible, at what may be called the optimum level.

The inflationists, and for that matter, the President, are properly strong for restoring the 1926 price level. It is easy to obtain that price level. It is necessary only to increase local and federal taxes sufficiently, as well as to insist on further increases in labor and other costs. If you question the truth of this statement, consider what taxation has done for whiskey. Its price level is above the 1926 level. But is anyone pleased?

But in a healthy restoration of prices by increasing the quantity of money, prices do not rise to any great extent 19

until activity is increased and unemployment is absorbed. 

This is the real test of true recovery.

Artificial price levels created by taxation, high costs, and restriction are not of this healthy character, do not provide quantity of production, lower the standard of living, and do not absorb unemployment. 

4. The fourth thing the fable does is to let the reader into the secret of how money is manufactured. 

Most people unconsciously believe that money exists by nature, is merely recognized by governments, by stamping at the mint, and that it is the deposit in bank of this child of nature that allows the banks to make loans "with their depositors' money." Nothing could be farther from the fact. Deposits are manufactured by the banks out of nothing, as a result of their loans, (or investments). While your money on deposit may have come to you as a transfer by check, or currency, of somebody else's deposit, nevertheless it was so created originally. 

Except in the case of fiat money (and often then), it is by the manufacture of deposits that our money is created. 

How this is done our traveler makes plain by what he did, except that in his case he passed out bills only, instead of passing out an entry in a check book, or a certificate of deposit. By acquiring a silver "investment" and a "loan" he enabled the innkeeper and the butcher to start passing around in the village pieces of intrinsically worthless paper, which nevertheless acted as veritable money. Not otherwise does a bank act when it manufactures deposits, by what I called in my letter a creative bookkeeping transaction. On the asset side is set up a "loan" or "investment" acquired, and on the liability side a credit subject to check; 20

in other words a "deposit". These "deposits" are transferred from person to person by check. These checks serve as actual money in more than 90 per cent. of the transactions of the country. 

When "loans" are called and "investments" sold by the banks, as occurred on an enormous scale all through 

1930-31-32-33, money is turned in to pay off the "loans" and buy the "investments", and when received, is destroyed, exactly as in the case of the bills of our traveler. 

Deflation follows, and depression and unemployment become extreme. The practical measure of this destruction is the shrinkage of the "loans and investments" of the banks; hence the figure of 19 billion dollars in my letter. 

Ordinary currency bills are not different in their inception. Bills are issued either in payment of deposits, or, to a very limited extent, for the direct purchase of an asset (e. g., gold or silver) in the fashion of our traveler. For, on principle, the purchase of gold or silver by the treasury, mint, central bank, or commercial bank, is as much an "open market policy" operation, as the purchase of a silver teapot, or any other asset or "investment", against the creation (out of nothing) of money,-whether or not this money in the first instance is represented by bills or a credit in a deposit account.

Observe particularly that the deposit currency is the important money of the country; because if more than the usual amount of bills are put into circulation by the government, they will promptly be deposited by the public in banks and thereby turned into deposit currency; except, of course, in times of extreme fright. This fact makes the importance given to ordinary currency in congressional de- 21

bates seem very unreal and aimless. The important factor goes unnoticed. The real barometer on which the eye should be focused is the deposits of the commercial banks, -our deposit currency. "Fiat" money it is scarcely necessary to discuss. I think the fable indicates clearly enough how, in effect, it is a capital tax. If the policy is pursued as a system of taxation it soon causes a general breakdown, as in the case of Germany, and is subject to a most surprising principle of diminishing tax return, as the German Government learned to its cost; but I cannot stop to discuss that here. 

To sum up:

There are two kinds of factory: (1) the central bank, treasury or mint (it makes no difference which), and (2) commercial banks. The central bank in this country is of course the Federal Reserve System. There are two kinds of product: (1) printed bills, and (2) checks (viz., deposit currency). These are really two forms of the same thing; for bills are virtually checks payable to bearer; and I am told that in England, where they love to keep up old forms, you may still be required to endorse a five pound note, if you present it at a shop where you are not known. I ignore silver dollars and subsidiary coinage for brevity, since they do not really alter the picture. There are three methods of manufacture: (1) by loans or "extending credit," (2) by purchase of assets or "open market policy", and (3) by printing press or "fiat".

There are no other ways by which money is now created. 

But you must not assume that each commercial bank is free to manufacture deposit currency at its own sweet will. 22

Not only is each bank restrained by its legal reserve requirements, but if it creates deposits at a more rapid rate than its neighbors, the normal transfer of deposits to other banks through its customers checks, without a corresponding influx of deposits from other banks, will presently get it into trouble; "for every movement forward by an individual bank weakens it, but every such movement by one of its neighbor banks strengthens it; so that if all move forward together, no one is weakened on balance". By the same principle, when some banks start contracting deposits by calling loans and selling investments, all banks are forced to retreat. "Each bank chairman sitting in his parlor, regards himself as the passive instrument of outside forces over which he has no control; yet the outside forces may be nothing but himself and his fellow chairmen." 

Since in 1929 there were 24,340 banks in this country, and we still have some 14,000, this analysis presents a picture of a churning mob, now milling forward and now churning back, and likely at any moment (as in 1929-33) to make a headlong rush in a new and wrong direction. The true picture, however, is not as bad as that. Fortunately, there is leadership. The central bank has power to control, if it will, this otherwise confused herd.

Unfortunately, on the other hand, the central bank in this country is the Federal Reserve System, which consists of twelve Reserve banks with separate and scattered boards, and the Federal Reserve Board in Washington consisting of eight members. This is Army Command by committeeor possibly by several discordant committees. It is not therefore surprising that in 1928-29 and thereafter, after the disappearance of the commanding statesmanship of 23

Governor Benjamin Strong, that the Federal Reserve control did not rise to its responsibilities. The currency was wrecked and the country deflated and bankrupted, because the System had not the coherence, courage and vision to use its resources and stupendous reserves effectively and in time. Since we were on the gold standard, our disease was irresistibly spread to the whole gold standard world, ably assisted by deflationary follies in England. 

And yet the banking mechanism is one of the most striking, important, and indispensable inventions of modern civilization. Without it the present population and high standard of living of mankind, and especially of the United States, would be impossible. This mechanism has been a slow and natural growth, far more complicated than the works of a watch. Most bankers are uncomprehending cogs -all the more useful often because they are uncomprehending. It is of the utmost importance not to destroy or disrupt this delicate structure. The important thing is to understand the system, remedy its obvious defects, and give it legal direction; for heretofore the central bankers have apparently been unwilling, without direct legal mandate, to assume the full responsibility belonging to their position. 

5. The problem of the butcher with his interest, and the reflections of the traveler thereon, are a crude attempt to illustrate certain striking views explained with elaborate charts by Dr. Robert Eisler on his recent visit to this country, and stated by him to be based on an important thesis of the brothers Guillaume presented in France in January, 1932. I have not been privileged to study the

Guillaume thesis, but the case as explained by Dr. Eisler 24

is far from proved. Nevertheless, the facts as to gold, interest rates and average rate of world production, and the occurrence of periodic crises, are outstanding facts which cannot be ignored.

What is it that causes the periodic checking of industry and employment? What is it that limits industrial expansion in the world to an average of 3 per cent per annum, and in the United States to 4.3 per cent per annum? What is it that has limited industrial expansion in the United States to 22 per cent per annum per individual, the same in 1830 and 1930?

Whether or not the Guillaume principle is responsible or, as I think, some other cause (as for instance, an average automatically struck between the struggles of the productive portion of mankind to produce, and the impossible accumulations of the compounding of interest due to savings), the underlying principle-whatever it is-has expressed itself in modern times through the scarcity of gold. Gold does not increase fast enough to allow money based thereon to match the increasing population and productivity of man. Since men persist in borrowing money, and trying to produce more and more, the industry of a whole country frequently gets into the position of the butcher, and panic and depression follow. The situation often is bad enough to cause one or another country to leave the gold standard and even to revalue its currency. 

England, for instance, has left the gold standard five times in a century, while other countries repeatedly have done the same and often have revalued as well. 

The same appears to have been true throughout the history of civilization. The pound sterling, for instance, 25

was revalued to a lower content on thirteen different occasions between 1066 and 1551, falling from 4995 gr. of pure silver to 400 gr.; while Lord Desborough quotes the historian Alison as attributing the two greatest events in the history of mankind-the fall of the Roman Empire, and the resurrection of mankind from the disintegration of society which is known as the Dark Ages-as directly due to the scarcity or abundance of the currency medium. The resurrection which followed the influx of new gold and silver from the Americas in the sixteenth century has been thus strikingly described: "There is no limit to human demand, and there is no limit to the power of mankind to meet that demand. For hundreds of years, in the middle ages, the laws of supply and demand were stationary. Everything worked, year after year, at a uniformly low level and all the population lived miserably. Then, on a sudden, wages began to rise, enterprises began to quicken-all kinds of new articles and comforts opened to the working people, the middle classes and the rich. The laws of supply and demand suddenly began to work on a much larger and expanding scale."

History tends to repeat itself and we now see a worldwide scarcity of money and credit and fall of prices bringing about a trend back to the middle ages; with the suppression of parliaments and individual liberty, the appeal to force and fascism, the control of prices, the creation of guilds and restrictions, and the universal impoverishment of the people. These facts make one appreciate the witty reply as to the probable length of the present depression: "The only comparable depression lasted more than a thousand years, and was known as the Dark Ages." 26

Until modern times there was no escape from the inexorable results of the scarcity of the precious metals, since all money was metallic. The invention of credit and paper money, however, and the new learning as to the principles governing it, give a way of escape from this needless enslavement. Yet the tradition of gold is strong in men's minds, and until now it has been necessary to keep money in close relation to gold; so that the relief has been halting and partial.

In order to soften the rigors of the connection to gold, resort has been had to various expedients euphemistically called "economizing" gold, such as: gathering all gold reserves into a Federal Reserve System, and permitting pyramiding thereon; lowering reserve requirements; retiring all gold coins and establishing a bullion standard; establishing a gold exchange standard (viz., counting gold twice, once at home and a second time in a foreign country) and various forms of "kiting". Only by these expedients, and by temporary desertions during periods of stress or war, and devaluations, has the gold standard been continued since it first became an international system about 1873. 

Otherwise it would have completely blown up long before now. But man is a patient and persistent animal, and after each eruption heretofore he has trustingly set to work to rebuild his villages on the slopes of this volcano. 

I can imagine the ruined villagers on Vesuvius also saying with our traveler "we are used to it; and we like it." 

If the connection to gold is loosened or entirely severed, and the money principles at work thoroughly understood, it should become possible through the money machine freely to adjust the problems of savings and compound in- 27

terest without restricting production; thus avoiding the useless waste of throwing whole populations into unemployment, piling up enormous new debts and taxes to feed or employ them, and bringing governments and capitalism to the very verge of revolution. The details are too complicated and controversial for discussion here; but I wish to suggest that if these things be true, they indicate an almost unlimited improvement in man's material standard of living, if only we are sane.

By now you are thoroughly bored; but nevertheless it is essential that I touch briefly on two further points. 

6. One of these is the principle of the Rate of Return: which is the determinant of the value of things not directly consumed.

Man so loves and deifies things that he usually thinks of them as valuable in themselves. Actually they are valuable only for what he can get out of them-their rate of return. 

It is true that some persons, like jackdaws, value things merely for accumulation. But these are a special case and do not alter the general rule. Nothingness, if it have a rate of return, is valuable, though it be no thing. Property that is not usable, that brings in nothing, and can bring in nothing, is worthless. 

Your beautiful garden on the Hudson would be valueless to you if you were deprived of sight and smell. But each Spring and Summer it brings you fresh and sure returns of perfume and beauty which reach your consciousness and are of real value to your soul. Your field beyond, which is now an unsightly tangle, you value because your imagination pictures the time when it too will be added to the garden and bring also its annual returns of perfume and loveliness. Its return is prospective. 28

In practical economics, the annual return is in money: present, prospective, or hoped for. money.

If a government issues an interest-bearing bond, its value is determined by its annual rate of return as compared with the prevailing rate of interest. If the bond bears no interest but has a fixed maturity, then its present value is its principal sum discounted to maturity. If the bond carries neither interest nor maturity, it can have no value; unless it is made legal tender; in which case it becomes a circulating writ of expropriation, namely, fiat

These things are so obvious that it might be thought unnecessary to recount them, were it not that there are many in the country who honestly believe that fiat money loses its vice if only we call it non-interest-bearing government bonds. And I am told that there are persons of prominence who are at a loss to point out the fallacy of paying off the presently outstanding government bonds by issues of fiat money; since both pieces of paper are thought to be obligations of the government, the fiat money differing only in bearing no interest and having no date of maturity. 

On the same principle, the loans against which banks create deposits, as discussed above, have real value even though, as is often the case, they are unsecured, or are made to persons of no substantial property. The bank makes the loans because of the banker's personal knowledge and experience of the customer which justifies him in believing that the customer has both the integrity and the ability so to use the deposit as to be able to account for the principal, when due, with a proper rate of return. If this judgment is sound, the value is real. 

A new invention is only an idea, which is no thing. But it creates literally millions of new value if it will bring 29

in sure returns which, when capitalized, equal such sums. 

Such creative nothingness may be worth far more than the expensive bricks and mortar of an ill-conceived plant, which only encumbers the real estate on which it stands. 

In like manner, if an enterprising business man can produce assured earnings where others have failed, he makes out of nothing a business which is worth the figure at which it properly can be capitalized. It is the prospect of creating this new capital value and the possibility of turning it over again into yet new ventures, valuable alike to the public and himself, that excites his imagination, his energy, and his daring. Such an individual does not wish to sit back like a small shopkeeper merely to receive the daily takings of the shop. It is a foolish government which does its best to render immobile these high, creative qualities by a capital-gains tax-especially taxation which takes no proper account of losses. The free movement of such men has built a large part of the wealth and greatness of the 

United States.

These instances show why, if the country is to be richer, it is essential that profits be made possible; for without profits there is no return and in the long run no capital values, whether for tax or other purposes. It follows that in order to restore prosperity and absorb unemployment and raise the price level and balance the budget, it is not sufficient merely to provide plentiful credit, or to increase the quantity of the country's money; it is essential to create conditions under which human beings can make profits and are encouraged to do so. One of these is to remove all obstacles to enterprise and the financing of enterprise, such as punitive taxation on enterprise, im- 30

proper Securities Act restrictions, drastic stock exchange bills, and policies attacking the profit motive. Another is to reduce the long-term rate of interest. Everything that 

I said in my Lippmann letter on this latter subject has since been justified by the result of the policy of the Treasury in purchasing long-term government bonds and maintaining a "good market" in government bonds. 

Since the first of the year, Secretary Morgenthau has, by quiet and able use of this method, lowered the long-term rate of interest and lifted the prices of all classes of bonds about ten points, re-established the credit of the government, and indirectly supported the prices of all classes of securities in what otherwise would have been a dangerously breaking stock market. Every security (whether bonds or stock) that has a return, or that is expected to have one, has been affected. The lowering of the basic rate of long-term interest does not (except in a secondary way) affect the real yield of fixed capital. But it directly affects the rate of interest at which the prospective money yield of fixed capital is capitalized in order to arrive at its present money value. This, in turn, affects the attractiveness of investment in new capital goods, and were not the way effectively barred by the Securities Act and other measures, new funds and credit would flow freely into the heavy-goods markets and start a broad absorption of unemployment. 

7. The second point is the function and effect of gold in foreign exchange. This is so intricate a matter that 

I can do little more than refer to it in this short space. 

But no one who is interested should fail to read the most brilliant exposition of this subject which has yet been pro- 31

duced-all in 38 pages. I refer to "The Coming Collapse in Gold" by L. L. B. Angas, pages 40-78.

Money of one country is not accepted in another. So if, having dollars here, you wish to spend money in Britain, it is necessary for you to sell dollars and buy pounds. If neither country is on the gold standard, the price at which this exchange can be made is determined purely by the supply and demand of the two articles-the exchange price varying in accordance therewith. Mr. Angas strikingly points out how it is entirely practical and convenient to conduct international business under this system. The international manufacturer and trader can protect himself from fluctuations through the forward exchange market, and in normal times the whole method is both reasonable and smooth. No gold is needed to settle international balances and no international money is necessary. This we know from actual experience of important currencies over long periods. Moreover, such a system has special advantages: "The beauty of the paper system is that as soon as any disequilibrium between demand and supply occurs, a sharp fluctuation in the price of foreign exchange immediately takes place, which, by reacting rapidly on both exports and imports, rapidly redresses the disequilibrium. The law of supply and demand is thus allowed to operate freely and quickly-as theoretically it should in any market. "Indeed, permanently to fix an exchange rate, i. e., the 'price, in a market subject to sharp short-run and long-run variations in demand or supply, arising out of such varied and haphazard factors as short- and long-term loans, political debts, investments, flights of capital, surplus imports, and so on, is theoretically uneconomic and unsound, since it interferes with the natural and extremely necessary correctives of the Law of Supply and Demand. Paper exchanges do not have this fault; gold exchanges undoubtedly do; the result is that under the gold standard serious reactions are set up elsewhere, e. g., in the home prices, which eventually do even more damage to general trade than is done by fluctuating paper exchanges. (L. c., p. 56.) 97 32

But the international banker, whose business it is to borrow and lend money and move it freely about the world without regard to national boundaries, naturally finds this system inconvenient. He reminds me of a New York friend, who spends a part of each winter in California and thinks it reasonable to demand that the school curricula of New York and of a small town at the other end of the continent, shall be exactly the same, in order that his two small sons may leave one classroom in New York and enter another in California without inconvenience or dislocation. The immense rigidity of such a system does not appall him, and he believes that 125 million people should be regimented and the autonomy of forty-eight separate commonwealths destroyed for his private convenience. 

The banker in like manner wishes all currencies tied rigidly together. Under the gold standard he accomplishes this linking on the simple principle that things equal to the same thing are equal to each other. All currencies are made convertible at fixed ratios into a single commodity (gold) which, by special privilege, is allowed to move freely across all national boundaries without tariff or other restrictions.

Gold derives its chief value, and is important in foreign exchange, solely owing to the fact that it has constituted a link between the money of different countries in the manner mentioned. Unless this idea is clearly grasped, it seems to me no clarity can be achieved in thinking about gold in foreign exchange.

The establishment of this link by a country is a surrender of its sovereignty in the most vitally important of its foreign relations. Such a surrender could never have 33

occurred had it not come as an unperceived result of a little-understood and innocent-seeming monetary device of international banking. "What that means is that every country has had to adapt its incomes to the incomes prevailing in all others. So long as the gold standard remained in effective operation, no one of them could afford to stand aside, for if its incomes failed to contract as fast as those of its neighbors, it would lose gold. . 

To each separate country it appears as pressure from the others. 

We no longer have to consider a single banking system, but a number of independent banking systems connected together only by the gold standard.

The expansion or contraction of incomes throughout the gold-using world is brought about by the unco-ordinated action of a number of independent authorities recognizing no common aim, no leadership, no responsibility to one another." R. G. Hawtrey, L. c., p. 13 ff.

If a country breaks the link by leaving the gold standard, or if the rigidity of the link is modified by providing a variable price for gold as we have recently done, immediately that country becomes master in its own house, while corresponding effects appear in the foreign exchanges. 

The surrender of sovereignty may benefit whoever can affect, or obtain control of, the movement of the available supplies of gold. Whoever momentarily has the knowledge, or the ignorance, or the selfishness, to grasp this opportunity for control, has the gold nations in his economic power. During the nineteenth century England developed and enjoyed this power without being challenged, because she used it with knowledge and moderation. But the disorganization of the post-war period brought ignorance and selfishness into play. We ourselves suffered bitterly at the hands of France, then of England. But now we are the offender. By bidding a new high price for gold, we have lowered gold prices wherever they exist, and in a most signal manner have changed the political face of Europe by causing deflation in France, which has struck down the 34

economic welfare, and hence the political coherence, of the French, whose military and diplomatic primacy has been the keystone of the European system since Versailles. 

Despite the bewildered running to and fro of envoys and diplomats, like ants who are unaware of the superior force which has disturbed their ant-heap, we have seen in a few short months the destruction of the French position, economically, militarily and diplomatically; the virtual crumbling of their alliances; the desuetude of their League of Nations; and the rise of the star of Hitler, who threatens to hold the balance of power in Europe, unless indeed he too shall presently be overtaken by the effects of the same disease. Such is the Achilles heel which a country that adheres rigidly to metallism exposes to foreign manipulation.

It is quite clear that if a country's money is rigidly attached to a world metal, it is subject to all the world forces which bear upon that metal. But if its money is based upon its own credit and management, its money is independent, and subject solely to its own control. 

We ourselves returned to gold at the end of January. 

For the moment we are protected by the fact that we have returned at a lower relative figure for the dollar than any other major currency, and by the fact that our enormous gold holdings give us tremendous elbow room for management of the currency before the loss of gold becomes important. Moreover, we are retaining, in theory at least, the right to prevent the link from becoming rigid, by changing the gold price as may suit our convenience, and by reserving the right to license exports. But unless we maintain this wide margin of elbow room, or keep the flexibility 35

of the link real, in practice as well as in theory, we will finally become adjusted to our new gold relation and at some later date will again be subject to all the dangers of having attached ourselves to a world metal. Moreover, if we foolishly agree to a stabilization of exchanges at fixed rates, we will immediately abandon our present advantages, and once again surrender our independence and sovereignty for the convenience of international banking.

While our link with gold is not now a close one, and may be called a loose and fairly long string, rather than a rigid link, nevertheless this is not the situation of certain other currencies which affect world prices. Sterling, moreover, is kept in close relation to the gold-tied franc for political and economic reasons; while traders throughout the world, by reason of inveterate habit, stupidly translate paper prices into meaningless terms of gold. The value of gold is therefore vastly important to world prices, even though not controlling. Our government, therefore, has done great harm to us and the world by raising gold's value when it should have done the exact opposite in order to raise world prices and help world recovery. 

It is here that the argument of the more thoughtful believers in the use of silver becomes important. By a proper use of silver and other collateral means to affect the mass psychology of selfish men throughout the world who are grasping for gold and hoarding it diligently in all countries, it is possible for our government by selling gold and buying silver, to cause the release of these hoards --which would be like finding new gold mines in its effect on world prices-while at the same time increasing the use 36

of silver as money. This is a "market" operation, the success of which depends upon its handling for its full effect. 

If properly done, small means can produce great ends. 

As I said in my letter, a certain amount of subtlety is necessary in monetary management, and "method is more than half".

This and my Lippmann letter discuss money, not banking reform. The latter, while closely related, is an independent subject; and its discussion tends to allow, all too easily, a mental escape from the main issue; which is to understand the existing system and principles, and properly use them. What I have discussed requires little or no change from the present monetary set-up (except easing the new restrictions on the capital markets), but only adjustment of the existing machinery, grasp of the principles at work, and courage to carry them out,—which last would come of itself if the vision of those in authority were clear. I fear that for this reason my views will seem empty for the type of mind which demands for each trouble the passage of a new law, or the creation of a new corporation, or "authority" or other mechanism, at least.

You will object, no doubt, that in a democracy we cannot expect to get management free from political influence, and therefore dare not trust ourselves to a managed money, even though it be in a "frame" of gold. But this ignores that we are in the water and therefore cannot debate whether or not to swim. It is literally a case of sink or swim. That management will be subject to human frailty and political difficulties, is of course true. But this applies to all government, everywhere and in all forms. 

Yet only the anarchist is heard to propose that we should 37

do without government. We are forced to manage as best we can, and he who ignores this necessity has insufficiently analyzed the facts.

This letter is long, but, considering the subjects it endeavors to illumine, is very short. I am painfully aware that while brevity can be the soul of wit, it can also be the soul of error. I can only hope that my eliminations for brevity have been sufficiently intelligent, and my simplifications for emphasis sufficiently sincere, to enable the complete picture to tell its story and yet be true, as I earnestly believe it to be. If that is so, meticulous details are unimportant. 

Yours sincerely,

B. H. INNESS BROWN.
