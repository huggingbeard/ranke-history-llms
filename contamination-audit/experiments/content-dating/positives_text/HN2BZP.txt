Cutting the questions short, I shook hands with those who had made the flight possible, and climbed in. With seven hundred gallons of gas aboard, the take-off was likely to be the most dangerous part of the flight, for the gas I carried was sufficient to take me all the way to Europe without a stop.

With a wave of my hand, I shoved in the throttle and the motor roared in answer. In a moment the hangars were slip-

ping past in the ghostly light. Faster and faster I came toward the end of the field-speed I had to have; speed to lift me up in the air before the runways ended. The ship fought to swing from the path as if dreading the weight it would have to support. I flashed past the place where De Pinedo later died, and then, as I pulled back the stick at the last possible moment, the ship rose from the ground, seemed to hesitate as it tried its wings, and then, with the exhaust spitting red fire in the gray dawn, we rose from the field and were under way! 

I settled down to my job at once; this was old stuff. There was no Benny Griffin to check my navigation, to spell me at the controls or to give me cheer, but I figured that what I had done before I could do again, and this ocean hop was something I had done before. Through much better weather than I had encountered on my first flight, I covered the 1200 miles to Newfoundland swiftly, and started across the ocean to Europe. Below me were icebergs in the bays, but the weather was clear. 

Three hours later I was in clinging fog. Ice began forming on the wings at 8,000 feet and I had to dive 6,000 feet to try to get the ice off the wings in warmer air. As I pulled the ship up out of the dive, I heard a sharp crack as the plywood in the right wing gave way under the load of the ship and the ice. For moments on end I waited tensely for the wing to give way entirely, but no new sound occurred. The ice on the wings began to melt as I settled the ship lower, and this gave me hope, but as night came on and the thermometer dropped, I found myself flying blind, with the altimeter reading zero, staring at my instruments and wondering what moment ice would form more thickly despite my minimum altitude; or what moment a towering wave would clutch at the landing gear and drag me headlong into the sea.

Hours were years, black night and fog seemed to crush in on me, and only the calm, cheerful crackling of the motor gave me hope. The wing seemed to be holding, but I could not trust it and over all my fears and fancies was the dread of seeing that great pinion give way. Then, dawn broke in the east and I found myself in a clear space in the sky. Down below I saw a fishing boat, and circled it, hoping the fishermen would point to land, but they did not. So I flew on for three more hours, straining my eyes whenever I emerged from storm or fog, hoping to see land. And relentlessly I found myself being forced more and more to the North to avoid storms, which swept my course. 

Finally when I was so exhausted that it was a struggle even to read my instruments, I saw a very rugged, dark strip of coast which I identified as Norway, and I started inland for Oslo. 

But the strain was too terrific and I found that my tired brain would not figure navigation, so I decided to set the ship down at once, before I should drift off to sleep. Below was a beach strewn with boulders, but I put the ship down anyway, and miraculously avoided cracking it up in a wild and turbulent landing.

While the world had given me up for lost, as I was fifteen hours overdue in Europe, I slept in the home of the lighthouse keeper for two hours, and then got up wearily to telephone Oslo for help. Oslo was a hundred miles away, but two airplanes arrived quickly and brought gasoline which I needed very badly. The pilots also helped me repair a tear in the wing. Horses pulled my ship to an open space and I took off again at two in the morning and made Oslo in forty-five minutes. 

One hour later, I took off again with 300 gallons of gas, flew blind, across the Baltic sea over Leningrad and on over Russia to Moscow, with the throttle wide open, making a thousand miles in five hours and forty-one minutes. At the airdrome

were aviators I had met the previous year and they tossed me in a blanket in their enthusiasm. I slept for three hours and took off at midnight for Omsk, eighteen hundred miles away. 

The terrain below was foreboding, the Ural mountains and the vast steppes. I roared on into and through a black thunderstorm and accompanying lightning that was far from a pleasant companion, and when my body was about ready to give in once more, I landed at Omsk, damaging a shock cord on the ship as I did so. There I took a queer bath in a wooden shack where an old woman had me stretch out while a man poured hot water over me from a frying pan and she beat me with a bushy broom. 

I felt better afterwards, however, and had four hours sleep on a cot in the airport.

The next jump was another of 1800 miles, to Chita. 800 miles out of Omsk a gas line broke, and the fumes nearly overcame me. As I struggled to retain my senses, I spied an open field below and landed in it, but I hit a stump in doing so and it knocked off the tail surfaces. I crawled from the ship and for the second time found myself violently sick on Russian soil. 

Peasants found me and sent for a Soviet ship, which arrived from Novo-Sibirsk with mechanics who fixed my ship. I had to drain most of the gas in order to take-off from the field, and that made it necessary to stop an hour and a half away at Krasnoyarsk, in mountainous country. After one hour there, I hopped off for Irkutsk.

By this time the flight had resolved itself into a vague series of landings and take-offs and accidents. It is impossible to describe the strain and effort of racing a ship on a solo flight across strange country without either a companion or an automatic pilot to spell the flier at the controls.

Here I changed spark-plugs and took on 400 gallons of gas for the two-thousand-mile hop across Siberia and Mongolia to Khabarovsk, on the eastern rim of Siberia. I had had no chance to sleep, and I took off when feeling almost too weary to continue. I climbed to 10,000 feet to cross the mountains on the west side of Lake Baikal and when over the lake a downdraft of air dropped me to within 3,000 feet of the surface. On the other side of the lake an updraft shot me again to 10,000 feet and enabled me to cross the mountains in safety.

On and on I flew, too tired to figure but knowing that I still had 1,500 miles to traverse before I got to Khabarovsk. Finally 

I struck the Amur river, but it looked like two or three rivers to my tired eyes. Unable to concentrate enough to find the way to Khabarovsk, I decided to crash the ship in the river and

call it a day. Piling pillows around my head in the event of a terrific crash, I brought the ship in over a low, sandy island.

I fully expected to turn over in the soft sand, but the beach was perfect and the landing ideal. With a gasp of relief I dragged myself from the ship and fell down in a sound slumber.

At dawn, Russian peasants were standing around me, having crossed to the island in countless rowboats, and they eagerly helped me get supplies, only I found myself up against the need of using tractor gasoline and crude oil. I put it in the ship, however, and took off for the 300-mile flight to Khabarovsk with a motor that was not running sweetly.

Six hours of sleep in Khabarovsk did wonders for me, and with 550 gallons of gas in the tanks, I took off at 4 a.m. for 

Nome, Alaska, feeling chipper despite adverse weather reports. 

800 miles out over the Okhotsk sea I met an icy wall of clouds. 

I tried to avoid ice on the wings by flying low as I had over the ocean, but it was clearly a losing battle and I was forced to retrace my steps for 800 more miles, arriving once more, weary and discouraged, at Khabarovsk.

With four hours sleep and the ship checked and refueled, I again took off at 4 a.m. and crossed the Okhotsk sea successfully this time, but had to fight fog across the Siberian Mountains. I missed a few of the peaks by margins that had me squirming nervously, but I finally left the mountains and started out over the desolate Arctic wastes. Below me passed the 

Anadyr river, ahead lay the Bering Sea-over there to the east lay America, 600 miles or four hours away!

It was then that the oil pressure failed. I switched on my reserve tank, but the lines were frozen. A wave of helplessness swept over me as I realized I was licked, absolutely and thoroughly licked, for with no one to take the controls I had no

CLOUD chance to free the reserve tank of clogging ice. Fog began to close in again, and I watched the ground below. It was rugged tundra-land, and a landing meant a crash. Nowhere could I see a sign of life, a movement; nothing but endless Arctic waste. 

With the motor losing revolutions, I ran it wide open, close to the ground and ripped off the landing gear cleanly. Now I had a chance to land without being turned over by wheels that would fall through the frozen crust. Carefully I brought the ship in, sliding it along the ground like a sled, but even so the motor was forced back into the cockpit by the impact, spraining my ankle. I sank back exhausted and surveyed the situation. I had, for food, three chocolate bars. There was a gun in the ship, but the plains were desolate. It was bitterly cold. The end was there to be seen, and while I couldn't give up hope, I began writing the story in my diary in case the worst happened. 

For many days I lived in the ship, and then went down to the Anadyr river, near which I had purposely landed, and built a hut out of grass. I lined the hut with maps to keep the rain and snow out, but it wasn't very effective. The ground was frozen and the only way I could sleep was by building a fire and then scraping it away and lying in the warm ashes. But in twenty minutes the frost from the ground would again seep up in my bones and I'd start aching all over.

The days passed in a sort of dull agony. The sun never set. 

Events made unemotional marks in my memory, strange highlights return. There was the duck I shot after patiently waiting, and the seagulls that stole it while I made my way wearily to the plane to get a cylinder to use as a stove. There was the raft

THE CHAPTER XI Anadyr and — Home! 

HE Eskimo family went down the river by easy stages, and for a week or more I lived with them, ate with them, but seldom conversed with them, having given that up as a bad job. They had very little curiosity about me, and I was so tired and worn out from my enforced starvation and the strain, that I was content to be left alone, and to dig my fingers in the community stew with the rest of them. Then they reached a camping spot

near the sea and we lived a more gay life for a few days, until from their conversation I suddenly understood that we were near Anadyr, a remote settlement on the Bering Sea. I didn't rest then until I got some of the Eskimos to take me to the settlement. 

From there went the news I was alive, news which startled a world that had long given me up for lost. Still very weak and gaunt, I went back up the river with Ivar Lindenoff, an Englishspeaking Russian, and salvaged the motor and instruments from my ship. These we crated for shipment home, but they never arrived. Then, a few days later, word was received by wireless that Fred Troutman and other friends of mine were waiting for me in Nome with a sister ship to my "Century of Progress." I could still be the first to complete a solo flight around the world! 

Eagerly I asked the wireless operators to get permission from Moscow for my friends to enter Russian territory and bring me the ship in which I could complete my flight, but to my great disappointment I ran up against a brick wall. Political expediency would not allow it. Russia insisted on the honor of landing me on American soil, even though I must wait for their famous ace, Sigmund Levenevsky to fly from the Black Sea to Anadyr. 

So with my nerves jumpy, I waited days until the Russian ship arrived and with Levenevsky I flew across the Bering Sea to 

Nome. 

From there I sped toward New York, across Alaska, Winnipeg and into Toronto. There I picked up a Lockheed equipped with radio transmitting apparatus, and flew down through the 

United States, Buffalo, over the Hudson and above New York, and at a height of 8,000 feet made the first international broadcast from a plane in the air. From there I circled Floyd Bennett Field-how many times I had wondered if I would ever again see that airport!—and a few minutes more my wheels

I am proud of the United States as the most air-minded nation on earth, and grateful that the opportunities were mine to represent her in some few phases of the world-wide race to conquer the globe with sleek planes and wide-open motors.

CLOUD COUNTRY AVIATORS TEACH LESSON OF PRECAUTION TO MOTORISTS By Jimmie Mattern 

THE veteran fliers who daily pilot their planes through the skyways of the world offer a fine lesson in precaution and safety. 

This statement may seem far from believable but here are some facts that should interest every thoughtful motorist. 

Transatlantic birdmen, crack aces of the army air corps, and airline pilots alike regard their planes as living things. 

They "baby" their motors at all times and make certain that no undue strain is ever placed upon them. A good airman never begins a flight until his motor has been thoroughly "warmed up"-from ten to fifteen minutes. Commercial airline planes often are "warmed up" for more than thirty minutes before the scheduled take-off time. 

The sound of a smooth-running motor is pleasant music to the ears of the airman. He detects immediately the slightest roughness, and should his motor drop even a few revolutions in speed, he turns it over to a skilled mechanic immediately. 

After every twenty hours of flying, airplane motors are thoroughly checked and tuned up. Fliers differ from motorists in that they refer to the number of hours their planes have flown instead of the number of miles they have traveled. This "twenty-hour check" is regarded with such importance by the United States Government that the Bureau of Air Commerce. requires all pilots to keep complete records of every hour their planes have flown and the names of the licensed mechanics who check their motors.

Many parts of the motor are inspected in this twenty-hour routine check-up. The valves are adjusted and the clearances set exactly right. Spark plugs are removed, cleaned and the gaps checked. Rocker arms and push-rods are scrutinized for any signs of wear.

The magneto points are cleaned and adjusted. Carburetor screens are cleaned and all of the gas lines checked closely. 

Next comes a general tightening-up of all the connections, bolts, and fittings. Then the oil is changed and the motor carefully lubricated. Now the airplane motor is ready for another twenty hours of dependable service.

This regular check-up smooths out an airplane motor, brings out new surging power, and assures the pilot that his motor will respond in any emergency.
