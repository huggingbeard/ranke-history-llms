ANDREW OLIVER 

LIEUTENANT GOVERNOR ANDREW OLIVER was born at Boston on 

March 28, 1706, a son of Daniel and Elizabeth (Belcher) Oliver.

There was no more distinguished and respected family in the province. Daniel was the ideal Puritan: a saint of unbounded charity and a business man who had amassed great wealth by industry and a well-deserved reputation for honesty. Such a man was as a matter of course elected to Her Majesty's Council. Andrew was like him, even as a boy notably "Sober and pious." At college he refused to run with the other wealthy Bostonians and although he sat for four years beside Belcher and Winthrop he never joined in their disorders. In spite of the fact that they out-ranked him in the class order because of the social distinction of their parents, it was he who was chosen by the college authorities to represent the Class of 1724 by taking a part in the Commencement of 1727 when they received their M.A. degrees. The question which he argued on this occasion was "An Præcepta Legis Moralis sub Evangelica Dispensatione sint Obligatoria?" As one would expect, he took the affirmative side.2

Oliver settled down in Boston where, on June 20, 1728, he took to wife Mary, daughter of Colonel Thomas Fitch. With her he acquired the Fitch pasture on Frogg Lane (now the cemetery on the Boylston Street side of the Common) and all of what is now the north side of Boylston Street. This was too far out of town for a busy young merchant who went daily to the warehouse at Oliver's Dock, so the young couple probably lived with Councillor Daniel in the family mansion in Oliver Street. Never were his official dignities too great for him to solicit a little business from English friends. When age as well as dignity demanded that he avoid active business he would still make long trips to Connecticut to look after the real estate of English associates. But it was industry, not greed, which drove him. Although he would sometimes stand "his Excellency's Life Guard" to a drink and then send the bill to the province, no man in the colony gave more generously of both money and time. He labored faithfully as trustee of the Daniel Williams fund for work among the Indians and as a trustee of the Hopkins foundation and the linen manufactory scheme, which was the great work-relief project of the day. When the town needed sterling exchange, it was he who furnished credit for it. Indeed, so much of his time and energy went into such public services that the extent of his private fortune and his business never rivaled those of the Belchers, Hutchinsons, and Hancocks.

One bit of business to which Oliver probably never gave a second thought but now through the prospective of time is significant, was his employment, in September, 1735, of a ship painter named Nathaniel Emmons. Without doubt this was the artist who made the portrait of Oliver as a young man.¹¹

Like most of the solid merchants, Oliver fought against the various inflation schemes of the Thirties and Forties. He was among those who signed public agreements not to accept the New Hampshire merchants' notes and the Massachusetts Land-Bank notes. To combat these inflationary schemes he subscribed to the Massachusetts merchants' notes project and was one of the directors of the Silver-Bank; his signature adorns some of the notes of the latter.¹2

In 1737 Oliver was chosen town auditor, and from that day to the last black year of spiteful persecution he served Boston faithfully. He was collector of taxes for one year and overseer of the poor for eighteen. He served on such committees as that to obtain cord wood, that to manage the almshouse, and that to build a workhouse; he was one of the largest contributors toward the cost of the last. As late as 1747 he served on such political committees as that to protest against the reflections which the governor had cast upon the town after the riot which had been intended to impress him. As one of the chief dignitaries of the town he served for sixteen years on the committee to visit the poorer parts of the town looking for trouble, and for twenty-eight years he was a member of the important committee to inspect the schools. On September 24, 1764, he was moderator of the town meeting but after that he became less and less active in Boston affairs. Modern writers have said that Oliver was "a professed churchman from a child" but they have in that been misled by the fact that he contributed 25/ toward the cost of the Christ Church bells, or by the tradition that most Tories were Episcopalians. In fact, he was, like his parents and grandparents before him, a faithful member of the Old South. John Adams described him as "a staunch Trinitarian and Calvinist who treated all Arians and Arminians with contempt and scorn." 13 Be that as it may, he was certainly a force for interdenominational peace even after the Episcopalians had, out of supersensitiveness, killed one of his pet projects for Christianizing the Indians, the Massachusetts Society for Propagating Christian Knowledge among the Indians of North America. ANDREW OLIVER deprecated the bad manners shown by certain Congregationalists at the time of the death of the Episcopalian minister, Ebenezer Miller (A.B. 1722). The Old South, like the town of Boston, could always call upon his time and purse. For thirty-three years he worked on committees to audit accounts, to manage the charity and building funds, to fix the price of pews, to adopt new hymn books, to assist at ordinations, and to call new pastors and test their orthodoxy. As an old supporter of the historical labors of Thomas 

Prince he was no doubt glad to serve on the committees to accept the gift of his library and to draw rules for the use of it. In general he was not a bookish man, although he had Paul Revere engrave a bookplate for his library. His refuge in the last years of strife and vilification was not in books but in the Old South where respect and consideration never failed him. 

In 1740 Oliver was admitted to St. John's Lodge of Masons but, although he reached the rank of junior warden, he was never at home among the recent immigrants and political radicals who dominated its membership.

When Oliver was in England he impressed the men who were active in the S. P. G. N. E. and soon after his return to Boston he learned that he and Spencer Phips (A.B. 1703) had been appointed to the council which represented it in Boston. Seven years later he became its treasurer, succeeding Adam Winthrop (A.B. 1694), who had misused or misappropriated money intended for work among the Indians.14 For many years as treasurer and later secretary of this organization he managed its missionary work. Among the many tasks involved was the overseeing of the printing of religious works in the Indian language, which gave him considerable typographical trouble.15 The province added to his labors by appointing him guardian of the Indians of Suffolk County. The solution which he urged was to use the surplus money to found a professorship of medicine at Harvard.20 In this he failed to convince his English associates. When the Ezekiel Hersey bequest for a professorship was announced, he remembered that at New York he had seen some human organs preserved for anatomical study by the injection of hot wax, so he ordered similar ones from London. Probably these were the "curious Anatomical Preparations" which he presented to the college in November, 1772.21

ANDREW OLIVER was appointed a justice of the peace in 1739, and in 1756 he sat as a temporary justice on the superior court, but that was the extent of his legal services. In 1743 the town began to return him to the 

House of Representatives where he served as a director of the lottery, signed the paper currency, and was a member of the committee of war. After three terms he was promoted to the Council, and 

Samuel Adams was elected to the House in his stead. On the 

Council he served in his usual way on committees to handle finance and public charity, as always dipping into his pocket for funds to support his public actions. In July, 1748, he represented the province at the Iroquois conference at Fort Frederick and was handsomely entertained by the Lydius family at Albany. In June and 

July, 1754, he attended the Indian conference at Casco Bay but the next year he declined to go to Albany "by Reason of his being absent in the Spring of the Year for about two Months in the public Service, his private Affairs had already suffered, and were in Danger of suffering so much, that he could not possibly accept of the Trust... it gave him the utmost Concern to think he could not serve, especially as the Choice was so unanimous." 22 However, within the month he was serving on the committee to distribute the Acadian refugees. Some of his duties were more pleasant, as when he was sent to welcome Lord Loudoun.

When it became evident in 1755 that Josiah Willard (A.B. 1698), the province secretary, was declining in health, Oliver cast an eye at his office. He felt that he did not know enough law for the position, but his Uncle Jonathan Belcher, now Governor of New Jersey and an authority on obtaining public office, told him that he was modest and diffident to a fault, but questioned his wisdom in taking an office which had cost his predecessor £600 sterling and was dependent upon the whim of the General Court for its salary.23 William Bollan remarked that as there was no relation between the labor and the profits of the secretaryship; he suspected that Oliver was actuated by a desire to be of public service.24 On "Journal of the House of Representatives of Massachusetts (Boston, 1755), Nov. 5, 1755. 23

Perhaps on this occasion he wore his deep wig and his canarycolored suit; probably the servant wore the family livery of “a yellowish coloured Cloth between an orange & olive lined with blue."

These were the years of Oliver's greatest activity in the affairs of the town, the church, and the S. P. G. N. E. The great fire of 

1760 left his Boston house with its gardens an island in the blackened ruin; he was on the town committee to relocate the streets in the burned area. That same year his daughter Grizzell married 

Samuel Waldo (A.B. 1743) and the next, she died; his letters show the religious faith and resignation more common in an earlier century. In 1762 his daughter Margaret married John Spooner, a young merchant, whose early death made serious business problems. 

His son Daniel graduated at Harvard in 1762 and went to read law with the great Jeremy Gridley (A.B. 1725). In later years 

Oliver looked back on this time as the happy days of the old order of which he was a part.

Some years later John Adams wrote that Oliver was one of "the original conspirators against the public liberty, since the conspiracy was first regularly formed and begun to be executed, in 

1763 or 1764. ." 31 There was, in fact, no conspiracy afoot, unless the determined purpose and the shifting expedients of Samuel Adams and his friends to drive out the hated hard-money oligarchy and gain control of the government were such. No white man had better right to call himself an American than did Andrew Oliver.

He was the fourth generation of his family in the colony, but the mobs which tormented him and the merchants who used them as tools were largely immigrants who knew nothing of the old Puritanism which was so strong in Oliver. Oliver was not lacking in courage; he would have tried to stand off the mob of the previous evening had not his friends dissuaded him, but he resented bitterly that not one finger had been raised to support him. As Oliver pointed out, Bernard's experience had shown that “confidential" meant nothing to a Whig politician who saw a chance to improve his position by publication of the documents with which he had been trusted. On this occasion as on the former one, there were politicians looking for such a springboard to office.101 The letters were printed, and public indignation mounted. When it had boiled enough, the House, on June 16, Resolved, That the Letters signed Andrew Oliver, considering the Person by whom they were wrote, the Matters they expresly contain, the Measures they suggest, the Temper in which they were wrote, the Manner in which they were sent, and the Person to whom they were addressed, had a natural and efficacious Tendency to interrupt and alienate the Affections of our most gracious Sovereign." 102 The Council went further and accused him of favoring the revenue acts to enrich himself.103 The House voted to petition the Crown to remove Hutchinson and Oliver on the grounds that they were "among the chief instruments in introducing a fleet and an army into this province, to establish and perpetuate their plans, whereby they have been not only greatly instrumental of disturbing the peace and harmony of the government, and causing unnatural and hateful discourds and animosities between the several parts of your Majesty's extensive dominions, but are justly chargeable with all that corruption of morals and all that confusion, misery, and bloodshed, which have been the natural effect of posting an army in a

ANDREW OLIVER 33 During the first days of the new year he was troubled by a sick headache and his old bilious disorders but he kept about and wrote interesting, if lonely, letters to his children. He "sedately approach'd death, in expectation of the honours of heaven." 111 On the evening of March 1, 1774, he was "seized suddenly," and lay "senseless and dying"; "after about 48 hours of a quiet lethargy, the candle went out" on the third. 

Chief Justice Oliver did not dare attend his brother's funeral lest the mob attack him. The younger Thomas Hutchinson sounded out "the powers that rule at present" and decided that it would not be safe for him to attend. Hancock ordered his cadet corps to march and to fire three volleys over the grave of the lieutenant governor. When Samuel Adams protested, Hancock said that the honor was to the office and not to the man, and went through with it. On the day of the funeral the members of the House of Representatives, who could not very well refuse the invitation to attend, were gathered in one house and the admiral, the attorney-general, and the officers of the army and navy in another. When the procession passed by, the military group came out and accidentally fell in first behind the family, at which the members of the House put their noses in the air, refused to march, and went off to the town house. This effectively broke up the funeral procession. 

After the military the greatest multitude that had ever been seen at a Boston funeral swarmed along "without Order & in promiscuous Confusion to the Grave." 112 Among the mourners was John 

Malcom, the customs officer, at whom the boys hooted and shouted. 

After the volley was fired and the grave covered, the boys or the Sons of Liberty (take your choice of versions) gave three cheers. So lived and died a most modest, faithful, industrious, and generous man, one of the best servants of Massachusetts in his generation. At the end of his statement of the Stamp Act riots he wrote, "Let it speak for me, when I can no longer speak in person." 115

Here, after a century and a half, it and other witnesses now speak for him for the first time. One of the best products of the old social order, he could not adjust himself to the rising democracy or accept its ways. The young democracy found him a symbol of the old order and treated him with the frightful intellectual cruelty which youth often inflicts upon age. Our nation is now old enough that it can, like individuals, look back upon such unkindness with regret. 

It is difficult to say how much of an estate Oliver left. The first detailed inventory amounts to less than 4000l,116 but after the period of inflation it came to nearly 200,000l.117 His papers were scattered; the original of the letter book which is frequently cited in this sketch is probably in England where it was in the possession of his greatgrandson, Peter Orlando Hutchinson, some years ago. The only one of his descendants who was not driven from the country at the time of the Revolution was his son Andrew.
