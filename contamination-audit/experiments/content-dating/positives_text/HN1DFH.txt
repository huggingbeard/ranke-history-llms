STALLION By JOHNSTON MCCULLEY 

A dull drumming in the distance, a lifting cloud of dust, the sudden thunder of galloping hoofs, and down from the mesa sweeps the wild horse herd, led by the big sorrel men call "the flaming stallion."

Only one man the stallion knew and trusted and loved, and in a time of desperate crisis this trust was turned to good account.

Men ride hard in this story, and to some purpose. Through the hills they ride, and out over level country to the town, alert and on guard, some of them following Steve Pondell and his men, who make a business of trapping wild horses and breaking them for the tough work of round-up, others trying to prevent that same Steve Pondell from doing his work.

It is a story of Utah in one of its most romantic periods, with the hill breeze whistling down through the canyons to stir the ashes of campfires around which men sprawl with blankets draped across their shoulders; with its torquoise skies, its far distances, its wide sweeps of country across which men and motives play.

CHAPTER I  

AROUND and around the makeshift corral, the horses rushed with a thunderous drumfire of hooves. Twenty of them were traveling in a circle at top speed. They were running like fools, seeking an exit they could not find, fearing the man-smell, bumping against one another, lifting a cloud of fine dust, kicking and squealing as they followed one another's tails. 

They had been putting on that performance at intervals during the last two days and nights, and old Pappy Gooch was commencing to get rather tired of it. Being the solitary guard at the horse-breaking corral, out there against the base of the hills far from the home ranch of the Bar Circle outfit, was a job which did not appeal to him particularly. 

II

Pappy Gooch was old, and he was lonesome, and he craved human companionship.

Squatting in the small patch of shade cast by a blanket he had draped loosely against a big clump of dry brush, Pappy Gooch watched the antics of the horses. He had nothing to worry about, as far as they were concerned. The corral was strong, and they would not be able to break out of it. And it was the horses getting winded and lathered, not he. Every mile they ran, around and around that corral, brought them that much nearer the state of complete subjection to man. 

But Pappy Gooch was wishing fervently that Steve Pondell and Midget Kaynes and the other boys would get back from Mustang Mesa with the new bunch of wild animals they had gone there to capture. He wanted to talk to somebody, about anything at all. He did not get much satisfaction out of talking to the horses or to himself.

Now he got to his feet slowly, knocked the dottle out of his pipe, stretched and yawned.

For no particular reason at all, the half-

broken animals in the corral suddenly decided to cease their senseless running. With heads and tails up, they stopped-stood quivering, lathered, captured but not entirely conquered, snorting defiance at the world as the big cloud of dust slowly settled around them. "I sure ain't blamin' you a mite for raisin' such a big ruckus," Pappy Gooch said to them, speaking in a thin voice which was almost a squeak. "A few days ago you were runnin' with the wild herd, and didn't know the feel o' bit or saddle, rope or quirt. But you'll soon get used to 'em. And you'll sure have a lot o' fun, come round-up time. Wait till you learn to brace against a rope and tumble a steer on his horns. You may not think so now, but you're damned lucky ponies!" 

Pappy Gooch stuffed his empty pipe into a pocket of his vest, and wandered on down to the corral. "Yeah, you're right down lucky!" he continued, in his squeaky voice. "Any fourlegged critter is lucky when he's got men to take care o' him. Look at my own horse,

picketed down there by the creek. He was runnin' with that wild herd once. He raised a rumpus when we caught and gentled him, too. But he knows a lot different now. He wouldn't change places with any o' the wild bunch, not even with the boss stallion himself. If you don't believe me, you ask him.” 

Pappy Gooch chuckled as though at a good joke, and turned away from the corral, back toward his little patch of shade. He started to gnaw off a chew from a plug of tobacco, but stopped abruptly. He gave an exclamation of pleased surprise as he glanced at the distant hillside. A horseman was riding slowly down it toward him. 

Pappy Gooch thrilled with the keen joy of anticipation. He would have a human being with whom to exchange talk. The rider was yet too far away to be identified, but Pappy 

Gooch hoped he was one of the Bar Circle boys, who had ridden out with orders and news. It might be somebody attached to Bart 

Soules' big Double Diamond horse ranch, however, or merely some range tramp wan-

dering across the land. Whoever he was, and whatever his status in life, he was a human being and would be welcome.

Pappy Gooch made haste to stir up the little fire he always kept going. He tossed more fuel upon the embers, hurried to the creek to get some fresh water, and put on the coffee pot. He would have refreshment ready for his visitors. In exchange for gossip, Pappy would extend hospitality. 

But the years had taught old Pappy Gooch many things, among them discretion and the necessity for using caution at all times. He did not entirely forget these teachings now. 

So he buckled on his belt, and examined his six-gun to make sure that it was loaded and ready for instant use.

He gave a toothless grin as he took these precautions. It seemed so damned silly! He could not imagine any reason why the man who was approaching the camp should attack him. He no longer was aggressive enough to have active enemies. And there was nothing around the camp for a man to steal. There

11 was food-but anybody could have that for the asking.

There were the horses in the corral, and Pappy's own mount down by the creek. But 

Pappy was not much afraid of any of them being stolen. Stealing a horse was not a frequent crime in that section of the country. 

Punishment for such a transgression was both swift and drastic. And this rider was not traveling like a fugitive with a posse on his trail, a man who might need a fresh mount in a hurry and would not care how he got one.

He was poking along as though half asleep. 

However, he was making straight for the corral and Pappy Gooch's camp. His handkerchief had been dropped from his face, and his hat was now on the back of his head Pappy could see that he was not one of the 

Bar Circle riders. And, as he drew nearer,

He howled both maledictions and insults, but they brought no response. 

So Pappy grew silent, straining his ears to catch every sound. He heard Jones walk away, heard saddle leather creaking, and guessed that the man had mounted. Then there was silence, save for the usual noises from the corral, and the rustling of the wind as it sang sibilantly through the dry brush. 

Pappy Gooch tugged with determination at the ropes which bound him, but soon gave up the idea of trying to work free. So he tried to make himself as comfortable as possible, listened, waited. He could not understand this. The only solution that came into his mind was that the stranger was stealing a horse. 

But he did not hear the sound of departing

hooves. And he did hear sounds which convinced him that the stranger was still near at hand. That made it all the more puzzling. 

What could the man be doing? Why had he covered Pappy's head, when Pappy already had seen him? The unusual situation caused

He ceased speaking suddenly, for a new sound had come to his ears, and it was one he knew well. In the near distance, hooves were pounding the hard earth. Not one horse was coming, but several. A number of mounted men, Pappy Gooch knew, were galloping toward the corral. 

A surge of relief came to him, for he was cheered by the thought that they might be

Steve Pondell, Midget Kaynes, and the Bar 

Circle punchers who had gone with them to

They would run him down and deal out proper punishment.

The riders approached rapidly, and stopped, and Pappy Gooch could hear a confused muttering of voices. But nobody rushed to release him. "Hey!" Pappy squealed, in his thin voice. "Hey! I'm over here!" 

Nobody answered his call, and that was another peculiar thing. And there seemed to be a sudden strange activity in his near neighborhood. He knew that horses were being ridden about, for bits jingled and saddle leather creaked. He grew frantic, because that sack over his head kept him from seeing what was going on. He imagined almost everything.

Then there came a sudden chorus of wild yells, and a fusillade of shots. Bedlam broke loose. Horses squealed in fright, men

screeched, hooves pounded. It seemed to 

Pappy Gooch that a swirl of vicious battle was around him. The unmistakable odor of lathered steeds penetrated to his nostrils. 

Again, he tugged at the ropes, until his arms ached and pains were running up and down his legs. But the bonds would not give.

He tried to visualize the scene, and could not. 

He did not know whether the Bar Circle boys were engaged in a finish fight with the others, whoever they were. 

But suddenly the sounds dwindled, then died. Now there was only the faint rustling of the breeze through the brush. Pappy wondered whether the invaders had been driven off by Steve Pondell and the others, and were being pursued.

Then it dawned upon him that there were no sounds at all coming from the corral, where the half-wild horses should have been running in a frenzy of fear. Down by the creek, Pappy's own horse whinnied a query. as to what this turmoil had meant. 

And finally there came to Pappy Gooch a

full realization of what had happened. Men had raided the corral, and had stolen the twenty animals which had been entrusted to his care!

CHAPTER II  

LIKE an avalanche of quivering flesh, the forty wild horses rushed down the treacherous gravel slope and toward the base of the hill. The constant drumfire of thundering hooves was deafening. Clouds of fine dust arose, to drift aside and settle anew on rocks and brush and grass. Dislodged rocks crashed through the dry brush, to rebound as they struck the hard ground at the foot of the hill.

Ahead, and a little to the right of the herd, rode Steve Pondell, the boss wrangler for the

Bar Circle outfit. He seemed to be a part of the horse he bestrode. He swayed gracefully in the saddle, yelped at the charging herd, waved his hat above his head to frighten them and urge them on. "Hi-yip! Hi-yip!" he screeched. 28

On the left side rode Luke Kaynes, commonly known as Midget because of his big and powerful frame. He was Steve Pondell's particular friend. Three other Bar Circle cowpunchers helped guide the wild horses in their break-neck run.

These forty animals had been selected and cut out of the big wild herd in Dead Horse

Canyon. They were being driven to the little box canyon just around the shoulder of the hill from the corral where old Pappy Gooch was waiting.

Nature seemed to have constructed that box canyon for the purpose to which men were putting it. Its only entrance was a narrow pass between two walls of solid rock. It was not more than a mile long and a quarter of a mile wide. And there was an abundance of grass, and a trickling stream. "Hi-yip! . . . Hi-yip!" one of the punchers screeched. 

The leaders in that mad race suddenly saw a gap in the stone wall beside which they were running, and headed straight for it.

The gap seemed to promise an avenue of escape for them. They dashed through it madly, and the others followed them after the manner of sheep.

They found themselves unexpectedly where it was shady and cool, where the thick, lush grass was knee-deep, and cold water trickled over the rocks and collected in deep crystal pools. And the man-beings who had been tormenting them were doing so no longer. 

There was no more wild yelling, no more startling revolver shots.

The wild bunch slackened speed gladly, trotted, and then walked, and finally came to a stop beside the creek. With their sides heaving, their heads held high and ears outflung, they looked back. The man-beings did not seem to be pursuing them now. They were not to be seen.

Though they were unaware of it, they had rushed headlong into a trap prepared for them, into that little box canyon with its steep sides and only one narrow entrance. • · •
