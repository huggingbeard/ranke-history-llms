HARY COLLEGS MEC 31 1935 LIBRARY The Author

One of the topics for a thesis given at a certain college was: "A biography of some person about whom nothing has ever been written before, so this is an attempt at the life of my mother's mother, Mary Macpheadris Warner Conner Harris, born at Portsmouth, New Hampshire, on August 20, 1797, died at Boston, November 30, 1886, in her ninetieth year. 

To go back one generation: Mary's mother, Abigail Warner, daughter of Samuel and Elizabeth 

Wentworth Warner, a little red-haired girl whose years were nine, was playing with her doll on the front steps of her father's house at the corner of 

Islington and Bridge Streets, Portsmouth (the old mansion now occupied by J. Verne Wood). Looking up from her play, Abigail saw a chaise stopping at the gate. Within, were her uncle Colonel Jonathan

Warner and his wife, the former Mary Macpheadris, a handsome dark-tressed woman. 1

"Well, little dear," said the Colonel, tossing 

Abigail in his arms, "where is your mother?" "In the house, Uncle, I guess,” replied Abigail, so the three went in and found her young widowed mother 

Said Uncle Warner, "Elizabeth, will you lend us this young lady for a fortnight to cheer us a little? 

You know how lonely we are since our darling Polly died. You have another little girl and we have none." "She might go for a little visit if she wants to," said Abigail's mother, "I can pack up a few duds and Daniel Street is not far away."

So Abigail was tucked in the chaise between her uncle and aunt, and liked Daniel Street so much she never went back to live in Islington Street.

There, in the brick mansion built by her aunt's father, Archibald Macpheadris, she was the cherished daughter of the house until her marriage to Captain Benjamin Conner, a widower, much older than herself.Captain Conner, a veteran of the Revolutionary War, was a romantic, dreamy-eyed man, always inventing something. It is told of him in later years, that he was sent for by Captain 

Robert Fulton, in perplexity, when his first steam boat refused to move, that September day, 2

on the Hudson River. Captain Conner so adjusted the cock hammer that the "Claremont" pursued its victorious way.

Colonel Warner took the marriage very hard. 

He never liked Benjamin Conner, a man nearly as old as himself, with grown children, who was dynamic where he was static, poor where he was rich. 

However, Abigail was married in the great parlor, and started housekeeping on Washington Street, in Portsmouth. 

There my grandmother was born August 20, 1797. Her first concious memory was of being held up to the window in her mother's arms, to see the mock funeral of General Washington, in December 1799. Her younger brother, Jonathan Warner

Conner, was born two years later. Then came a time when Mary had a bad bout of scarlet fever. 

She was nursed back to health by her mother, who paid dearly for her assiduity, for, after giving birth to boy twins, she contracted the fever herself and died. One twin died soon after birth, but the other, Charles Abigail Conner, survived to young manhood.

Here then were three little children to be looked out for. The natural refuge was the Warner 

House in which conditions had changed since Abi- 3

gail was a child. Her aunt, Mary Macpheadris 

Warner, had died, and Colonel Warner had married again. His third wife was Miss Elizabeth Pitts, of 

Boston. She brought with her two nieces, Elizabeth 

Pitts (who afterwards married Gerard Caseaux, the 

French consul), and Margaret Pitts, for whom my own mother was named. Then, too, Abigail's sister 

Elizabeth Warner had married Nathaniel Sherburne, who died of yellow fever, the year after, leaving her with a little baby, John Nathaniel Sherburne, born 1793. Her uncle had taken her home with her little boy. Elizabeth offered to care for her sister Abigail's children if they came to the old house. "No," said Colonel Warner,"not so fast, Lizzie.

Let's see what Captain Conner will provide. After all, they are his children. What about it, Captain?"

My great-grandfather spoke, his sensitive face lighting up, "Indeed, they are my very dear children, Colonel, only I never thought I should outlive my young wife. You know what my life has been-here today and gone tomorrow; making my living on the great sea. Of my former marriage, there are three children: my son Sauveur, my maiden daughter 

Betsy, and my elder daughter Margaret, Mrs. 

Honeywell, of Gloucester. She has children of her

own, so maybe she will open her home to these little ones." So Grandmother and her little brothers were packed off to Gloucester, to the Honeywells, and great-grandfather went to sea. 

Poor Aunt Sherburne cried and cried; she said: "It is sad enough to lose my only sister, but to think I can't have her little children!" 

To quiet her, Uncle Warner said:"Now Lizzie, listen to me. If we have the children here now, it means a couple of nurses, and interfering with household arrangements. When the children get to be seven years old, they can manage without nurses. Just as soon as little Mary is seven, she shall come here; and after that, her brothers too, but, for a few years, Captain Conner must have the responsibility." 

Little Mary was most uncomfortable at her half-sister's in Gloucester. Margaret Honeywell already had her hands full with her own brood, and Mary missed her mother's love.

When her father came home from sea, even his dreamy intelligence saw how matters lay, so he hired a house, put his daughter Betsy in charge of the children and started off again. 

All through her life Grandma loved her sister 5

Betsy. She wasn't the only one who did, however, for Betsy was wanted in marriage by Mr. Piper, of 

Newburyport. In the spring of 1804, he pressed his suit with such convincing vigor, that Uncle Warner said he would take Mary home. even if she wasn't quite seven, and leave Betsy free to marry if

Margaret Honeywell would care for the two boys a while. Thus Mary came into her own. In two more years, there were four children playing in the Warner House: John Sherburne, a big blue eyed boy of thirteen; Mary, nine; Warner, seven; and little Charles. Their favorite play-place was the third story hall, where still hung the hooks for the lanterns that illumined Polly Warner's parties.

One door led to the cupola, and little doors led to delightful "hidy holes"in the eaves, designed by an architect of olden days, when inhabitants of these old houses might need to make themselves scarce for a time. Games of hide-and-go-seek were real games in those days. At dinner, the children were tanght to hold up their glasses when the toasts were given, but to put them down without drinking. 

The boys went to the brick school on State Street, and Mary to a private school in "The Academy," now the Public Library. Mary was a dainty child, and sometimes, as she hesitated to cross muddy

Market Street in her little slippers, a young lawyer, Daniel Webster by name, would pick her up and carry her across on his strong shoulders. 

At the age of nine, Mary worked her lovely, green sampler that hangs on my wall. The letters of the alphabet are followed by this verse: "Be good, dear child, and ease thy parents' care 

Choose to be wise, rather than fair.

Abstain from those who would thy youth betray 

And from the path of virtue never stray." (She never did) 

Mary Macpheadris Warner Conner aged 9, February, 24, 1806.

The whole is surrounded by a border of pink flowers, in Kensington stitch. She also made her brothers' shirts, sewing them with tiny stitches.

Sometimes, her father would return from his voyages. I remember hearing her tell how he took her to Exeter to see his old father, a bent old man, sitting by the fire, burdened with the weight of his hundred years.

Again, Captain Conner would tell her of the land he had bought in the far West, where, in after

years, rose the city of St. Paul, land which would be of no use to him or to her, but would bring untold wealth to her children and their children's children; of the caves on the borders of the great river, (the Mississippi). a vast domain inhabited by Indians. "Pooh", said Colonel Warner, sotto voce, "The man would better put his twenty thousand dollars into farms at Newington, or Greenland.”

The Colonel was right, for our government abrogated the Indian deeds, and not a penny has ever been received. (The deeds are on file in 

Philadelphia). Yet the old man meant well. 

I also recall my grandmother telling me how she was taken out to Uncle Warner's farm at Greenland, where the negro servants he had freed lived in little cabins on the estate, some tilling the soil, others working in blacksmith or carpenter shops.

In 1806, came what is still known in Portsmouth as "The Great Fire". It raged around the town and burned St. John's Church on the hill above the Warner House. Destruction seemed near. 

The portraits were taken from the walls and laid in the door-yard, to be moved to a place of safety. 

Unfortunately they were torn from the walls so hastily, that some of the frames were broken,

which accounts for their framelessness to this day. 

Polly Warner's trousseau books, bound in calf, with "Miss Warner" on them in gold letters were taken from the book-case and piled outside. 

During the transit, some of the volumes were lost. In the midst of the confusion, Aunt Sherburne kept her head. "Mary," she commanded, "This is no place for children. Take your little brothers by the hand and run out to the farm at Greenland." So the children trotted off, but were sent for next day with the glad news that the fire had been extinguished before it had reached the house. 

In 1810, occured the tragic death of the third 

Mrs. Warner, (Elizabeth Pitts). When last seen by the family, she was sitting by the fire in the big chamber, spreading her transparent old hands over the unfendered fireplace, for warmth. When they returned, she had fallen forward over the fire and had smothered in its fiery breath. They snatched her up, beat the fire from her clothes and stretched her on the great bed, but her spirit had fled. 

This death left Mary's "Aunt Sherburne" mistress of the house. Four years later, Uncle 

Warner's sister Sarah, Mrs. Henry Sherburne, came to visit him, and the old brother and sister talked together of their happy childhood days. I

like to think of them sitting in the old parlor, he in his knee breeches and buckled shoes, for he refused ever to wear such monstrosities as "pantaloons". 

His portrait, by Blackburn, hangs in the Boston 

Museum of Fine Arts. Death, however, broke in on the happy visit. First Uncle Warner sickened; then his sister. Within two days of each other, they died, and their coffins rested side by side in the front parlor. 

Jonathan Warner was pre-eminently a kind man. When I think how he brought up two generations of children, not only reared them but loved them and made their lives happy, one is thankful for his loving and cheerful spirit. He gave orders that a kettle of soup should always be kept on the fire. "For," said he, "Perchance, some poor way-farer might come hungary to our door and should not be sent away without food." 

My grandmother was the purest minded woman I have ever known. Like many descendants of seafarers, she possessed the gift of second sight. The earliest recorded instance of this gift is the story of her brother Charles' death. Charles grew up, a quiet, fairhaired boy, in the old house' and when sixteen went to sea, as most boys did in those days. One night, lying in her bed in the hall 10

bedroom of the Warner House, Mary dreamed a vivid dream: She saw before her a vast, icy plain, with cakes of rough ice rising over the surface. 

Along this glassy surface, walked two men, Charles and an older man. Charles walked more and more uncertainly, until finally he sank down on a block of ice. His companion tried to raise him, but he, sank back exhausted; he put his hand into his coat, drew out his watch and wallet, and handed them to his companion. Like a thunder clap, consciousness returned to Mary and she awoke crying bitterly. 

When the lilacs bloomed in the door-yard, a seafaring man walked up Daniel Street, rapped with the knocker on the door, and asked for Miss

Conner. When she entered the parlor, she saw the face of Charles' companion on the ice pack. "You come from my brother," she said. "Yes, Miss, I do," he replied. "He sent you these," and drew from his pocket the watch and wallet. He told her the story, how he and Charles became separated from the rest of the crew, of how Charles sank exhausted on the ice, and how he himself made his way back to the ship alone. 

In the years after Uncle Warner died, Mary lived with her Aunt Sherburne, her cousin John, and her brother Warner. In the records of St. John's 11

Church, is stated the fact that Miss Mary Conner was admitted to communion in 1816. In those days, the Bishop's visits were few and far between, and people were admitted to communion without confirmation. She went coasting in the South End, with her cousin John Sherburne and the girl whom he afterwards married, Eveline Blunt. 

Eveline had a cousin Eliza Blunt, whose dark, vivid beauty contrasted with Mary's blond gentleness.

There, too, she met Captain Theodore Jackson, Harris, a handsome, dashing, fair young man who seemed utterly devoted to Eliza Blunt. Rumor said he was engaged to her. Nevertheless it was not 

Eliza he married, but Mary, my grandmother.

The story is that Eveline Blunt and her brother were going into a jewelry shop, just as Captain 

Harris was coming out. "Do you know that gentleman?" said the chatty jeweler. "Why, yes," cried the Blunts, "It is Captain Harris." "Well he's been in to buy a wedding ring," was the reply. "Oh, for 

Cousin Eliza Blunt, I suppose," ventured Eveline. "Why no, Miss Eveline, it isn't for Miss Blunt, it's for Miss Conner." 

Theodore Jackson Harris and Mary Macpheadris Warner Conner were married Septemper 3, 1820, in the great par or of the Warner House. We 12

still have spoons marked with her initials "M.M.W. 

H." hallmarked 1820.

My Grandfather's custom was to go to sea one year, and stay at home the next. His father,

Abel Harris, of Portsmouth was a ship-owner, so 

Theodore sailed his father's ships, going to Mobile for a cargo of cotton, and carrying it to Havre. Later, he owned his own ship, the "Harriet Rockwell", named for the wife of one of his officers. He wanted to name it the "Mary", but Mary would not allow this; publicity was not for her. Soon after the marriage, Grandfather went to sea, leaving his bride with her Aunt Sherburne. The next June, her eldest child, Elizabeth Mary Harris, was born in the 

Warner House. 

When Grandfather came home from Havre, he bought the large house next door, where the gas station now stands, so Mary did not go far away. 

The next year, John Sherburne married

Eveline Blunt and brought her home to the Warner 

House. Just as John Sherburne and Mary Conner had grown up as brother and sister under the same roof, their children played together in and out of the two houses. If asked where they were going, they replied: "Into the other house". John Sherburne and Eveline, his wife, and Mary and Theordoe Harris were a 13

united quartette.

The years went on. The Warner House held six children, Elisabeth Warner Pitts Sherburne --afterwards Mrs. Penhallow ---, Eleanor Mary 

Sherburne, (who married General Amiel Whipple, killed at Chancellorsville,) Charles Blunt Sherburne who died in his early twenties, John Sherburne, later captain in the regular army, Nathaniel 

Sherburne, and, as an afterthought, little Evelyn Sherburne. 

Theodore Harris and Mary, his wife, had three daughters: Elisabeth Mary, afterwards married to Dr. E. G. Tucker; Mary Aston Harris, married to John A. Shackford; Margaret Pitts 

Harris, my mother, who married Philip John Aubin. 

Then followed four sons: Theodore Sheafe Harris ; 

Robert Lewis Harris, who married Lucy W. Kimball, (and was the father of Mary Aubin Harris and Edith Kimball Harris); James Duncan Harris, who, while serving as sergeant in the Union Army, was drowned in the Mississippi River off New

Orleans, when trying to save the lives of two of his men; and Joseph Whipple Harris, Lieutenant U.S.N., who died at Panama, of yellow fever, in 1861, on board the U.S.S. "Lancaster". At the birth of each child, a bottle of wine, inscribed with the day and 14

year, was duly laid away in the cellar. 

There never were two people less alike than Grandfather and Grandmother Harris; Grandfather was gay, laughing, a lover of company, Grandmother was gentle, quiet, retiring, not given to entertaining. Every other year, when Grandfather went to sea, she lived a quiet life, but when he returned, goodbye to quiet. There is recorded a great party given in the Daniel Street House, when Grandfather led out the dance with Miss Hannah 

Sewall, the belle of the town.

Grandma kept two maids, as was the custom, at $1.50 a week for each. The older one did the cooking. If she married, the younger one took up the torch and an understudy was found to replace her. An old colored woman came on Mondays to help with the wash. 

Grandfather Harris was especially fond of

French cooking and returned from sea, one year, bringing with him a French chef, remarking that he wanted something fancy to eat. Then ensued wild turmoil between the Frenchman and the New England cook; the latter evidently came out on top for the Frenchy lasted only three months. 

Once a week, Grandma Sherburne, as she was called, (John's mother) the former Elisabeth 15

Warner, came into her niece Mary's house to do the family mending. She was an old lady, wearing a cap and a one-piece, dark, calico gown buttoned all the way down the front. She sewed all day, patching and darning. Just before the one o'clock dinner, one of the Sherburne boys --- either John or Nat would bring in a foaming jug of ale, for Grandma to drink with her dinner. She must have been a dear old lady and a devout Christian, for St.

John's Church never opened its doors for a service that she did not attend. On Communion Sundays, she always brought home the chalice, paten, and flagon, which Queen Caroline long, ago had given to the church and put them away in the cupboard, at the left of the fireplace, in the sitting room. 

There were other people, too, who helped 

Grandmother bring up her seven; Aunt Betsy 

Piper, in Newburyport, was always glad to have a niece come to stay with her and watch her make pies in her sunny kitchen with the sanded floor. 

Then there was her sister-in-law Frances Mary 

Harris ("Aunt Mary") who had married James 

Murray Robbins of Brush Hill, Milton, and who loved to fill her house with her young nieces; to say nothing of Grandfather's aunts, the Coffins of 

Summer Street, Boston. 16

Grandmother's life, filled with the cares of her family and complicated with social duties, ran fairly smoothly until 1837, when Grandfather developed consumption. The doctor prescribed a winter in the south of France, so Grandfather made preparations accordingly. "The Harriet Rockwell" was made ready for sea; a mate, Mr. Jewett, who held a captain's license, was engaged, so that he might navigate the ship at need, and Grandfather started at the age of forty-three, on his last voyage. 

His children, from Lizzie, a handsome fairhaired girl of sixteen, to Whipple, a baby in arms, were in Portsmouth, with the exception of Margaret, my mother, a little girl of nine, visiting her Aunt

Mary Robbins at Nahant. It was there that she caught her last glimpse of her father, for he came to the cottage, near Swallow's Cave, to bid her goodbye. As she was waving adieu, a dog jumped at her and she winced a bit; her father called back to her: "Darling, don't be afraid of the dog." 

Grandma must have had a sad winter. Her eyes, after the birth of her youngest child, were very weak and she had to stay in a darkened room. 

How she must have suffered to think of her absent husband! 17

One Sunday morning, in the spring of 1838, Grandma was sitting in her bedroom, while her daughter Mary was combing her hair for her. Mary looked out of the window, and suddenly exclaimed "Why, Ma, here comes Captain Jewett." "Your father is dead", cried Grandma, and fainted away.

His passing, as told by Captain Jewett, was this: everything on board the "Harriet Rockwell" went smoothly until January 28, when, in the English

Channel, he was taken with a hemorrhage and died. 

He asked, as he was stricken, where they were, and gasped out: "My wife has a horror of seaburial, so, if you can make Portsmouth, (England), bury me there, if not, put in to Falmouth. Goodbye." 

The ship "Harriet Rockwell" put in to Fal. mouth, and my grandfather, followed by his officers and crew, was carried up the steep hillside and bur. ied in the churchyard of St. Charles the Martyr, the parish church of Falmouth, Cornwall. 

The next Sunday after the tidings came to 

Portsmouth, Grandma, leaning on Uncle John Sherburne's arm, followed by her three girls in black silk dresses, and Theodore her eldest son, walked up the aisle of St. John's Church, where Dr. Burroughs peached a funeral sermon. 18

My grandfather's business had to be settled.

Grandma, in her sheltered life, knew nothing of affairs. There seemed money enough at first, but during the minority of seven children, it dwindled away. After the three daughters married, Grandma moved to Jamaica Plain,so that her son, Robert,might attend the Eliot School, where he learned surveying, and became a civil engineer. Her son Theodore had an apothecary store at the corner of Tremont and

Winter Streets, and since he wished his mother near him, she lived in Boston. Her house there was the rallying place of her children and grandchildren until her death on November 30, 1886.
