FOREWORD 

This brief biographical sketch of the dedicated life of one of the most constructive thinkers among the Friends of his generation is more than a record of an earthly life. It is an interpretation of the spirit and influence of John Wilhelm Rowntree written by his close friend and associate. It is designed for senior classes and mature people (not as one of the series of sketches for juniors).

JOHN WILHELM ROWNTREE By RUFUS M. JONES 

The great creative leaders of the Quaker movement, in the important epochs of its three hundred years of history, have usually been young persons in the prime and vigor of their life. John Wilhelm Rowntree, whose earthly life came to an end when he was thirty-six years old, belongs in the list of the greatest of these young creative leaders. He possessed the rare gifts of a spiritual prophet, and during the ten years from 1895 to 1905 he was the unique inspiring leader of that epoch of our Quaker history. 

He was born in York, England, the 4th of September, 1868, the eldest son of Joseph and E. Antoinette Rowntree. His father, Joseph Rowntree, was built on great lines both of life and thought. He was in the best sense of the word a far-seeing Quaker statesman. 

His mother was a niece of the distinguished minister Benjamin Seebohm, and she brought to him a dowry of racial inheritance of a unique order. His love of art and music and his high imaginative quality of mind

stemmed back to this important Seebohm ancestry. The home in which he was nurtured, with its remarkable breadth of hospitality, its stream of Quaker visitors, its atmosphere of thought and spiritual interests, was an admirable environment for this gifted boy. His uncle, John Stephenson Rowntree, in 1859 had won the prize of a hundred guineas for the best Essay on "The Causes of the Decline of Quakerism," and this remarkable Essay, entitled "Quakerism Past and Present," which proved to be a historical turning point, had a notable influence on the formation of the spiritual outlook of the family, and it put an emphasis on the importance of historical study. 

And yet it must be admitted that there was little promise of his future greatness in the period of John's boyhood. He had a constant struggle with his passionate propensities; he had to fight a strong tendency to selfishness, he was highly nervous and his course of study in school was not marked by peculiar distinction, though he always showed a great love of beauty both in nature and art and a striking gift of expression in speech and writing. It seems pretty clear that his hearing, of which I shall have much to say later, had already begun to fail in his school-boy days, though I do not find any evidence that his sight had begun to fail in this early period. He received his systematic education at Bootham Boys School, with an interim

period at Oliver's Mount School in Scarborough, and he finished the school years with little promise of those unusual powers which became so evident in his early manhood.

On leaving school he entered at once the Cocoa Works at York, of which his father was the head, beginning naturally in a subordinate position. But with diligence and energy he forged ahead and quickly became fitted for the responsible posts which he eventually held, early becoming a director of the firm and later its treasurer. It is important to realize that throughout the entire period of his spiritual development he was heavily weighted with business cares and responsibilities. It has been the case that a number of the foremost Quaker contributors in the line of ministry and in the output of literary contributions have been heavily weighted persons in one sphere or another. It was inevitable that a person like John, of inquiring mind, eagerly interested in the march of scientific discovery, should be beset by the grave difficulty of holding the untested and unanalyzed faith in which as a member of York Meeting he had grown up. He was coming to maturity just at the period when 

Herbert Spencer's formulation of a universal scientific system was a dominant feature of the intellectual world of that epoch. Nobody with a quick, keen mind could escape the powerful impact of this and other

attempts to bring every aspect of the universe under the rigid interpretation of a system of cause and effect. 

Waves of doubt and questioning swept over the young man, and with his love of truth and his spirit of honesty he was more or less bound to pass through, as he did do, a period of agnosticism, though he was never a confirmed and satisfied agnostic. It was brief, reluctant agnosticism. 

What he did was to strip away the unrealities of his earlier faith and open his mind to fresh insights and new interpretations as the growing light dawned upon him. Dr. Richard H. Thomas of Baltimore, on a religious visit to England in 1889-90, did more than any other one person to bring to John the spiritual help he needed in that crisis, and when the turning point came, he made rapid advance in his insight toward the formation of a live and vital faith, with immense capacity for expansion. That capacity for expansion was the essential feature of John's new found faith. He had discovered a principle of life that had nothing to fear from the progress of knowledge. Christ was for him now and henceforth a life-giving Spirit, an invisible, ever-present guiding inward Spirit of life, while at the same time he saw and felt the tremendous importance of the Christ of history, and he was smitten with a passion of love for the Great Galilean, a passion and a devotion that went on growing with the years. He felt

from the first discovery of Him that Christ was the type of spiritual leader he wanted for his life and for his life-work. But he began at once to be interested in the historical and critical study of the Bible and he forthwith formed intimate friendships with the outstanding Biblical scholars of the time. He leaped almost at once to a new level of life and broke his way into a new realm of vitality and growth. He had crossed a great divide. He had discovered a vitalized Christianity. 

About the time this new stage of his life was taking shape, in 1892, he was married to Constance M. Naish of Bristol. It was a love match at first sight. John saw

Constance come up the aisle in the York Meeting House, and he said to himself on the spot that is the person I want for my wife. The marriage which followed was in every sense of the word a major event in his life. Constance became the mother of five children, one son and four daughters, and she was the maker of a home of exceeding happiness, with an atmosphere of love and joy and radiance, at first in 

York and later in Scalby, near Scarborough. 

John Wilhelm's leadership first came into evidence in London Yearly Meeting in 1893. On that occasion the young Friends took a notable part in what was considered "a wonderful session" on the state of the Society. John came home profoundly stirred and wrote soon after: "The Yearly Meeting has stimulated me. I

see more [clearly] what is wanted and feel more determined than ever to devote my life to making the Society of Friends a real and living force in the world." 

That resolution dominated the rest of his life, and it marks a turning point in his life. He boldly declared in that Yearly Meeting session that much that was said in the way of ministry in meetings for worship did not reach the youth of his generation. It passed them by and did not strike home. As for himself he was determined "to have done with unreality and to go beneath the crust of words and almost of thoughts to the living springs of actual living experience." 

That was the secret of his power. I cannot date the great experience that came to him in London, though I received the account of it from his own lips. Just as he was beginning to feel the dawning sense of a great mission opening before him—perhaps this year of 1893 -he discovered that he was slowly losing his sight as well as having a steady decrease of his range of hearing. He went up to London to consult a specialist and was told that before he reached middle life he would be totally blind and deaf. Dazed and overwhelmed by the diagnosis he staggered from the doctor's office to the street and stood there, holding on to a post on the curb. Suddenly he felt the love of God flow around him and through him as though a visible presence with everlasting arms enfolded him, and immediately a joy

filled him, such as he had never known before. He had another similar experience of this invading love of God when he received the news of the death of my son, Lowell, which news came as I was landing in Liverpool in 1903. The rest of his life, after that visit to 

London, was lived with the consciousness that he was afflicted with a stubborn, insidious disease which was attacking the very citadel of his powers, his sight, his hearing and possibly his memory, but this consciousness only made him more heroic and gentle, and greatly deepened his character and his dedication. He turned his necessity to glorious gain.

He threw himself with striking power into the leadership of an Adult School for railway employees and other laboring men in the suburban village of Acomb. Here he revealed unique teaching gifts, but what was more significant, rare pastoral gifts and a spirit of friendship and fellowship which powerfully influenced the men and their families of this village. 

But the mission in life which supremely absorbed him was the recovery and expansion of the life and power of the Society of Friends. "If," he wrote, "as a Society we could present the Christ-ideal both as it affects the individual and the community, as the force which makes for social progress, the development of the highest and best in men and nations, focussing in him

all the various efforts and graspings after light, we would, I think, soon speak so that men would listen."

John had a considerable share in organizing the extremely significant Manchester Conference of 1895, the predominant feature of which was a determination to get the Society of Friends ready to take its place in the spiritual work of the world. His address on that occasion gave the striking prophetic note of the Conference. In conclusion he said: "God grant to our

Church the spirit of understanding which shall give her the eye of a seer, the voice of a prophet, the place and power of a leader. Is there indifference to the higher life? Then, O Christ, convince us by Thy Spirit, thrill us by Thy Divine passion, drown our selfishness in Thy invading love, lay on us the burden of the world's suffering, drive us forth with the apostolic fervor of the early Church! So only can our message be delivered:-Speak to Thy people that they go forward." This young prophet was then twenty-six years old.

John and I met first two years after the Manchester Conference, in 1897. He did not attend Yearly Meeting that year, or I should have met him then, and he was not in York when I visited Friends there, but Rendel 

Harris and I went on an extensive walking trip in

Switzerland in July, and there in Mürren we found John Wilhelm and many members of the Rowntree

family. Here began our immortal friendship. We spent an unforgettable Sunday together. Monday we climbed the Schilthorn-a ten thousand foot peak—and all the time we talked and talked of our future joint work for the Society of Friends. He was full of dawning ideas for his future work—though already so blind that we climbed the mountain arm in arm. Plans were then shaping for the epoch-making Summer Schools for religious study. He was forming also the lines of his proposed monumental History of Quakerism. He was dreaming of the founding of a religious Educational Institution where Friends could be trained for spiritual and social service. He was shaping a scheme for a 

Periodical that would minister in the same direction. 

All the plans and all the dreams focussed upon the one purpose of preparing the Society of Friends for its mission in the modern world and for deepening the ministry in the meetings for worship. Two plans were settled between us during this visit: First, that I should write the History of Mysticism and work out the historical background of Quakerism down to the period of George Fox, and that he should write the History of the entire Quaker Movement, and secondly we proposed to meet each year of our lives either in England or America—a plan which we actually carried out. It should be said here that John had discovered that there was a celebrated doctor in Chicago who had worked

out a new scientific method of treating the peculiar disease that afflicted him, which John called "Retinitis pigmentosa," and John made many visits to America to consult this doctor, though he always made the visits at the same time the occasions for extended social and spiritual concerns.

The Summer School project was the first of the fertile plans to be realized. The first one was held in 

Scarborough in August of that same year, 1897, to promote reverent, constructive study, stimulate thought, and prepare Friends to meet the issues of life and action in a new epoch. John Wilhelm and William

Charles Braithwaite were the joint Secretaries for its preparation, but John Wilhelm took charge of the extensive correspondence. There gathered round him from this time onward in all the lines of his work a remarkable group of Friends, many of whom were young. Foremost among the older Friends were J. 

Rendel Harris, Joshua Rowntree, Edward Grubb, John 

William Graham and Thomas Hodgkin. In the inspiring group of younger Friends were William C. Braithwaite, George Newman, M. Catharine Albright, T. 

Edmund Harvey, Lawrence Richardson, Roger Clark, Joan M. Fry, L. Violet Hodgkin, Arnold Rowntree, A. Neave Brayshaw, Ernest Grace and Ernest E. Taylor.

As time went on, this group of young leaders continu-

ally expanded, and the list became too long to be recorded here.

More than seven hundred persons attended this first Summer School, and it proved to be a very remarkable instrument for the intellectual and spiritual qualification of the membership. Many more such Schools were to follow in great succession. In 1898 John launched his periodical which was called Present Day Papers. The first Issue contained Essays by leading 

Friends of the period and occupied 94 pages. Volume 

II contained a searching Article by John Wilhelm on The Problem of Ministry which had become in his mind the foremost problem of the Society. Here he dealt with the question, "Is the Quaker conception of public worship of essential and permanent value?" So important did this issue seem to him that he came to America in company with A. Neave Brayshaw in 1899 to make an extensive first-hand study of types of meetings and ministry and ways of worship in America, especially in sections where a Pastoral system was in operation. The results of this research are given in his 

Essay, A Study in Ecclesiastical Polity.¹ Meantime he had begun his carefully planned systematic research for his proposed History of Quakerism. The first fruits of that research were given in three Lectures at Kirby

Plans were all the time being matured for more summer schools, as the one at Scarborough had demonstrated the effectiveness of the method. The second one was held in Birmingham in 1899 and was a tremendous success. It was at this Summer School that John Wilhelm gave his remarkable lecture on "The Problem of a Free Ministry," to which I have already referred,later printed in Present Day Papers. It was, I think, the most statesmanlike treatment of the subject which up to that time had ever been made. One short extract from it is all that can be given: "It must be clearly recognized that the characteristic note of the Quaker Meeting, with its cherished freedom of dependence on God, is endangered not by the intellectual equipment of the ministry, but by its restriction to one man or to one type. It is difficult to over-estimate the extent of the mischief which this dread of the intellect has

wrought. In the first place it has led to a limited view of what constitutes a call to the ministry. It is profoundly true that God does call his servant unexpectedly in the presence of a congregation and bids him speak unprepared. We may rejoice that Friends have not minimized that supreme prophetic gift. It is also true that no minister can rightly speak without a strong conviction when he rises, that what he says is said in season. But the deduction that there can be no call during the week, and no right preparation of the message, is wholly inadmissible. God works in many ways, and Friends living in the freedom of the spirit should be the last to maintain such an artificial limitation."

The next important Summer School was held in 1900 at Haverford College. John Wilhelm came over for it and so also did Rendel Harris. Then came the second

Scarborough School in 1901 which was lengthened out to a period of four weeks. It was in every sense an epoch. I lived during the period of it in John Wilhelm's home in nearby Scalby, and here I could see and feel the high quality of his spiritual leadership. 

In 1902 the Summer School was in Windermere in the Lake District, and in 1903 came the great gathering at Woodbrooke. Meantime as these Summer Schools were doing their illuminating work on the membership of the Society, John's plans for a permanent school were

taking shape. In December 1899 he worked out in an Article for Present Day Papers the outlines of his long dream for "a Quaker Settlement." It had been developing in his mind for three years and was hardly ever absent from his thought. The realization of the dream was finally made possible through the generosity of George Cadbury, who presented his old home, "Woodbrooke", also with gifts for endowment, and the Quaker Settlement which was born in John's mind became a visible reality in 1903. 

All through these busy years of accomplishment John was at work on his proposed History of Quakerism. 

He collected a Library of two thousand volumes and pamphlets as a preparation for the great Opus which he expected would occupy him for ten years.¹ Meantime he had built "Friedensthal" as a guest-house and holiday home for Adult School families, on the extensive grounds of his new home, "Low Hall”, at

Caronia docked in New York. His wife accompanied him, and fortunately I met the steamer and got him at once to a hospital and in the care of one of the foremost doctors in New York City. The disease had seriously affected his kidneys and had produced extensive uremic poisoning, so that his brain was in abnormal condition. He kept calling piteously all the time for me but steadily refused to believe that I was the "real Rufus" whom he wanted. We did everything humanly possible, but there was no recovery, and he passed away from us on the 9th of March. His cousin

Arnold came over at once and we held the funeral services at Haverford on the 17th of March and interred the body of our beloved Friend in the little yard at the Meeting House. In the midst of our grief we learned from the Chicago doctor, who came to the funeral service, that the severe treatment which John had been undergoing was proving ineffective, and that the insidious disease was not being checked. This fact somewhat reconciled us to the event of his death, which at the first seemed to be an overwhelming disaster, for we came to see that the sudden going, when his

extraordinary mental powers were unimpaired, had spared him the slow but irresistible march of the contraction and limitation of his unique capacity for life and service.

It can be said without any qualification that John Wilhelm Rowntree was one of the most remarkable of all the young leaders in our Quaker history. In the first place he understood with an almost uncanny insight the weaknesses that beset the Society, and he saw with equal clarity the right line of advance and the right means to promote it. He was an inspiring person. He possessed the quality which Madame Guyon called "spiritual fecundity", but he never counted on these striking traits or relied on them to accomplish his aims. He was constantly bringing forth out of his fertile mind creative methods and constructive means for attaining what he had in view. He possessed in a high degree an imaginative faculty of foreseeing what would work and what would promote the cause which possessed his being. The supreme asset of course was his luminous and radiant personality. His gifts were unusual, his culture was broad, his love was intense, he understood with swiftness and accuracy the character of persons with whom he was thrown, and he possessed an astonishing capacity for work.

Few things show the true, deep nature of a person more clearly than does his humor. The play of John's

spirit was one of the loveliest things about him. He thoroughly enjoyed life and his happy spirit flowed out in sheer joy. He was full of good stories which he told with striking effect, and one could hardly believe that this man, weighted with his sense of mission and constantly facing the breakdown of his powers, could be so gay and so full of humor. A little party of us in 

1903 made an excursion from Birmingham to Fenny Drayton to see the birthplace of George Fox. It was a most congenial group, on a most interesting excursion, but what everyone remembers most was the way John lighted up the entire trip with the happy touches of his humor and the brilliant scintillation of his mind. 

He was intensely human, and he could pass with perfect ease from the strain of work, or from the solemn meditation of spirit to complete relaxation in happy innocent play, as though one were as truly a part of life as the other. He believed that religion meant the abundant life, the full life, and in the completest way he practiced this faith. 

The greatest single gift John possessed was, I am convinced, his gift in the ministry, his power to interpret spiritual realities. All his work in all the many lines of it focussed upon the improvement and deepening of ministry in Friends Meetings. That was his constant concern. And all the time he was revealing, from strength to strength and from glory to glory,

what ministry might be and might become. Each year the power and range of his speaking took on new growth. I have heard only one person who in his preaching seemed to me to surpass him in impressiveness and depth of utterance. That was Phillips Brooks,

America's greatest preacher. John possessed many of the qualities which united to form the greatness of 

Phillips Brooks' preaching. He had a depth of conviction, a fervor of expression, a solid body of truth, a wealth of illustration, a melodious voice, a mystical note and a radiant personality behind every word he spoke.

The greatest sermon I ever heard him preach was in Twelfth Street Meeting House, Philadelphia, in 1902. 

He spoke on the odd text in Acts: "And Paul reasoned daily in the School of Tyrannus for the space of two years." He went on to show how in this School of a philosopher of Ephesus Christianity in a great city met the pagan Greco-Roman world and conquered it. He pictured the Greek world which surged around this Lecture Room. He marshalled St. Paul's message and presented it so vividly that the whole wealth of St. 

Paul's Aegean Gospel unfolded before us with its conquering power. And then all of a sudden he pictured the modern world with its materialism and its vast problems and presented the message which, if brought in reality and power, would transform it and spiritualize

it. Joshua Rowntree was present at that Meeting and later referred to it as one of the great occasions in John's ministry. That morning John had been given an enormous dose of strychnine, injected into his veins, as a feature of his medical treatment. It produced a powerful "jumping" of his muscles, and all the way to the city that morning his arms constantly jumped and twitched with convulsive movements. This had subsided by the time he rose to speak, but on the way home I playfully remarked that I was going to get a doctor to treat me the same way so that I might preach like that! Many persons who were present in the great 

Meeting in the Plymouth Meeting House, near Norristown, Pa., thought that was John's greatest sermon, but 

I was not present and cannot judge. If it was greater than the Twelfth Street one, it must have been wonderful. Many Friends who heard his last sermon in 

York, before he sailed in 1905, remember that was an Epoch. He closed on that occasion with the words of the hymn: "Hold Thou Thy Cross before my closing eyes, Shine through the gloom, and point me to the skies; 

Heaven's morning breaks, and earth's vain shadows flee. 

In life, in death, O Lord, abide with me." 

I should like to say at the end, what a loving friend wrote for the tablet to R. L. Nettleship in Balliol Col-

lege Chapel: "He loved great things and thought little of himself. Desiring neither fame nor influence, he won the devotion of men and was a power in their lives; and seeking no disciple, he taught to many the greatness of the world and of man's mind." 

I finished the last Volume of the Rowntree Series of Quaker History with these words: "In the summer of 1897 John Wilhelm Rowntree and I, planning together our future Quaker work, climbed the Schilthorn in Switzerland. As we came round a curve in our ascent we saw the morning star just above the peak of the Jungfrau. A little later it disappeared, swallowed up in the greater light of the rising sun. The bestowal of the morning star seems thus to be the promise of a fuller day, a growing future and an expanding life. 

I have wondered all through the fifteen years which I have worked at this series of histories whether the Quakerism whose history, with labor and fidelity, we have now recorded is to prove to be a waning or a growing light-the morning star heralding a larger spiritual dawn, or an evening star slowly sinking with a narrowing area of light. May the promise to Thyatira be granted to the later successors of Fox-"I will give them the morning star."
