FOREWORD 

In 1960 after two years of intensive study a group from New York University published a report known as the "New York University Workmen's 11 Compensation Study. This study was concerned primarily with (1) how effective rehabilitation services might be made promptly available to workmen's compensation claimants under the New York Workmen's Compensation Law and (2) changes, legislative, administrative and otherwise, which appeared necessary to best establish a practical system of achieving the primary objective. 

Although legislation based on such recommendations was introduced in 1962, it was not enacted. Within the two years which have elapsed since the "New York University Workmen's Compensation Study" was published, a number of administrative actions have been taken by the New York State Workmen's Compensation Board which have aided greatly in the provision of better and more rapid rehabilitation services to disabled claimants. 

One of the primary objectives of the New York University Study was to recommend methods whereby prompt referral of cases which might benefit from rehabilitation procedures could be ensured. We found one of the deterrents to early referral is the delay which may arise in the early stages of the case in establishing liability of the carrier or selfinsurer. If there is a contest at this point, the claimant's right to medical care under the Workmen's Compensation Law remains uncertain. uncertainty remains pending hearings which are often prolonged. Until liability is established the long-term rehabilitation plans are likely to be postponed with the result frequently that a disability which might have been corrected or substantially minimized through early treatment becomes permanent.

Although the number of controverted cases we found to be a relatively small percentage of the total, we thought the volume sufficiently great as to warrant a special study dealing specifically with this problem. 

The report which follows is based on such a study. It is the first of its kind to the knowledge of the investigators and this writer. 

A review of the findings shows the value of the study in that some suppositions commonly held by authorities in workmen's compensation were found not to be true. Other suppositions were confirmed. 

The principle value of this study is that which results from new knowledge. New all well-conceived and well-executed research knowledge based on research is greatly needed in the field of rehabilitation. Too many so-called "studies" of workmen's compensation both in 

New York State and other jurisdictions in the past have been a potpourri characterized by opinions, prejudices, vested interests and misinformation rather than cold facts.

CHAPTER INTRODUCTION 

As the earliest form of social insurance provided in this country, the workmen's compensation systems have been subjects of continuous investigation by both public and private agencies. Some inquiries have been concerned primarily with administration, others with substantive issues and many have given attention to both areas in the belief that administration and substance are inseparable parts of the same basic problem. The author of a recent comprehensive study of workmen's compensation remarks that of all economic security measures the workmen's compensation laws "are probably the most investigated and least understood". One reason given for the multiplicity of investigations is that, in spite of their existence in some form for a period of half a century, the laws have never been considered quite satisfactory. 

They are difficult to understand because of the variety and complexity of the various statutes and the absence of a body of reliable and comparable data on their operations. 

Each state has enacted its own statute and federal laws cover federal civilian employees and certain private employees engaged in longshoring and harbor work within the maritime jurisdiction of Congress. 

Statistics of operations and other data collection generally reflect the provisions of the specific laws. These results do not fit neatly into an over-all pattern. Therefore, much of the study in the field has attempted to bring these diverse materials together in order to obtain a view of developments in a particular state or to examine the workmen's compensation system as a whole. 1.

Purpose and Method of Study: 

By comparison with the many broad-based investigations of workmen's compensation, the scope of this study is a limited one. The purpose here is to examine the effect of controverted cases in which liability under workmen's compensation is denied on the process of rehabilitation of workers incurring industrial accidents or occupationconnected illness.

Special interest in this segment of workmen's compensation cases developed during the New York University Study undertaken for purposes of recommending to the New York State Workmen's Compensation 

Board methods for obtaining prompt evaluation and referral of claimants for rehabilitation. A group of physicians under the chairmanship of 

Dr. Howard A. Rusk expressed concern about the delays due to litigation over terms of settlement of claims and were particularly interested in the controverted cases in which liability is contested. In such instances the claimant's income is cut off by inability to work and the insurance carrier, or the employer, is not liable for medical payments until a hearing is held and a decision has been rendered in favor of the claimant. Under the New York State statute (Sec. 13-f) a physician is prohibited from accepting fees from compensation claimants, so an injured workman may have difficulty in securing medical attention of any sort during the period of uncertainty. These conditions place the claimant under severe economic pressure and are alleged to induce many settlements on the basis of such pressure rather than on the merits of the case. 

Investigation by the Research Unit of the New York University

Study revealed that there was a paucity of information on the nature

of these disputed cases. The New York State Workmen's Compensation 

Board provided statistics on their volume and final determination, but no qualitative information was found to be available in New York or in any other jurisdiction. While recognizing that these cases constitute a relatively small volume of the total case load, the New York University Study Team concluded that they were of significance and further explora- 2 tion was recommended, especially with respect to the methods of securing individual treatment during the period of uncertainty, the costs involved and how medical expenses are paid.

Background of Workmen's Compensation: 

Workmen's compensation legislation evolved from the belief that the application of the common law as a means of dealing with the cases of industrially injured employees created undue hardship for the worker and was, therefore, socially undesirable. Before the passage of workmen's compensation laws, in order to receive indemnity for wage loss and medical costs, the injured worker had no recourse but to sue his employer for damages to recover losses incurred by accident or illness in the course of employment. Many workers failed to institute

suits and when they did, they experienced difficulty in establishing a case, since it was necessary to prove employer negligence and the worker without fault. Employers' liability laws were enacted to limit the common law rules and ease the position of the worker. But under both methods of dealing with industrial accidents long and usually expensive litigation was necessary to establish a claim. At the time when financial aid was most needed the worker was left without income for maintenance of himself and family or for meeting medical expenses. 

Workmen's compensation laws introduced the principle of 

Under workmen's liability without fault and set up a system holding the employer responsible for payment of a portion of lost wages for the duration of disability and for assumption of medical costs, with certain limits on one or both as prescribed in the specific statutes. Hence the intricate legal issue of negligence was removed from cases involving work accidents and illness held to be connected with employment. compensation the question of fault arises only in exceptional instances. 

Many statutes exclude disabilities which are attributable to such causes as worker intoxication, or willful violation of safety regulations on the part of the employee. In instances where a law permits worker choice between the compensation system and a damage suit, the common law rules of negligence apply in the prosecution of the suit and, therefore, determination of fault is necessary. 

Employers covered by the statutes may insure themselves against the risk through a private insurance carrier, a state fund, or under specified conditions they may qualify as self-insurers.

In a few states there are exclusive state funds while others provide for insurance through

private carriers only.

Whatever costs are incurred are calculated as costs of business operation and are absorbed eventually in the price of the commodity or service.

The first legislation embodying this new principle was enacted in 1908 when Congress passed a statute applying to civil employees of the federal government. In 1927 a similar statute brought longshore and harbor workers under a federal system. 

Following the initial action of the federal government, the 

Eleven states states began to enact workmen's compensation statutes. passed such laws in 1911 and by 1920 all but six had adopted some type of compensation statute. At the present time all states have set up workmen's compensation systems. The early state statutes tended to be restrictive in nature in that they covered only accidents in specified hazardous occupations. The scope of the laws has been extended so that most of them now cover accidents in the general run of employments and also occupational diseases and other illness considered as resulting from the nature of employment.

Some of the early laws were held unconstitutional on the grounds that they were compulsory. For this reason a number of the original enactments were elective and have remained so, although the compulsory laws have long been sustained by the courts. 

Rehabilitation and Workmen's Compensation: 

The underlying objective of workmen's compensation is the restoration of an injured worker to the maximum level of physical wellbeing as a person and the highest possible efficiency as a wage earner.

Inherent in this objective is a three-fold program for dealing with the 3 Occupationally injured and disabled. 

The first is the prevention of accidents and industrial illness. Since the best of preventive measures fails to eliminate all hazards, a second measure is the program for cash benefits and medical care during the period of disability. Then there is the final stage of providing after-care and restorative measures needed to return the disabled worker, in so far as possible, to his former state of health and to income earning employment. 

During most of the period since the passage of the statutes, activities relating to the third phase of the program of workmen's compensation have been neglected. Many employers and administrators have endeavored to create interest in preventive measures, with a resulting decline in the incidence of industrial injuries, but on the whole the major emphasis in most systems has been on the medical care and cash benefit programs.

In recent years, however, there has developed a positive emphasis on the rehabilitation of the worker, an interest which goes beyond mere physical restoration as a part of conventional medical care. The objective is to employ specialized means for enabling the injured to function as actively as possible and extends to the physical preparation for placement of the worker in suitable income-earning employment.

This emphasis was accelerated greatly following the successful experience of the Veterans' Administration in rehabilitating a number

Controverted Cases: 

By eliminating the necessity of establishing negligence in a court of law, the workmen's compensation statutes have greatly reduced the complexity of legal issues arising in cases of compensation for work connected disabilities. Nevertheless, there are areas of controversy remaining in the administration of workmen's compensation. controversial issues and legal processes remain is attested to by perennial criticism leveled against the "legalistic atmosphere" prevailing in the administration of the statutes. The basis of disputed workmen's compensation claims may be classified in two categories. The first includes those in which liability under the statute is denied. Grounds for denial may be: (1) the absence of the employer-employee relationship; (2) the contention that the injury was not incurred in the course of employment; (3) the allegation that the occupational disease or other disabling illness giving rise to the claim were not related to the occupation or the specific conditions of employment of the claimant. 

The greater proportion of controversial cases are found in the second category. These are cases in which liability is assumed but there is dispute as to the nature and extent of the injury, the degree of residual disability and the type of medical treatment appropriate for a given type of injury.

- It is obvious that controversy results in much delay in the settlement of claims. A succession of hearings for presenting claims and counterclaims may consume many months and in some instances several years. Where liability is assumed and the controversy involves disagreement over the extent of the disability and specific terms of settlement, the insurance carrier, or employer, is responsible for provision of cash benefits and medical care during the period of dispute. In such cases the injured worker receives assistance while his claim is unsettled.

This is not the situation in the controverted case where liability is questioned. 

It is these cases, in the first category above, that are the particular concern in this study. Where there is denial of liability the injured worker is responsible for payment of medical bills, except in those cases where a carrier or employer may defray the initial expense "without prejudice" to the ultimate decision on the compensability of the claim. Physicians who render service during the period of dispute are uncertain about the payment of their bills. Should the injury require immediate treatment involving surgery and extended hospitalization beyond the ability of the worker to defray the costs, should the claim be ruled non-compensable, the problem is compounded.

More important, however, than the uncertainty over responsibility for payment of medical cost is the question as to whether or not long-range restorative treatment is provided during the period of controversy. Covered employers may insure through private carrier or the State Fund; or, under Board authorization, they may be self-insured. 

The State Fund is required to accept all applicants and, therefore, tends to insure a large proportion of "bad risks". On the basis of amount of premiums, it accounts for about 28 per cent of the Workmen's compensation insurance written in the state. The rest of the business is done by approximately 70 stock and 31 mutual private companies, with about 40 per cent written by the five largest carriers. 18.

A comprehensive examination of the program is found in Louis S. Reed, Medical Care Under the New York Workmen's Compensation Program, Sloan Institute of Hospital Administration, Graduate School of Business Administration, Cornell University, Second Edition - October 1961.

- New York is one of the states which has a special rehabilitation program under its workmen's compensation system, but the statute itself contains few references to rehabilitation. CHAPTER ASPECTS OF MEDICAL TREATMENT OF CLAIMANTS 

Under the New York State statute injured workers falling within the jurisdiction of the Workmen's Compensation system have free choice of physician, with stringent restrictions on any interference with this freedom. If the case is compensable, medical care with respect to cost and duration is unlimited. The concept of medical care has been interpreted to include rehabilitation in order to achieve maximum possible restoration of the injured. Although the Board has technical authority to supervise the nature and quality of the medical care rendered, generally speaking, it has not exercised this authority. However, the Board receives notices of medical expenditures and, in instances of disagreement in medical testimony presented, may refer the issues to its Medical Department or to a panel of specialists for an impartial opinion prior to making a final ruling on a case.

Using these reports to the Board and the records of insurance carriers it has been possible to accumulate a considerable body of information about the pattern of medical care in the controverted cases. 

The data are limited and at times not altogether clear for two major reasons. First, in a number of cases the medical reports contain insufficient details about what was done. In some instances only the total bills are included, indicating that treatment was received and perhaps the specialized status of the physician rendering the service. 

Because of these circumstances it has been necessary at times to fit pieces of information together and, therefore, to assume a tentative attitude in drawing conclusions. 36-

The other chief reason for limitation in the data is the status of a claimant in a controverted case. As discussed above, such a claimant has no de jure right to receive medical care until the question of liability has been settled.

Whatever medical care he receives is on his own motion and responsibility, except in cases where employers or carriers may assume cost, usually "without prejudice". For cases held compensable there is at least a partial record from the date of injury to the time treatment is terminated, but for non-compensated cases often the early record is sketchy and there is practically none after a final ruling has gone against the claimant.

The following analysis of the more important aspects and the general pattern of medical service rendered this special group of claimants has been developed with awareness of limitations to which attention has been called. These materials are descriptive primarily, since no effort is made to evaluate the quality or adequacy of medical care. Related issues of the length of the period of controversy and cost of medical services will be discussed in later sections.

CHAPTER MEDICAL COSTS OF CONTROVERTED CASES 

One of the significant tasks of this study was to investigate the costs of medical care in the controverted cases, fees of physicians, hospital bills, and discover how these costs were met while responsibility for payment was in doubt.

It was felt at the outset that the uncertainty on the claimant's part as to whether his claim subsequently would be ruled compensable or not, and thus whether he himself would or would not be forced to bear the financial costs of medical care, added a psychological burden to the physical one already in existence. The element of uncertainty in controverted claims has still another possible adverse manifestation; that is the allegation that often insurance carriers put physicians on notice that a particular claim is being controverted, thereby discouraging them from undertaking extensive or expensive medical treatment as the doctors might have to collect their fees directly from the patient rather than from the insurance carrier. On the claimant's part, the fear of having to incur substantial medical costs is likely to result in a marked reluctance to accept referral to a specialist and hence in certain instances to less than optimum medical care.

Because of the delay in the settlement of medical bills in those cases for which the carriers assume responsibility eventually and the problem of collecting for services rendered those whose claims are held non-compensable, especially when the treatment has been costly, some physicians believe that the present system places undue burdens on those who accept workmen's compensation patients. One dilemma is making a decision as to appropriate medical care and another is the practical matter of the - 48.

possibility of unpaid bills.

These factors affecting claimants and physicians appear to have contributed to lower costs of medical care among the non-compensated cases, suggesting either that the latter category represents injuries of lesser severity or that insurance carriers and physicians have some prior knowledge on the basis of experience as to whether or not a controverted claim will be ruled compensable. 

In interpreting the data on costs, the reader is cautioned that information on costs of both doctors' and hospital bills was reported in a rather unsystematic fashion and that in a large percentage of cases there was failure to report any costs whatsoever. application of relevant statistical techniques indicates that the magnitude of error will not be greater than 15 per cent.

CHAPTER SUMMARY OF FINDINGS, IMPLICATIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS 

The preceding chapters have reported specific findings based on statistical evidence and have in many instances included generalizations and interpretative comments. The purpose of this chapter is to recapitulate the more important conclusions and to discuss them within the context of the broad dimensions of the controverted cases and to make certain recommendations with respect to the handling of such cases. 

After certain significant issues emerged in the course of the investigation, these matters were discussed in some detail with a variety of persons concerned with facets of the workmen's compensation system. These included members of the Board staff, representatives of the carriers (private and the State Fund), medical personnel, and attorneys specializing in workmen's compensation. Efforts to obtain opinions on the specific issues from certain labor representatives brought negative results, but a few published statements were found helpful.

Opinions and ideas from these experienced sources are interwoven in the following discussion of the implications of the controverted cases and have influenced to some extent the direction of the recommendations made on the basis of the findings. 

Summary of Findings: 

The more essential conclusions based on specific findings may be summarized as follows: 

1. Although controverted cases constitute a small percentage of the total, they are of sufficient importance to warrant special treatment in a social insurance system such as workmen's compensation. 2. 

Most of the cases do not involve injuries of a catastrophic nature. Reliable comparable data are not available, but, on the basis of what is known about the distribution of injuries for all workmen's compensation cases, there is nothing unique in this respect about the controverted segment. 64 -

· · 

3.

The characteristics of claimants 

sex, age and occupationshow no sharp divergence from general labor force patterns. 

Claimants in the low and upper age brackets are not outstanding in numbers, but their presence is more pronounced in non-compensated cases; nor are the numbers in the upper age groups suffering from what might be termed chronic illness noticeably great. 

4. The most frequent reasons for controverting a case are denial of occurrence of an accident and no causal relationship established between disability and employment. 

5. Most of the cases are settled within a year, but a significant number run for much longer periods. In view of the nature of most injuries, these extended periods appear to have no reasonable basis. 

6. The results do not indicate any significant differences in the controverting practices among the insurers. The completed cases indicate a higher percentage of contested claims and cases lost by the State Fund, but this conclusion is not sustained on the basis of the original drawing of cases. It is noticeable that self-insurers, with a small proportion of total cases, appear to be more successful than the carriers in sustaining contested claims, possibly because they are able to exercise greater discrimination in the selection of cases to controvert. 

7. Except for a small number of non-compensated cases, all claimants received some type of medical care. But for a significant number in both categories of cases, observation and diagnostic tests constitute the only type of care given. Specialized medical services are provided more frequently for compensated than for non-compensated claimants. Most claimants are not hospitalized and for the majority medical treatment is terminated before final rulings are made. 

8. Information available on the cost of medical care in controverted cases shows a wide range for both medical fees and hospital cost. 

The range for non-compensated claimants is distinctly narrower, with a larger percentage having total costs of less than $300. The total medical cost during the controversy is $757,331 for compensated and $550,514 for non-compensated cases. Post-ruling costs for compensated cases amount to $263,345. These figures cover a six-month period and indicate a greater number of more costly cases in the compensated category. Since the distribution of types of injuries in the two categories fails to reflect such a marked difference, the conclusion is that the more severe injuries requiring more expensive treatment are concentrated among the compensated claimants.

· 669. For a considerably number of cases there is no information about the payment of medical costs during the period of controversy. The majority of those reporting indicate that the bills were not paid. In the small number of cases with bills paid, a few claimants assumed the cost themselves; in others the carriers assumed full or partial cost. 

10. The numbers of claimants referred for rehabilitation evaluation and treatment are small, both before and after rulings. 

The numbers increase slightly in the latter period, with an increased number of referrals through the Board rather than by a physician; also, greater use is made of specialized rehabilitation facilities. For non-compensated cases, no use is indicated for specialized rehabilitation centers, the few cases reporting rehabilitation being treated in the office of a physician. The absence of truly comparable figures prevents a judgment in whether or not the proportion of cases referred is unduly low. 11.

The numbers of claimants with serious residual disabilities are low. The majority returned to work eventually, either to their former employment or to new jobs.

Implications of Findings: 

That the controverted cases are of sufficient significance to warrant special treatment is agreed to by most of the knowledgeable persons in the field whose opinions were sought. Dissenting opinions on the part of a minority ranged from the contention that there was "no problem" to assertions that a problem exists but a skeptical attitude expressed concerning the possible advantages of "stirring it up."

The inter- There is general consensus that time consumed in settling many of the disputes over liability is too protracted and that, except in unusual cases, extended delays are unnecessary. ested groups tend to place the blame for delays on each other. It may be noted that many of these persons, when given data on the whole picture, expressed surprise at the average length of time cases are pending.

No evidence or opinion was found to support the allegation often made that physicians refuse to treat claimants when responsibility for payment is uncertain. Although in some instances claimants appear to receive no medical treatment, most of them are seen by a physician. However, as has been stated in the body of the report, specialized treatment is often postponed until the question of who is to defray the cost is settled. Delay of this kind may prolong the period of treatment, and thus greater medical costs may be incurred. 

This situation is especially acute for claimants in need of rehabilitation, since it is generally agreed that the most effective restorations occur when treatment is provided promptly. 

It is generally agreed that the insurance carriers take the responsibility for controverting a case, employers intervening only in rare instances where there appears to be some personal prejudice against the employee. Efforts to learn if there is a pattern in the cases which carriers contest were not conclusive. One carrier representative stated that it was company policy to file denials in all hernia cases and all claims of elderly persons regardless of the nature of the injury. Figures on the types of injuries in controverted cases show a large percentage of hernia cases, many of which are upheld, but no great concentration of elderly claimants. How general this automatic policy of controverting is and its possible effect cannot be determined.

One other general comment is significant. Feeling was expressed that claimants of low income status, and especially those with language handicaps, are likely to have their claims pending for longer periods than those of skilled workers, who are articulate

- and may have assistance from a union in pressing a claim. 

The suggestion was made that special counselling services would be of great benefit to the less privileged and less sophisticated claimants. 

The investigation revealed that there is apparently no regular procedure for counselling the claimants. This appears to be of more serious import for the claimants whose cases are lost. 

In a legal sense the responsibility of the Board terminates when a case is held non-compensable, but a systematic plan for referring appropriate cases to the Cash Disability Benefits program, which is administered by the Board, might be worked out. Medical care is not provided in this program but injured workers found to be outside the scope of the workmen's compensation system might qualify for cash benefits. Also, cases requiring continued medical care without sufficient personal means might be referred automatically to appropriate medical and welfare agencies. The non-compensated claimants with injuries usually requiring rehabilitation would benefit especially by intelligent referral to a rehabilitation center.

Major suggestions for improvement in the handling of controverted cases are of two types: (1) those relating to matters of procedure; and (2) those concerned with issues of medical care during the period of controversy. 

Suggestions For Procedural Changes: 

Underlying the proposals for procedural changes is the belief that the processing of controverted cases needs to be, and can be, accelerated. The most usual suggestions are for development

-. of special calendars through which contested claims be given priority, for greater use of pre-hearing conferences (used effectively in the administration of the Federal Employees Liability Act and the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act) and using the Medical Department of the Board or impartial specialists to obtain a speedy resolution of cases in which medical opinion is crucial, this action of the Board to be taken when the cases have been pending for 60 to 

90 days.

In this connection it should be pointed out that, since the inauguration of this investigation, the Board has set up a new procedure designed to achieve the objective of greater speed while at the same time safeguarding the rights of the interested parties. 

The revised procedure provides for immediate indexing of notice of controversy and the carrier or self-insured employer is notified within four or five days. Notice of a pre-trial hearing, wherever practical, is then set for between 15 and 21 days from the date of indexing. One morning a week is set aside in each calendar part for the purpose of clarifying issues. At this time the carrier may concede some or all points at issue. However, should the carrier wish to press further one or more points at issue, a full hearing is set, if possible, within 21 to 30 days. According to officials of the Board, processing of controverted cases has been expedited under the new system, but no detailed study has been made to test the overall effect. In fact, the procedure became operative so recently that few of the carriers indicated an awareness of the change.

Proposals For Medical Care During Controversy: 

Practical proposals for improving the situation of claimants with respect to medical care while their cases are pending are more difficult to develop than those relating to procedure, for the most 31 obvious reason of the cost element. A closely related problem is that any system for assumption of responsibility for defraying the cost of medical care prior to the determination of liability may encourage the filing of numbers of cases of quite doubtful validity. A multiplicity of such claims for which medical costs would be assured until the date of settlement would place an undue burden on funds provided and would also increase the case load so that improved procedures would be vitiated.

The most radical proposal is that medical care for all types of disabilities might be provided by general health insurance laws. In this event the medical care functions of workmen's compensation administration would be transferred to the health insurance agency. This has occurred in 

Great Britain since the National Health Service was set up. The possibility of such a comprehensive system in the United States seems so remote at present that discussion of a policy depending on it seems unrealistic. However, in this connection it may be significant that there has been some discussion in New York State of adding medical benefits to the existing Cash Disability 

Benefits system.

A less drastic proposal is that the Board make provision for medical care and rehabilitation as needed in all controverted cases until liability has been determined, the funds to be accumulated 31.

Should a case be decided in favor of the claimant the carrier or employer responsible would reimburse the fund for the outlay. In non-compensated cases, the fund would absorb the cost prior to the decision. Thus the non-recoverable outlay would be that for non-compensated claimants. 

According to the cost figures developed in this study, this would amount on an annual basis to approximately $1,100,000. This assumes that the volume of cases, the types of injuries and the pattern of medical care and costs would be generally as described here. To the extent that the higher medical costs indicated for compensated as compared to non-compensated cases are attributable to greater severity of injury to claimants who happen to fall into the compensated category rather than less good medical care provided for the non-compensated, there might be little change in total costs. But if all claimants in need of specialized treatment received it immediately and not after the ruling on their claims, it is likely that total costs in non-compensated cases would be greater than the above amount. Also, the effect of medical cost coverage on the volume of cases brought to the Board and the number controverted is unpredictable, but most opinions indicate the likelihood of an increase in both volume of cases and amount of controversy. 

A more selective system proposed is one which would provide medical costs for a group of injury types and levels of severity which in the opinion of medical experts need immediate and specialized treatment in order to achieve maximum recovery in a minimum period of time.

-- · - Data contained in this study supply at least tentative indications of what these costs might be. The numbers of non-compensated claimants by type of injury are shown in Table 13; in Table 28 are found the average costs of treatment of each type of injury during the period of controversy. 

If, for example, medical experts should list all muscle injuries and all fractures, disc disorders, injuries of internal organs, and those of brain, spinal cord and peripheral nerves as injuries needing immediate special medical treatment, using the median cost of each type, the total amount of outlay on an annual basis would be approximately $228,000. 

This estimate includes all cases in these classifications regardless of severity, but the median cost of treatment figures tend to eliminate the extremes in cost. Should medical opinion indicate additions to or subtractions from this list, similar calculations of what are probably minimum costs could be made. It should be reiterated that this estimate is based on the distribution of injuries and the costs indicated in the data collected in this study. 

Another suggestion is that the carriers accept all cases without prejudice and assume the medical cost until decisions are made, as apparently they do in certain instances at present. It is felt in some quarters that this procedure would be effective in arriving at decisions with a minimum of delay, especially when the Board is alerted to the need for special handling of cases in which liability is disputed. Here again the data contained in this study provide clues to the overall costs but do not indicate what the costs might be for individual carriers.

Recommendations: 

It is beyond the scope of this report to recommend substantive changes in the Workmen's Compensation statute with respect to the controverted cases. Possible changes should be based on a number of considerations which may have been suggested here but have not been explored sufficiently. However, in the light of the findings much can be done to shorten the period of controversy and to render special service to claimants in controverted cases within the framework of the present law. 

The new procedure for handling controverted cases recently adopted by the Board indicates an awareness of their special character. 

The cases are a small proportion of the total case load and many of them are settled within a reasonable length of time. The remaining ones, however, are those of particular concern. 

Although all contested claims are tinged with more than the usual atmosphere of adversary proceedings, claimants with more serious injuries are likely to experience confusion and manifest ill-feeling toward the compensation system.

It is recommended that the Board's new procedure be broadened to provide: 

1. Concerted effort to encourage settlement of issues in informal pre-hearing conferences. 2. 

Greater emphasis on the established procedure to provide careful screening and counselling for all claimants in controverted cases through the Rehabilitation Unit or a special section designated for this purpose. Through such counselling, claimants who in the opinion of the Medical Department are in need of rehabilitation evaluation or treatment should be referred to available facilities in the community as soon as possible. Especially for those cases likely to benefit from vocational re-training and thus return to income-earning status, early reference to the state Division of Vocational Rehabilitation is recommended. 

730-504 O - 64 - 6

74 . Special effort in behalf of non-compensated claimants to review the nature of the injury for purposes of making appropriate referrals to the Cash Disability Benefits program, to health and welfare agencies or to vocational and employment services. 4. 

An adaptation of the "R" form for use in controverted cases, the Board to be notified of reasons that cases are unsettled at the expiration of 60 days. If reasons for delay involve disputes over medical questions, the Medical Department to make determinations or to seek opinions from impartial specialists, thus resolving the issue.

It has been noted that few "R" forms were found in the records during the course of the investigation and that in some instances plans for rehabilitation on the forms submitted apparently had not been carried out. Therefore, it is recommended that the 

Board make special effort to inspect the forms and to check on performance when controverted cases are reported. 

Any plan for providing payment of medical costs through the Board would require statutory change, since at present the Board has neither the funds nor the authority to undertake this responsibility. In view of the considerable number of controverted claims which are made but not pursued seriously and the numbers involving injuries of minor severity, overall assumption of responsibility for payment of medical bills seems unnecessary. 

However, there is a residual group of cases involving more serious injuries for which specialized therapy is usually indicated. 

The data indicate a tendency to postpone such treatment until liability is established and, where treatment is made available, there are delays and uncertainty regarding the payment for medical service. 

Such a situation is unsatisfactory from the viewpoint of the injured as well as from that of persons or institutions rendering the service.

Therefore, it is recommended that the Board's Advisory Committee on Rehabilitation explore the proposition that the Board assume responsibility for medical supervision and payment of medical costs for certain types of cases, which in the opinion of medical experts need prompt referral for special treatment, including rehabilitation. Implicit in this recommendation is consideration of the source of funds to defray medical costs and of the impact of such additional responsibilities on the personnel planning of the Board. 

Reference has been made at several points in this report to the incomplete and confusing state of the case records, especially with respect to expenditures for medical treatment. In many instances it appears that neither the Board nor the carriers have accurate records of how much was paid out and for what particular service. Probably this condition is not confined to controverted cases but is characteristic of all cases. Since an orderly record seems important not only for students of the cases but also for the Board and the carriers, it is recommended that joint efforts be made to develop a systematic and uniform system of reporting medical expenditures.

APPENDIX PART A SAMPLING AND WEIGHTING PROCEDURE 

The universe from which the sample was drawn is defined as the state-wide file of controverted cases closed between January 1 and June 30, 1961.

The universe was divided into two strata: (1) compensated cases and (2) non-compensated cases. 

Compensated Cases: 

This stratum covered controverted cases included in the semi-annual Carrier Performance Study of the Workmen's Compensation 

Board Research and Statistics Office. This includes all controverted compensated cases closed except for the following classes, which are excluded from Carrier Performance: (1) schedule only, without temporary disability beyond 7 days; (2) reimbursement of employer; (3) facial disfigurement only; (4) death; (5) lump sum for substitute hire only (6) no insurance; and (7) volunteer firemen. Also excluded from the universe were cases indexed prior to January 1, 1955.

The compensated case universe so defined numbered 2,004 cases. 

The 6th digit of the 8 digit Workmen's Compensation Board case number was the basis for random selection; six digits, 0,2,4,5,6,8, were selected to provide an expected 1,200 cases (1,000 for the sample, plus 200 "substitutes" in the event that some sample cases could not be located. 

Workable cases number 726; thus, the ratio of sample size to the universe was 726/2004, or 0.362. Uniform weights were applied to each case. Examination of the tables will show a fair number, varying from one table to the next, of respondents not reporting a given question. In tables reporting costs of treatment, and length of treatment, persons not reporting were left out of account, which of course alters the ratio above. The varying ratios are indicated in Part B of this Appendix. 

Non-Compensated Cases: 

Non-compensated cases are divided by the Workmen's Compensation Board into 11 strata, according to the principal reasons for closing of cases; in these, controverted cases numbered 4,837. It was considered that the bulk of controverted cases are concentrated in two of these strata, and therefore only these two were sampled. 

They were:

A sample of 500 cases was selected from this universe of 2,004 cases. Again, the 6th digit of the 8 digit Workmen's Compensation Board Case Number was the basis for selection. Four digits, 1,3,5,9, were selected to provide an expected 600 cases, 500 for the sample and 100 substitutes in the event that some cases could not be located.

Workable cases number 345; thus, the ratio of sample size to the universe was 345/2004, or 0.172. Uniform weights were applied to each non-compensated case. In tables reporting costs of treatment and length of treatment, persons not reporting were left out of account. 

The altered ratios are indicated in Part B of this Appendix.
