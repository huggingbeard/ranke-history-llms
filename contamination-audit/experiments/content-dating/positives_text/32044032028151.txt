OUTLINE OF PATENT OFFICE INTERFERENCE PRACTICE Introduction 

The subject of interference practice may be said to embrace the adjective law, or the administrative law, governing the origination, formulation, trial and determination of rival claims to patent rights in identical or closely related subject matter. It is derived from certain provisions of the statutes of the United States enacted pursuant to 

Article I, Section 8, of the Constitution of the United States and the rules of the United States Patent Office promulgated thereunder. Underlying is the principle that there may be only one valid patent for a given invention. 1 The subject is technical. The presentation of it is complicated not only by a revision of the rules of practice which took effect March 1, 1949, except in certain cases, but by the revision and codification of the laws relating to patents and the Patent Office enacted July 19, 1952, effective January 1, 1953, and the new and revised rules of the Patent Office promulgated conformably therewith on December 2, 1952, effective January 1, 1953.2 Sec. 4(a) of the

Act of July 19, 1952, makes the Act applicable "to all applications for patent filed on or after" 

January 1, 1953, and "to all patents granted on such applications"; to "further proceedings on applications pending on such date and to patents granted on such applications except as otherwise provided"; and to 'unexpired patents granted prior to such date except as otherwise provided. 99

The exceptions, insofar as they are pertinent to the subject of interference practice, will be noted in connection with the presentation of the various provisions. 

Previously the patent laws were identified as sections of the Revised Statutes and as sections of the United States Code. Henceforth they will be identified and cited only as sections of Title 35 of the United States Code, for the Act of July 19, 1952, repealed previously existing statutes.

Section 135 of Title 35 of the United States Code, which may be regarded as a jurisdictional statute, provides in part that- "Whenever an application is made for a patent which, in the opinion of the Commissioner, would interfere with any pending application, or with any unexpired patent, he shall give notice thereof to the applicants, or applicant and patentee, as the case may be. 

The question of priority of invention shall be determined by a board of patent interferences (consisting of three examiners of interferences) whose decision, if adverse to the claim of an applicant, shall constitute the final refusal by the Patent Office of the claims involved, and the Commissioner may issue a patent to the applicant who is adjudged the prior inventor."'¹ 

It is apparent from the wording of the statute, as well as the provisions of Rule 201,2 and the decisions are to the effect that the object of an interference proceeding is to determine the question of priority of invention and ordinarily that is the only question that can be considered in such a proceeding. (Emphasis supplied.)

The cases in which the question of priority of invention is determinable are limited to two types: (1) when there are two or more applications for patent pending in the Patent Office which, in the opinion of the Commissioner, would interfere with one another, or (2) when there is pending in that office an application for a patent which, in the opinion of the Commissioner, would interfere with any unexpired patent. 

As the subject for consideration in an interference proceeding is thus limited, so the practice in such proceedings is restricted to the origination, the formulation, the trial and the determination of issues relating solely to the question of priority of invention. This practice will be outlined by presenting the principal steps involved in an interference proceeding in the Patent Office from its commencement to its conclusion in this sequence: (1) The steps preliminary to and including the declaration of an interference and tentative formulation of the interference issue. I. 

THE STEPS PRELIMINARY TO AND INCLUDING THE DECLARATION OF AN INTERFERENCE 

Requisites for Declaration of an Interference A prerequisite to the declaration of an interference is the filing in the Patent Office of a complete application for patent containing patentable subject matter which is common either to an application for patent of another also pending in the Patent Office or to an unexpired patent. 

The application may be for an original or a reissue patent. 

The patent may be either an original or a reissue patent. 

The parties must be different. 

Ordinarily it is not enough that there be merely a disclosure of common subject matter in an application. Ex parte Hallock, 81 USPQ 316. Such subject matter must be claimed. Especially is this so in the case of a patent, for, generally speaking, the issuance of a patent without claim to patentable subject matter disclosed therein constitutes a waiver of claim thereto. Moreover, the claim must be allowable in each application involved.¹ 

Rule 203(a) requires: "Claims in the same language, to form the counts. of the interference, must be present, or be presented, in each application."

Claims; Junior Party may be required to file Affidavit 

Supporting Right to Make 

Rule 202 provides that, in order to determine whether there may be a question of priority of invention, any junior applicant, that is, one whose application was filed later ¹ See Rule 201, which carried forward in substance a part of old 

Rule 94. Compare Rule 234(a), p. 46, infra.

than that of another, may be required to file a statement under oath of the date and the character of the earliest fact or act, susceptible of proof, which can be relied upon to establish conception of the invention under consideration for the purpose of establishing priority of invention.¹ 

In this connection, it is provided in Rule 202: 

The statement filed in compliance with this section will be retained by the Patent Office separate from the application file and if an interference is declared will be opened simultaneously with the preliminary statement of the party filing the same. In case the junior applicant makes no reply within the time specified, not less than thirty days, or if the earliest date alleged is subsequent to the filing date of the senior party, the interference ordinarily will not be declared."

THE STEPS SUBSEQUENT TO THE DECLARATION AND PRIOR TO THE TRIAL OF AN INTERFERENCE ISSUE The Preliminary Statement; its Purpose; Requirements 

The preliminary statement derives from the Rules of Practice promulgated by the Commissioner rather than the statutes. Its purpose is to obtain from each party to an interference an honest statement of the facts and dates essential to proof of his claim to priority of invention. 

This is to enable a determination of whether or not there is a substantial basis for the claim, to afford a comparison of the dates relied upon with the filing date of his adversary, and to fix the limits of proof to be submitted by a party in advance of his obtaining information of his adversary's case. A preliminary statement has some of the aspects of a bill of particulars in a civil action and largely outlines the proof to be relied upon by the party making it. 

While in exceptional cases a party has been permitted to establish dates earlier than those stated, under the present rules that is not contemplated. A preliminary statement, therefore, should be prepared with great care and the fullest possible understanding of the interference issue and the evidence available to the party to establish his claim to priority of invention.¹

The granting of an extension lies wholly within the discretion of the Examiner of Interferences and his decision will be disturbed only in unusual cases of hardship. A review of his decision may be obtained by petition to the Commissioner for the exercise of his supervisory authority.

If a preliminary statement be filed late without an extension, it should be accompanied by a motion supported by an affidavit presenting good and sufficient reasons why the statement was not presented earlier and an extension previously requested.

Subdivisions (c) and (d) of Rule 223, which include respectively the provisions of old Rules 115 and 117, read as follows: 

"(c) If a party to an interference fails to file a statement, testimony will not be received subsequently from him to prove that he made the invention at a date prior to the filing date of his application. "(d) The preliminary statement can in no case be used as evidence in behalf of the party making it." 

Rule 224, which has no counterpart in the old rules, contains restrictions on the reliance by a party to an interference on a prior application for the purpose of obtaining the benefit of its filing date. This rule, as amended effective January 1, 1953, reads: "A party will not be permitted to rely on a prior application to obtain the benefit of its filing date unless the prior application is specified in the notice of inter-

ference or is set forth in the preliminary statement. A prior foreign application cannot be relied upon unless the necessary papers to prove a date of priority under 

35 U.S.C. 119, including a translation, Rule 55, are filed within three months or within such extension of time as may be granted, from the filing of the preliminary statement, if they have not previously been filed. A motion to amend the preliminary statement to recite a prior application may be brought under Rule 222 but a copy of such prior application must be served on the opposing party with the motion."

Occasionally an application becomes involved in several interferences with different adversaries, and it may develop that a party is entitled to earlier dates than those stated in his preliminary statement in a previous interference. 

In such case, he may set forth the earlier dates in a preliminary statement in a later interference involving a different adversary. The procedure in such a case, if the 

Examiner restrict the party to his earlier stated dates, is to traverse the action of the Examiner.2 

Rule 219 requires that a preliminary statement must be enclosed in a sealed envelope before filing and the name of the party filing it, the title of the case, and the subject of the invention indicated on the envelope. The envelope should contain nothing but the statement, which is to be opened only by an Examiner of Interferences. 

If he find it to be satisfactory in form, he approves it. 

If he find a statement to be defective in any particular, the party filing it is notified of the defect and given a specified time within which he may remedy it by an amended statement, subject to restriction as specified in the notice of defects under Rule 221, in the further proceedings in the interference if he fail so to do.

Procedure after Approval of Preliminary Statement 

Rule 226, differing somewhat from old Rule 108, reads in part: "After the preliminary statements have been received and approved, or the time for filing them has expired, the parties will be notified, and given the serial numbers and filing dates of the applications of each adverse party, including any applications which the parties may be entitled to inspect, and the parties will be permitted to see or obtain copies of each other's applications, except copies of affidavits filed under

The preliminary statements are re-sealed by an examiner of interferences and shall not be revealed to the opposing parties except as provided in Rule 227."

The preliminary statements, under Rule 227(a), are not made available until final disposal of motions to dissolve under Rules 231 to 236, inclusive, if any be filed, and if none be filed upon the expiration of the motion period.¹ 

Amendment of Preliminary Statement 

According to Rule 222, correction of a material error in a preliminary statement arising through inadvertence or mistake may be made on motion accompanied by a satisfactory showing that the correction is essential to the ends of justice. The motion should be made promptly upon discovery of the error and in conformity with the requirements of Rule 243. Such motions are generally granted when made before approval and opening of preliminary statements to inspection. 

A motion to amend a preliminary statement following approval of the statement as filed must also be made in conformity with the provisions of Rule 243. Such a motion must be accompanied by the proposed amended statement, 1 See Ferm v. Nachtman, 99 USPQ 166.

and a satisfactory showing under oath of want of negligence in the preparation of the original statement. If the proposed amendment be based on newly discovered evidence, the specific nature of the new evidence, as well as the circumstances resulting in its discovery must also be so shown. 

This requirement is to enable the Examiner of Interferences to determine whether in the exercise of reasonable care the evidence could have been discovered earlier. Such motions are strictly regarded and every effort should be made to avoid having to make one. That a party may have misinterpreted a count when preparing his original preliminary statement has been held not to justify the allowance of an amendment.

The Court of Customs and Patent Appeals has held that it is without authority to treat a preliminary statement as amended in view of newly discovered evidence where the discovery was made too late to move to amend the preliminary statement under old Rule 113. Botnen v. Dorman (CCPA), 179 F(2) 249.

The Examiner of Interferences may defer ruling on a motion for leave to file an amended preliminary statement until the final hearing. If the motion be denied earlier, the party making it may appeal immediately to the Commissioner in person. Otherwise an appeal may not be taken until after a final decision on the merits of the interference. 

The appeal then is to the Court of Customs and Patent Appeals in connection with an appeal on the merits. The reason for this difference in procedure is that the amendment of the preliminary statement will injure the opposing party only insofar as his case will be prejudiced by the admission of evidence showing the true facts concerning the development of the invention while the denial of such a motion may result in the grant of a patent on a technicality to a party who was not the first inventor.

The amendment of a preliminary statement places a greater burden of proof on the party filing it if filed subsequent to inspection of his adversary's statement. It will then be remembered that it was filed with knowledge of the other's dates, and so the evidence in support of it will be regarded with suspicion. An amended preliminary statement will be stricken if it should subsequently appear that the Patent Office was misled into permitting the amendment.

Arrangement of Parties; Burden of Proof 

As previously indicated, the parties to an interference will be presumed, in accordance with Rule 257, to have made their inventions in the chronological order in which they filed their completed applications for patents. The burden of proof rests upon the party seeking to establish a different state of facts. This presumption is not disturbed by dissolution of an interference under Rules 232 or 237, without an award of priority or by an award of priority based solely upon ancillary matters, and a senior party is not deprived of any claim solely on the ground that it was not added to the interference by amendment under Rule 233.

Motions; Period for Making; types

Rule 226 (differing in this respect from old Rule 108) provides that the notices called for by it will ordinarily specify the motion period under Rule 231 (which has no counterpart in the old rules), and may also include an order to show cause.

Rule 231 reads: 

"After the preliminary statements have been received and approved, or the time for filing them has expired, a period will be fixed within which the various motions specified in Rules 232 to 236 may be brought by the parties. The period, not less than thirty days, will be fixed by an examiner of interferences in the notice referred to in Rule 226. In the case of a junior party under order to show cause (Rule 225) the period specified for answer to the order is the motion period and such motions may be brought as constitute an answer to the order."

Within the motion period the parties, and especially those other than the senior party, must determine what course they intend to pursue and take the necessary steps to protect their interests. The determination of this course may be relatively simple or difficult, depending on the number of counts involved, the number of parties, the subject matter of the counts, and the number of other applications or patents which a party or an assignee of

an entire interest may own and desire to have brought into interference with one or more applications or patents of another party. In view of the possible resulting ramifications, great care should be exercised in making it. 

A party subject to an order to show cause is necessarily limited in his choice by the provisions of new Rule 225, as previously stated, to motions other than one denying the patentability of any claim constituting a count of the interference. Other parties are not so limited. The selection, of course, must be made without knowledge of the adversary's dates, at least so far as access in regular manner to preliminary statements is concerned. But the other party's application (and under old Rule 108 the file history of it) is available and should be obtained promptly and carefully studied. 

If the allotted time be insufficient to permit adequate consideration of the matters involved, an extension of the motion period should be sought. This is preferably obtained by stipulation or joint motion. If a stipulation be unobtainable, a motion supported by a showing of cause should be made upon notice and proof of service, as required by Rules 247, 248.

The motion period is provided to enable the parties to an interference proceeding to investigate, consider, and file motions for the purpose of obtaining a determination, before the interference reaches the state of taking testimony, of such questions as: 

1. Was the interference regularly declared? 

2. Have all proper and necessary parties been brought in? 

3. Have any conflicting applications or patents been omitted?

4. Do the interference counts correctly define and adequately cover the subject matter as to which there is a conflict of applications or applications and patents? 

5. Have the parties been arranged as they should be arranged?

It is highly important that proper steps be taken during the motion period, either by motion or by an answer to an order to show cause which may include a motion, especially in the case of a junior party. Such a motion, under Rules 225, 235, 236 and 258, for example, properly made operates to save points, which otherwise might be waived or forfeited, until the final hearing.¹

When a question between contending parties as to the priority of invention is decided in the Patent Office, the decision there made must be accepted as controlling, upon that question of fact in any subsequent suit between the same parties, unless the contrary is established by testimony which, in character and amount, carries thorough conviction. Heston v. Kuhlke (CCA 6), 179 F(2) 222;

755. The findings of fact of the Board will be allowed to stand on appeal unless clearly against the weight of the evidence. Israel v. Cresswell (CCPA), 166 F(2) 153, 157.

Office held that they were supported when Steinbach's application was before that tribunal for ex parte consideration, and the Board of Interference Examiners has held so in the inter partes proceeding. This court is not at liberty lightly to disregard those holdings by tribunals made up of individuals of long training and experience not only in patent law but in technical matters of structure." 

Differences between Appeal and Action under 35 U.S.C. 146 as to Scope of Relief 

An unsuccessful party to an interference proceeding, including a patentee, in contemplating a review of an adverse

award of priority of invention, should carefully weigh the questions involved in the case in the light of decisions of the courts to which he may have access by appeal or by action under 35 U.S.C. 146.

One question which has occasioned difficulty is that of patentability of the interference issue. That this question is not determinable on appeal to the United States 

Court of Customs and Patent Appeals, see Masciarelli v. 

Foerste (CCPA), 197 F(2) 539. Decisions in cases arising under Sec. 4915 R.S. (superseded by 35 U.S.C. 146) are to the effect that that question is determinable in such cases. 

There has, however, been conflict between Courts of Appeals as to the determinability of the question in cases where the plaintiff has failed to establish priority of invention. 

See Heston v. Kuhlke (CCA 6), 173 F(2) 222; Smith v.

Carter Carburetor Corp. (CCA 3), 130 F(2) 555; Minneapolis Honeywell Regulator Co. v. Milwaukee Gas Specialty 

Co. (CCA 7), 174 F(2) 203; Knutson v. Gallsworthy, 164

F(2) 497. This conflict was resolved in Sanford v. Kepner, 344 U. S. 13, where the Court (Black, J.), after quoting the pertinent portions of the statute, said (p. 15): "The obvious purpose of the quoted part of R. S. 

4915 is to give a judicial remedy to an applicant who has been finally denied a patent because of a Patent Office decision against him and in favor of his adversary on the question of priority. When the trial court decides this factual issue of priority against him and thus affirms the refusal of the patent by the Patent Office, he has obtained the full remedy the statute gives him..."

The Court then quoted from Hill v. Wooster, 132 U. S. 

693, 698, and said (p. 15): "... The principle of the Hill case is that the court must decide whether claims show patentable inventions before authorizing the Commissioner to issue a patent. 

No part of its holding or wording nor of that in Hoover 

Co. v. Coe, 325 U. S. 79 [65 USPQ 180], requires us

to say R.S. 4915 compels a district court to adjudicate patentability at the instance of one whose claim is found to be groundless. Sanford's claim was found to be groundless. "It is unlikely that this equity proceeding would develop a full investigation of validity. There would be no attack on the patent comparable to that of an infringement action. Here the very person who claimed an invention now asks to prove that Kepner's similar device was no invention at all because of patents issued long before either party made claim for his discovery. There is no real issue of invention between the parties here and we see no reason to read into the statute a district court's compulsory duty to adjudicate validity." 

In this connection, care must be taken to distinguish cases in which the question of patentability of the interference issue is directly involved and cases in which that question is involved only as affecting the interpretation of interference counts. In cases of the latter type a different rule applies when a count is derived from a claim of an existing patent. 

In Kliesrath and Sanford v. Kesling, 33 CCPA 1008, 154 F(2) 514, involving interference counts derived from claims of an issued patent relating to a hydraulic gear shift mechanism, the question was whether the phrase "movable longitudinally" used in relation to a control lever meant movement longitudinally of the axis of the car or longitudinally in respect to operating means of which it was a part, as disclosed in that patent. It appeared that if the quoted phrase were interpreted to mean movement longitudinally of the axis of the car, as contended by the adverse party, the count would read on a prior art patent. The court while recognizing the rule that interference counts generally are to be broadly interpreted, held that it should not be applied in that case, and adhered to its previous decision in In re Kesling, 29 CCPA 786, 124 F(2) 193, 197, inter-

preting the claims as limited to the structure disclosed in the patent in which they originated. It would seem that the same rule should apply in cases where broad interpretation of an interference count would render the claim unpatentable as describing an unpatentable aggregation or a mere function or a result.

It should also be noted that on an appeal the evidence is restricted to that produced before the Patent Office. 

Nolop v. Smith (CCPA), 36 F(2) 838. It is otherwise in the case of an action under 35 U.S.C. 146.¹

It should be borne in mind, however, that the plaintiff in an action under 35 U.S.C. 146, as held in Morgan v. 

Daniels, 153 U. S. 120, 125, applying Sec. 4915 R.S. then in effect, has the burden of establishing "by testimony which in character and amount carries thorough conviction" that the decision of the Board of Interference Examiners was incorrect. Sanford v. Kepner (CCA 3), 195 F(2) 387, 93 

USPQ 57, 58.

As to review of a final judgment of the Court of Customs and Patent Appeals on petition alleging fraud perpetrated upon the court by the successful party to an interference proceeding, after issuance of patent to him, see Smith v. 

Hayward (CCPA), 193 F(2) 198, 92 USPQ 126, 659 O.G. 5, in which serious doubt as to the timeliness of the petition in view of FRCP 60(b) was noted but the petition was considered and denied on the merits, citing Walling v. 

Reuter Co., 321 U. S. 671, and Precision Co. v. Automatic 

Co., 324 U. S. 806.

V. 

PUBLIC USE PROCEEDING 

A public use proceeding is covered by Rules 292 and 259. 

Such a proceeding may be instituted upon petition or upon recommendation of the Primary Examiner or the Board of Patent Interferences, in the discretion of the Commissioner. See II Rivise & Caesar, pp. 1346-1395; Amdur, pp. 1030-8. Ordinarily, a public use proceeding will not be instituted during the pendency of an interference proceeding. See Stern v. Schroeder (CCPA), 36 F(2) 518, 521. The Commissioner may, in the exercise of his discretion, suspend an interference to determine whether or not one of the parties is barred by a public use from receiving a patent, but he cannot be compelled so to do. Svenson v. 

Coe, App. D. C., 101 F(2) 684.

Protests on the part of the public to the grant of a patent are dealt with as provided in Rule 291, which is a modification of a portion of old Rule 11.
