Annual Report of the Governor of American Samoa Peter Tali Coleman, Governor Introduction HE NON-SELF-GOVERNING territory of American Samoa, the ly United States territory in the South Pacific, may be classed as under-developed area facing most of the social, political, and onomic problems of other areas needing technical cooperation and sistance. Sixty years of United States administration of the affairs 

American Samoa have shown that American Samoans as American tionals are proud to be Americans and proud to be Samoans. The moans are acutely conscious of their shortcomings, their general ck of education and sophistication, and their lack of resources, but ey aspire earnestly to enable their children and future generations earn the benefits of Western civilization. 

The effort to earn these benefits requires patience on the part of e Samoan people and the United States administration. As in her under-developed areas there is a shortage of resources needed effect social, political, and economic development. Money, resources, ateriel, and trained personnel are scarce in Samoa. 

Political and social development, difficult to assess and more difficult attain, presents a problem in that the administration must conantly be on the alert to pace political and social growth with onomic growth. A balance must be maintained between the political, onomic, and social development of the territory. No part of the amoan life should outstrip its other component parts. We have arned that in some under-developed areas there are often tendencies r economic development to outstrip the political and social developent to the detriment of the whole society. 1

In their own efforts to meet the challenge of new ideas, hopes, a aspirations, to maintain a pride in their racial background and pol cal institutions, and to combine the new and old ways as best th can, the Samoans seek to preserve their cultural identity. As Samoa develo therefore, the task is to provide the people of the territory with means of making cultural adjustments to the changes taking pla This requires a recognition of the integrity of the Samoan cult and respect for the traditional cultural values, in accordance with

American Samoa is an insular possession of the United States a ministered by the Department of the Interior. It comprises eastern islands of the Samoan group which are located along the 14t of latitude south of the Equator at about the 170th meridian w longitude. These islands are approximately 2,300 miles southw of Hawaii and 1,600 miles northeast of the northern tip of New Z land, in a direct line. Western Samoa (77 miles westward) is trust territory administered by New Zealand pursuant to a trust ship agreement with the United Nations. 

There are seven islands in the American Samoa group: Tutui where the capital, Pago Pago, and central administrative offices a located; Aunu'u; the Manu'a group, which includes Ta'u, Oloseg and Ofu; Swains and Rose, both of which are coral atolls, the latt uninhabited (total area 76 square miles). 

The people of American Samoa are American nationals and repr sent one of the few remaining societies of Polynesians with a relativ ly small group of persons of mixed blood. The population of t territory has increased from 5,697 in 1900, when the jurisdiction of th Government was taken over by the United States Navy, to 20,045 a cording to a preliminary count of the 1960 census. 

The main islands are of volcanic origin and are now mostly a seri of ridges rising abruptly from the sea. Tutuila is of irregular shap approximately 18 miles long and 6 miles across, at the widest poin and contains 42 square miles of land. A mountain range extend almost the whole length of the island which is nearly bisected by Pag Pago Bay, one of the finest and most beautiful harbors in the Sout Pacific.

The climate of the islands is tropical but pleasant. From May 

November moderate southeast trade winds blow; during the othe months the winds are variable. The heaviest rainfall occurs fro 

December to March and the average per year for the past 40 years ha been approximately 200 inches. The yearly temperature ranges fro 

70° to 90° F., and the humidity is about 80 percent. The soil of the islands is of two kinds-clay and sandy loam. the valleys it is alluvial and quite fertile for growing tropical fruit and taro. Due to the heavy rainfall the soil on hillsides is thin with little subsoil. Certain elements necessary to temperate zone plan life were apparently burned out at the time of the formation of thes islands. American interest in the islands of Samoa commenced with the reort made by the United States exploring expedition, which visited e islands under the leadership of Lt. John Wilkes in 1839. 

It was ot until some 30 years later, however, that a formal relationship was tered into with the people of Samoa by a representative of the nited States. Primarily as a result of commercial interest in obining harbor facilities and rights for a coaling station on the shores f Pago Pago Harbor, the U.S.S. Narragansett visited Tutuila in 

372 and Comdr. Richard Meade entered into an agreement entitled 

Commercial Regulations, etc.," with High Chief Mauga, the high hief of Pago Pago. While this treaty was never ratified by the 

S. Senate, it served effectively to prevent foreign influence from sserting any strong claim to the harbor.

In January of 1878, a further treaty of friendship and commerce as negotiated with the leaders of the villages adjacent to Pago Pago nd this treaty was ratified later in the same year. It was proclaimed intly by the United States and "the Government of American Saoan Islands." This treaty remained in force for more than 20 years ntil it was superseded.

As a result of international rivalry between Great Britain and Gerany and because of warfare between various factions of the Samoan opulation, the United States, Germany, and Great Britain entered to a general act on June 14, 1889, for the purpose of providing for the security of life, property and trade of the citizens and subjects f the respective governments who were residing in or having comercial relations with the islands of Samoa." This act also had as is aim the desire "to avoid all occasions of dissensions between their espective governments and the people of Samoa" while at the same me "promoting as far as possible the peaceful and orderly civilizaon of the people."

Under this tripartite agreement a form of government for the isands was brought into being; however, after a trial of some 10 years, proved to be ineffective and destructive of the ends for which it was reated and was superseded by the convention of 1899. This treaty etween the United States, Germany, and Great Britain had the aim f "advancing amicably the questions which have arisen between the ree powers in respect to the Samoan group of islands" and provided

that it would supersede and annul all previous treaties between t powers relating to Samoa.

A major provision of the convention was the renunciation by Gre Britain and Germany of any claims to the islands of the Samoa gro east of longitude 171° west of Greenwich. Reciprocally, the Unit

States renounced in favor of Germany all rights and claims in resp to the islands of Upolu and Savaii and all other islands west of 17 longitude west of Greenwich. This treaty was proclaimed, after ra fication on February 16, 1900, and on February 19 of the same ye

President McKinley directed the Secretary of the Navy to "take su steps as might be necessary to establish the authority of the Unit States" in the island of Tutuila and other islands of the Samoan gro east of longitude 171° west of Greenwich.

The Secretary of the Navy established a naval station at Pago Pag In April 1900, deeds of cession were negotiated with the leading chie and orators of Tutuila and Aunu'u, and on April 17 the U.S. flag w raised for the first time over these islands. On June 14, 1904, Hig

Chief Tuimanu'a, the King of Manu'a, and the chiefs of Manu agreed to cede their lands to the United States. 

The islands remained under naval administration with Pago Pag as an active naval base from 1900 to June 30, 1951. During this tin considerable progress was made in the establishment of public work medical and educational facilities. Little, however, was done to di turb the traditional village life of the people. 

Under the terms of the deeds of cession, the United States agree that the chiefs of the villages would be permitted to retain their i dividual control over their separate villages, provided that their co trol was in accordance with the laws of the United States pertainin to Samoa and provided that such control was not obstructive to t peace of the people and the advancement of civilization. As result of this commitment, no large tracts of Samoan communal owned land have been alienated during the 60 years during which th United States has had administrative responsibility for these island Although the deeds of cession were not formally accepted by the Co gress until February 20, 1929, the fundamental personal rights of th inhabitants of Samoa were protected by the Constitution and th people of American Samoa were given the status of American n tionals. Judicial Branch 

The judicial power exercisable in American Samoa is vested in th 

High Court and five district courts. All courts are courts of record

The judicial branch is independent of the executive and legislativ powers. The High Court has a trial division, a probate division and an appellate division. The judicial branch is under the general ad ministration and supervision of the Chief Justice of American Samoa During the fiscal year ending June 30, 1960, a total of 418 persons were tried on criminal charges; 362 of these were convicted and 56

cquitted. Thirty of those convicted were imprisoned, 290 paid fines, nd 42 performed light labor for the public benefit under the direction f the pulenu'us (village mayors) of their respective villages. Durg the fiscal year, the courts disposed of 217 civil cases. 

Criminal cases are disposed of with as much promptness as circumcances permit, due consideration having been given to the defendant's onstitutional rights and his rights guaranteed by the American amoa Bill of Rights. 

Charges of felony are prosecuted by the Attorney General. If a efendant pleads guilty to a charge of felony, the Government is evertheless required to prove a prima facie case in order to avoid the ossibility, remote though it may be, that the plea of guilty may have een made by mistake.

In the case of a misdemeanor, the Court must be satisfied that the efendant clearly understands the charge and that the plea is made oluntarily. If in any case it appears to the Court after a plea of uilty that there may be a reasonable doubt as to the defendant's guilt, e is invariably permitted to withdraw his plea of guilty and plead ot guilty in order to avoid a possible miscarriage of justice. 

The Government provides a public defender who is available for the ssistance of persons charged with crime. 

The Constitution of the United States protects the fundamental ights of the people of American Samoa. These rights are also proected by the American Samoa Bill of Rights which is patterned after he Bill of Rights in the Constitution of the United States.

State of Nutrition of the Population.-In general, the Samoan popuation over the age of 5 to 6 years is well-nourished. Taro and banana constitute the staples of the diet, with the addition of fish, pork, hicken, papaya, etc. The outstanding dietary deficiency is in the realm of green vegetables, which are not included in the diet and which undoubtedly is a factor in the mild anemia which exists. Specific vitamin deficiency syndromes are rarely seen. 

Malnutrition or undernutrition is seen usually in the age group dependent on others for their feeding; i.e., the age group of 0-2 years, although there is an occasional case in the 2-4 age group. Most of the problem is related to the tendency for Samoan mothers to feed infants on taro and boiled green banana as weaning foods. The use of pow. dered milk after weaning has been the most successful method of dealing with this problem.

Socio-economic changes undoubtedly have affected the state of mutrition of the population, particularly in the young age groups; as more mothers become employed, there is a marked decrease in breast feeding of babies with a rise in the incidence of malnutrition, and there probably is less fishing done by the men in the village because of employment. Another factor is the reluctance of the Samoan people to feed fish to small children.

In the next year a continuing survey of dietary habits and da caloric intake is planned.

Objectives and Policies

The basic approach to education in American Samoa is to provid training which will enable the people to serve more effectively withi their social, economic, professional and political structure. At th same time, education must provide suitable background for those wh will find it possible to take advantage of opportunities for highe education in the United States or other countries. Proficiency in th 

English language must not be the sole subject goal, but proficiency i basic skills related to science, medicine, business, the arts, and othe varied fields must also be accomplished. Therefore, it becomes th responsibility of the Government as the Samoans are influenced mor and more by contact with Western civilization, to conserve the bes of Samoan culture, and at the same time to guide them in developing social changes which are to their advantage, and to bring them to better understanding of what is best for their well-being, health, and general improvement.

It also becomes necessary to help them preserve and develop their economic resources, to enable them to cope with the problems of an increasing population and increasing demands which accompany modern society, and to become a government as self-supporting as possible.

As English is the international language and the official language of the Government, it is essential that the children become literate in that language while maintaining the bilingual nature of their society.

In line with the above objectives, it is the policy of the Department of Education to maintain a program adapted thereto in the following fields:

(a) Curriculum.—(1) Instruction in both the Samoan and English anguages in the elementary schools; (2) the fundamental processes of reading, writing, and arithmetic; (3) science and health; (4) ocial studies with emphasis on Samoan culture, geography, governnent, and environment; (5) vocational preparation; and (6) avocaional pursuits. (b) School organization.-Village elementary schools (grade 1 to 

3); junior high schools (grades 7 to 9); the senior high school (grades 

10 to 12); vocational high school (grades 10 to 12); the two latter schools for a select group of junior high school graduates; and, the eacher training college (grade 13) for high school graduates. The eacher training college will become a 2-year school (grades 13 to 14) at the beginning of the 1960-61 academic year. (c) Compulsory education.—This is a long established and popular policy of compulsory education between the ages of 7 through 15. (d) Buildings and equipment.—An expanding school population makes it essential to keep up in this respect. The general policy is a cooperating enterprise between the local communities and the Government, but as a school becomes more advanced in character, the Government is responsible for additional support. In the elementary school, the villages provide the school buildings and teachers' living quarters; the Government provides teachers, equipment, and supplies. 

In the junior high schools, high school, vocational high school, dependents' school, demonstration school, and teacher training college, the Government has sole responsibility. (e) Scholarships.-The Government desires to provide for and promote scholarships and special training for qualified Samoans in needed fields. (f) Standards. It is the policy to upgrade the entire school system as rapidly as possible, with emphasis being placed on the elementary level with the improvement of the teaching staff, and on the junior high school level where the schools will send a better-trained group of young people into the high school and community life. Special attention is being given to the teacher training college and related demonstration school. This is essential in order to provide facilities whereby teachers can be better trained to do their job in upgrading the school system.

Structure of the Education System 

A description of the school levels with regard to curriculum and language of instruction is as follows:

Preprimary school.—None. 

Primary schools. The elementary grades extend from grade 1 through grade 6. These grades are administered and taught by locally educated Samoans. The average age at entrance is 7 years. The languages of instruction are English and Samoan. The ratio of language used in grades 1 through 3 is 75 percent Samoan and 25 percent

English. The ratio of languages used in grades 4 through 6 is 50 percent Samoan and 50 percent English. The junior high school division consists of grades 7 through 9. The administration and instruction in the junior high schools are carried out by locally educated Samoans. 

The average age at entrance to the junior high school is 13 years. The 

English-Samoan language ratio is 75 percent and 25 percent, respectively. The present elementary and junior high school curriculum consists of the usual school subjects used for those levels of education

such as health, general science, social studies, arithmetic, English spelling, reading and comprehension, music, art, and, in addition courses in Samoan culture, tradition, history, environment, govern ment, and the values of the Samoan way of life are taught in th elementary schools. Samoan language books will be added to th curriculum for the 1960-61 school year. Instruction is based princi pally on standard elementary textbooks published in the United States; all students have textbooks as a result of a most satisfactory and inexpensive book rental program. This book rental program i: being extended to the private schools desiring it during the next schoo year. Information regarding the United Nations found in the textbooks is used as the basis for instruction on the subject. Magazine materials and radio information are also used. The Feleti Memoria 

Demonstration School and Teacher Training College had special programs for United Nations Day during the school year. 

Secondary schools.-The high school is composed of grades 10 through 12. Admission is based upon examination. At present 120 new students are admitted yearly. The language of instruction is English. The instructors are university graduates. The subjects taught meet the requirements of United States certification agencies. The average age at graduation is 20 years. The instructional materials used are standard high school textbooks and equipment from the United States. The vocational high school is operated to provide special training in crafts and the use of trade tools. Boys, who are selected ninth grade graduates, are trained to do cabinetwork, carpentry, machine work, electricity, boat repair, logging, sawmilling, plumbing, with the ultimate objective to do work typical of local conditions. Thirty-five new students are admitted yearly. Second- and third-year students worked in on-the-job training projects with the 

Department of Public Works during the year. The Feleti Memorial 

Teacher Training College operated as a 1-year course during the year. 

With the addition of two instructors this will become a 2-year course with the start of the 1960-61 school year. Students admitted must be high school graduates. Graduates from the college are in great demand and are employed immediately upon graduation to teach in the elementary and junior high schools. A Teachers' Institute is held yearly to keep the Samoan education personnel abreast of new techniques and methods. Approximately 5 weeks is devoted to this activity attended by all Samoan teachers, principals, and supervisors. In addition, a large class of candidates is accepted every year to receive basic training so that they may serve as substitutes and fill vacancies during the school year. Many mission school teachers also attend the

Institute. For the first time since before World War II guest instructors (three teachers from Hawaii and one teacher from Louisiana) taught in the 1959 Teachers' Institute.

Students who wish other university or advanced technical school training usually go to Hawaii or to the mainland of the United States. 

A few high school graduates are sent to the Central Medical School in Suva, Fiji, for medical and dental practitioner, pharmacist, and laboratory assistant training.

There is no formal system of apprenticeship. Instruction exists informally in on-the-job training for the young helpers of specialists, technicians, and mechanics employed by private industry or by the Government.

Economic Conditions Natural Resources 

The primary natural resource of American Samoa is the land and the agricultural crops produced from it. The agriculture of the are is basically on a subsistence level, with copra the major export. Ther are no known mineral resources. Although there was an ample sup ply of wood in the forest areas for native-built house construction there has never been an attempt to process timber commercially for local use. Agriculture

Through the Department of Agriculture the Government of American Samoa has the policy of providing assistance, guidance, and encouragement to its people in improving and increasing plantings of

+ 

xisting subsistence crops and to introduce additional practical supplemental subsistence crops. The objective is to enable the people o become more self-supporting and to help self-government by delegating responsibility through the political authority of the traditional chiefs, and to develop through long- and short-range programs the potential production of an agricultural economy. 

At present the department is headed by two Samoans who are esignated as Acting Directors for Field and Administration. Under he Director, an entomologist conducts field research for injurious plant pests, and breeds parasites and predators in the laboratory for release in badly infested areas to assist in the problems of control. 

Seventeen persons are employed in the Field while two staff members manage the office.

Experimental farms.—Extensive work was carried out on all the 

Government of American Samoa farms in development of new pascures, improvement of local pastures, and building up of soils to production level. As a result, at Tafuna about 25 to 30 acres of uncovered rocky land were improved to a high producing level, and pasture and native grasses were established on the improved area. The Taputimu depleted areas have been built up to production level, and pasture and native grasses were introduced for cattle grazing trials. At the 

Mapusaga farm, introduced native grasses are now growing vigorously; palatability to cattle was fair, but nourishing contents may be low due to the high soil moisture. To improve the Mapusaga area to higher livestock carrying capacity, certain areas must be drained, the Soil aerated and bush cleared. 

Land utilization.-The Government of American Samoa policy on land ownership is to retain it in the hands of the Samoan people. 

Land tenure is under the matai, who is the caretaker of the land for the family group. Land may be leased or rented to Samoans without restriction. In cases of nonindigenous persons, however, permission must first be obtained from the Government. As most of the land is under the matai or communal system, the chief of the family rents the land with the consent of the family. There is no agricultural indebtedness in American Samoa.

There is a small amount of freehold land owned primarily by churches and a few merchants. The Government owns land in the harbor district, controls the airfield by lease, and leases 248 acres used as experimental farms at three locations: Tafuna, Taputimu, and Mapusaga. 

Extension service.-Agriculture extension was placed in the hands of the traditional leaders, the chiefs, who hold the political authority in the villages.

Copra fund. The Copra Fund is maintained under the direction of a board of directors whose members are appointed by the Governor and who serve without pay. The board is the policymaking body of the Copra Fund and has authority to make administrative decisions.

Its function is to carry out the policies and procedures set up by the board, purchase copra from the producers, negotiate copra sales, and maintain equipment and accounting records. Public Finance 

The financial structure of the Government of American Samoa consists of two major categories: (1) Federal activities.—including the office of the Governor, the 

Judiciary, and the Legislature. (2) Territorial activities.-including the operation and maintenance of the territory and its seven executive departments and several organizations.

The Federal activities are financed by direct Federal appropriations on funds drawn on the Treasury of the United States. 

The territorial activities are financed by the collection of local revenues supplemented by a congressional grant-in-aid. These funds are commingled and drawn on the Treasury of American Samoa. 

The revenue collection responsibilities, and the disbursements of both Federal and territorial funds, together with the accountability therefor, the entire budget program for each, and the overall housekeeping activities of the territory are vested in the Department of 

Budget and Finance. 

Much-needed rehabilitation or replacement of public utility systems, including a new power plant to keep abreast of increased demand, an expanded water reservoir and distribution system, extended telephone service, complete road paving program, plus accelerated and broadened public health services in this undeveloped territory will render the achievement of total financial self-sufficiency difficult within the foreseeable future.

Appendix III AMERICAN SAMOA-STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES AND POLICIES 

Objectives

The objectives of the Department of the Interior in the administration of merican Samoa are (1) To provide for the orderly and progressive development the people toward self-government and (2) To assist the people to attain the aximum possible self-support. Policies

Political advancement.—It is the policy to support the continuance of the erritorial government with separate legislative, executive and judicial departents, constituting a system of checks and balances. 

Within this framework, the political structure of the government shall be in ccord with the desires of the Samoan people in regard to such adaptations as ay be desirable by virtue of Samoan customs, traditions and land ownership. 

During the period of development of self-government, the people and their esources shall be protected against undesirable exploitation. 

It is also the policy to delegate to, and encourage the acceptance of increased uthority by local Samoan officials, including the district governors, county hiefs and village mayors. As the legislative advisory body demonstrates a ontinued ability to legislate effectively, increasingly greater measures of authorty shall be granted › it.

With respect to organic legislation, it is the policy to support such legislation when the Samoan people desire it and are ready for it. It is the policy to seek he views of the Samoans and consult with them as regards such legislation in rder that it will contain adequate protection for the rights of the people, ncluding their historic land rights. In the meantime, the Samoans are encouraged to take such forward steps as may be consistent with organic egislation if and when such legislation is considered desirable. 

Economic advancement.-Economic policy in the territory shall be directed 

Coward creating a sound basic economy that will (1) be suited to the needs and resources of Samoa, (2) will provide adequately for the food and monetary needs of the rapidly increasing population, and (3) will establish a firm foundation for self-government and maintain Samoa for Samoans. The territory's limited and resources and rapidly growing population make it essential that every effort be made to increase agricultural production, industrial development and tourist trade.

Since the economy of the area is basically agricultural, emphasis shall be given to encouraging and stimulating agricultural production. This includes the improvement of food crops, poultry and livestock, and the increased production of copra and other exportable crops.

Protection of Samoans against the loss of their family lands is an important policy not only as regards the economy, but also as may affect the Samoan "matai" system. It is the policy to maintain this protection. 

In view of Samoa's lack of resources, and the existence of a substantial labor pool previously accustomed to wage income, it is the policy to look with favor upon the establishment of processing and other minor industries which will bring a source of income to the people. In view of the need for this type of

+ 

economic activity, favorable consideration shall be given to industrial incenti in the form of tax and other benefits.

As the principal employer of wage workers in the territory, the Government American Samoa shall maintain a wage structure and conditions of employm which are consonant with the advancing social and economic conditions of territory.

General advancement.—It is the policy to respect the Samoans' desire to p tect the Matai system and to consult fully with the Samoans, through th legally constituted representatives, on any question which may affect the pr servation or destruction of the system. It is also the policy to encourage t acceptance of such nonindigenous social concepts as would be beneficial and provide such social regulations and services as may be necessary in the light local conditions and dictated by precepts of common humanity and government responsibility. (a) Health.-The Public Health program shall be administered so as to pr vide necessary medical and dental services, to improve the health and sanita conditions of the people, to minimize communicable diseases and to train ñati health personnel. (b) Education.-It is the policy to maintain a free public school system f the education of American Samoans and to provide in that school system a cu riculum which will (1) prepare students for life in American Samoa, and (2 establish a firm foundation for the political, economic, and social advanceme of the people and thus hasten the date when more Samoans can replace stat side employees in their government and Samoa can achieve a greater degree self-government.

Relationship with Western Samoa.-Close cooperation and consultation b tween the Governments of American and Western Samoa shall be encourage in order that action taken by the two governments will take into account th close ties and relationships between the peoples of the two areas.

Appendix IV CONSTITUTION OF AMERICAN SAMOA 

Whereas, the Congress of the United States, in its Act of February 20, 1929 provided that until the Congress shall provide for the Government of the island of American Samoa, all civil, judicial and military powers shall be vested in such person or persons and exercised in such manner as the President of the United 

States shall direct;

Whereas, by Executive Order No. 10264 the President of the United States directed that the Secretary of the Interior should take such action as may be necessary and appropriate, and in harmony with applicable law, for the adminis tration of civil government in American Samoa; 

Whereas, it is appropriate that, in the process of developing self-government, the people of American Samoa should enjoy certain rights and responsibilities inherent in the representative form of government; and

✦ 

Whereas, it is desirable that these rights and responsibilities be clearly set rth in a Constitution, and the adoption of a Constitution is in harmony with plicable law:

Now, therefore, this Constitution, having been ratified and approved by the cretary of the Interior and having been approved by the Constitutional Conntion of the people of American Samoa, is established to further advance vernment of the people, by the people, and for the people of American Samoa. 

ARTICLE I-BILL OF RIGHTS SECTION 1. Freedom of religion, speech, press, rights of assembly, and peti on.-There shall be separation of church and government, and no law shall be acted respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise ereof, or abridging the freedom of speech or of the press, or the right of the eople peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of rievances.

SECTION 2. No deprivation of life, liberty, or property without due process.

o person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of tw, nor shall private property be taken for public use without just compensation. 

SECTION 3. Policy protective legislation.—It shall be the policy of the Govern ent of American Samoa to protect persons of Samoan ancestry against aliention of their lands and the destruction of the Samoan way of life and language, ontrary to their best interests. Such legislation as may be necessary may be nacted to protect the lands, customs, culture, and traditional Samoan family rganization of persons of Samoan ancestry, and to encourage business enterrises by such persons. No change in the law respecting the alienation or ransfer of land or any interest therein, shall be effective unless the same be pproved by two successive legislatures by a two-thirds vote of the entire embership of each house and by the Governor. 

SECTION 4. Dignity of the individual.-The dignity of the Individual shall be espected and every person is entitled to protection of the law against malicious nd unjustifiable public attacks on the name, reputation, or honor of himself or f his family.

SECTION 5. Protection against unreasonable searches and seizures. The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers and effects, against uneasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated, and no warrants shall ssue, but upon probable cause, supported by oath or affirmation, and particularly describing the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized. 

Evidence obtained in violation of this Section shall not be admitted in any court. 

SECTION 6. Rights of an accused.-No person shall be subject for the same offense to be twice put in jeopardy of life or liberty; nor shall he be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself; and the failure of the accused to testify shall not be commented upon nor taken against him. In all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall have the right to a speedy and public trial, to be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation and to have a copy thereof; to be confronted with the witnesses against him, to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor, and to have the assistance of counsel for his defense. Every man is presumed innocent until he is pronounced guilty by law, and no act of severity which is not reasonably necessary to secure the arrest of an accused person shall be permitted. All persons shall be bailable by sufficient sureties except for capital offenses and rape, where proof is evident or the presumption great. Excessive bail shall not be required, nor excessive fines imposed nor cruel or unusual punishments inflicted.

SECTION 7. Habeas corpus. The writ of habeas corpus shall be granted wi out delay and free of costs. The privilege of the writ of habeas corpus sh not be suspended except by the Governor and then only when the public saf requires it in case of war, rebellion, insurrection or invasion. 

SECTION 8. Quartering of militia.—No soldier or member of the militia shall, time of peace, be quartered in any house without the consent of the owner or t lawful occupant, nor in time of war, except in a manner prescribed by la The military authority shall always be subordinate to the civil authority in ti of peace.

SECTION 9. Imprisonment for debt.-There shall be no imprisonment for de except in cases of fraud.

SECTION 10. Slavery prohibited.-Neither slavery, nor involuntary servitud except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly co victed shall exist in American Samoa. 

SECTION 11. Treason.-Treason against the Government of American Sam shall consist only in levying war against it, adhering to its enemies, or givi them aid and comfort. No person shall be convicted of treason except on t testimony of two witnesses to the same overt act, or a confession in op court.

SECTION 12. Subversives ineligible to public office. No person who advocate or who aids or belongs to any party, organization, or association which adv cates, the overthrow by force or violence of the Government of American Sam or of the United States shall be qualified to hold any public office of trust profit under the Government of American Samoa. 

SECTION 13. Retroactive laws.-No bill of attaide ex post facto law, n any law impairing the obligation of contracts shall be passed.

SECTION 14. Special or exclusive privileges not to be granted.—The power the Government to act for the general welfare of the people of American Samo shall never be impaired by the making of any irrevocable grant of special exclusive privileges or immunities. Special acts conferring corporate power shall not be enacted. Corporations may be created under general laws, bu all such laws may be amended or repealed. 

SECTION 15. Health, safety, morals and general welfare.-Laws may be e acted for the protection of the health, safety, morals and general welfare, of th people of American Samoa.

SECTION 16. Education. The Government shall operate a system of free an non-sectarian public education. There shall be compulsory education for a children between the ages of seven and sixteen years. The government will en courage qualified persons of good character to acquire further education, locall and abroad, both general and technical, and thereafter to return to America: 

Samoa to the end that the people thereof may be benefited. 

SECTION 17. Child labor.-The employment of children under the age of sixtee years in any occupation injurious to health or morals, or hazardous to life o limb is hereby prohibited. 

SECTION 18. Unspecified rights and privileges and immunities.—The enumera tion of certain rights in this Constitution shall not be construed to impair o deny other rights retained by the people. No law shall be made or enforced which shall abridge the privileges or immunities of the citizens of American Samoa.

Symbolic of the United States flag and emblem, the flag of American Samo contains the official red, white, and blue colors and the American Bald Eagl The eagle appears in flight on a background of white which is bisected by a re spearhead band from the larger blue background. The head, tail, talons, an beak of the eagle are yellow while the body ranges in colors from tan to dar brown. In its claws are a Samoan fue (yellow), representing wisdom throug traditional Samoan counsel, and an uatogi (yellow) or Samoan war club, repr senting the power of Samoa that was channeled into peace and order under th guidance of the United States.
