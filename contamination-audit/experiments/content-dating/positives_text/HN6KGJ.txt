INTRODUCTION 

An important criterion for the appraisal of any institution is its adaptability to new and changing needs. If the institution has risen to meet highly specialized but temporary needs, society may with impunity let it degenerate or die. If the institution has risen to serve rigidly enduring functions, society may safely permit it to crystallize. If its purpose in whole or in part is to perform a function associated at one time with one group of needs and at another time with a different group of needs, lack of adaptability will result in social loss. 

The school system falls in the last category. Society cannot view with equanimity a public school system which is not responsive to current educational needs. It must demand that the school system be aware of the current aspects of needs which in their fundamental nature are persistent, and that it respond to new needs arising from economic and social changes and from advancement in education as a science. 

This instrument is designed to serve as a basis for the appraisal of individual school systems with respect to their adaptation to current needs different in greater or less degree from those of past generations as a result of economic and social developments or of improved understanding arising from the progress of educational science. It is built upon a broad sampling of such items of need in the areas of classroom instruction, special services for individual pupils, educational leadership, physical facilities and business management. Applied to a school system it will reveal areas adapted to current needs as defined above and those areas where there is a lag in adaptation. 

The issue of institutional adaptability comes strongly to the front in the present authors' current studies of local taxing power. In the literature 

American, English, French, German and Russian - it stands high among the defenses for the local support of education and other governmental activireviewed 

ties. ii

The claim that local taxing power contributes to institutional adaptability is no exception to the rule that the arguments for local taxing power are in the realm of belief, supported mainly by sporadic personal observations. The fragmentary evidence collected to date would have to be characterized for the most part as anecdotal. In the financial support of American education the current shift of emphasis from unrestricted local taxing power to central limitation and control provides the stimulus for a series of studies now under way designed to make a more systematic appraisal of the advantages and disadvantages to education of local taxing power. They demanded a measure that would make it possible to compare centrally supported and locally supported schools from the important angle of institutional adaptability. 

Building upon the attempts to describe the nature of the educational program on different expenditure levels in the recent school finance surveys of New Jersey and Maine, the scope of the items considered was broadened by a rather extended survey of economic and social trends with respect to their implications to education and scientific development or the last two or three decades in the field of education itself. The authors were assisted in this appraisal by Professors Brunner, Bryson, Evenden, Gulick, Haig, Jersild, Kandel, Mills and Newlon. An extensive list of needed educational adaptations resulted. 

Most of these had already been made by some American school systems and none could be described as universally characteristic of American school systems. 

They were analyzed to discover the ways and means by which the presence of related adaptations in a school system could be recognized. 

The resulting instrument was applied by twelve field workers to forty communities in New Jersey, Pennsylvania and Delaware during the spring of 1935. 

This increased the understanding of the nature of each adjustment and thereby led to the improvement of each item in the instrument with respect to its validity as an indication of the presence of the need to which it is related. iii

In addition, it provided comparative data which made possible a detailed analysis of the reliability of the procedure. 

The revised form was then applied to a single school system by three different judges. This provided a check on the items modified in the light of the first field study and gave a basis for determining the reliability not only of the instrument as a whole, but of the individual items. A second revision was made and published in preliminary form in October 1935 under the title 

A Test of the Educational Lag of School Systems. 

During the spring of 1936 it was applied to six large cities in the State of New York and was used as a basis for observation of school systems in the Union of South Africa. The New 

York application was carried on by two trained field workers who checked their results in detail and thereby brought considerable additional improvement to the instrument. The South African application was chiefly valuable, so far as this instrument is concerned, in broadening the interpretation of some items of adaptation. 1 Before the final revision, the standards for adjustment items (those designated as a, b, c .....) were submitted to eighty graduate students in 

Teachers College, Columbia University, and twenty-four educational officials from the Union of South Africa, for purposes of checking the clarity and validity of statements and establishing judgment as to the relative importance of various sections of the guide. On the basis of these reviews, editorial revisions were made and weightings were established for the relative significance of sections. An additional weighting factor to allow for differences in magnitude and variability among sections was developed through the application of the preliminary form to six cities in the State of New York. These weightings, combined and simplified, appear in the score sheet for purposes of calculating a numerical score of the extent of adjustment in a school system. iv

Of the original purposes of the case study guide, the measure of extent. of adjustment was secondary. In addition to this statistical measure, the form was developed for purposes of facilitating a fairly extensive descriptive analysis. In many respects such data are much more significant than that obtained through the calculation of a single numerical score. Further phases of the original study demanded consideration for the time at which adjustments are made and for an analysis of sources of factors contributing to the introduction of innovations in a school system. This is not reported here and would not add to the information readily available to the local official making use of this device for his individual school system.

INSTRUCTIONS 

For each of the subdivisions of the guide, adjustments or indications of adjustments are stated as positively desirable items. Each of the 23 subsections, designated in Arabic numerals, may be summarized simply by totaling for the section the number of adjustments, a, b, c ....., for which "yes" has been encircled. Where the guide is to be "self-administered" by staff members within the system, proper entries may be made after some items on the basis of previous observations or first-hand experience. The underlined statements give the suggested form of questions to be used where interviews with various members of the personnel serve as bases for recording adjustments. Where the greatest reliability is desired, interviews should be had in many cases with a number of individuals in order to eliminate misunderstanding or bias in the answers. Where possible, the basis for judging the presence or absence of adjustment is to be obtained through actual observation. 

Suggested sources of information are listed along with each adjustment. 

The presence of items listed under each adjustment is indication of the presence of the adjustment itself. As a matter of record the various sources of information should be entered separately in the right-hand margin beside each adjustment and indication of adjustment. This shows the thoroughness of the investigation and the reliability of results. This may be done by means of a simple coding system of which a sampling of sources would be:

For some, evidence will seem unconvincing or inconclusive until two or more sources have been examined. Where several sources are examined, separate recordings should be made in such a way that working entries are not to be confused with final entries. This may be accomplished through use of temporary pencil checks under response "yes" or "no," as indicated.

The final recording of an item is to be taken from the weight of evidence as given by the number of "yes" and "no" entries for the item from various sources. Where otherwise inconclusive, an adjustment is to be credited if manifested in any single one of the ways listed as indications of adjustment. 

If the investigator is from without the system, he may wish to make notes of a descriptive nature in the space for "evidence." Copies of forms and publications and samples of work of pupils should accompany the guide as part of the final report. vi
