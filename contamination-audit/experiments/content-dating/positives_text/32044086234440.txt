JAMES S. OTTENBERG

"The masses of people always mean well and will always act well when they can obtain a right understanding."

GEORGE WASHINGTON 

The Lexington Democratic Club is a unique fusion of practicality and idealism. Basically we believe in the desirability of widespread participation in the Democratic Party. We assume that people are fundamentally good and consequently both government and politics will improve as more and more people understand and participate.

Our goal is to achieve a liberal program of good government, to aid social reform and to support an enlightened foreign policy. We are con vinced that the two-party system will continue to flourish in the United States and that the Democratic Party, whatever its shortcomings, is more hospitable to liberal ideas than the Republican Party. 

Since its inception the Club has campaigned in behalf of many issues stemming from the American liberal movement in general and the Democratic Party in particular. Actually the Club combines two American traditions: that of the good government reform liberals, interested in honesty and efficiency in government, and that of the social reform liberals concerned primarily with working toward the abolition of poverty and inequality of opportunity.

The Lexington Democratic Club was founded early in 1949 after a small group of young Democrats met in Manhattan's "Silk Stocking" 9th

Assembly District and decided that a new organization was needed to replace the existing local Democratic Club in that area. The "regular" club, run by a small group of insiders, was both ineffective and undemocratic. It had no specific program. It did nothing to encourage young people to participate in politics. It failed to campaign aggressively for the 

Democratic Party in the District. It was completely dominated by the New 

York County Democratic organization, popularly known as "Tammany Hall."

After four years of intensive work at the grass roots level as an “insurgent" group, the Lexington Democratic Club became the official Democratic club of the 9th Assembly District in 1953 by defeating the candidates of the former regular club, supported by the county organization, in a primary fight for the District Leadership.

Since then several of our members have been elected to party office, others nominated for public office. In 1954 our Congressional candidate, Anthony B. Akers, came within 314 votes of election, a startling achieve ment considering that previous Republican pluralities had ranged from 

13,000 to 24,000.

The Club's activities are carefully watched by professionals of both major parties. Its unique political philosophy, techniques and democratic constitution have become a model for other groups desirous of challenging entrenched and stagnant leadership. Our membership has grown from the original handful to over 1,300. Adlai E. Stevenson at a Club party in his honor called it "the most spectacularly successful group of its kind in the United States." The Citizens Union has said the Club "represents all that is best in politics."

The practical technique that the Club has pioneered is to form a local unit organized on a permanent basis and committed to working within the 

Democratic Party. 

With its concept of permanence, the Club differs from American reform movements founded on a strong personality that are unable to continue once the central figure has left the scene. It also differs from movements based on a single reform issue whose settlement one way or the other diminishes, if it does not entirely destroy, the original impetus. 

The Club's commitment to working within the Democratic Party distinguishes it from third parties whose success has been limited due to their inability to organize at state and national as well as at local levels. The 

Club also differs from non-partisan groups that might be classified as liberal, yet whose sphere of activity for reform is restricted. The purpose of such non-partisan organizations is often admirable. However, they cannot parti cipate directly in elections and are completely removed from the nominating process, vital in any democracy and particularly so in ours where in many areas nomination is tantamount to election. 

Unfortunately, many well-intentioned and idealistic individuals, because of their failure to understand how our political parties function, do not enroll in any party. They pride themselves on being independents, claiming this leaves them free to vote for the best man in general elections. They do not seem to understand that enrollment in a party is not a specific commitment. It simply indicates a general sympathy with the party's program and the intention of supporting its candidates. All registered voters can vote on Election Day for the candidate they prefer. However, the candidates presented to the electorate in general elections are usually selected in party primaries and in order to qualify to vote in primaries, party enrollment is essential. It is important that independents realize that by not enrolling they are partially disenfranchising themselves and contributing to the rule by a few that they so often lament, when in general elections they find themselves confronted by candidates representing a choice of evils. 

Campaigns For Issues - Won And Lost 

Because of its emphasis on ideas and issues, the Club differs from conventional regular Democratic clubs. A prime focus of the Club's activities is reform of the Democratic Party in order to make it more responsible to the will of party members.

A party reform issue for which the Club worked was the direct election of District Leaders. This was instituted in 1955 by Carmine G. De 

Sapio, County Leader of New York County. The Club also favors such election law revisions as permanent spring primaries, mandatory use of voting machines in primaries and state-wide permanent personal registration. 

The Club is interested in the reform of State Legislature practices, in the selection of people of real ability for appointive office and in the abolition of useless patronage jobs.

In 1957 the Club conducted a vigorous campaign for the passage of a referendum for a State Constitutional Convention to review the state's outmoded constitution. Among the many antiquated provisions of the present constitution are the built-in gerrymandering provisions that give the Republican-controlled State Legislature a powerful weapon for selfperpetuation and control. The referendum carried the 9th A.D. by a vote of 22 to 1. However, elsewhere in the city, Democrats did not work hard enough to offset the upstate vote against the referendum and it was defeated. According to state law, under which such a referendum need only be submitted every 20 years, the next State Constitutional Convention is not likely to be held until 1978. 

The Club has always stood for progress in the field of civil rights. 

It took a public position in favor of modifying the Congressional cloture rule to make it more difficult to filibuster against civil rights legislation. 

It favored the Sharkey-Brown-Isaacs bill against discrimination in housing in New York City which was passed by the New York City Council in 1958. It endorsed the Metcalf-Baker bill against discrimination in New 

York State housing. It has also supported more low and middle income governmentally financed housing, as well as continued rent control of residential housing, the latter an important issue in the 9th A.D.

The Club favors a forward-looking, realistic foreign policy, in the tradition of distinguished Democratic Presidents of modern times. Although we have concentrated on intra-party and local issues, on occasion we have taken a stand on foreign policy. In 1958 we endorsed the extension of a strong Reciprocal Trade Act. That year we also issued a statement protesting the Eisenhower-Dulles position on Quemoy and Matsu and urged that this issue be referred to the United Nations. 

After the overwhelming Republican victory in New York State in 1958 in the face of a nation-wide Democratic trend, the Club adopted a statement, reaffirming its basic beliefs and calling for specific reform actions. on the part of state Democrats to revitalize the party. This statement appearing in the Appendices reaffirms the principles for which we stand and spells out the goals toward which we are working. It has been widely circulated to party leaders in the state and nation and provides a rallying point for liberal Democrats.

Regularity Vs. Independence 

A Running Battle 

There is no doubt that the Club's philosophy of involving as many people as possible in the Democratic Party runs counter to a basic American feeling that partisan politics is something to be avoided. A Gallup poll of parents showed that 70% of those polled disapproved of their children entering politics, while only 20% approved and 10% had no opinion. 

Another poll disclosed that a decisive majority of young voters felt that politics was not for them.

These attitudes are partially attributable to the unethical conduct of some American politicians, partially to certain irrational, though deeply rooted, attitudes in our culture. Conflict is the essence of politics. Today, however, many people are unwilling to concede the necessity of conflict in life or to engage in partisan activities. They prefer to believe all problems can be settled amicably. Although compromise is also an essential element of partisan politics, paradoxically this disturbs them. They feel there must be a right and a wrong side to every question and consequently compromise is reprehensible.

Club members are not unduly disturbed by the presence of conflict in politics, but many of them share the general public's aversion to compromise. 

Some think being "regular" is wrong and being "independent" is right.

Others take the opposite viewpoint. Both groups ignore the fundamental concept on which the Club was founded: the simultaneous combination of regularity and independence.

Although the Club is committed to working within the Democratic Party as its regular A.D. representative, it is also independent in the sense that it is ultimately responsible to its members only, not to any party boss. 

Club members often do not understand the problem posed by the reconciliation of independence with regularity as it emerges in specific instances. 

They are disturbed by apparent inconsistencies. They do not realize that to conform to the Club's fundamental concept each situation must be resolved on its merits.

In practice the Club has been true to its basic commitment. Our regularity is expressed in our constitution which provides that all nominated Democratic candidates are deemed to have received Club endorsement unless 2/3 of the Club members, present at a meeting, vote otherwise. Consistent with this fundamental position, the Club has, at times, accepted undistin guished running mates of a superior candidate when not to have done so would have caused disproportionate difficulties. 

We have expressed our independence on occasions such as 1956 when we supported Stevenson for the Presidential nomination although almost every other party unit in the state supported Governor Harriman. Again in 1958, the Club spearheaded the drive for the nomination of Thomas K. 

Finletter for the U. S. Senate against strong organization opposition. 10

Traditionally, regular local Democratic clubs are interested only in operating party structure within their own area under the direction of the County Leader. They are opposed to reform and to widespread participation in the party. Furthermore, they have shown a marked lack of interest in ideas. Our Club is vitally interested in operating the party structure within its own area. Our primary obligation is to the residents of the 9th 

A.D. However, our goal of widespread participation in the Democratic 

Party cannot be achieved by only one club operating in one Assembly District of one city in one state. Our ideas must permeate into other groups.

The Club has worked out a practical solution regarding its political activities outside the 9th A.D. Because of its philosophy and tradition of independence, it participates, albeit within the party framework, in nomination campaigns of national or state-wide significance. Ordinary “regular” clubs do not do this. However, in local primaries involving insurgent clubs seeking to emulate ours, the Club's policy is not to endorse such groups. 

This official position is an expression of regularity. Regular local clubs do not openly fight other regular local clubs. However, individual Club members are free to aid insurgent groups if they choose and in fact have often done so.

This policy of non-involvement as a club, with no restriction on the activity of individual members, allows for the support of groups working toward goals similar to ours yet prevents the Club from having to make numerous and often quite complex decisions on what groups to endorse. 

Middle Class Membership - Asset and Liability 

The fact that the 9th A.D. is known as the "Silk Stocking" District provides a clue to another special problem with which the Club is faced. 

To a great extent our membership is drawn from middle and upper income groups. This has both assets and liabilities. 

The principal asset is that people in these categories are not subject to the same practical everyday pressures as those in the lower income groups. 

Consequently, when so inclined, they have more time and energy to give to monetarily unrewarding work. Certainly not only the economic factor, but the accompanying educational one, has provided a reservoir of strength that has greatly helped the Club to grow and expand as it has over the past ten years.

The principal liability is directly connected with the principal asset and is actually almost inseparable from it. Those people who, because of their economic status, have the time and energy to devote to monetarily unrewarding work are more inclined than others toward the general American resistance to involvement in partisan politics. They are the ones who are most likely to think partisanship is not very nice. Some cannot accept the Club's desire for permanent activity. They refuse to participate between campaigns, work in campaigns only intermittently, and then only for 11

candidates they like personally or for issues they find inspiring. Others cannot accept the positiveness of the Club's commitment to working within the Democratic Party. They are either caught up in the myth of being independent of any party or are unable to reconcile in their own minds the Club's approach to simultaneous independence and regularity. 

Or they are happy only in the role of insurgents, inspired by idealistic campaigning against what they regard as a clear-cut evil. Finally, very few people in the more comfortably situated economic groups are willing to enter politics at the maximum or professional level. It is at this point that the prejudice against entering politics is, unfortunately, the strongest. 

It is here that the cliche that politics is a dirty game exerts its greatest influence. The result is that at the level where proper leadership by qualified people is most important, those best qualified abdicate responsibility and leave the field open to party hacks. 

The prejudice against politics being a dirty game can only be overcome by inducing honest, qualified people to enter politics. In a nation that is becoming increasingly prestige conscious, particularly in the rapidly growing suburbs, every effort must be made to make people feel that no career is more satisfactory, more worthwhile or more highly regarded than a career of public service.

The irrational prejudice against the conflict and compromise elements of politics will not be easy to overcome. Education is the basic remedy. 

People must be made to realize that partisan politics is an integral part of our political tradition, that government concerns everybody, that conflict and compromise are both positive forces and that neither necessarily indicates something is wrong. Whenever anyone pontificates about the importance of young people entering politics, it is important that those addressed should realize how this can be done. They should be informed of the practical steps necessary to join or to form clubs like ours. Letters by Club members to newspapers or appropriate publications explaining our approach have proved helpful in this respect. 

During the period 1919-1942, when Alfred E. Smith, Franklin D. 

Roosevelt and Herbert H. Lehman (who subsequently became an honorary member of the Club) were Governors of New York, the Democratic Party in the state was a strong, vital organization to which liberals throughout the nation looked for inspiration and guidance. The party deteriorated during the Dewey decade and was unable to regain its former stature during Governor Harriman's tenure. Our 1958 defeat in the state has, of course, had a depressing effect. However, by forcing a scrutiny of party leadership it has actually presented liberals in the state with a real opportunity. 12

THE FIGHT TO ELECT DISTRICT LEADERS 

1949-1953 When the Club was formed in January 1949, Harry S. Truman had just defeated Thomas E. Dewey for the Presidency. Dewey was com pleting a second term as Governor of New York State. A Democrat, William O'Dwyer, was serving his first term as Mayor of New York City.

The 9th Assembly District was a single district, represented by one male and one female District Leader. In early 1949, Judge Jeremiah T. 

Mahoney, the male District Leader, resigned and sought to appoint a co-worker as his successor. This appointment did not suit the plans of the leaders of Tammany Hall. 

Eventually a compromise was reached: the District was split into the 

9th A.D. North and the 9th A.D. South. The Tammany candidate was made Leader of the South. Mahoney's appointee was given the North. 

The undemocratic way in which this situation was handled intensified the determination of the founders of the Lexington Democratic Club to push forward with their plans for reform. 

The Club faced not only the hostility of the regular organization but many other obstacles as well. There was the indifference of the electorate. 

Ordinarily only 10-25% of the enrolled voters participate in a primary. 

Another handicap was the cumbersome method of electing District Leaders. 

District Leaders, at that time, were not elected directly by the enrolled Democrats in the Assembly District, but indirectly by the County Commit teemen who were themselves elected by the enrolled Democrats in each of the Election Districts contained in each A.D. As a result of this indirect system, in order to elect District Leaders in the 9th A.D., it was necessary to elect a majority of County Committeemen in the 60 to 80 E.D.s of which the 9th A.D. was composed. This meant running 60 to 80 different campaigns for 600 to 800 County Committeemen, because different County Committeemen were on the ballot in each E.D. Another obstacle was that 

Primary Day was in the summer which necessitated campaigning when many residents of the 9th A.D. are away. There was also the problem of large apartment buildings in which canvassing is discouraged. 

The election law governing primaries is full of technicalities and insurgent organizations like the Club have to learn how to avoid its pitfalls. 

The Club learned the hard way. The New York City Board of Elections, composed of two Democrats and two Republicans, is theoretically nonpartisan. However, at the time of the 1949 primaries, the Board was united in opposition to all insurgents. Many Club petitions were thrown out for reasons such as the use of the wrong colored ink or abbreviations of street addresses.

Despite these obstacles, the Club succeeded in electing over 200 County Committeemen in the primary. However, this was not enough and we lost the fight to elect Leaders in both halves of the District. 13

During this same summer, Carmine G. De Sapio, the District Leader of the 1st A.D. South, was elected Leader of Tammany Hall by the other 

District Leaders. 

Tammany had reached an all-time low in power and influence. 

Founded in 1789, a few weeks after the Constitution of the United States went into effect, Tammany has been described as the oldest and most powerful institution of a political and sociological nature in America. 

Aaron Burr was its first prominent leader. It survived the scandals of the 

Tweed and Croker eras and reached the zenith of its power between 1902-1924 under the leadership of Charles F. Murphy. It deteriorated in the Walker administration, was discredited by the Seabury investigation and ignored by LaGuardia. Its historical function as a welfare agency was usurped by New Deal programs of relief agencies, social security and unemployment compensation. Furthermore, it opposed the nominations of 

Franklin D. Roosevelt and Herbert H. Lehman before they were elected 

President and Governor respectively, and thus virtually read itself out of the party.

De Sapio had first run for District Leader as an insurgent in 1939. 

Although he had won the primary, he had been refused his seat in Tammany Hall. In refusing to seat De Sapio, the Tammany gang was not only punishing an insurgent but showing its resentment of an Italo-American challenging what was regarded as an Irish-American prerogative. In 1941 

De Sapio ran again, won and again was refused his seat. Finally in 

1943 he succeeded in being seated. In 1949, when De Sapio was finally elected County Leader, a factor in overcoming any remaining opposition to him was the influence of Edward J. Flynn, boss of the Bronx and the strongest Democratic leader in the city. O'Dwyer himself, although openly feuding with Tammany, had been engaged in behind-the-scenes maneuvers to take over the organization. The stumbling block had been De Sapio whom Flynn had come to regard as his protege. 

In the 1949 election, O'Dwyer was re-elected Mayor defeating 

Newbold Morris. A relatively unknown Democrat, Vincent R. Impellitteri, was elected President of the City Council. Herbert H. Lehman was elected to the United States Senate.

In 1950 unpleasant rumors reached Flynn about the O'Dwyer administration and information in the hands of the Kefauver committee. In 

September 1950 O'Dwyer unexpectedly resigned as Mayor and accepted an appointment as Ambassador to Mexico after Ed Flynn had gone to Washing and had had a talk with Harry S. Truman. 

When O'Dwyer resigned, Impellitteri, as President of the City Council, automatically succeeded him.

Despite Impellitteri's favorable position, De Sapio supported Ferdinand Pecora who received the Democratic nomination for Mayor in the November special election. Impellitteri formed the Experience Party and ran as its 14

candidate. The Republicans nominated Edward R. Corsi. Impellitteri, although supported by some of the most reactionary elements of the Democratic Party in New York City, posed as an independent and was elected. In this election Dewey was also re-elected Governor, defeating 

Walter A. Lynch. Herbert H. Lehman was re-elected to the United States 

Senate. We were still an insurgent club but succeeded in obtaining the 

Democratic nomination in the 17th Congressional District for one of our own members, Irving M. Engel. He ran an impressive though losing race. 

In 1951 the Club again campaigned for County Committeemen and District Leaders in both halves of the 9th A.D. By this time there were three clubs struggling to gain control in the South: the pro-Impellitteri regular club, a pro-De Sapio insurgent club and the Lexington Democratic Club. The Lexington Club gained a plurality in the South, but lost in the 

North. However, the County Leadership, by virtue of its control of a majority of the overall 9th A.D. County Committee, consolidated the two halves into a single A.D. Although the candidates had run and the voters had voted on the basis of two separate districts, the leadership was given to the two candidates who had been campaigning against the Club just in the North. This post-election gerrymandering only increased the Club's determination to elect its own District Leaders.

In 1952 the Club prevailed upon two of its members, Dorothy Schiff, publisher of the New York Post, and Lloyd K. Garrison, former Chairman of the National Labor Relations Board and of the War Labor Board, to run for the Democratic State Committee from the 9th A.D. Both The 

New York Times and the Herald Tribune commended the Club for its selection of candidates and editorially urged their election. Despite the opposition of the regular club, we scored our first victory in electing Mrs. 

Schiff and Mr. Garrison by approximately a two to one vote. 

In the general election that fall, the Club spearheaded the campaign for Stevenson in the 9th A.D. and worked so effectively that Eisenhower ran behind Dewey's 1948 percentage in this district. 

In 1953 the Club ran County Committee candidates in the consolidated 9th A.D., scored a decisive victory in the September primary and elected 

Jack Baltzell, Executive Vice President of "Vision," and Alice Sachs, an editor of Crown Publishers, as Leaders. This made the Club the regular club of the District. 

1953 was also a Mayoralty election year. Impellitteri, running for re-election, had the support of the Democratic County Leaders of Kings, Queens and Richmond. 

Robert F. Wagner, then Borough President of Manhattan, was backed by Flynn and by De Sapio, who had almost been deposed by Impellitteri elements. Rudolph Halley, President of the City Council, was the candidate of the Liberal Party but decided not to enter the Democratic primary. 15

Wagner, whom the Club supported, won a decisive victory over Impellitteri in the same primary in which the Club won its District Leader victory.

Before the September 1953 primary, the party had been out of power at city, state and national levels. With the election of Wagner as Mayor, the Democratic Party regained control of City Hall. Edward J. Flynn had died during the Mayoralty campaign and Carmine G. De Sapio, the only surviving County Leader who had supported Wagner for Mayor, thus became the undisputed, though unofficial, Democratic leader of the city. 16

THE FIGHT AGAINST THE REPUBLICANS 1953 TO DATE 

After the Lexington Democratic Club had become the regular club of the 9th A.D. as a result of the September 1953 primary, it had complete responsibility for running the District's campaigns against the Republicans. 

The Club's first campaign was for the election of Robert F. Wagner as 

Mayor. Wagner was elected, defeating both Halley and Harold Riegelman, the Republican candidate.

In 1954, a gubernatorial year, the Club's candidates for delegates to the State Convention as well as our choices for the State Committee, Lloyd K. Garrison and Marietta Tree, were elected without opposition in the primary.

Governor Dewey decided not to run for re-election. The Republicans nominated Irving M. Ives. Franklin D. Roosevelt, Jr. and Averell Harriman were the leading contenders for the Democratic nomination. Roosevelt had considerable support among upstate delegates to the State Convention. 

However, a majority of the delegates were from New York City. When De 

Sapio announced his support of Harriman, Harriman's nomination was assured. The Club's delegates also pledged support to Harriman. Roosevelt accepted the nomination for Attorney-General. 

Harriman defeated Ives by the narrow margin of 11,000 votes out of 5,000,000 cast, a figure approximately the same as that by which the Republican plurality in the 9th A.D. was reduced by the Club's aggressive campaigning. George DeLuca was elected Lieutenant-Governor and Arthur 

Levitt won out for Comptroller, but Roosevelt was defeated by the 

Republican candidate for Attorney-General, Jacob K. Javits.

Three Club members, campaigning for office, Charles M. Kinsolving Jr. for the Assembly in the 9th A.D., Florence P. Shientag for the State Senate in the 20th State Senatorial District and Anthony B. Akers for Congress in the 17th Congressional District, ran impressive but losing races. Akers' defeat by 314 votes made this the closest Congressional race in the country and was particularly encouraging in this highly Republican district. 

By 1955, direct election of Democratic District Leaders, a reform the Club had been seeking since its inception, had been instituted. This meant that enrolled Democrats in an A.D. voted directly for the Leaders, instead of voting for County Committeemen who in turn elected Leaders. Jack 

Baltzell and Alice Sachs were re-elected as Leaders without opposition in the September primary.

In 1955 the only general election contests were for judgeships. Deputy 

Mayor Henry Epstein, a member of our Club, was elected to the State 

Supreme Court. 

Shortly after Adlai E. Stevenson announced his candidacy for the 

Presidential nomination in November 1955, the Club membership voted to 17

support him.

Club members took the lead in forming and running the 

New York State Stevenson-for-President Committee. The state delegation was overwhelmingly for Harriman. However, Jack Baltzell attended the

National Convention as an avowed Stevenson delegate after the Club threatened a primary fight unless adequate Stevenson representation was obtained in our district.

After the National Convention, Club members held key positions in the Stevenson-Kefauver-Wagner Committee. Thomas K. Finletter was 

Co-Chairman, John J. B. Shea, Executive Chairman and Richard A. Brown, Director of Public Relations. 

In the 1956 primary Lloyd K. Garrison and Marietta Tree were re-elected to the State Committee. The Club obtained nominations for public office for two of its members, Tony Akers for Congress in the 17th 

Congressional District and Alice Sachs for Assembly in the 9th A.D. 

The Club endorsed Alfred E. Santangelo in his primary fight for the 

Democratic nomination for Congress in the 18th Congressional District in which he was opposed by Caspar Citron and the reactionary incumbent, James G. Donovan. We carried our E.D.s for Santangelo in a bitter contest. 

Santangelo won the primary and later the general election. 

In the 1956 general election Eisenhower carried the state by a record margin. State Attorney-General Javits defeated Mayor Wagner for the

U.S. Senate seat vacated when Herbert H. Lehman retired. A Republican, Louis J. Lefkowitz, succeeded Javits as Attorney-General.

The Club campaigned enthusiastically for the Stevenson-Kefauver- Wagner and local tickets. Although our local candidates were unable to survive the Eisenhower landslide, they ran approximately 5% ahead of corresponding candidates in 1952, the last comparable year.

After Stevenson's defeat certain liberal Democrats were disturbed by the prospect of four years during which the voice of the party would be that of its conservative Congressional leadership. Consequently, they formed the Advisory Council of the Democratic National Committee. Herbert H. 

Lehman and Thomas K. Finletter played prominent roles in the Council's activities.

Prior to 1957 New York City had operated under a system of annual personal registration. Voters had to register each year in order to be eligible to vote in primaries and general elections. In 1957, Permanent 

Personal Registration (P.P.R.), a reform for which the Club had fought, was instituted in New York City. Under this system, which is presently in effect, voters, once registered, need not re-register unless they move or fail to vote for two successive years.

In the 1957 primary Jack Baltzell and Alice Sachs were again re-elected District Leaders without opposition. Mayor Wagner was re-nominated by 18

the Democrats. The Republicans selected Robert Christenberry, a political neophyte, as their candidate.

In the 1957 general election Wagner was re-elected Mayor by the overwhelming margin of 900,000 votes. In this, the first year of P.P.R., the 

Club worked hard to turn out a large Democratic registration. This effort, coupled with the one-sidedness of the Democratic victory, contributed to a plurality for Wagner in the 9th A.D. on the Democratic line alone. This was the first time in recent history that a major Democratic candidate had been able to carry the 9th A.D. without the help of the vote from the 

Liberal Party line. 

1958 was another gubernatorial year. The Republicans, at their 

State Convention in Rochester, nominated Nelson Rockefeller for Governor, Malcolm Wilson for Lieutenant-Governor, William Lundy for Comptroller and Kenneth Keating for U. S. Senator, and re-nominated Louis J. Lefkowitz for Attorney-General.

The Democratic State Convention was held in Buffalo on August 25th and 26th. There was no question that Harriman would be re-nominated for Governor and little likelihood that DeLuca would not be re-nominated for Lieutenant-Governor and Levitt for Comptroller. The Democrats had also to nominate candidates for the U. S. Senate seat being vacated by the 

Republican Senator Irving M. Ives and for the office of Attorney-General held by Lefkowitz.

The leading candidates for the Democratic Senatorial nomination were 

New York County District Attorney Frank Hogan, former Secretary of the

Air Force Thomas K. Finletter, former Atomic Energy Commissioner 

Thomas E. Murray, former Postmaster-General James A. Farley, and

Edward Dickinson, Director of the State Department of Commerce. Our convention delegates supported Finletter, whom we considered the best qualified candidate.

Mayor Wagner had issued a statement early in August confirming his previously announced position: he was not a candidate. He had promised the voters during the 1957 Mayoralty campaign that he would not abandon City Hall and run for national office as he had done in 1956. He was determined to hold to that promise. There was some doubt, however, as to whether he would be able to resist the pressures of party leaders who thought he would strengthen the ticket. The New York Times reported that Governor Harriman's first choice for the Senatorial nomination was 

Finletter, his second Murray. Later Harriman denied ever having made such a statement.

The convention opened in a state of confusion. Carmine G. De Sapio, New York County Leader, with the support of County Leaders from the Bronx, Kings, Queens and Richmond, had enough votes to nominate his choice, Frank Hogan, on the first ballot. However, there were rumors that 

Wagner might still be prevailed upon to reconsider. This might have changed the whole picture. 19

On the second day of the convention, both morning and afternoon sessions were cancelled while Harriman, Wagner and De Sapio conferred. 

At 11 p.m., many hours after the convention had been scheduled to adjourn, came the announcement that the three leaders were unable to agree. 

Wagner had refused to run. He and Harriman were supporting Murray. 

De Sapio was adamant regarding his candidate, Hogan. The contest would go to the convention floor.

The names of Murray, Hogan and Finletter were placed in nomination. Hogan won easily on the first ballot with 772 votes. Except for 20 

Finletter votes and Wagner's individual vote for Murray, Hogan received the votes of the entire New York City delegation. Murray got 304 votes,

Finletter 66 (20 from New York County, including those of the Club delegates, 41 from Nassau County and 5 from scattered areas). 

Harriman, DeLuca and Levitt were renominated. Peter Crotty, Democratic Leader of Erie County, whose convention votes had been cast for Hogan, was nominated for Attorney-General. 

The convention left a great deal of bitterness. Harriman and Wagner were openly critical of De Sapio. The New York County Leader was annoyed at Harriman. From De Sapio's point of view Harriman had handed the Republicans the bossism issue by failing to assert leadership, then blaming De Sapio for stepping into the breach. Liberal Democrats were antagonized by the way the convention had been run and by the way Finletter had been treated. The Liberal Party refused to endorse Crotty and nominated their own candidate, Edward Goodell, for Attorney-General, thus assuring the re-election of Lefkowitz. 

Finletter did what he could to help in this generally unfortunate situa tion. He declined the Liberal Party's Senatorial nomination, endorsed 

Hogan and agreed to head the Citizens' Committee for Harriman and

Hogan. Nevertheless the repercussions of this intra-party conflict were felt throughout the campaign. 

Rockefeller swept to victory by 550,000 votes, carrying Wilson,

Lefkowitz and Keating with him. Only Levitt, who had not been involved in the Buffalo fracas, was able to withstand the Republican tide. With this state-wide defeat, the party took its first backward step since the Club became recognized in 1953. 

Once again an overwhelming Republican victory at the top of the ticket had grave consequences for the Club's local candidates. Tony Akers campaigned aggressively in the 17th Congressional District against John V. 

Lindsay, who had obtained the Republican nomination as an insurgent following the retirement of the incumbent, Frederick R. Coudert. However, Akers was unable to overcome the Republican tide. Also going down to defeat in spite of hard-fought campaigns were two other members of our Club, although Alice Sachs, running for the second time for the State Assembly in the 9th A.D. ran better than she did in 1954 and Louis Otten 20

running for Justice of the Municipal Court in the 9th Municipal Court District won a larger share of the vote for this office than any previous 

Democratic candidate.

In 1958 we also faced our first primary contest as a regular club. 

A former Club member tried to put up candidates of his own to oppose 

Jack Shea and Marietta Tree, the Club's candidates for the State Com mittee. He failed, however, to get a sufficient number of signatures. 

Nevertheless, the Club treated this challenge seriously, campaigned vigor. ously and demonstrated its ability to hold the District. 21

IDEAS, ACTION AND OUTLOOK Party Reform 

The Club is interested in the party's improvement at all levels-Election District, Assembly District, county, city, state and national. The Club achieved its goal at the Assembly District level when it became the regular club of the 9th A.D. in 1953. However, we have continued to press for various reforms at the four higher levels. Our principal efforts have been at the county level since this is not only the best organized, but also the most accessible.

The Club has pressed and will continue to do so for more democratic procedures within the county organization, including regular meetings of the District Leaders to discuss issues and candidates, regular financial reports from the county organization, a more open method of nominating judicial candidates, the abolition of useless patronage jobs and the appointment of people of real ability to governmental positions. 

Since the Lexington Democratic Club was organized, four other reform clubs, encouraged by our example, have become regular Democratic clubs in New York County: the Samuel J. Tilden Democratic Club in the 1st

A.D. Middle, the West Side Democratic Club in the 5th A.D. Middle, the 

Riverside Democratic Club in the 7th A.D. and the New Democratic Club in the 8th A.D. South. Other reform clubs, at the present time insurgent, hope to elect District Leaders and thus become regular clubs. The pressure of the reform clubs has already caused the County organization to institute certain measures of reform.

At the state level the Club would like to see a more active and effective State Committee. At the national level the Club is interested in increasing the effectiveness of the Advisory Council of the Democratic National Committee.

District Aims 

Within the 9th A.D., the Club's first aim is to hold our position as the regular Democratic club against reactionary elements of the party who challenge us in the primaries. We must elect a Democratic Assemblyman from the 9th A.D., a State Senator and a City Councilman from the 20th

State Senatorial District, a 17th Congressional District Congressman and some judges from the 9th Municipal Court District. There is reason to believe that even in these traditionally Republican districts we can elect our candidates. However, this is contingent upon the selection of good, strong, local nominees running on a ticket headed by a candidate capable of rolling up a plurality of at least 300,000 over his Republican opponent in the state. We must also expand the Club's services to the residents of the 9th A.D. 

New Issues

In addition to our work at the local level we must, as responsible liberals, be bold in recognizing new problems and solving them imaginatively and practically. 22

The acceptance by the Eisenhower Administration of so much that was achieved during the New Deal seems to have left present-day liberals in a curious state of intellectual sterility. If we can break the pattern of thinking in the old, accepted categories, we will find ourselves challenged by many vitally important issues. To give only two examples, there are: 

1) The problems of interstate metropolitan areas, such as new forms of government, overall planning, finance, traffic and transportation, recreation, air and water pollution. 

2) The significance of modern technology, atomic and hydrogen power, electronics and automation on future political development.

Participation Enrolling in a political party and voting might be described as a minimum level of partisan political activity. 

The next step, or middle level, would be to join a political club such as ours or to engage in activities outside the Club such as serving on the County Committee or in campaigns which are not purely Club campaigns. 

The maximum level of political activity involves participation in professional politics. This includes not only those who earn an income from politics, but those who engage in politics as seriously as they would engage in a profession but without recompense. Communication A primary step toward creating the basis for effective liberal Democratic leadership in New York on the county, city and state levels, is to establish some practical means of regular communication between liberal groups. Liberal Democratic club leaders even in New York County did not communicate regularly until shortly before the New York State Convention in 1958, although their clubs are within a comparatively short distance of each other.

If intra-county communication has been difficult to establish, intra-city and intra-state communication will require even more imagination and understanding. Such communication will have to include all serious and potentially helpful groups while at the same time avoiding those whose primary interest is merely publicity for themselves and their own particular interests. There must be recognition of the fact that there are often legitimate rivalries between liberal Democratic groups and personality prob lems with various leaders, as well as a basic community of interest. At the present time there is no clearly defined and functioning liberal wing of the Democratic Party in the state. However, elements for its creation may be found in: 1) regular clubs along the lines of ours, 2) insurgent groups struggling to become regular clubs in New York County and in other 23

counties in the city, 3) the Nassau County Democratic organization and 

4) groups in both the suburbs and in upstate New York.

Leadership 

Besides developing communication between groups in order to fill the vacuum in leadership, it is equally important to develop individual liberal leadership. The most effective individual political leadership is that exercised by officials in the elective and legislative branches at city and state levels and by certain appointive officials in the executive branch. 

A question we must ask ourselves is: "Where is the individual liberal leadership coming from in New York State during the next decade?" Aside from certain elder statesmen in the party, it would seem that such leadership must come out of clubs like ours and from the other groups we have already mentioned. At the present time, the able individuals in such clubs and groups are not sufficiently well-known politically to be able to attain important elective or appointive office. It will probably require between five to fifteen years for them to qualify. Yet with this goal in mind, every attempt should be made to encourage potentially suitable candidates for higher elective office to build up a reputation in any of the following ways: 

1) Through general civic activity in relatively non-political groups. 

2) By running for elective offices such as City Councilman, Assemblyman or State Senator and by continuing to do so even if defeated. 

3) By qualifying for appointive office.

This latter category is of particular significance for members of our Club. Appointive positions are relatively numerous, whereas in the gerrymandered districts where the Club operates it is particularly difficult for a Democrat to be elected to office. Anyone interested in a political career should realize that the best training is in government itself. 

The logical culmination of the Club's basic philosophy of involvement in partisan politics is to have competent people, interested in ideas, seeking to make politics a career. It is a novel concept. 

In the past, people interested mainly in ideas have tended to avoid politics and competent careerists have been found largely in the non-political civil service. A political career involves insecurity and a comparatively low income. These are both very real hazards. However, unless a sufficient number of young liberals can be persuaded to assume the risks and limitations of a political career, it will be impossible to develop the type of leadership the party needs.

Other Approaches 

Although our specific "insurgent" approach may not be applicable to other communities throughout the United States, we hope that our ideas and activities will encourage new groups to enter the political arena and more young people to participate in politics. The Club started as an insurgent 24

group fighting to oust entrenched and stagnant Democratic leadership in a Republican District in a city where the Democratic Party is the majority party.

In communities where the Democratic Party is the minority party, as in Nassau County and in certain areas in California, it may not be necessary to go through a battle with fellow Democrats. The Republicans can be engaged at once by a freshly organized liberal group. Those living in an area where there is already a liberal Democratic club are fortunate in that all they have to do to become politically active is to join it. 

Old Foes and New Friends

As our Club grows and our ideas spread, we must be prepared to face more active hostility on the part of old-line elements of the party organization. There has already been an example of this in the formation of a group with connections outside the 9th A.D. attempting to oust us. 

One basic question has yet to be answered: "Can people in the lower economic groups and those less familiar with the importance and value of ideas be attracted to a club like ours?" If the answer is no, the field of operations for such a club is definitely limited. Otherwise the possibilities are infinite. 25

CLUB ORGANIZATION AND ACTIVITIES Campaigns, Committees And Headaches 

Although the Club is an unusual type of political club, its principal activity is campaigning, the same as any other political club. Campaigning usually begins in early spring and ends on Election Day in November. 

Between campaigns the Club is open on week-days and on Monday and Thursday evenings. During the height of a campaign it is open every evening and on week-ends.

Campaign activities follow successive phases: 1) Organizing for petition period. 2) Petition period. 3) Preparing for Primary Day. 4) Primary Day. 5) Preparing for registration period. 6) Registration period. 

7) Preparing for Election Day. 8) Election Day. The E.D. Captains under the direction of the Campaign Committee do most of this work. 

It is they who contact the voters directly. The Campaign Committee, assisted by the Legal Committee and the Inspectors Committee, holds ultimate responsibility for all campaign activities. 

The internal organization of the Club is prescribed by its constitution which was adopted and can be amended in a democratic fashion. The basic deliberative body of the Club is the Executive Committee. The Executive 

Committee consists of: 

1) Eight Club officers - President, four Vice-Presidents, Secretary, Treasurer, and Corresponding Secretary, elected by the Club membership. 

2) 25 members-at-large, elected by the Club membership. 

3) The two District Leaders, elected by 9th A.D. enrolled Democrats. 

4) The two State Committeemen, elected by 9th A.D. enrolled Democrats. 

5) All former Club Presidents who have held office during the past three years.

The officers and members-at-large of the Executive Committee are chosen in the following manner: 

1) A nominating committee of nine is elected by the Club membership at a meeting held in the late fall. 

2) The nominating committee then presents its slate of officers and members-at-large of the Executive Committee to be voted on by the 

Club membership at a meeting held in January. 

3) Anyone dissatisfied with the slate of the nominating committee may nominate additional candidates by circulating petitions that must be signed by 20 members and filed ten days before the January meeting. 26

This method of choosing officials is a compromise between the town meeting type of selection and rule by a few. It ensures careful screening and selection by an elected group, yet allows for opposition. It is democratic in that 20 Club members out of the entire membership can put a name in nomination and all members have the opportunity to vote. The membership is informed and has the opportunity to study the qualifications of all candidates before making a choice. Members elected to the nominating committee are often nominated to be Club officers. This is not surprising since they are apt to be the best known and the most capable Club members. 

The officers, District Leaders, State Committeemen and past Presidents constitute an informal group known as the Officers Committee that usually meets the week before Executive Committee meetings to consider recommendations to be made to the Executive Committee. Executive Committee meetings are ordinarily open to all Club members. All major issues, includ ing the endorsement of candidates, are voted on by the Club's membership. 

A group of prominent Club members serves as an Advisory Council. 

The Club has two paid part-time Executive Secretaries who are at the Club during the day. Otherwise all Club activities are handled by volunteers.

Committees handle most of the Club's important activities. The 

Judicial Committee works on court reform and on improving methods of selecting judges. There are separate committees on City, State and National Affairs. A Committee may be formed to study a special issue, as in the case of the State Constitutional Reform Committee or of the 

City Charter Reform Committee. Committee work is implemented in various ways. Recommendations are made to the Executive Committee that the Club take a stand on a specific issue, either through a statement that has first been submitted to and voted upon by the Club membership or through individual letters or telegrams to public officials. Testimony is given before legislative bodies, various Democratic Party platform committees and special groups, such as the Temporary Commission on the 

State Constitutional Convention. 

Despite the Club's broader interests, it is primarily a 9th A.D. Club. 

Committees render specific services to the residents of the area. The Rent, Housing and Services Committee has published a booklet, "Housing and Rent Review for Tenants" and gives advice to tenants. It helped a group of tenants who were being evicted to make room for the Coliseum by obtaining moving expenses for them and time to move. In 1958 the 

Community Affairs Committee instituted a program of polio shots at the Club, organized "Friends of P.S. 612" to help a school for problem children in the 9th A.D. and worked for the re-opening of the 96th Street branch of the New York Public Library. The Newsletter Committee sends out a newsletter several times a year. The Spanish-American Affairs Committee deals with the problems of Puerto Rican residents of the district. The New 

Apartments Committee extends a welcome to new residents. 27

The Club holds meetings and social functions throughout the year. 

Our general meetings, arranged by the Program Committee, feature prominent speakers or panel discussions on subjects of current political interest. (See Appendices). In special workshops our members can learn about the history and philosophy of the Club, the Democratic Party's structure and how the party operates.

A Special Functions Committee arranges two fund-raising parties each year, one in the late fall or winter, one in the spring. In addition to the two annual functions, the Social Committee arranges smaller parties, picnics and boat rides. A special activity in 1958 was a chartered round-trip plane flight to Europe, which was so successful that it may become an annual event. 

Much time and energy are given by Club members to the actual operation of the Club. There are Finance, Budget, House, Membership and Publications Committees. 

A club as active as ours, run entirely by volunteers, presents organiza tional problems that still require careful study. 

In order to ease the tremendous load of work that began to devolve on the President as the Club grew from its modest beginnings to its present size, the President shifted some of the coordinating and supervisory work to the Vice-Presidents, each of whom now has charge of several of the 28

committees. Although this has somewhat alleviated the situation, the presidential workload is still too heavy.

As the Club continued to grow, the Executive Committee found itself bogged down in administrative detail. In the Club constitution, the Execu tive Committee alone is authorized to make administrative decisions. Consequently the Executive Committee, meeting only once a month, found itself with little time to discuss political matters once it had considered the work of its committees.

The informal Officers Committee was formed to expedite the work of the Executive Committee. This Officers Committee, being a smaller and more informal body, could more easily winnow material. However, even this solution is not an ideal one. 

Also there is no permanent long range planning committee in the Club, although a special "Role" Committee did some excellent work along these lines, and issued a report in 1957. 29

PARTIES, PEOPLE AND POLLING PLACES Voting Cycle 

New York State has a four-year voting cycle. 

The first year is the Presidential year when the President and Vice- President of the United States, Congressmen, State Senators and State 

Assemblymen are elected.

The second year is the Mayoralty year when three city-wide officialsthe Mayor, the Comptroller and the President of the City Council, are elected as well as Borough Presidents and City Councilmen. 

The third year is the Gubernatorial year when the Governor of New York State and other state-wide officials are elected as well as Congressmen, State Senators and Assemblymen. 

The fourth year is an off-year when no major officials are elected unless there is a special election. 

United States Senators are elected for six-year terms. New York will elect Senators next in 1962 and 1964 unless vacancies occur earlier. 

Political Parties And Candidates 

Today there are three political parties in New York State: Republican, Liberal and Democratic. A political party in this state is an organization which polled at least 50,000 votes for its candidate for Governor in the last election. The principal function of a political party is to nominate and campaign for candidates for public office. Independent groups may also run candidates for public office. These groups, as well as political parties, are strictly controlled by the election law of the state. 

To be elected to public office, a candidate must be nominated by one of the three parties or by a qualified independent group, organized according to the election law. Nominations for certain public offices are made by official parties at conventions instead of in primaries. 

Candidates for President and Vice-President of the United States are nominated at National Conventions. 

In this state, the candidate for Governor and the candidates for other state-wide offices are nominated at State Conventions. 

Candidates for U. S. Senator are nominated at State Conventions although they may also be nominated by a party's State Committee in nongubernatorial years. Only regular parties may nominate candidates at State 

Conventions. Independent groups nominate candidates for state-wide office by circulating petitions.

The names of candidates for city-wide office, for Congress, State Senate and the Assembly are placed on the Primary Day ballot by obtaining the prescribed number of signatures on "designating petitions. 99 30

Only enrolled members of a party may sign that party's designating petitions or vote in its primary. Primary Day is two to five months before Election 

Day. If, as is usually the case, only one name appears on the ballot for each office, nomination in the primary is a formality. If there is more than one name for a particular office on the ballot, there is a primary contest. 

The person receiving the most votes becomes the party's nominee. Inde pendent groups nominate for these offices by petitions without primaries. 

However, their nominating petitions require more signatures than the designating petitions of regular parties. 

Party candidates for Supreme Court Justice are nominated by a 

Judicial Convention in each Judicial District. 

Party candidates for City Court and Municipal Court Justices are designated by petition and nominated in primaries. 

On Election Day, the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November, a general election is held. A general election, as opposed to a primary, involves candidates from more than one party or group and is for public office.

Democratic Party Office 

Nation And State

In addition to public office, there is another type of office — party office which includes, in the Democratic Party, the National Chairman, the National Committeemen from each state, the State Chairman, the State 

Committeemen, the County Leader, Assembly District Leaders, County 

Committeemen and delegates to National, State and Judicial Conventions.

State Committeemen, Assembly District Leaders, County Committee men and delegates to National, State and Judicial conventions are designated by petitions and elected directly by enrolled Democrats at the primary. 

The County Leader is elected by the District Leaders. 

The State Chairman (male) and the State Vice-Chairman (female) 

are elected by the State Committee. 

The two National Committeemen (male and female) are nominated by the State Committee and approved by the State's delegation to the 

National Convention. 

The National Chairman (male) and the National Vice-Chairman 

(female) are elected by the National Committee, consisting of the two National Committeemen (male and female) from each state and the Canal Zone, District of Columbia, Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands. These party officials are responsible for the operation of the party at their respective levels. The only person to receive a salary for his work is the 

National Chairman. He is the year-round publicity chief for the party and its principal fund raiser. He must keep in close touch with the Presi dent, if the latter is a Democrat, and with the party leaders in Congress. 

He channels high level national patronage to the states. 31

The National Committee fixes the time and place of National Conventions. It can nominate a candidate for President or Vice-President if the party's candidate dies between the convention and the election. 

The State Chairman's role is, on the state level, similar to that of the National Chairman on the national level. He publicizes the party throughout the state, raises money and participates in channeling state and federal patronage to County Leaders who receive recommendations from their 

District Leaders. 

The State Committee, in addition to nominating the two National Committeemen (male and female) selects the state's delegates-at-large to the National Convention and designates Presidential electors. 

Democratic Party Office 

County And District

The New York County Democratic organization is more tightly knit than either the state or national organizations. The Election District (E.D.) is the smallest geographical unit used for election purposes. It is also the smallest unit in the party organization. In New York County an E.D. generally contains between 300-1,300 registered voters and ranges from a fraction of a square block to several square blocks. There are presently 

1,050 E.D.s in New York County. The Democratic County Committee consists of three or four County Committeemen elected by the enrolled Democrats of each E.D. in the County. Although the Committeemen must be enrolled Democrats residing in the Assembly District (A.D.) which they represent, they need not live in the E.D. they serve. Their principal function is to meet with other committeemen and ratify party rules which have already been prepared by the county leadership. The County Com mittee may fill the post of Assembly District Leader should this position become vacant between primaries. In some circumstances, the County 

Committee may also nominate party candidates for the office of Assembly. man, State Senator, or Congressman for a special election. Theoretically it is the party's governing body in the County, but actually the Assembly 

District Leaders hold the real power today.

The A.D. is a geographical unit consisting of 36-115 E.D.s from which a State Assemblyman is elected. New York County contains 16 Assembly 

Districts. Some of these are subdivided for the purpose of electing District 

Leaders. The enrolled Democrats of each District or subdistrict elect one male and one female District Leader in primary elections held in odd numbered years.

The District Leaders, male and female, constitute the Executive Committee of the County Committee of New York County, known as "Tammany Hall". They elect their officers including a County Leader who is usually one of themselves. A strong New York County Leader like De Sapio is frequently in a position to influence not only patronage appointments but nominations for elective office in both the city and state. 

Yet no County Leader can succeed in such endeavors without the backing of the majority of the District Leaders. 32

There is no official city-wide Democratic organization. City-wide nominations are generally arrived at by agreement of the five County 

Leaders. 

District Leaders play a vital role in the selection of candidates for local offices. In New York County, where Democratic nomination is tantamount to election, this is an extremely important function. Similarly, District 

Leaders have an important voice in determining who shall fill appointive positions.

District Leaders maintain year-round contact with the residents of their districts, operate through the facilities of their local Democratic clubs and assist in problems of a local and political nature. 

The District Leaders, particularly when it comes to getting out the vote at election time, depend on the industry and effectiveness of their 

Election District captains. 

Election District captains are chosen by the District Leaders or by the local clubs. They are not elected and are not a part of the official party structure. They are, however, the basic unit of party machinery. Their work is of tremendous importance. They are the representatives of the party on the grass roots level, not only during but between campaigns. 

To be an effective Election District captain in a city like New York requires energy, endurance and a gift for establishing contact with every type of voter. It is also the best possible initiation into politics. 

As the regular Democratic club for the 9th A.D., the Club recommends two Democrats for appointment as paid Inspectors in each E.D. With their 

Republican counterparts, they are in charge of and responsible for the mechanics of local polling places on Primary Day, during registration period and on Election Day. 

Legislative, Congressional, And Judicial Districts

STATE AND CITY LEGISLATIVE BODIES 

The New York State Legislature consists of two bodies, the Assembly and the State Senate. Each of 150 Assembly Districts elects one Assembly. man for a two-year term. Each of 58 State Senatorial Districts elects one 

State Senator for a two-year term.

New York City has two bodies with legislative functions. Its powerful 

Board of Estimate, which also has administrative functions, consists of the three city-wide officials - the Mayor, the Comptroller and the President of the City Council, with four votes each and the five Borough Presidents with two votes each. Each of these officials, except for the President of the 

City Council, has significant administrative duties apart from his Board of Estimate work. The New York City Council has limited legislative powers. 25 City Councilmen are elected from State Senatorial Districts in the City for four-year terms. 33

GERRYMANDERING 

As a result of various restrictive provisions of the state constitution, the State Legislature is apportioned so that urban areas like New York City have less than their fair share of representation. Furthermore the 

Republican-controlled State Legislature “gerrymanders" State Senatorial, Assembly, Congressional and Municipal Court districts; that is, fixes their boundaries so as to favor the Republicans. In Democratic areas like New 

York County, the Legislature makes sure that Republican strength is con centrated in a few districts so that the Republicans are sure to elect at least a few legislators and judges. 

20TH STATE SENATORIAL DISTRICT 

The 20th State Senatorial District covers the entire 1st, 8th and 9th Assembly Districts and is gerrymandered in favor of the Republicans. 

17TH CONGRESSIONAL DISTRICT 

Unlike the boundaries of State Senatorial Districts, the boundaries of Congressional Districts are not contiguous with Assembly District lines. 

The 9th A.D. occupies 1/3 of the 17th Congressional District which is carefully gerrymandered in favor of the Republicans. 

18TH CONGRESSIONAL DISTRICT 

Only 4 E.D.s of 78 in the 9th A.D. are in the 18th Congressional

District, which covers portions of East Harlem. This District was formerly the stronghold of the late Vito Marcantonio and is predominantly Democratic. 

1ST JUDICIAL DISTRICT The 1st Judicial District embraces the counties of New York and the 

Bronx. State Supreme Court Justices are elected from this District, which is Democratic.

MUNICIPAL COURT DISTRICTS 

There are nine Municipal Court Districts in New York County, each of which elects two to six justices for ten-year terms. The Municipal Court is one of the City's busiest and most important courts, handling civil suits which involve up to $3,000. It deals with almost all landlord-tenant disputes and has a Small Claims Part where people engaged in litigation involving $100 or less can receive justice without the necessity of hiring a lawyer.

The 9th A.D. makes up part of four Municipal Court Districts. 

About 2/3 of the Assembly District is in the 9th Municipal Court District, the most heavily Republican city district. About 1/5 is in the 6th Municipal Court District and small parts are in the 3rd and 4th Municipal Court Districts. These three Districts are predominantly Democratic. 34

APPENDIX E Statement of Principles and Goals 

Adopted by the

Lexington Democratic Club on December 18, 1958

STATEMENT OF PRINCIPLES... 

TODAY the Democratic Party in New York State is in low public esteem.

We believe that the principal causes of this dissatisfaction are the failure of the party leaders to follow the principles believed in by the overwhelming majority of New York Democrats and the public's loss of confidence in the ability of elected public officials to provide scandal-free administrations and imaginative solutions to the ever-increasing problems of state and municipal government. 

In organizing the Lexington Democratic Club ten years ago, we outlined a program for the practical application of the principles that we believed should guide the Democratic Party, and we have worked to achieve them. The principles that motivated us then are equally applicable today. We think it more important to look to the future and to restate our beliefs at this time rather than to attempt to assess blame on particular individuals for the Democratic defeat in New York in 1958. 

The Lexington Democratic Club believes that the Democratic Party should be open to and should encourage participation by all who believe in it and that it must be run democratically. The idea that the leader always knows best, and that all who differ are not true Democrats, must be done away with. Throughout the State as well as in New York City, the party organizations must speak for and represent not only the professional party leadership, but the much larger number of voters who believe and support the principles of the Democratic Party. We must recognize that the Democratic Party consists not only of party workers but of millions of persons who look to the Democratic Party to furnish intellectual leadership and outstanding candidates. We also must recognize that any political party exists not as an end in itself or as a means for personal advancement, but rather to serve the people. 

The Democratic Party also must be a party of liberalism. Voters interested in complacency can find it in the Republican Party; we can never compete with the 

Republican Party in its appeal to those voters who are motivated only by the desire to stand pat. The Democratic Party must continue to seek and develop new ideas and new leadership, to attract young people into its ranks, and to present candidates with outstanding qualifications.

We are aware that the failure to follow principles and the abuses and problems of which we complain are not peculiar to the Democratic Party. In many aspects, the Republican Party is less democratic than ours. But we are Democrats, we be lieve deeply in the principles of our Party, we believe that it is the most potent force for good within our nation, and we take pride in its potential strength and decency. Republican inaction, Republican backwardness and Republican indifference offer us no excuse. Our Party is founded on the concept that it truly represents the people. We cannot allow it to betray that premise, whatever the opposition may do or be. 

GOALS WE SEEK ... 

There is no one road to the goals we seek, but the following program represents the minimum necessary action for the Democratic Party to regain public respect. 

1. Removal of artificial barriers that hinder participation by all Democrats in the affairs of their party. Thus, we favor: 

Permanent spring primaries. 

Use of voting machines in primaries. 44

Statewide primaries to replace the present State Convention system. 

Mandatory direct election of party district leaders. 

Reform of the Board of Elections to make it an independent nonpolitical administrative body. 

2. Basic renovation of the State party organization. The Democratic State 

Committee now little more than a name, a letterhead and an annual dinner should be expanded into an effective organization that will represent all of the 

Democratic voters in New York State. Specifically, we urge: Development of the feeling that the State Committee is responsive not only to the party "regulars" but to all Democrats throughout the State.

Establishment of an active, functioning Executive Committee so that the State Committee will no longer be a one-man operation. 

Establishment of subcommittees to work on specific problems, such as upstate organization and problems of the suburban areas. 

Development of a professional research, public relations and organizational staffs. 

More frequent meetings of the State Committee. 

Adoption of policy statements on important issues of the day and of other means of informing Democratic organizations and the public about these issues.

A program to publicize inadequacies of the Republican state adminis tration and its failure to meet campaign promises. 

3. Increased democratization of the Party at the local level. This includes the encouragement of local organizations which will be democratic as well as Democratic, which will truly represent the people in their district, which do not exist for the sake of patronage, which will not bar women or any others from participation and which will be willing to take positions that may at times be contrary to those taken by the party leadership. Incumbent organizations should take steps to achieve these goals. If they refuse to do so, new organizations should take their place. be: 

4. An end to the secrecy and mystery that surround party affairs. There should 

Frequent meetings of party committees.

Full financial reports to members on funds received and disbursed by party committees.

Cessation of the practice of announcing decisions of the party organi zations at the last possible minute and thus preventing an opportunity to form an effective opposition. 

5. Discarding of outmoded practices which should have no place on the 

American political scene. We must abolish:

Over-emphasis on the need for balanced tickets from a religious, racial and geographical standpoint.

The farcical judicial convention system by which judicial candidates are selected.

Nomination of candidates whose only qualification is their participation in the professional party leadership group and appointment of persons whose only claim for preferment is their alleged party regularity. 

6. The establishment of definite criteria for filling appointive positions. No job should be filled that does not serve a useful purpose and only fully qualified people should be appointed. We also urge a thorough reconsideration of the patronage system which has been the principal bulwark of both Republican and Democratic 45

This system has led to the appointment of incompetents to positions of public trust and has prevented the appointment of qualified persons to public office merely because they do not have the right political support or come from the wrong part of the City or belong to the wrong political club. It has done much to discredit the Democratic Party in the eyes of the public.
