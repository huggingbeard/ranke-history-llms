The Commonwealth of Massachusetts SUMMARY OF REPORT. 

In accord with the instructions of the General Court, this report is concerned with means of absorbing the "labor surplus" among workers in the Commonwealth who are 45 years or more of age, especially the age group of 45 to 64. It contains no original material, being limited to past findings and recommendations of authorities in Massachusetts and elsewhere.

UNEMPLOYMENT OF OLDER WORKERS. 

Nationally, it is anticipated that the number of persons aged 45 or more in our population will increase from about 48 millions at the present time to over 63 millions by 1975, due to advances in medicine, nutrition and living standards. If the population of 

Massachusetts behaves similarly, its present estimated 1.5 millions older persons will exceed 2.0 millions by 1975. 

Unfortunately, the number of job opportunities available to persons aged 45 or more has not kept pace with this population trend. 

Thus, such older male workers constituted only 35.9 per cent of the 

Massachusetts male civilian labor force in covered employment in recent years, yet they were filing 59 per cent of unemployment compensation claims; women workers in this age group show about the same situation. A fairly recent study of 927 displaced New England textile workers who were unable to find new jobs, showed that 

58.5 per cent were in the age group 46–65, and 11.8 per cent were above that age.

BARRIERS TO EMPLOYMENT OF THE AGING. 

All unemployed workers are confronted with certain obstacles in seeking employment, but unemployed workers over 45 experience special difficulty from the six special barriers of a psychological,

technological, economic and contractual nature which are discussed below: 

1. Insurance Barriers. Beliefs and practices connected with the workmen's compensation laws are a principal obstacle to the employment of aging workers. The following important factors are involved: (a) Heart disease rulings. Insurance authorities estimate that nearly half of all workers over 45 years of age have some kind of heart condition. Decisions of state courts and workmen's compensation boards hold increasingly that heart attacks suffered by employees on the job are occupational in origin, with consequent liberal awards to the employee or his survivors. Since employer premium payments for the workmen's compensation are determined by these awards, the employer is prone to avoid employment of older workers to cut his workers' claims and his insurance costs. (b) Accident proneness fears. Many employers believe that older workers "slow down" physically and mentally as they age, with a resulting decline in alertness and an increasing susceptibility to accidents. Actually federal and state industrial accident statistics indicate that older workers have fewer accidents per worker than younger age groups. Unfortunately, however, the injury of an older worker is frequently more serious than one suffered by a younger worker; and requires a longer period for recuperation.

The accident rates of some companies may be due not to the employment of older workers, but to inadequate industrial safety measures or to the failure of employers to keep the physical characteristics of the worker in mind when assigning him to his tasks. 

Similarly, group insurance plans for employees may cause employers to hesitate to hire older job applicants. Premium rates for group life insurance coverage increase with the age of the workers covered. Employers also fear that the illnesses of age, and the longer period of recuperation required by older people increase their group health and accident plan costs. 

2. Other Employer Attitudes and Practices. Employer objections to the employment of older job applicants may spring from: (a) The belief that absenteeism among workers increases with age. Recent federal studies of workers in manufacturing industries showed that workers over 50 lost fewer days, from all causes, than younger workers in those industries.

1957.] 

(b) The belief that worker efficiency declines with age. A University of Illinois study, made in 1953, indicated that this was not true of the majority of 3,077 older workers employed in selected. retailing, office, managerial and manufacturing firms. (c) Personnel problems encountered in hiring older workers. 

These include seniority problems, in-service training aspects, and human relations difficulties within the employing firm. The common denominator of all of these factors is the company's need to hire capable younger employees who can be developed gradually for positions of higher responsibility in the organization. (d) Faulty job specifications. Physical qualifications prescribed by an employer may include requirements not really needed for the duties to be performed. Occasionally, federal and state civil service systems have been criticized on this score by groups seeking to better the lot of older workers and handicapped workers. (e) Retirement pension plans. A number of formidable obstacles to the employment of the aging have appeared in the retirement pension plans which are spreading through industry. These include: (1) the non-transferability of employer contributions, (2) increases in pension costs under plans where age is a factor in determining employer and employee contributions, (3) specific age and service requirements for participation in the pension plan, and (4) the reluctance of employers to utilize waiver provisions under which they can hire older workers without increasing pension plan costs. 

3. Worker Attitudes and Practices. - Seniority provisions of labor union contracts, while protecting the livelihoods of older employed workers in a plant, tend to prevent their being hired in jobs which are commensurate with their skills and experience. 

Older workers themselves may have attitudes which impair their chances of gaining employment, or keeping the new job which they find. These attitudes include: (a) fear and anxiety of new situations, (b) resistance to change and (c) sensitivity with respect to their status. Thus they may insist on applying for positions at previous levels of skill or pay, without regard for changed conditions. They may display an unwillingness to retrain, to travel or 

1 to change their residence.

4. Employment Agency Practices. The attitudes and practices of job placement personnel of both private employment agencies and state employment services may adversely affect the older job

Overly strict interpretation of the requirements of their statutes by state vocational education officials may restrict unnecessarily the furnishing of vocational training and retraining needed by displaced workers aged 45 or more.

REDUCING BARRIERS TO EMPLOYMENT OF OLDER WORKERS. 

Over the years, various recommendations as to means of reducing or removing the above barriers to the employment of older workers have been made by the authorities in the various States, including Massachusetts. These authorities believe that the adoption of their proposals will minimize job discrimination, thereby increasing employment opportunities and reducing unemployment. 

The more important of these proposals, and Massachusetts action on certain of them, are summarized as follows: 

1. Workmen's Compensation Law. - Two suggestions have been made with respect to state workmen's compensation laws, with a view to offsetting insurance factors which cause employers to reject older job applicants. An insurance company expert has suggested that the second injury fund principle be applied to the coverage of workers with cardiac conditions. A Massachusetts recess commission recommended in 1935 that a competitive state workmen's compensation fund be established, in order to remove alleged profit motive considerations which the commission blamed for discrimination against older workers. 

2. Retirement Pension Plans. - Proposals have been made for raising the compulsory retirement age prescribed by governmental employees retirement laws and private industry employees pension

plans. Recent legislative proposals to raise the retirement age of public employees under the Massachusetts state retirement law were rejected by the General Court in 1955. 

3. Vocational Training. It has been suggested that programs for the vocational training and retraining of displaced older workers be developed more vigorously. The 1956 General Court enacted a law permitting the payment of unemployment compensation benefits for an additional 10 weeks to unemployed workers who enter into a state-approved vocational training course. Another recent 

Massachusetts statute has authorized free university extension. courses for older persons. 

4. Job Placement Services. The recommendations have been made for special training of state employment service job placement personnel in the placement problems of older workers. With the support of the federal government, such special training is being instituted for employees of the Massachusetts Division of Em ployment Security. 

5. Sheltered Workshops. - A proposal is currently pending before the General Court for the establishment of a sheltered workshop program for older workers. This proposal has been endorsed by the 

Massachusetts Council on the Employment of the Aging. 

6. Fair Employment Practices Proposals. — In a number of States, there has been agitation for legislation prohibiting age discrimination in employment. Such legislation was enacted by Massachusetts in 1937 and in 1950. The Massachusetts Civil Service Director currently compels state and local government agencies subject to the Civil Service Law to show just cause for any physical requirements or age restrictions which they wish to establish for civil service positions. Such requirements must be "bona fide" in terms of the work to be performed. 

7. Special Agencies for the Aging. - Proposals have been made for the establishment of state agencies for the aging which would co-ordinate the various state programs for the aging, conduct educational programs designed to eliminate discrimination against the aging, engage in research on problems of the aging, and perform many other functions with respect to the needs of older people generally, or older workers in particular.

During the past 26 years, a total of 74 bills have come before the General Court which were concerned in whole or in part with the problem of elderly men and women who seek employment or who fear the loss of employment due to their age. Chapter III. Barriers to the Employment of the Aging. 

NATURE OF BARRIERS. 

General Characteristics. 

The obstacles which unemployed workers over 40 encounter in seeking work are varied in their character, and closely interwoven. 

The obstacles are psychological because they involve prejudices and attitudes of employers, employees (both young and old), governmental officials and the general public. 

They are technological and economic, in that they concern factors of obsolescence of machines and skills, mobility of industry, re- 

1 Statistics reproduced in Table 7 of the 1955 Annual Report of the Massachusetts Division on the Employ ment of the Aging. 

2 Ibid., Table 6.

1957.] 

quirement of new skills, and the ratio of available labor supply to the demand for labor-factors which may play a decisive role. 

Finally, the obstacles are contractual in so far as they concern insurance and pension considerations, and provisions of labor union contracts.

With relation to these employment barriers encountered by older workers, a report on age discrimination in employment submitted to the Massachusetts General Court in 1931, observed that In studying the problem of the unemployment of the older worker, there are two things to be remembered: first, that the whole problem of "age discrimination" must be divided into discrimination against the aged, and discrimination against the middle aged; and second, that this so-called discrimination does not itself cause unemployment but rather the substitution of one worker for another. 

Discrimination against the aged worker has always been with us. It is largely based on his failing health and general slowing up. 

Middle aged discrimination, however, affects not only the man and woman over 45, but frequently the man over 40 and the woman over 35. 

This is extremely dangerous if it becomes widespread, as the man from 40 to 55 should be at the peak of his earning power certainly he is at the peak of his load. 

This is the period when he is educating his family, and generally he has not yet had time to accumulate an estate. 

It is probably true that the question of age discrimination depends to some extent upon the type of work done by the individual. Grouping all employees broadly, we might say that manual workers, both skilled and unskilled, except machine workers, suffer discrimination because of age less than any other group. 

Clerical workers and machine workers feel the discrimination quite keenly, and executives, excepting, possibly, major executives of the first rank, feel it most of all. 

The reasons for this are not impossible to understand. Men hired for executive positions are usually hired with the hope and expectation that they will be able to progress into positions of still larger responsibilities. If they are of middle age or past, it is felt doubtful if their years of service with the company will enable them to do this.

People hired for clerical work are also quite frequently engaged with the hope that they will be able to qualify for positions of executive or semi-executive rank. 

Here, again, the argument often used against them is that if men or women have reached the age of 40 or 45 and have never progressed beyond a low salary, it is doubtful if they will ever be able to.

Machine workers feel the discrimination largely because the question of speed is paramount, and the younger workers are apt to be quicker in their motions.

Discrimination against the older worker is not particularly the product of a period of depression. As a matter of fact, surveys made by one or two social agencies would seem to indicate that in times of severe depression there is a tendency for the younger worker to suffer more than the older worker.

DIFFICULTIES OF OLDER JOB SEEKERS IN FINDING Work. 

The relative success of the older workers in finding work is indicated in a recent study of 1,705 displaced textile workers in selected central and southern New England mill towns.2 In the study Professor Miernyk showed that of 778 workers who were able to obtain new jobs, 42.3 per cent were 46 to 65 years old, and 

0.8 per cent were over 65. However, of the 927 workers still unemployed, 58.5 per cent were in the 46 to 65 age bracket, and 11.8 per cent were 66 years of age or more. The study comments:

Perhaps the greatest barrier to inter-industry mobility is the advanced age of many of the displaced workers. Although not all of the younger workers had found jobs, those under 40 were relatively more successful than those past this age. Many of those between the ages of 40 and 65 felt they were being prematurely forced out of the labor market.

Professor Miernyk found that of the workers who obtained new jobs 45 per cent found them through friends and relatives; 35.1 per cent got work by applying personally without help; 7.1 per cent found work via newspaper advertising; 6.6 per cent were placed by the United States Employment Service; and the remainder used other means.

INSURANCE BARRIERS. 

For a number of years, the practices of government, employers and insurance firms with respect to the insurance of employees in private industry have been cited as one of the major barriers to the employment of older workers. Among these insurance practices the primary target of criticism has been workmen's compensation legislation and related considerations. Some criticism has been directed toward certain "fringe benefit" group insurance programs for employees, but these difficulties seem considerably less important.

Workmen's Compensation Legislation. 

General Statutory Features. Under the workmen's compensation laws in effect in Massachusetts (General Laws, c. 152) and in other 

States, liabilities are imposed on employers when employees suffer injury or illness arising out of their employment. Statutory schedules of insurance benefits are prescribed which the employer (if he is a self-insurer) or his insurer must meet in settling employee claims. 

Statutory procedures provide for adjudication of claims, for various administrative aspects including an administrative board, for management of funds built up by employer premium payments and for rehabilitation of injured workers.

Insurance Premiums and Employer Beliefs. - The premium which an employer must pay for coverage under the workmen's compensation law is governed by three major factors: (1) frequency and severity of occupational illnesses, and injuries of his employees; (2) the benefits payable to employees; and (3) administrative costs to the insurer, including his profit. 

These things being so, it is to the employer's interest to pursue personnel policies which minimize illness and accident rates. Likewise, it is to his interest, as well as to the insurer's, to oppose liberalizations of the workmen's compensation laws which increase the number and scope of benefits which must be paid. 

Many employers believe that workers over 45 years of age constitute poor risks.

How come?

The answer is not simple. It stems in part from the fact that about 50 per cent of all men over forty-five have some coronary-artery disease "heart trouble", more or less. As a result, an appreciable percentage of industry in certain States believes it cannot afford to employ older workers.

Effect of Other Prior Conditions. -Fears of employers with re gard to the cardiac problems of older workers extend also to the aggravation of their other prior illnesses and injuries. 

Normally, if such aggravation is occupational and is covered by the terms of a state second-injury law, the employer has some protection. However, if this prior illness or injury was non-occupational, or is occupational in character but not covered by secondinjury statutes, the employer is unprotected against higher compensation costs. An older worker sustaining an industrial injury which aggravated a prior "uncovered" illness or injury may well become an expensive case under the workmen's compensation law, particularly if his recuperative powers are lessened by his age. 

Necessity of Proper Work Assignments. - If an employer is to hold down his workmen's compensation costs, it is most important that he assign workers to their tasks with due regard for their physical and mental abilities, as well as their technical capacities. 

Heart trouble is not confined to workers over 45, although job applicants in this age group are reported to bear the brunt of employer fears on this score. To the extent that older workers are victims of inadequate industrial safety measures and poor company personnel management, their employers are likely to gain the impression that these older workers are poor insurance risks.

Personnel Problems in hiring Older Workers. In filling positions the employer frequently looks for a man who can "grow with the company." Thus he seeks intelligent applicants who are willing to "work their way up the ladder" from apprentice positions to responsible posts. In this way, over a period of time and through a process of elimination and training, the employer hopes to offset losses of experienced key personnel in the middle and upper positions through death, retirement and other causes. Hence, the policy of hiring young applicants and holding them through promotional incentives, the prospect of higher pay, and the granting of fringe benefits conditional upon length of service.2 

Employer preferences for younger applicants are strengthened by psychological aspects. To maintain morale, workers in the lower echelon must know they can and will qualify for advancement to higher levels. Moreover, it is imperative that present employees of the firm be given preference over "outside" job applicants in filling vacancies in the higher echelons. 

In many instances, an "outside" older job applicant possesses the experience and technical qualifications for employment in a middle or higher level position, but the employer may fear that this applicant would be unhappy in a job on a lower level than that

He may also fear damage to the morale of his other employees if the older worker is placed in a supervisory or executive position which present employees feel should go to one of their number. In this situation, the employer may simply solve his problem by not hiring the older job applicants. 

In filling lower echelon jobs, the employer may find that the younger applicant, having less experience, can be hired at a lesser salary or wage than older applicants. While this is not an important consideration in a labor shortage situation, it can be important where there is unemployment. 

Job Specifications and Older Applicants.

Older job applicants may encounter further employment difficulties in the form of overly stringent, unrealistic job specifications. 

Minimum physical qualifications prescribed by the company for employment in a position may include requirements not really necessary in view of the duties to be performed. One authority has remarked that

In spite of the fact that it has amply been proved that most jobs can be performed effectively by persons with physical limitations, providing they can meet the specific physical demands of the job, some firms persist in giving general . . . (medical) . . . examinations that exclude from employment anyone with even a minor physical ailment. Since increasing age causes some deterioration of physical capacities, often of little significance in relation to specific job demands, such medical examinations deny to older workers an opportunity to work at jobs they are entirely capable of performing. Fortunately, the recent emphasis on the employment of the physically handicapped by selective placement methods has done much to eliminate the older practice of physical examinations without regard to the specific job to be performed. ¹ 1 Failure of an employer to review job specifications from time to time, in the light of changing duties and technological improvements, may result in the continuation of out-of-date minimum physical qualifications of an exacting nature, long after their modification has become possible.

The cost of providing a pension to a worker, if he is to receive a flat benefit or a benefit based on a percentage of his salary or wages during the period immediately preceding his retirement, will increase in proportion to his age. Under pension plans, workers may qualify for benefits under three types of provision normal, early, and disability retirement. Virtually every pension plan has minimum requirements which the worker must meet in order to qualify for these benefits. These are usually expressed in terms of an age which the worker must attain or an amount of credited service which he must have, or a combination of both. Labor Union Contracts. 

The seniority provisions of labor union contracts provide partial protection for older workers. With reference to those provisions, Dr. John W. McConnell has stated that 

Labor unions have a deep concern for the job security of their older members.

Rejection of Higher Retirement Age Limit. 

The state's policies with respect to its own personnel over 40 years of age have been the subject of continuing criticism. Governor Herter recommended therefore to the General Court in 1954 (House, No. 2479) that the compulsory retirement age of 70 years for state employees in non-hazardous occupations be extended to 72 years. The present requirements of the retirement law relative to the compulsory retirement of public employees was recently summarized by the Executive Secretary of the State Retirement Board as follows: 

No person who has attained age 65 may commence employment in a Group B position, that is, as a member of a police or fire department, as a Registry of Motor Vehicles employee having police powers, as a conservation officer having police powers or in a position which requires the employee to have the care, custody, instruction or supervision over insane persons, prisoners or defective delinquents.

At the same time the law requires that any person who is engaged by the Commonwealth or any of its subdivisions in such a position must terminate his employment on the last day of the month in which he attains age 65.

Any employee of the Commonwealth classified in Group A, that is, a nonhazardous position, must terminate his employment on the last day of the month in which he attains age 70 and no person may commence employment after that date.

The State Police have a law entirely unto themselves which requires that they shall terminate on attaining age 50.

SECTION 73. There shall be a board, to be known as the council for the aging, hereinafter called the council, to consist of the commissioner of education, the commissioner of mental health, the commissioner of public health, the commissioner of public welfare, the commissioner of labor and industries, or their respective representatives, and four members to be appointed by the governor, with the advice and consent of the council.
