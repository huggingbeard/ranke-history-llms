MORGAN COMPANY 

Closely interwoven with the history of Oshkosh is the firm of Morgan Company. It was only two years after the City was incorporated that the Morgan family began operating a factory at Oshkosh. All the trials, tribulations, achievements, discouragements and successes of our City were identical to that of the Morgan Company. Prosperity for Oshkosh meant good times for Morgan Company and vice versa. 

In 1855, two brothers, John R. and Richard T. Morgan located in Oshkosh and immediately obtained employment in one of the local sash and door factories. Later in that year the Morgan brothers with other employees bought out the plant, which represented some real work and sacrifice on their part. This was the forerunner of the present business. These two brothers brought up their families in Oshkosh and by 1855 the second generation was represented in the business by three cousins, Thomas R., Albert T., and J. Earl Morgan. On February eleventh, 1889, they bought out the business of Carlton Foster & Co. which was located on the site of the present plant, and the firm name of Morgan Co. was adopted.

The third generation is represented by U. Morgan Davies, a grandson of John R. Morgan and who has been connected with the company since 1912. Aside from the Oshkosh plant, warehouses are located in Chicago and Baltimore. Morgan Co. is proud to have grown up with this beautiful city. The thrifty pioneer sought to get the most for his hard earned cash, and this company has always strived to give value received. Meet the 

Author WILLIAM DAWES 

Mr. Dawes was born in Oshkosh on June 12, 1878, and has always taken a keen interest and pride in the development of this City. Some folks "watch" a city grow, while he helped to "build" it.

At the age of 8, he assisted his brother at a saw mill, piling lath. 

In those days they were air dried before being tied in bundles. When he became 11, he had to leave school and worked in a factory, at this time. wages were five cents per hour. Later he was local salesman for a wholesale grocer for two years. His education was obtained in Home study, night sessions at a Business College and graduated from the University of Wisconsin in 10 Correspondence Study courses. He also graduated from the Oshkosh School of Expression and for many years he delighted audiences in this and neighboring cities with his Readings, which comprised the choicest in Poetry and Prose. 

In 1900, he ranked highest on the eligible list and was selected for a Postal Clerk and for 25 years served in the Stamp and Registry division. He took an intense interest in this service. He published two books on the Postal Service, also wrote a number of articles and gave addresses on the Postal and Civil Service and he served under 4 Postmasters.

Friends who have been accustomed to buying stamps from him for many years, frequently called him "Abraham Lincoln" as he bears quite a strong likeness to the emancipation president and does not take unkindly to the reference.

He resigned from this position in 1925, to become a partner in the Oshkosh Millwork Co., first as salesman, then in charge of advertising and superintendent. Since May, 1930, he conducted the Wm. Dawes 

Lumber Co., and also erected about 50 homes, for people of moderate means, which he sold on the monthly payment plan. 

Thus in many practical ways, he has made his influence felt in Oshkosh, as a force for the good and the advancement of those things that make a city a better place in which to live.

Mr. Dawes is a lover of good books and has an exceptionally fine library of his own. He still adheres to the rule that to enjoy life you must enjoy work. -2-

THE HISTORY OF OSHKOSH 

There is probably no city in this country of ours, with such a colorful historical background as obtains in the 

City of Oshkosh. Once a wilderness, with only a sprinkling of wigwams, has been transformed into a great city teeming with all the arts, and industries known to the grandest era of the world's history.

Nature spared nothing to make this section of the country a most beautiful spot, and endowed it lavishly with the things necessary to make a living. 

The abundance of game, fish and fur-bearing animals, the wild rice. which grew luxuriantly in the shallow portion of these waters, the rich soil, and its facilities for canoe travel made it especially attractive for the none too ambitious Indians. Up to the time that white settlers arrived in 1836, the present site of our attractive City was trod only by the moccasined feet of the 

Aboriginal Indians. A race of wonderfull physical endurance, they yet lived. a stationary life, and their only goal and object was the enjoyment of the forest and the stream. They had reason to rejoice, having found the most. beautiful and livable spot in the northwest, for with fish and game a plenty he was quite contented, these he cooked over fires that sent wreiths of smoke curling above wigwam tips, cultivating the soil or building homes, and the many other things we fret about did not worry them in the least. 

The history of this section of our country is intensely interesting because of its bearing on the development of our nation. For it was only fourteen years after the landing of the Pil-. grims at Plymouth Rock, that in July of 1634 Jean Nicolet passed through 

Oshkosh and up the Fox River as far as Berlin, which was then an Indian village.

He came at the behest of Governor 

Champlain of Canada, who heard, and had dreams of a short route to China, and the riches of the Orient. He believed that it lay just beyond the

Great Lakes. In this he was greatly disappointed for Nicolet found only

Indians and an undeveloped country. 

However, he marvelled at the potential possibilities of his explorations from Green Bay to Berlin, for his glowing reports on his return soon brought other missionaries, explorers and fur-traders. Jean Nicolet was the first white man to see Wisconsin and accomplished much in persuading the Indians that the white man was their friend, that he wanted their furs and to trade with them. He paved the way for future white visitors, and was followed by Father Claude Allouez, who in 1668 founded a mission at Fort

Howard and another near De Pere in 1669.

These were built of reeds and bark, and were mere huts. On April 20, 1670, he landed at our Menominee Park and a little later established another mission at Berlin, Wis. He was enraptured with the lovely inland sea of Lake 

Winnebago. It's beautiful surroundings, and it's many advantages for convenient and profitable trading posts. 

In June 1673, Father Jacques Marquette took charge of the missionary work, and with him was an explorer by the name of Louis Joliet. 

They traveled up the Fox River then the Wisconsin River and on July 

17, 1673, they discovered the "Father of waters" the Mississippi. They paddled south as far as the Arkansas River, then returned by the same route and were back in De Pere in September of this same year.

These were soon followed by other 

French explorers. Next came the fur traders and before many years this 

Fox River Valley and farther up to the 

Mississippi River became an attractive place, for the explorers sent glowing descriptions of the opportunities prevailing here for trading and especially in furs and pelts. In later years this fur trade developed into one of great commercial magnitude. This location being accessible by water from the 

French settlements in Quebec and surrounding country, was the reason it was developed more than one hundred years before Chicago and other inland. sections were tapped, for water routes were the only means of transportation at that time. Up to the year 1600 the Sioux

Indians were the sole occupants of this region. They were succeeded by the 

Chippewas, the Potawanomies and later the Menominees and Winnebagoes.

Indians were the last of the Mohicans. The site that is now Oshkosh became the favorite stopping place of the early explorers, who were attracted by the beauty and commercial advantages of this locality and the delightful and fertile country adjoining now known as Winnebago County. The transi tional period that followed was struggle between two races. Here was a paradise for Indian life, his choicest hunting grounds, its waters teeming with fish and its woods and rich prairies filled with game; where his mode of barbaric life required little effort to subsist. But now they were confronted by the incroachment of civilized life the French traders-for they were daring adventurers and very energetic, and consequently the Indians relinquished with greatest reluctance.

A severe clash was recorded to have taken place at Butte des Morts (Hill of the Dead) hundreds of Indians were slain in this contest and were buried on the spot. The fur traders did not worry the Indians so much as the miners and agricultural settlers. This changed the entire situation as far as the Indians were concerned. For they saw more of their lands taken each year by immigrants who showed no signs to loosen their grip. These activities were bound to interfere with the hunting grounds of the natives and the Indians began to show a disposition to be hostile and conflicts became more numerous. 

The early "Trading Post", was correctly named, for here the white men offered the red men such articles as beads, colored calicoes, felts, guns, powder, axes, knives and hooks and lines, salt, sugar and whiskey, in exchange for the furs of the bear, deer, beaver, marten, otter, fox and many others. No money was used, the measure of value was the beaver skin for many years.

These furs and pelts were then taken to Green Bay and Mackinac to be sold to some large Fur Company. John

Jacob Astor had a large interest in this business which grew to enormous proportions. He had a representative here as early as 1818. 

Green Bay is well known not only as one of the oldest cities in our state but also as one of the oldest cities in our country. For it was used as a Fur 

Trading station and as a center of missionary efforts by the Jesuits, as far back as 1660, although the first French settlement is usually given as occuring in 1745.

Indians once occupied the Island in 

Lake Winnebago seven miles north of 

Oshkosh which they named Garlic Island. About one thousand Indians comprised this tribe and part of them lived on the mainland opposite the island. Chief Wild Cat was in charge and this was about 1823 to 1828. 

Four Legs was another very impor tant Chief of the Winnebagoes. His village was on Doty Island and the huts were built of bark. From 1800 to 1846 

Chief Black Wolf occupied the land seven miles south of Oshkosh, known as Black Wolf Point. He was a very large man with a dark, ugly face and with his mass of thick black hair, he looked fierce. He had more than forty huts in this village. 

In 1852, most of the Indians were removed to their new domain-the 

Menominee Indian Reservation at 

Keshena. This is seventy miles north of Oshkosh and comprises 276,480 acres or 432 square miles of timber land.

The first settlers in this vicinity were Augustin Grignon (pronounced 

Gree-no) and James Porlier, who established a trading post in 1818 near the head of Lake Butte des Morts, also 

Robert Grignon nephew of Augustin, who acted in the capacity of Government agent. A few years later, Robert

Grignon came to Algoma and estab lished a trading post there. He was the first settler on territory now comprised within the limits of Oshkosh.

Mr. L. B. Porlier, Mr. Peter Powell and his son Captain William Powell were the next settlers in our near vicinity. However, it was not until 

September 3, 1836, that the day of civilization dawned in this locality, for on that date a Treaty was made with the Menominee Indians by Governor 

Dodge acting as U. S. commissioner which resulted in the cession to the United States of about 4,000,000 acres of land lying north of the Fox River and bounded on the west by the Wolf River and as far north as the Menominee River.

This treaty is known as the "Treaty of the Cedars" and was signed by 

Henry Dodge, Territorial Governor, and Oshkosh, the Menominee Head 

Chief. Cedars, refers to the locality, Cedar Point or Cedar Rapids as the place was then called, the site has a granite marker and is located on Highway 41 opposite the village of Kimberly. In this treaty the name of the 

Menominee Chief is spelled "Oskosh".

The governor, while on his return home from the treaty-council was taken across the river by Webster 

Stanley who operated the Ferry, beyond West Algoma, whom he informed of his result. In August of that year Mr. Stanley was joined here by 

Chester Gallup his father-in-law who brought with him his three sons, Henry A., Amos and John P., all from Ohio.

Immediately all of them made their Homestead Claims, for this Government land. Mr. Gallup occupied the point on the north side of the mouth of the river, between the river and the lake embracing 170 acres. 

The Stanley family procured the tract just west, along the river to Main Street consisting of 117 acres. They lost no time in erecting thereon one house which was occupied by November 1. 1836. 

The first winter these two families lived together in this small house the size of which was sixteen by sixteen and a half feet, a mere shanty which had been used by Webster Stanley while he operated the West Algoma ferry. This he took down and with this lumber he built a house on his own claim of land. This was the first house in Oshkosh. The two families lived in this house of Mr. Stanley until the following November. Mr. Gallup then built a log house on his own claim. The nearest white settlers were at Neenah and Fond du Lac, but the nearest place to get food, tools and clothing was Green Bay.

No roads led to this isolated settlement, for the only highway in this city was the Indian trail entering from the north to Stanley's Ferry about where the Riverside cemetery is now, thence along Algoma Street to Lake Winnebago. 

When Webster Stanely moved from 

West Algoma to his own claim, the ferry was taken over by James Knaggs, a French Indian who came from Butte des Morts where he had a ferry and trading post.

Mr. Stanley then began operating a ferry at the foot of Broad Street about where the C. & N. W. bridge now stands, but a few years later moved it to Ferry Street. Mr. Stanley dug the first well in Oshkosh. 

The land to which these settlers laid claim is now occupied by a number of palatial residences, George Nevitt, Jr., Morgan Davies, P. A. Richardson, Erwin Spoo, Phil H. Sawyer and

Thompson's Point (with the lighthouse). In the spring of 1837, George

Wright and family located a claim west of the Stanley-Gallup tract consisting of 154 acres. The next settler was David 

David Evans followed shortly thereafter by his brother Thomas in 1838. To these gentlemen belongs the honor of being the founders of this fair city of ours, for the future City of

Oshkosh now had its permanent residents.

Henry Gallup wrote some very interesting recollections of his first visit here. It seems they were trying to decide where to settle. In that day, the country from Oshkosh to Neenah belonged to the Menominee Indians. 

From Oshkosh south to Fond du Lac and then around to Calumet belonged to the Government. From Calumet north for six miles was the Brothertown Indian Reservation, and the next six miles north belonged to the Stockbridge Indians. The Government owned the land from there around to Menasha. 

So he decided to make a trip completely around the lake to look over the available sites. Seven days were required to make this trip and on that trip he encountered only one white family. It was the family of Colwert 

Pier who had just built a log cabin on 

Fond du Lac Creek. When they got back they summed up the situation in this way-between Oshkosh and Green 

Bay, a distance of fifty miles, there were but three white families. There was but one between Oshkosh and 

Portage, and Mr. Pier's family was the only family between Oshkosh and 

Sheboygan or Milwaukee. The Gallup's were better pleased with Oshkosh than with any place they had seen. 

In the earliest years of the settlement of Oshkosh, it was necessary to go to Green Bay for all grocery and dry goods supplies. In winter one could take the dangerous route of crossing the lake to the east shore and striking inland to the Indian trail to the north. In summer the water route was quicker but more dangerous, due to the rapids in the river, for the drop from Oshkosh to Green Bay is 160 feet. Oshkosh is 760 feet above sea level while Green Bay is only 600 feet. 

Since then this danger of shooting the rapids is obviated by the Gomernment's installation of seventeen locks and dams. At that time the travel was usually by foot over the winding Indian Trail. Two neighbors would go together with knapsacks strapped to their backs, empty except for a lunch.

The settlers who needed supplies would give their orders and money to the ones making the trip, who would then take their leave for the journey, a round trip of about 120 miles. It usually took four days to make the round. trip and buy the supplies. On the footsore and tiresome return trip, the knapsacks would be crammed full. 

When the travellers arrived home, the neighbors would assemble to get their provisions and to hear any news of the outside world and also to get the mail, because that was long before there was any Post Office at Oshkosh. 

Mr. Wright also tells us of the first 

Sunday School in Oshkosh and Winnebago County. It was in 1844 he called together five boys and one girl.

The school was held on a fallen log in the woods near where Algoma Street now runs. A few hymns were sung. 

Mr. Wright read a lesson from the

Bible and explained it as best he could, a prayer was offered and then the school was dismissed. 

In these early days entertainments were exceedingly meagre for the early settlers, and their pleasures were simple. After a hard day of toil they would gather at some home and sing or dance by candle-light each would bring a candle. On Sunday they would listen to some traveling preacher. 

The Indian mode of living required but little effort for subsistance, for they were satisfied to go down to the river or lake and catch fish, or go into the woods and shoot a deer. While the white man was not accustomed to such a diet. He required wheat and corn, therefore the early settlers would raise their own grain and the Indians would steal most of it. Then it was necessary to grind it to flour and the nearest flour mill was at Kaukauna. 

Strange to say it was easier to get to the mill in the winter than in the summer, because it was possible to travel on the ice. W. W. Wright one of the Oshkosh Pioneers, recalls making a trip to the Kaukauna mill on a cold 

January day walking into the teeth of a severe northeast wind. It was necessary to keep running and whaling the oxen to keep from freezing to death. The whole day was consumed in reaching the mill only to discover it was frozen up and no grinding could be done. After spending the night at a half-breeds' hut they journeyed on to the next mill at Green Bay. -* 11 ..

After a few years, a grist mill was built at Brothertown. Many of the early settlers used small coffee grinders to grind their own grain. Flour at this time cost $20. a barrel. In 1848 a grist mill was built in Algoma. 

During all this time, the tract of land lying north of the mouth of the river was named Athens and goods were so marked at Green Bay destined for this place. While the settlement on the south side of the river was called 

Brooklyn. But at a meeting of the inhabitants, called for the purpose of choosing a new name for this new settlement held in the kitchen of the house of George Wright, in the winter of 1839-40, it was decided that this place should be known thereafter and forever as "Oshkosh". The names proposed at this meeting were. Athens, Fairview, Osceola, Stanford and Oshkosh. The latter won the day and was chosen in honor of Chief Oshkosh, the Menominee Indian. The original orthography of the name was Oskosh, but by some mischance it was changed to Oshkosh. 

The selection of this name was probably influenced by the fact that the meeting was attended by many of the traders who lived up the river all the way to Butte de Morts including the 

French and Half-breeds. 

These folks were greatly interested in keeping the good will of the Indians with whom they did business and they insisted that it be named after the great Chief, who ruled over this territory. After several ballots, the name 

Oshkosh won. Oshkosh was Head-Chief of the 

Menominee tribe. He was born in 1795 and died in Keshena, Wisconsin, on 

August 20, 1858 as a result of a drunken brawl. He became a warrior when seventeen years of age. He was a brave man, was medium in size and had a lot of good sense, but strong drink (fire water as the Indians called it) often got the best of him and two of his three sons surpassed him in that beastly vice. Chief Oshkosh is now buried in the land he once governed. in beautiful Menominee Park in the beautiful City of Oshkosh where the name of this renowned Chief is very properly perpetuated. His grave is marked by a large monument of an Indian facing Lake Winnebago. He was a familiar figure to the early settler of this region and when the Menominees were sent to the Menominee Indian 

Reservation with headquarters at Keshena, Wis., he paid frequent visits to his old home, and he was always given a hearty welcome. His descendents are still the nominal heads of the Menominee people on their Reservation.

Chief Oshkosh was once tried on a murder charge, a mural painting of the trial is hung in the Supreme Court room in the Wisconsin State Capitol at 

Madison, Wisconsin. The date of the scene in June 14, 1830.

The trial of Chief Oshkosh was considered important in Wisconsin history, for it established a precedent in Chief handling early Indian affairs.

While the Indians had no religion, they did however recognize a superior being who knows all things and governs all the world. He is called the

Our early settlers had unusual experiences in watching the adventures and primitive hardships of this strange and rapidly receding race. They knew nothing of modern conveniences neither had they a desire for them.

With him came the intensive cultivation of land, the rush and roar of the railroad, the throb and hum of the manufacturing establishments, the crush of a new race with higher ideals and nobler aspirations than their predecessors. With the advent of the white race the age of a stationary life was a thing of the past. 

This glowing age of steam spread its conquest far and wide, and has opened up the broad empire of the west to that wave of immigration. which rolled ceaselessly across the country, peopling its remotest solitudes with a race that took permanent possession and looked forward for improvements and advancement.

Comparatively few, indeed, are the survivors of the sturdy Indian race today, only 11,548 in Wisconsin and a total of 332,397 in the U. S. according to the census of 1930. 

The Indians disappeared from this vicinity about 1870, for at that time the government took steps to confine them to Reservations in the State. Here the government furnished them with 

Homes, Schools, Hospitals, etc.besides, they were given cash payments annually, so they could live without working as they were accustomed to. All this was done in compliance with the Treaty of 1836. 

In this way the Indians were compensated for relinquishing their lands, and, let it be said also that the Indians have always been loyal to all with whom they have made treaties. 

The Menominees and Winnebagoes were the last tribes to inhabit this vicinity, and future generations will only know what history preserves of this curious and mythic race. "Old times are changed, old manners gone- 

A stranger fills the Red Man's throne".

For now the Indian wigwam and the pioneers log cabin have been supplanted by comfortable homes and humming factories and for a while at least, we were considered the commercial center of the West.

In 1850, a stage line was put into operation from Oshkosh to Fort Winnebago now Portage.

Oshkosh was granted a city charter on April 5, 1853 and Edward Eastman was elected its first Mayor. The first 

Council meeting was in a room at the home of Mayor Eastman, or one of the six Alderman (two from each ward) at seven o'clock on a Monday evening 

April 11, 1853, On May second a room was rented in the Marks block for sixty dollars a year, and furniture was purchased for $39.50.

The Marks block was a two story building at the south east corner of Main and Ceape Streets where the Standard Oil Co. now has a filling station.

What is now West Algoma was then a separate village known as "Algoma", and did not become a part of Oshkosh until 1856. 

That the German speaking population was considerable at that time is in evidence from a petition submitted to the council asking that the council proceedings be printed in German, the request however was not granted. 

The Fox River Valley in the early days was no place for lazy people, or those lacking initiative. In fact there was opportunity only for those who were willing to work hard for the final rewards the slow accumulation of wealth thru tilling of the soil, the products of the forests, and the gradual building up of industry. Pioneers in this region were of necesssity individuals of many accomplishments "jacks of all trades" they were not only farmers but also stone masons, carpenters, blacksmiths, leather workers, tanners, butter and cheese makers, etc. and it took a number of years before these trades became specialized.

In 1839, Conrad J. Coon built the first sawmill in Algoma, the same year the first Trading Post of general merchandise was established there also, and Mr. Knaggs, a French Indian, began the operation of a Ferry between 

Riverside cemetery and Coon's Point.

Later it was moved nearer the location of the present West Algoma bridge. 

In 1840 a Post Office was established and J. P. Gallup appointed Postmaster. The first mail from this place was done up in a piece of brown paper, and carried by Chester Ford, mail contractor, in his vest pocket whose route was semi-monthly from Wrightstown to Fond du Lac on an Indian Trail. -✰ 15 }-

Progress was sadly interrupted however, by the great fire of May 10, 1859.

In this fire Oshkosh was practically wiped out of existence. But the courage and energy of the population. proved equal to the emergency, and in six months nearly the entire burned district was rebuilt with a better class of buildings.

Of course, the unprecendented prosperity that our industry had enjoyed up to this time, was certain to be dimmed by the disappearance of our nearby forests and the vanishing of our mills. Our splendid water transportation was no longer useful for it was displaced by the Railroad and later by 

Trucking. Although our development slowed up, it did however continue steadily, slowly, but surely, which after all, is best to build and maintain a substantial community. Of this situation we are justly proud, for our record as a Home-owners town is the envy of many, we do not boast of "booms" neither do we despond in "slumps". True, the saw mill has disappeared forever, but we have diversified our Industry and our heads are still above the water.

Although at times it seemed to our advantage to give this country back to the Indians, yet if we consider that our city was destroyed by fire three times, and the good citizens did not falter, but built it up again, we too should be resolute and forget about the high cost of living, and the still higher taxes, and make the most of it. 

We should remember that we still have a beautiful city, our shaded streets, our parks, lakes and summer resorts, our good fishing is known the country over. Besides, we are situated in the midst of a rich agricultural section. Surely, Oshkosh presents a wonderful combination for business and pleasure. We have endeavored to depict settlement, pioneer life and the social and industrial development of Oshkosh and vicinity, from the time. that Old Chief Oshkosh lived in a wigwam and traveled in a birch bark canoe to the present mode of living, and traveling a faster pace, and yet in a more comfortable way.

The log hut of the sturdy pioneer trapper and hunter, long since faded before the palatial home of the busy manufacturer and the wide-a-wake merchant.

The forests which densely covered these picturesque acres have fallen, to make room for fields of corn, comfortable homes and humming factories. In- 

Idian trails have been broadened into paved streets and smooth walks, in place of the ox team we see the locomotive, the tractor and the automobile. 

The blazing pine knot in the Indian's smoking teepee has been displaced by the even glow of the electric lamp in stores and homes.

The monotonous sound of the tom tom is gone, but the cathedral organ and the radio are more pleasing. The 

Menominees and Winnebagoes depart ed to make room for 41,000 busy and happy people, for we are surrounded with all the conveniences and improvements known to modern civilization. 

Indeed it is interesting to study a compilation of municipal statistics, and contemplate the public conveniences and improvements that now cover the territory which, but comparatively a few years ago, abounded in forests and 

Indian wigwams. It should be reassuring that the destiny of Oshkosh is positive and certain, and it is safe to say that the progress will be still more substantial, for we have survived the period of groping for something to replace the lumbering of former years.

In conclusion, the writer pauses to soliloquize "what of the Future", one course would be to obey orders of the great Admiral sailing on uncharted sea, seeking an undiscovered country: 

What shall we say, brave Admiral say, If we sight naught but seas at dawn? "Why, you shall say at break of day: 

Sail on, Sail on, Sail on, and on."

Or, shall we say, "I'se regusted and give it back to the Indians."

In conversation with Reginald Oshkosh, a few years ago, I asked him what the Indians would say if we should offer to return this land to them. He replied: "Your offer to give back to us the land we once occupied would be rejected." It is no longer fit for our mode of living. You have destroyed the forests, and with it the 

Deer, the Buffalo and Bears, we no longer could eat venison or trade Furs.

Your conservation laws have done away with Fish and Game. There isn't a log left to make a canoe, nor a Skin to make a Wigwam. 

Your Roads, are too smooth, your 

Laws are too rough. You have built dams, and now much land is under water. "Chief Don't want it B'Gosh." 

However, our mode of living is quite different, and yet our Lake and River are as beautiful as ever, and the fishing that lured the Indians is still excellent in these waters. Nature was lavish in supplying this vicinity with splendid opportunities for rest, relaxation and enjoyment and this heritage is unspoiled today.

Man has added to these modern modes of travel, and many other conveniences to make life more enjoyable.

All these in addition to the things that pleased the prehistoric and the red. man of days gone by. What we did was designed to fashion it to our habits. 

Outstanding features that should keep 

Oshkosh Growing!

Its favorable location for shipping, should atract industries to locate here. 

The fact that 175,000 people reside within a radius of forty miles, should be interesting to merchants. We have an unusual number of Home owners in Oshkosh, 6,915 of them, or 67.2%.

This speaks for stability, and which means much to the employer of labor, for a shifting population is unreliable. 

Encouraging and supporting home industry, keeps the wage earner employed.

Oshkosh citizens realize the advantages to be secured by the thorough practice of "trading at home", such a course of conduct requires the inteligent co-operation of the merchants as well as the buyers. Fortunately, we have had a generous observance of this ideal. For Oshkosh boasts many progressive business houses, where service to the buyer, is as much the keynote as profits to the seller. 

Of course this policy exists, because, in the long run, business men know that it is an adjunct to profit-making and City building. Our local buyers as a rule do patronize local businesses, and we are convinced in the majority of instances with pleasure and profit. 

Because of it, we have developed this city from a wooded glade, to the metropolis of the Fox River Valley. "Optimism" has been our guiding star, and "backbone" the means of getting there.

Since lumbering disappeared, and woodworking industry diminished, we have attracted a wide diversity of industry, and consequently have experienced continual growth, in population, size, manufacturing and retailing.

Since 1871 Oshkosh had the reputation of making more sash and doors. than any other city in the world. This reputation probably still holds, although the Paine Lumber Co. has not operated since 1932. However, this plant has reopened in a small way and it is confidently expected that Oshkosh will again be supreme in this field, this mill would produce 15,000 Doors in one day, and was the largest, Sash and 

Door plant in the world. 

Ever mounting freight rates, are a serious problem to Our industry, especially in lumber and millwork manufacturing, as lumber must be brought here from the far south or the western coast. In some products it can be partially offset by trucking, but not so in our main line of endeavor, the woodworking industry, therefore, our course lies in diversifying our industries to a greater extent than we have heretofore. -27.

THE WISCONSIN MATCH CORPORATION 

The people of Oshkosh are justly proud of this manufacturing establishment. It originated away back, in 1862 under the firm name of the Star Match

Works, J. L. Clark, Proprietor. 

For some time, its power was furnished by a large horse traveling in at tread machine, which in reality was a machine of one horse power. At this time, matches had sulphur tips, and one good whiff almost gassed one into unconsciousness.

In 1871 it produced hand made matches to the value of $90,000, had one hundred employees, about one-half of whom were children from ten to fifteen years of age. In 1881 it produced 6,510,240 boxes of matches. That year the plant was sold to the Diamond 

Match Co., and George W. Gates became the manager. In 1884 the output amounted to $180,000 with 250 hands employed. Since then it has been enlarged and modernized. The company has been reorganized and is now the 

Wisconsin Match Corporation. 

Modern chemistry has done much for the Match industry, for it has eliminated the old fashioned poisonous match which was so dangerous to the health of the employees. No expense was spared by this company to adopt all scientific means that make for the comfort and the health of the workers.

Today it is considered the largest and most up-to-date match plant in the world.

J. L. CLARK MFG. COMPANY The J. L. Clark Manufacturing Com pany is located at the corner of High Street and Forest Avenue and is engaged in the manufacture of heavyduty cabs. Its product is standard. equipment with many of the largest truck companies in the Middle-west and its products are used chiefly on trucks operating oil fields, strip coal mining, public utilities and highway maintainance work. Originally known as the J. L. Clark Carriage Works, it was incorporated in 1881 by Mr. J. L. 

Clark. It became one of the largest carriage factories in the country manufacturing spring wagons, buggies, carriages and cutters. 

In 1885 Mr. Clark's son, H. M.

Clark, became the active manager, and since 1922, Carlton H. Foster has been

General Manager of the company. The company normally employs from 100 to 125 men and the main factory building has a total floor space of 125,000 square feet. J. I. CASE COMPANY 

The J. I. Case Co., has been represented in Oshkosh for a great many years. In 1872 Fletcher and Everitt were the dealers here. At our County Fair in 1880, a special feature was the grand cavalcade of premium stock, headed by J. I. CHRONOLOGICAL HISTORY OF OSHKOSH 

1634 Jean Nicolet, explorer, was the first white person to see Wisconsin, and is believed to have passed through here, and went as far as Fort Winnebago now Portage. 1665 

Father Claude Allouez, took charge of missionary work here. 

1673-Father Jacques Marquette and

Louis Joliet, passed through Oshkosh, on their way up the Fox for exploration, and on July 17, of this year discovered the Mississippi River. 

1686 Father Marquette stopped at 

Oshkosh on his return trip, and was amazed at our beautiful surroundings and potential trading posts. 

1795-Chief Oshkosh was born, in "Old King's Village," one half mile up the Fox River from Green Bay, Old 

King was his grandfather. 

1818-August Grignon pronounced (Gree-noh) and James Porlier, established a trading post at Butte des 

Morts, the first settlers in this vicinity. 

1836 All land in this location was ceded by the Indians to the U. S. Webster Stanley built the first house in 

Oshkosh, a log cabin. 

1837-Two more here. families settled Miss 

1838 Joseph Jackson and 

Emeline Wright, subjects of the first marriage. First white child born, George W. Stanley on August 26. 

First white girl born, Elizabeth daughter of Chester Ford. 

1839-J. C. Coon erected the first saw mill in Algoma. Name of "Oshkosh" given to this city, up to this time the South side of the river was known as Brooklyn, and the north side was Athens. 

1840 First steam saw mill erected, by Morris Firman, at the south end of 

Mill Street. First Postoffice estab lished. July 4, first celebration of Independence Day consisted mainly of shooting, eating and speech making. 

1842-Winnebago County organized. 

1843 First store opened by J. H. Osborne. 

1844 First steamboat in these waters. First frame house erected now it is 1134 Osborn Ave. 

1846First census showed 782 per sons in Winnebago County. First school in Oshkosh, foot of Washington Street. First Hotel. 

1847-A traveler had this to say of 

Oshkosh on September 28: "It is a place of not much business, no water power, no mills. They have got four little stores without anything in them, $1000 ought to buy them with buildings." 

1848 First grist mill built. The price of Pine Lumber was $8 per 1000 feet, two thirds to be absolutely clear. 

First plank sidewalk built, this type of walk was in vogue for many years, for it was the cheapest material at hand. 

The regulation walk was 5 feet 4 inches wide the reason for this was that most lumber was cut in 16 foot lengths, therefore, one full length would make 3 pieces 5 foot 4 inches. without any waste. In extreme cold weather, these these walks would play pranks, the frost would pull the spikes and in the still of night it would go bang, a sure reminder that it was below zero, and folks would get up and put another chunk of wood in the stove. The "plank" sidewalk was followed by "brick" then "tar Macadam". 

In the summer of 1910, "Cement" walks were being laid on Main Street, to replace old and uneven walks of tar macadam. 

1849-A saw mill erected at Butte des Morts. 

1850 First Stage Coach started be tween Oshkosh, Fond du Lac and 

Grand Rapids (now Wis. Rapids). St.

Peter's Church erected, a very small church. Ferry Street paved with plank after much hard labor removing tree stumps.

Oshkosh was the pioneer in the Grass Rug industry, which grew to immense proportions. For many years, this product of the upriver marshes which was wholly unfit for food for cattle was utilized in the most unique industry. The grass rug period was from 

1900 to 1930. Its discontinuance was due to the keen competition from Japan. It has almost entirely been displaced by the new 

Fibre rug which is more beautiful and cleanly and without the odor of the marsh grass rug. Although the grass rug is still preferred for porches because of its durability for outdoors. 

In 1912 this company built an immense warehouse, in size it was 214 x 109 feet, and was named the Bull Moose building, for it was in this building on October 11, 1912, that Theodore Roosevelt addressed a crowd of 11,000 people. The building had just been completed and Emil Steiger donated the use of it for this gala event. The grass rug on which he stood while making his address was sent to Mr. Roosevelt's home at Oyster Bay, New York. Mr. 

Roosevelt was delighted with Oshkosh and said it was a "bully" city.

This company was established in 1900, under the name of Oshkosh Grass Matting Co. Leander Choate was its President and Emil 

H. Steiger was the manager. In 1915 the company was reorganized, and the name changed to Deltox Rug Co. That this company has prospered, and Oshkosh has profited, is evidenced by the fact that this company now employs 250 men with an annual payroll of $300,000.00. -44-

Years of Continuous Operation 

It was in the year 1863. Lincoln was president, and to most people this appeared as the most improbable time to establish a business. Not so with the 

Challoner family. For in that year they began a business in Oshkosh, Wisconsin, that has been continuously operated since that year, and in 1938 celebrates its 75th anniversary. Today it is known as the Giant 

Grip Mfg. Co.

During the entire 75 years only two families have operated the plant. 

First the Challoner family, who started with a shingle machine line, followed by the Everhart family, the present owners, who continued with the shingle machine line and acquired and developed the now famous "Giant Grip" drive calk horseshoe line. The first drive calks made in this country were manufactured under the "Giant Grip" brand. 

In more recent years this company has developed the Giant Grip Rubber Heel Calks-New Oshkosh finished horseshoes Traction equipment for trucks-Giant Grip steel and rubber pitching horseshoes and many other forged products.

Yes, 75 years is a long time to be in business. This country has passed through several depression periods, wars, and business booms. The Giant 

Grip Mfg. Co. record is unique in that only two families have owned and operated the business during three-quarters of a century.

SIMON HORWITZ 

Although young in years Simon Horwitz has already won an enviable position in legal circles in Oshkosh. He is a son of Philip and Gertrude (Cohen) Horwitz, with whom he came to this city from Russia about thirty years ago. They have four children, one of whom, Dr. Harry Horwitz, is an eminent pediatrician in Oakland, Calif. 

Having completed his preliminary studies he matriculated in the University of Wisconsin from which he obtained the degree of Bachelor of Laws in 1927 and immediately after his graduation returned to Oshkosh. He then entered the office of Frank 

B. Keefe, with whom he had been previously associated during school vacations and during the years 1929 through 1932 he was an Assistant District Attorney of Winnebago County under Mr. Keefe. As such Assistant District Attorney he had taken an active part in the investigation of certain County Officials charged with misconduct in office and had proven himself to be an able, honest and fearless public prosecutor, discharging his important duties in a manner that has won him high commendation. From 1929 through 1935 he was a member of the law firm of Barber, Keefe, Patri and Horwitz. 

His mind is analytical and logical in its trend and his arguments are forceful and convincing.

He belongs to the Benevolent Protective Order of Elks and has held the office of Secretary and President of the Oshkosh Lodge of the B'nai B'rith and is a member of the National Executive Committee of the Jewish National Fund. He is also a member of the Winnebago County, Wisconsin State, and American Bar Associations, as well as the National Lawyer's Guild.

Studious, industrious and capable, Mr. Horwitz has risen rapidly in his profession and his strength of character and genuine worth has gained for him many friends in Oshkosh where he has resided since 1910. 

Since February, 1936, Mr. Horwitz has successfully engaged in the general practice of law, conducting his own private office at Oshkosh. -54

S. J. WITTMAN 

S. J. Wittman, and "Chief Oshkosh" the smallest of his racing planes, have brought considerable fame to the city of Oshkosh. He has flown to many parts of the country, and participated in many air races, and with marked success. "Witty" was asked to describe the sensation of traveling 300 miles an hour in a plane. He said, the speed is not noticeable at high altitudes, but assumes a sensational aspect when zooming along just over the tree tops, as is necessary in speed racing or record flying. Wittman is a modest man. He said that he is much more at home in the air than anywhere else. 

In Oct. 1937, at Detroit, he nearly won the Thompson trophy race, the richest prize in American aviation for 

18 laps. Wittman's plane developed trouble and he was forced to be content with a minor position. His plane cut through the air at 266 miles an hour up to and including the sixteenth lap.

In the seventeenth his plane developed trouble and Wittman discovered that one magneto was out completely while the other one was missing badly, besides, the ship started to spray oil from almost the start of the race. He said, "The oil clouded my windshield and my only vission was through small portholes directly beside me." The oil spray was due to the vibration set up by a damaged propeller at the take-off.

THE FOSTER LOTHMAN MILLS 

This company is engaged in the manufacture of Sash and Doors in solid pine only, no veneered doors are made. Since the erection of this plant in 1890, many additions have been built, and the capacity has been greatly increased. Much of the product of this plant finds a market in the east and south, and goes to jobbers and dealers only, no retail business is transacted. It was in this factory in 1896 that the Dowel door was first made, and was soon universally adopted because it required from 2 to 4 feet less of clear lumber than did the old mortise and tenon style of door. A byproduct of this mill is the dust from the sanding machines. About a carload of this bagged dust is shipped each month to the eastern Rayon mills to be used in the manufacture of imitation silk. 

This company employes from 300 to 350 men. It is located on 5th Street, between Minnesota and Iowa Streets. This is one of the prominent establishments of our city and has contributed in no small way to the development of Oshkosh. George W. Loper is Superintendent of this plant. WISCONSIN AXLE DIVISION THE TIMKEN DETROIT AXLE COMPANY 

Along with the growth of Oshkosh was the amazing development of this Company. 

This plant was originally the E. B. Hayes Machine Co., making wood-working machinery which back in 1912 had only 14,500 square feet of floor space, employed forty men part of the time, who earned about fifteen dollars a week, at a scale of 25 to 30 cents an hour. 

The company's weekly payroll was about $600.00. 

This company kept pace with the times, first making machinery for saw mills, then for sash and door factories, and when the Dowell system of door making was introduced this company was the leader in designing machinery for that purpose. Later when the automobile and tractor made their appearance this company was quick to realize the new opportunities presented for machine shops, and the Axle proved to be the wedge for this company's entrance to a new line of activity. 

On May 1, 1919, the Wisconsin Parts Co., predecessor to the Wisconsin Axle Co., was organized. Factory floor space was increased to 90,000 square feet, and 75 men were given steady employment with a weekly payroll of $1350. The small frame building was replaced by a large modern plant which was designed to produce a high grade product efficiently. and it also provided facilities for the convenience and comfort for the employees, such as wash rooms, showers, lunch room and hospital service with a Nurse in constant attendance.

Now, this company employs nearly 500 men on a weekly payroll of $15,000.00. 

Average wages are $29 a week, compared to the Oshkosh industrial wage average of $18.79. A trip through the plant revealed many pleasing surprises for it is a marvel of efficiency and speaks highly for its management. 

This company is also the chief customer of several other Oshkosh industries, contributing in that manner to the local industrial program. Among these firms doing business with this company are-The Brickham Stamping Co., Universal Foundry, Triangle 

Manufacturing Co., Mathwig Co., Paine Lumber Co., from whom warehouse facilities are rented, Bangs Trucking Co.

The citizens of Oshkosh are, and have good reason to be proud of this manufacturing establishment.

WATER WORKS COMPANY 

This company was organized in January 1883, with a capital stock of $200,000. Daniel C. Libbey, president; 

George Burnell, secretary: and G. W. 

Roe, treasurer.

The building was completed in 1884. 

There were 22 miles of pipe laid, and 206 Fire Hyrdants put in. The equipment consisted of two compound duplex engines. The capacity was 4,500,- 

000 gallons daily each. The Holly 

System, which is the direct pressure, was used.

This plant was operated by steam power, and it had a whistle with healthy vocal cords. It could be heard in all parts of the city, and when the wind came from the west it could be heard on the east shore of Lake Winnebago. This whistle was used to sound each alarm, but in those days we had no autos to chase the fire department. But everybody knew where the alarm came from, and all firemen off duty were expected to respond. The writer, had a neighbor who was fireman of this plant, and whose duty it was to blow the whistle, giving the box number. One evening he told me that he got into trouble today, and explained that an alarm came from box 

27 and instead of sounding 2 blasts, pause, 7 blasts, he blew 7, then 2, and of course the department went helter scelter to box 72. I asked him how he happened to do that, aach he said, "I blowed it in German." 

This whistle, although somewhat subdued, is now used by the American 

Excelsior Corp. plant today.

In 1912 the plant was purchased by the city, and was enlarged and modernized. It now has a capacity of 14,000.- 

000 gallon. The daily average pumpage is 2,200,000 gallons with 98 miles of mains. 782 Fire hydrants, and 8.742 

Water Meters in use. The value of the plant is $1,236,396, and delivers chemically tested pure water, and since our Sewage Disposal plant has been put in operation, it should be better than ever.

The water is drawn from

In 1936, while building our sanitary sewer system, some early history of Oshkosh was revealed, for, while digging on Main Street, near Ceape, and at a depth of 14 feet, the big mechanical digger produced evidence of at least three distinct road eras in Oshkosh.

Just above the black soil at the bottom of the excavation is an old plank road, mostly long slabs. On this road the early pioneers used to drive their ox teams down to the ferry, which operated to "Brooklyn" as the South

Side was then known. It crossed the 

Fox River where the Main Street bridge now stands.

The old planks and logs were well preserved. Above the old plank road was a layer of sand, evidently used to fill in where the wood block pavement was put in, this pavement was called the Nicholson, and was put in place in 1868. It was found to be about two feet below the present road bed, the ties of the old street car tracks were also discovered.

PATRIOTIC 

OSHKOSH At the outbreak of the Civil War, Oshkosh responded spontaneously. 

Winnebago County sent 1657 "boys in blue" to the battlefields, of this number 

Oshkosh supplied 442. In April 1861, at the beginning of this war, Fire 

Company No. 1, offered its services. along with other citizens. These volunteers were accepted for the 

Second Regiment. On May 4, 1861,

Company E was the first to depart, with Gabe Bouck as Captain. several companies of the twenty first Regiment were ordered to rendezvous at Camp Bragg on September 1, 1862.

The 

These were stirring days, old faces disappeared, and boys became men over night, all eager to do their part.

In 1863 the Draft and filling the quotas were the great events, prices advanced and population decreased. 

The twenty first Wisconsin Infantry left Oshkosh on September 5, 1862, with 1171 enlisted men, 306 were lost, were in 21 engagements, and mustered out July 12, 1865. The thirty second 

Wisconsin Infantry departed on September 25, 1864, with 1474 men enlisted, of these 286 were lost, fought in 18 engagements, and were mustered out 

June 12 1865. 

Camp Bragg occupied the space between East Irving Street and Parkway, and between Hazel and Evans Streets, up to this time the spot had been the Fair Grounds. The name 

Camp Bragg was decided upon in honor of the late General E. S. Bragg of 

Fond du Lac, of the famous Iron Brigade.

A marker has been placed to perpetuate the site of this military camp and on a bronze tablet is this inscription:

Camp Bragg Memorial 

"Near this spot, in the autumn of 

1862, the 21st and 32nd Regiments. 

Wisconsin Volunteer Infantry were encamped for organization before proceeding to the front."

This spot is called Camp Bragg Memorial Park, and is located at the 

Northwest corner of Hazel and Cleveland Streets. The close of the war in 

1865 brought relief and filled the people, and the city, with renewed hopes, business improved, and the city again forged ahead, and the old camp grounds reverted back to the Fairgrounds.

When war broke out with Spain in 1898, Oshkosh was again ready to do its part.

For we furnished two full companies, Companies B and F of the 

Second Infantry. A total of 222 men, of these two were killed, and 40 died of malaria. This war was caused by the sinking of our battleship Maine in

Havana Harbor, and was declared on 

April 21, 1898. It ended August 12, 1898. 

Trouble with Mexico on the border found Oshkosh with Company B on the job.

Oshkosh and Winnebago County were also well represented in the 

World War. The men went to the front, while the women did their duties courageously at home. The men enlisting from here were mobilized at 

Camp Douglas, now Camp Williams, and became soldiers in various units. 

Many of the Oshkosh soldiers were in the thick of the fighting. This City and County paid a great price in young men killed in this war, numbering 135. 

Winnebago County sent 3242 men, of this number 1850 were from Oshkosh. 

This war was declared on April 9, 1917, and the Armistice was signed on 

November 11, 1918. 

The "American Legion" is an organization of American Veterans of the World War. In May of 1932 the local unit, the Atley H. Cook Post No. 

70, acquired the handsome and spacious club house formerly owned by the 

Oshkosh Yacht Club at the foot of 

Washington Boulevard. Oshkosh and

Winnebago County may well feel proud of their splendid record made in the defense of their country. -77-

Riverside is as beautiful a cemetery as can be found anywhere. The calm. beauty of its natural landscape is fitting. The many drives are well graveled and make all sections easily accessible. OSHKOSH FIRE DEPARTMENT 

On September 28, 1854, our city purchased the first of its Fire Equipment, which consisted of five fire hooks and six ladders. On February 8, 1855, the 

Council authorized the appropriation. of $200 for the purchase of a wagon and other fixtures. October 23, 1856, No. 1 Niagara Co. was formed and on 

February 26, 1857, we had No. 2 Germania Co. 

Captain N. Emerson was elected first Chief Engineer of the Oshkosh

Fire Dpartment. 

At that time it was a volunteer department, and when trumpets sounding a fire alarm were heard, men could be seen coming from all directions to haul the apparatus to the fire. These companies, crudely equipped as they were, gave excellent service.

In 1865 we had three hose houses, the Phoenix on Division Street, between Main and High in a building where the Opera House now stands, the Germania on Shonaon Street, and the Union Hook and Ladder Co. on 

High Street between Division and 

Light Streets. In 1866, the hose house on W. Algoma Street was built, and for a long time, the second floor was used as a dance hall and social center. 

On June 21, 1866, we added to our equippment a steam Fire Engine. On 

April 11, 1867, a team of horses was purchased to be used on this engine. 

The Brooklyn Engine Co. was formed in 1867. On January 2, 1868 another engine was purchased.

The story goes that the chief, after gathering all the boys together, suggested that an appropriate motto be placed over the fire house. The thing was debated at some length and several suggestions were made. Finally one man rose up and said: "I move the following motto: May this fire engine be like all the old maids in our village-always ready, but never called for."

ALFRED CRAFT MCCOMB 

In the year 1849, Mathew McComb age 

27, and his wife age 16, arrived at Green

Bay, Wisconsin and walked to the place that was to be their future home, in the town of Hortonia, which was about onehalf mile west of Hortonville, a distance of thirty miles. Here they located on a little farm of 160 acres.

The country was then a wilderness, with only Indian trails in place of roads. 

Here they built a small log cabin, but without a roof, however, they lived in it, and after a month when the first saw mill was completed some boards were procured and a roof was built on it. At this time there were but eight white settlers.

This sturdy couple emigrated from Ireland in 1849, and proceeded directly to their Wisconsin home, where, with true Scotch-Irish pluck, began the task of clearing the timber from the land, and preparing it for cultivation. 

After settling near Hortonville, nine children were born to this couple, all in this log cabin. A. C. was born on February 20, 1857.

The Father was a lawyer, and for two years was Register of Deeds for Outagamie County, also served as Justice of the Peace. In this capacity, the Father had much to do with the Indians, such as contracts, leases, deeds, agreements, etc. From childhood, A. C. came in contact with the Indians with whom the family was always friendly. 

Chief Oshkosh and his grand son Reginald, were friends of the family, and were liked by all the whites, because they were always ready and willing to protect the whites against the troublesome Indians. A. C. McComb attended the village school and graduated from their high school, later attended Lawrence College in Appleton, from which he graduated in 1878, and for six years, served as trustee of this college. After that he taught school. Was principal at Grand Rapids (now Wisconsin Rapids) in 1880, and from 1882 to 1883 at Tomah, Wisconsin. In 1883-1886 at Bozeman, Montana he was Superintendent of Schools. Then he drifted into the real estate business. He founded the town of Rib Lake, in Taylor County, Wisconsin, where he built an Opera House and twenty houses for the lumber jacks.

In 1892 he came to Oshkosh. Here he met Ella G. Wilson and married her on June 13, 1894. The children were Goldy Belle now Mrs. Watterson of Chicago, Illinois, Mary Jane now Mrs. Harold Milton Zaug of New

London, Wisconsin and Mark W. who was killed in an airplane crash at 

Milwaukee on March 11, 1929. 

Mrs. A. C. McComb died on January 22, 1916.

Mr. McComb is very devoted to his family and he loves work as well as travel. -863-

His real estate ventures began early, starting with city lots, then additions to city plats, he made two of them in West Algoma, Oshkosh. 

Gradually he drifted into timbered land. He would buy 40 acres, and sell, usually with this amount he would be able to buy three times as much as he had before. His deals grew in number, and in size as well. Asked how he got the idea of "Timberland" he said, "I clearly observed that Sawyer,

Hollister, Gould and some others he knew were making good, therefore I saw no good reason for not trying it also," and at one time, he owned over 100,000 acres of timberland in Wisconsin. Besides, these early lumbermen confined their dealings to White Pine only, Mr. McComb would tackle 

Hemlock and Hardwoods as well. He always bought for cash only, which naturally enabled him to buy at a much better price than anyone else. 

For, he said, in those days everybody wanted to sell, timberland was a drug on the market, and nobody had money to buy. Later when prices climbed in Wisconsin he tried buying cheap lands and timber in other states. and even ventured into big deals in South America. He also owned and conducted a Peacock ranch in Montana, from 1890 to 1894. Besides Wisconsin he had many tracks of timber in Louisiana, Arkansas, Texas, Michigan and Missouri. On August 29, 1903, he sold 21,795 acres of timber in one lot, consisting of long leaf Yellow Pine and Cypress, another lot of 50,000 acres of Yellow Pine both of these were in Florida, the latter one was bought in 1901 for $30,000. Four years later it brought him the munificent sum of $100,000. This tract was from 3 to 7 miles wide and 22 miles long. 

Mr. McComb was exceedingly fortunate in not having a single forest fire, which in those days were almost unknown due to the fact that trains were few, hence no sparks from passing locomotives, and no highways for tourists to build fires, nor travelers to throw lighted cigarettes, for at this time those coffin nails were unknown.

However, he had one narrow escape, for he sold 2000 acres, and one week later it was destroyed by fire. Asked how he could go about and inspect the land offered for sale, he said, the task was no easy one. He usually traveled on horse or mule back, with a pack containing a blanket, some food, and a flask of water, the owner would cut some treets to make a path, sometimes he would follow a logging road and usually a very crude one at that, sometimes the trip would require three or four days.

At nightfall, some twigs were cut from trees to make a mattress, and with the use of the blanket they carried, would soon be ready for a night's lodging, but sometimes it was not so easy, especially when rain would fall, or get into mosquito infested regions, and sometimes the ground would be soft and marshy, making hardships that are almost unbelievable. 

For nearly all his activities were in the primitive stage of our country. 

Still later, when the primeval forests of the southearn states disappeared he extended his activities to South America, where he bought and sold large tracts of Cyprus, Pine, Gum, Ash and Oak, a venture that would be fatal to most northerners because of the malaria infested regions, but due to his physique, enjoyed good health through it all, and was never even bitten by a snake, a remarkable circumstance, since he walked over them and among them a good deal of the time. -87

During the peak of his dealings in timberland, which must have been immense, his taxes alone amounted to $60 a day, and the premiums on his life insurance policies were $17 a day. 

He usually sold to saw mills in large quantities. Often he was referred to us a lumberman, but he really was a timberland man. He retired from active business since about 1926. Now at the age of 81 he has no holdings in timberland. He is a truly self made man, yet he has made a remarkable success in life for he was broad and liberal. He was the originator of the present Real Estate Board in this city. His donations to the unfortunates were numerous and novel, and because of this he acquired the title of "Maple 

Sugar Mack".

One of the outstanding holidays of this city, proved to be "Chief Oshkosh Day" on May 25, 1926, this event was sponsored by Mr. McComb. A pageant, in the form of over 200 historic floats, with seven bands, was the main feature of the observance. The past and present of Oshkosh was vividly shown in the many floats of various descriptions. Following the procession, at Menominee Park, the remains of the old chief were laid to final rest, amid the beating of the tom tom of native Indian Chiefs. The grave is in front of a large monument of Chief Oshkosh, placed there some years ago by the generosity of Col. John Hicks.

In direct response to the query, what prompted you to bring the remains of Chief Oshkosh to our city, Mr. McComb said, "It belongs here," besides, he knew the people of Oshkosh desired it but it was difficult to coax the funds necessary to make the transfer from the City Council, or from any other source. He felt it would be a fine contribution to Oshkosh; a personal pride; and a great favor to the Indians of the Keshena Reservation, for they feel that now the remains of their beloved chieftain rest in a secure spot, which otherwise could not obtain if left in the crude burying ground, they feared as time went on that the grave would not be properly marked and consequently lost in oblivion. 

Although the undertaking cost him $12,000 he felt posthumously rewarded by the gratefulness of our citizens. The occasion proved to be very educational, in that we learned much about the red men, and for the children especially, for it visualized much that could not be gained by the reading of books, for a large group of Indians came here from the Keshena 

Reservation to participate in the festivities.

Over the remains of the old chief is placed a granite slab bearing the inscription "Chief Oshkosh-1795-1858 A Man of Peace-Beloved by All."

WHAT'S IN A NAME? 

When Sir Walter Raleigh arrived in North Carolina, his first question was "What is the name of this country?" the Indians replied — Win-gin-da-coa, so for many years the State had that name, later on, it was discovered that the aborigine said in this phrase, "Those are very fine clothes you have on," and so North Carolina carried a fashion plate label to unsuspecting readers.

Indian cuss words are held responsible for many strange names. Early settlers seeing Brook or Lake would ask friendly "Brave" to seek appropriate name. Half the time the Indian would shake his head. He either wouldn't know or he wouldn't know what the white man was talking about.

Finally the Indian would say in his own tongue, "I don't know, white man big fool for asking" and that would go down as the name for the brook or lake or camp site.

Poor spelling among the early settlers often garbled many of the Indian names that were given out correctly. 

There is a lake near Webster, Mass. which has for its full name: "Chargoggaggogginanchangagoggchanbunagu - nanang". Officials think the name is the result of an Indian or a Colonial or both imbibing too freely of some strong seventeenth century stuff.

During the summer of 1883, Oshkosh marveled at the first permanent electric generating plant. The original franchise was granted on September 26, 1882 to three Oshkosh citizens: D. H. Forbes, C. C. SCHOOLS OF OSHKOSH 

The early settlers gave immediate consideration to the education of their children. For, tradition has it, that 

Miss Emeline Cook taught a temporary school here in 1841, which was conducted three summer months. It had 7 pupils and was held in a lean-to of the first house built in Oshkosh, by 

Webster Stanley. But the school was discontinued when Henry A. Gallup married the teacher. 

In 1846, Miss Lucy T. Alden taught a small private school which was supported by the parents of these pupils: 

Henrietta Wright, Betsy Cady, William Lloyd, Flora Wright, Ed. Finney, Matilda Stanley, John Cady, two of 

William Wright's children, Julius

Cady and three of Mr. Bowen's children. This same little log cabin, which was used as a schoolhouse was also used on Sundays for a Sunday School, which was well attended by both children and their parents. This school stood on the grounds now occupied by the American Legion Memorial Club 

House at the foot of Washington

Street. The school desks were hewn out of logs. 

The teachers recompense was small, about ten dollars in money each month.

In the year 1848, there was a small building on High Street, where the Athearn Hotel now stands, it was called Gill's Hall, and was used as a school in day time and for all sorts of gatherings in the evenings and Sundays. The first grade class consisted of 4 pupils, Mary E. Murdock, Charles 

Barber, Nellie Austin and Timothy Crane. 

On September 27, 1847, the city

Council appropriated $200 for the purpose of building a school house, but the lowest bid was $217 equipped, by omitting the porch, the bid came within the appropriation. It stood at the southeast cornert of the present Merrit and Jefferson Avenue, it was called the "Jefferson Street School", and later the "Union School" until 1868, when this one was discontinued and a new one built at the corner of Irving, Mt. 

Vernon and Dale Streets. This build ing was 22 by 24 feet and one story high, and its cost was $800. At this time school was also held in the Courthouse, on the second floor. In 1853 we had 371 attending school, with 5 teachers. In 1855, we had an attendance of 

474, this year we purchased 2 acres of land on Algoma Street, on which we built our first High School in 1868.

C. C. Bishop, June 1921OSHKOSH STATE TEACHERS COLLEGE The Oshkosh State Normal School is one of nine in Wisconsin. At the time of deciding on a location there. was much agitation to locate it near the Menominee Park, but after a while. the present site was selected, and the land purchased from A. B. Knapp for $3600. It was erected in 1870, and opened in one humble building on 

April 12, 1871, with 46 students, at the end of the first year it had 158 students and 5 teachers. In 1872 there were 309 students. On June 25, 1897 the total number of pupils was 738, with 85 teachers. Miss Rose C. Swart, Teacher of Primary Department began her service December 8, 1871, and remained here permanently up to 1923, with the exception of one year. The school was enlarged in 1876 and again in 1888. On March 22, 1916 the school burned down, causing a loss of $150,- 000. The present building was ready for occupancy in September 1928, and dedicated in 1929 to Rose C. Swart, as the "Rose Swart Training School."

The college is located in the heart. of the residential district of our city, on a campus of especial beauty. 

The enrollment at the present time is: 718 Normal Proper; 479 Training

School; 564 Summer School, a total of 

1761 for 1937.

The property value of the College is $1,500,000. The college library contains 30,000 books, besides there are about 20,000 volumes in the free text book library. The purpose of the college is primarily and exclusively to "prepare teachers for the rural, elementary, and scondary schools of this state."

Constantly expanding curricula have marked the developement of the school, in consequence, in 1925 the name was changed to the Oshkosh State Teachers College.

The State Teachers College is a great asset to the city of Oshkosh in various ways. It affords many of our young people an opportunity to get a college education, who otherwise would be denied this privilege if they depended on a College far from home, for the added expense would be prohibitive. Besides, the presence of the 

School in this city elevates the cultural and educational tone of the community, it increases our population with a very desirable class of people. The school is also a good a good advertising medium for Oshkosh, for it ranks high among the five best normal schools in the United States.

The schools activities in debating and sport contests, speak well of Oshkosh.

Then too, from a financial standpoint this school is an asset to our city, for it is estimated that the amount of money spent in Oshkosh each year by students, faculty and purchasing supplies is close to $1,000,000., and of course much more each time a new building is erected. Oshkosh is ably represented on the Board of Regents by our Edward J. Dempsey..

EDWIN G. BEARDMORE 

Edwin G. Beardmore was born on a farm in the town of Vinland, in Winnebago County in 1869. His Mother was a descendant of one of the signers of the Declaration of Independence. 

In 1849, at the age of two and one half years, she emigrated from New York State to Fond du Lac County, by ox team. The Father came from England in 1851.

Edwin spent his boyhood days on a farm, attended country school and in his late teens enrolled in the Oshkosh High School and was in the graduating class of 1892.

At this time the High School enrollment was 110 and the graduating class was composed of 15.

He then taught in a County school for six months after which he was graduated from the Elementary and complete course at the Oshkosh Normal School in 1895. Then he taught school for several years.

In 1897, he was chosen principal of Smith 

School, following with Franklin and Washington Schools in the same capacity. Since that time, the population of Oshkosh has about doubled, while the enrollment at our High School has increased 16 times, this is due to the desire of our people for more education, for we have raised the compulsory school age, to meet the new demands courses in the Grade and High School have been changed, from the simple to the rich and elaborate. We have added 

Art, Science, Commercial, Music, 

Band, Dramatics, Domestic Science, Manual Arts, Deaf, Sight Saving, Opportunity, Open Air, Vocational and

Adult education. 

To maintain these schools in 1937, required an expenditure of $550,000. 

The activities of Mr. Beardmore have been varied, he owned land in 

Arkansas, then shifted to land and ranch in Alberta, Canada, specializing in Hereford cattle, then, to owning the 

Winnebago County Telephone Co., all as a vacation diversion.

In addition, he was selected to administer two large estates. He has seen Oshkosh emerge from a saw mill town to a City of diversified activities. 

In 1900, he married Stella H. Jellson to whom three children were born, Sarabel, Malcolm and Mildred, all of whom graduated from Universities, and are living at Evanston, Illinois. 

Mr. Beardmore has devoted his life to the promotion of our Public School system, as student, teacher, principal and for the past forty years, he has been engaged in his present field of activity, of being the business agent and secretary of the Board of Education and also of the Vocational Board of 

Education. 

Oshkosh, and especially its school system, has profited much by the activities and experiences of Mr. Beardmore. 

He is a member of Trinity Episcopal

Church, where he served as vestryman and warden for 25 years, is a member of the Rotary Club and is vitally interested in all civic and political questions pertaining to Oshkosh and her Schools. -101 -

Main Street BRIDGES 

In 1836 a ferry was operated by 

Webster Stanley from Ferry Street to 

Kansas Street. Previously he had operated it where the C.&N.W. bridge is now located, it was a flat bottom scow with a railing built around it, and could carry passengers and cattle, a toll had to be paid by all. 

In 1848 The Fox River Bridge company commenced the erection of a bridge on Ferry Street. The bridge was later taken over by Abel Neff and completed on the evening of July 3, 1849. 

Colonel Miller was the first man to cross with a horse, the next day the entire population came to see the bridge. to cross, and re-cross. This was a float bridge, replacing Stanley's ferry. toll was charged on both of these. 

In 1854 we purchased the Float

Bridge for $2,000.00. This was built of logs and timbers, while it was privately owned a toll was collected, but became free after the purchase by the city. This bridge caused considerable excitement, for at one time it became loosened and went out into the lake with some cattle on it, and had to be towed back. 

A sign on this float bridge read: "Five dollars fine for driving faster than a walk." Even the fire department obeyed this rule, not that they feared the fine, but the firemen did not want to rock the bridge into the river.

In 1865 we built a beautiful new bridge in its place at a cost of $21,000. 

It was built of wood trusses with iron rods. This bridge was completed just in time to permit the soldiers returning from war to march over it, so it proved to be a double gala event.

In 1880 when the Yankee-Robinson Circus came to town they had a ponderous elephant which they had to swim across the river near this bridge, because it was feared the bridge would not hold it. This elephant weighed 

6000 pounds and cost $18,000, but on 

June 29, 1911 this elephant plunged into Beaver Dam Lake near Cumberland, Wis. and drowned.

In 1882-1884 we constructed another bridge at a cost of $37,852. This was built of steel. The present bridge was built in 1904 and cost $115,108.

The bridgetender in charge of this bridge is Paul Missal, who has held this post for sixteen years. He is one man with a steady job, for he works 

24 hours a day, and six days a week. 

One day each week he is relieved. The rules forbid his family from visiting him during working hours, but even though the rules were suspended, it would be difficult to visit him for his "shanty" is atop the framework of the bridge and the narrow stairs to it is steep and dizzy. 

Each time the draw is opened, a dozen or more lives are dependent upon the good judgment of the bridgetender, for in spite of his fair warning with bell and flash lights, some careless motorist might be trapped on the draw or the swinging gates.

After the alarm is given, no motorist. is permitted on the bridge. Perhaps it would be interesting to know about the frequency of opening this draw bridge.

In 1935 it opened 1293 times. 

In 1936 it opened 1614 times. 

In 1937 it opened 1471 times.

The Dowel Door, takes the place of the mortise and tenon method of door manufacturing, was the invention of 

Mr. Sippl, who at the time was foreman in the Door department of the Foster Hafner Mills. It was in this plant in 

1896 that the experiments were made, and the first doors produced with this. new joint. Since then, this method has been universally adopted, because it makes a better door, it is more rigid and cannot sag, besides, at least 4 feet of clear lumber is saved on each door. 

The Veneered Door is another innovation emanating from our mills, about 1890. For doors of Hardwoods cannot successfully be made of solid lumber, they would be too heavy and would soon get out of shape. The veneer door is a built up door, a core is made of pine strips or blocks, then the veneer is applied both sides, and a handsome yet durable door is the result. These doors however are not practical for outside doors, as moisture will ruin them.

The value of the Church to our individual lives and to the life of the community as a whole, can not be gainsaid by any serious minds. Often it is criticized, yet the fact remains that the Christian Church is a splendid, inspiring, beneficient influence in our lives. 

Its touch upon our lives is wholly toward nobler things. Therefore, it would be a loss beyond the measure of words if men should build their cities minus the Church of Jesus Christ. We should take pride in the growth and progress of human accomplishments, such as Schools, Libraries, Factories, Sto.es and Homes, but amid our building and planning, our getting and spending, we need that divine institution the Church. So, whatever be its name or form, let its bells be as God's welcome call to your hearts. To use the words of Charles Dickens: "Though it has never put a scrap of gold or silver in my nocket, I believe it has done me good and will do me good and so I say, God bless it." 

The Presbyterians and Congregationalists had a very early beginning in Wisconsin. For the first English speaking Protestant house of worship was built in Green Bay in 1845 and it bears the name of Union Congregational Church. It was forty by sixty feet in size, with a seating capacity of 300 and cost $3,000. The bell which cost $300. together with a gift of $500. were donated by John Jacob Astor.

The early settlers and later immigrants were people of high character, as is evidenced by the fact that they brought their religion right with them. 

Which accounts for our sound development and numerous creeds from such an early date.

Oshkosh citizens are justly proud of their Museum, for we feel sure that it is not equalled anywhere in a city of our size. Instead of a conglomeration of things, as many are, ours is well planned and so arranged that it serves. as a background of our development, the Indian room in particular, is one that fascinates visitors, this, together with the pioneers room, enables people to visualize the life of the redmen and later the pioneers who settled here.

The Museum was established in November 1905, and was housed on the second floor of our Public Library un til November 1924, when it was removed to its present location. For it was in this year, that Edgar P. Sawyer donated his palatial residence to the city of Oshkosh, with the proviso, that it be used as a Public Museum. HONORABLE ARTHUR H. GOSS 

Among the men of prominence in 

Oshkosh, none is better known or more highly esteemed than Mr. Goss who for many years presided over the Municipal Court. He was born in Oshkosh in 1862, he attended the grammar school, and High school of our city and the State Normal school, completing his education in the University of Wisconsin from which he was graduated in 1884, with the degree of L.L.B. 

With his admission to the bar he began his career as a lawyer in Oshkosh and engaged in practice for eleven. years. In 1895 he was called to the bench, becoming Judge of the Municipal Court of Oshkosh and Winnebago County, and for over 35 years has been retained in this important office, establishing a notable record of long faithful and efficient service.

In 1900, while serving this court, Judge Goss introduced the "Juvenile Court" which had never been tried before, this innovation proved so successful that the State of Wisconsin adopted it for all Municipal Courts. Its purpose was to correct minor offenses of children, with the aid of parents. As a consequence, whenever the parents. would cooperate and carry out the instructions of the Court, the children. would benefit by avoiding the disgrace. of a criminal record, which they could not escape if their trial was held in 

Municipal Court.

In 1896 Mr. Goss was married to 

Miss Hallie M. Maxwell. One daughter Marion was the only child of this union.

Mr. Goss belongs to the Fraternal 

Order of Eagles, the Independent Or der of Odd Fellows, The Knights of 

Pythias, and the Benevolent Protective. 

Order of Elks, and is Vice President of the Fraternal Reserve Association, and is a member of the First Congregational Church.

The Public Library 

Oshkosh made an early start toward 

Public Library. An organization known as Young Men's Library Asso ciation, was launched in 1857, and started with 245 volumes. The books were kept in Raymond's Store.

In 1868, the Oshkosh Library Association was organized. The officers were George Paine, President; S. P. 

Gary, Treasurer; L. W. Halsey, Secretary; Miss Mary Ann Olcott, Labrarian. At this time, the Library consisted of 1500 volumes of Standard works, and the regulations were, "All proper persons can have access to the Library by paying three dollars per annum.'

In 1871 the association reported that it was in a flourishing condition, having added 300 new volumes during the year. In 1875 the Library burned down. On October 23, 1895 it began as the Oshkosh Public Library, and up to the time that the present building was erected, the Library occupied quarters in our City Hall. 

To Marshall Harris and his wife, Abbie Harris, the citizens of Oshkosh are indebted for our beautiful Library, for they willed $75,000 to the City of Oshkosh for the erection and maintainance of a public library, on condition, that the municipality raise a like sum within three years. The late Senator

Philetus Sawyer contributed $25,000 of the required amount and the city raised $50,000.

The Library was opened to the public on April 1, 1896. At the present time we have 70,432 available books, 16,745 customers, and the circulation for 1937 was 416,454 volumes, sufficient to average 10.3 books per capita and 24.8 books a borrower.

The average circulation of each book in the Library during 1937 was 6.5 times. For the convenience of the reading public, two branch Libraries have been established, one at 1017 Oregon

Street, and another at 278 West Algoma Street. Miss Natalie T. Huhn is the very efficient Librarian.

Local Transportation 

Horse drawn street cars were operated from 1882 to 1897, and they were really run for the accommodation of the public. A woman would call from an upper window, and the car would. wait while she finished dressing. They would also allow passengers to alight, buy a loaf of bread or meat, and then go on. Sometimes mules were used for power, and often the result was "balking". Many folks recall being late at school for this reason, in aggravated cases the passengers would get out and push the car against the mule and it usually did the trick. These cars were lighted with a single kerosene lamp and in winter-time straw was spread on the floor to keep the feet of the passengers from freezing. This company had four and one-half miles of track, six cars, and twenty horses or mules. The first electric street car was introduced here on June 16, 1897. This company operated sixteen miles of well laid track, with thirty cars in service. 

The electric power was transmitted through the overhead trolley wires. A massive power house and car barn on Lake Drive at the south end of Mill Street, was an important part of the equipment. This company also operated a line to Electric Park, three miles south of the city. An Inter-Urban line was built from Oshkosh to Neenah in 1898, and another one extended from here to Omro in 1901, and one from Fond du Lac to Oshkosh in 1904. 

Busses for Inter Urban lines were introduced in 1923, since then, Bus service has been greatly developed and extended in all directions. City Busses started operating in June 1928, but complete substitution of busses for electric cars took place on May 31, 1930 at 11:07 P. M.

ROBERT BRAND & SONS CO. 

Established in 1866

Three generations of the family have devoted their efforts to the development of the business now conducted by Robert Brand the executive head of Robert Brand & Sons Co., one of the oldest industrial concerns of Oshkosh and widely known as manufacturers of store, tavern, and bank fixtures. Robert (I.) arrived in Oshkosh in 1866, at that time river traffic was at its height and his experience and skill in boat building made him a useful citizen, for soon he built boats here. Later he turned his attention to the manufacture of store fixtures. 

From 1866 to 1876 the firm name was Brand & Cole, the partnership was then dissolved. The company was reorganized and since then the firm name has been Robert Brand & Sons Co. 

In 1908, fire destroyed the entire plant which was located at 5557 Ceape Street. They then purchased the immense factory of the Oshkosh Furniture Co. across the street and have been there ever since. William Brand, the son of Robert, became active in the building of this enterprise.

He was an outstanding figure in business circles of Oshkosh and was also identified with financial affairs as a director of the New American Bank. In community affairs, he was likewise active. 

He passed away in August, 1926. Robert Brand (II) was a son of William and in 1918 he become actively identified with the Robert Brand & Sons Co. and has ably performed the task of guiding the business on a successful basis, and meeting new demands. The output includes all types of fixtures required in an up-to-date business establishment. The woodwork and fixtures in the new County Court House were furnished by this company. 

As president and treasurer, Mr. Brand directs the activities of some 125 employees. He also has financial interests, being a director of the New American Bank. Establishments like the 

Robert Brand & Sons Co. are a real asset to our city. -117--

A distinguished member of the legal fraternity of Oshkosh, and an orator of note. 

He was born in Winneconne, Wisconsin, September 23, 1887, and is a son of Thomas M. and Kathryn (Forsythe) Keefe, the former of Irish lineage and the latter of Scotch descent. The Father, also a native of Winnnebago County was a son of John and Mary (Bateman) Keefe who were married in Ireland. To Mr. and Mrs. Thomas M. Keefe were born six children of whom Frank B. is the third. Walter F. is well known in the theatrical world, for many years being general manager of the Western Vaudeville Association, which he organized and subsequently serving as general manager of the Marcus Loew, and also for the Pantages Vaudeville circuits. The other members of the family are Rudyard, Kathryn, the wife of Horace Craig, Edith, now Mrs. George Schissler and Maude the wife of James Hayward, dean of the agricultural school of Notre Dame University.

Frank B. Keefe was reared in his native country and attended the State Normal 

School in which he obtained both his elementary and advanced education being the only person to enjoy this privilege in the school. On completing his course at that institution, he engaged in teaching and was made principal of the school at Viroqua, Wisconsin. 

Attracted to the legal profession he enrolled as a student in the University of Michigan, from which he won the degree of L.L.B. in 1910, and then became associated with Daniel Grady a prominent lawyer of Portage, Wisconsin.

Mr. Keefe next opened a office in Omro, Wisconsin, where he practised for three and a half years and has since followd his profession in Oshkosh with marked success. 

He first gained prominence in public affairs while acting as assistant to District Attorney McDonald, who was later called to the bench. 

Mr. Keefe's efforts in behalf of his party, his familiarity with the duties of District 

Attorney and his widely recognized legal and oratorical ability led to his selection for that office which he has filled for six years from 1927 to 1933, thoroughly justifying the confidence reposed in him by the majority of Winnebago County's voters. 

Strong, fearless and efficient, he allows no clique or political faction to interfere with the conscientious performance of his duties and the office under his able administration has rendered to the citizens of Winnebago County service of a high order. 

As a public speaker he is forceful, epigrammatic and eloquent and it is a dull mind that does not respond to the touch of his thought, the play of his fancy and the strength of his logic.

Because of his unusual oratorical gifts and pleasing personality, he has been frequently called upon to address large public gatherings and with a ready adaptation he has appeared before insurance conventions, civic organizations and fraternal bodies. 

Always militant in political campaigns, he is a familiar figure on the stump in his state, and to him is due much credit for the swinging of many doubtful districts to his party in various campaigns.

THE HISTORY OF WINNEBAGO COUNTY 

The history of this County is of absorbing interest.

In this section of the country we are living under the third flag. The French ruled from 1634 to 1763, small detachments of French soldiers were at Fort 

Winnebago (Portage) prior to 1763.

The English were in charge from 1763 to about 1815. The United States took military possession in 1816. Fort

Howard (now the west side of Green Bay) was built that year, and occupied by American soldiers.

Winnebago County abounds in natural beauty, because of its Lakes and River scenery. It attracted the attention of the early explorers and adventurers who made it their favorite stopping place in their travels from the 

Great Lakes to the Mississippi River. 

Lake Winnebago and the Fox, Wolf and Wisconsin Rivers formed the great commercial highway of the northwest before the age of the Railroad. Many glowing descriptions were written by the early explorers of this beautiful Lake and River country now called Winnebago County, and since we have cultivated its fertile soil, it is regarded today as one of the most favored spots for the habitation and enjoyment of man.

To the French belongs the credit of the first settlement in Winnebago County. In 1818 Augustin Grignon (pronounced Gree-noh) and James 

Porlier established a trading post near the present site of the Village of Butte des Morts and on the George Overton farm where a marker has been placed by the Winnebago County Archeological and Historical Society. It was at this spot where the Indian trail between Fort Winnebago (Portage) and Fort Howard (west side of Green 

Bay) crossed the Wolf river. Viewing it now, it seems improbable, for the river is very wide and rice fields abound, but it must be remembered that when this crossing was used, the Menasha Dam had not yet been built, and the river then was narrow and the rice fields were land, and in some seasons the water was so low, that the river there could be forded.

A little later, Robert Grignon located and established a trading post at Algoma. In 1884, the first frame house was built in Oshkosh by Joseph Jackson, at the northwest corner of Main and Algoma Streets. Mr. C. J. Coon arrived in 1839 and purchased the site. of the present Sawyer and Paine property, and built his house near the Paine mill. 

Stephen Brooks and family came in 1839 and took up land near the site of the Asylum. Samuel Brooks came in 

1842, and settled at Brook's Corners.

He was a surveyor and run out the first roads which were opened in this vicinity, and was the first County surveyor. Wm. A. Boyd, settled on what is now the Roe farm, in June 1840. He brought with him 21 sheep, the first ever brought into this county. He also brought in the first stock of leather, and manufactured the first boots and shoes ever made in this county, and was one of the first mail carriers. He 

Joseph Rollette, a Canadian, was one of the prominent early settlers. traded between Green Bay and Prairie du Chien. He was the first to introduce swine into this county, and was engaged in the trading business here from 1804 to 1841, when he died at 

Prairie du Chien.

He was generally known as "Five 

More" because as they said, let them offer him what number of skins they might in bartering for an article, his terms were invariably "five more". 

Upon one occasion a lady remarked to him: "Oh Mr. Rollette I would not be engaged in the Indian trade, it seems to be a system of cheating the poor 

Indian." Let me tell you Madame, replied he with great emphasis, it is not so easy a thing to cheat the Indians as you imagine, I have tried it these 

20 years and have never succeeded. 

At a very early date, Winnebago and Brown counties were one, but by act of the Legislature passed January 6, 1840, Winnebago County was set off from Brown County and made a separate county, with boundaries just. as they are today, but it was not organized until February 18, 1843, and thus became one of the 71 counties in Wisconsin. The white population at this time was 143. Oshkosh became the County seat on March 24, 1847 -121-

Winnebago County Roads 

The period from 1840 to 1850 developed the "plank road", the great abundance of timber argued in favor of this material for road building, as better transportation was demanded inland, and the railroad had not yet reached this section of the country. 

Large companies were formed, and plank roads built and toll was collected for travel thereon, as the State offered no financial assistance in those early days. One company was formed to build a plank road from Oshkosh to Fond du Lac. The road cast $15,000. 

Now we have:

In 1847 Ganem Washburn, a lawyer, settled in Oshkosh, but with scarcely 

100 people here his sledding was poor, he had no money to pay room and board, which at that time was $1.50 per week, when the arrears amounted to $30 the landlord asked him to give it some consideration. Before many weeks elapsed the parents sent him the money to pay up. Then several -125-

months passed before he had his first client, and received a fee of $5.00.

From then on things went smoother, soon he was made Justice of the Peace. 

In 1858 he was appointed County 

Judge. But before he became Judge, he changed his name to read G. W. 

Washburn. The reason for changing was the constant annoyance of explaining the spelling of his first name, and he had no middle initial, but thought it would add dignity by adding the W. was 

The origin of Ganem peculiar, his father and mother very on named their honeymoon, somewhere in the state of Maine, drove their oxen team to a nearby settlement, to see their first "show". The hero in this show was a very likable character 

Ganem, and they decided that if ever they would have a son, he would be named Ganem. Judge Washburn resigned because of failing eyesight, for at that time the court did not have a court reporter, the Judge was required to write in long hand all evidence presented as well as all records pertaining to the Court.

COUNTY LIBRARY The Winnebago County Library oc cupies an important niche in the cultural and social life of the county, and provides one of those services that are entertaining as well as instructive. 

Miss Natalie T. Huhn, chief of staff at the Oshkosh Public Library, also holds the position of county librarian. 

It is located on the ground floor of the Oshkosh Public Library, it serves 123 book stations, placed in schools, stores, banks, granges, villages and farm homes, thus providing convenient places for people to obtain books. Mrs. 

A. L. Anderson is deputy county librarian.

This library was established in 1927, and contains 19,000 books, and for 1937 the circulation was 130,663.

SCHOOLS IN WINNEBAGO COUNTY 

Of the early history of the schools. but little is known. It is remembered by some of the old settlers, however, that Miss Emeline Cook, afterward the wife of Henry A. Gallup, was among the first to open a school in Oshkosh. 

The date is fixed at 1841, but the school was closed when Gallup married the teacher.

In 1855, the number of pupils was 4,128, this included Oshkosh, Neenah and Menasha, and had 90 school houses, of which 10 were brick, 51 frame and 29 built of logs.

In 1882, we had 16,000 pupils and 100 school houses, including Oshkosh, Neenah and Menasha. 

In 1937 Winnebago County had 96 

Public Schools, 130 Teachers and 2461 pupils, aside from Oshkosh, Neenah and Menasha. Total number of pupils in County 14,570. In 1910 we had 12,111.

Eva C. Monson, is the County 

Superintendent of Schools.

THE OSHKOSH BREWING COMPANY 

This company was established in 1864 by Horn and Schwalm. It is the oldest existing brewing firm in the city. as well as one of the oldest and best known establishments of its kind in the State. Other breweries have since been erected and become prominent, not only for their rapid growth but for the excellence of their product.

These enterprising concerns were J. 

Glatz & Sons and L. Kuenzl's breweries. These two joined Horn and

Schwalm in the firm now known as the 

Oshkosh Brewing Company, which was incorporated in April, 1894, with a capital stock of $250,000, for the purpose of brewing all malt beverages, manufacturing malt and malt extracts, bottling beer, and to buy and sell grain. 

The following were the officers: A. 

Horn, president; J. Glatz, Vice President; L. Kuenzl, Superintendent; Wm. 

Glatz, Treasurer; F. C. Schneider, Secretary. The office was at 31 Doty Street.

After joining forces, the capacity of the Oshkosh Brewing Company was 

100,000 barrels of beer annually. The plant consisting of the three establishments above mentioned, which have been improved in a marked degree since the organization of the company. 

The capacity of these establishments individually was: Horn and Schwalm 

40,000 barrels; J. Glatz & Son 30,000 barrels, and L. Kuenzl 20,000 barrels annually, making a total capacity of 

90,000 barrels before making any improvements.

Many have asked why this company was organized. The answer is very simple. Because of the influx of foreign. beer, competition became so strong that in order to cope with it we have been compelled to combine our interests and also to overcome the prevalent idea that because many foreign breweries were large, that their product is better than can be made at home.

In this connection it might be appropriate to give a brief resume of each of the three breweries that formed The 

Oshkosh Brewing Company. 

Horn and Schwalm Brewery 

Established in 1864 by Lenhardt

Schwalm and August Horn. This business was conducted successfully under their ownership until they joined the 

Oshkosh Brewing Company of which their plant was the main building and office.

Mr. Schwalm was a native of Saxony, Germany, where he learned the art of Beer Brewing according to German methods. 

Immediately after coming to America, he embarked in his chosen profession with marked success. 

Mr. Horn was born in Bavaria, and upon his arrival in this country he joined Mr. Schwalm in the brewing business, this was in 1866, and through his excellent business qualities the success of their enterprise was assured. 

Mr. Horn was made President of the company. 

J. Glatz & Son Brewery

This brewery was established in 1869 by Glatz and Elser, and operated under the above name until 1879, when Mr.

Glatz bought out the interests of Mr. 

Elser and conducted the business alone until 1886, then he took in his son, Wm. Glatz as business manager, which position he creditably filled until they consolidated with the Oshkosh Brewing Company. 

John Glatz was born in Baden, Germany in 1829, and at the age of four- (continued on next page) -129-

teen began to learn brewing. In 1853 he came to America and filled the position of brewer in some of the leading breweries in the United States. In the merger of the plants, J. Glatz was made 

Vice President and Wm. Glatz the

Treasurer. This plant was located on 

Doty Street near 25th Street.

L. Kuenzl Brewery 

Kuenzl and Walter established this brewery in 1875. It was located on 

Harney Street, between Eveline and 

Frankfort Streets.

This soon became a prominent business enterprise in the city. It was afterwards bought out by Mr. Kuenzl, who assumed the sole ownership and management and conducted the business successfully until he joined The 

Oshkosh Brewing Company. 

Lorenz Kuenzl was a native of Bohemia. He learned brewing in Germany, and had from an early age made the art of brewing an especial study and was therefore considered an authority on the brewing of all malt beverages. In the consolidated plant 

Mr. Kuenzl became the Superintendent. 

In 1911 a new complete Brewery was built, modern in every way, of fireproof construction, and equipped with the facilities to brew the finest of beer. 

Then the war broke out, followed by the Prohibition period from 1919 to 1933. In this period the company busied itself in other lines of activity, such as, storage of eggs, manufacturing Malt Syrup and Near Beer.

Since 1933 this company has spent $186,000 for remodeling the plant, using only local labor, and materials from Oshkosh. Fifty men find steady employment in this establishment, and its capacity is 125,000 barrels per year, 25,000 pounds of Hops are used annually.

This company is a firm believer in the trade at home policy, during the past year 44,000 bushels of Barley were. consumed, and all from the vicinity of 

Oshkosh. Home grown cereals are preferred because they are as good as can be obtained elsewhere, besides being a benefit to our neighboring farmers. Even the fuel is bought locally, in the early days this item was of considerable importance because wood was used in place of coal, and farmers were kept busy hauling their land clearings to this plant, and for which they received a neat sum. This company is continually improving and enlarging the plant, which of course, adds to the commercial importance of Oshkosh, and plans are now in the making for the erection of a modern office on 

Doty Street.

The whole plant is a model of neatness and cleanliness, and like the product, is appetizing. Only the best malt and hops are used and these handled in such a careful, scientific manner that it makes the product of this brewery excel in quality, purity, flavor and general superiority. The popularity of this Beer is attested by the enormous sale, which for 1937 amounted to $580,000.
