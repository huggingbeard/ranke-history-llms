IF F PRACTICE of insurance protection in its many phases may be said to have a home, the informed unhesitatingly turns to London. 

It is there that the sagacious Lombards, long experienced in marine, ransom and crop insurances, settled during the 13th and 14th centuries, preferring fog and cold of this lower strip of English isle to more clement weather of Genoa and Barcelona with their increasingly irritating conflict between civil and church authorities. While each form of insurance may be said to have had its origin in other lands, some extending their roots to an earliest period of Babylonian dominance, it is in London that these were coordinated, protective features evolved and distribution facilities perfected. London also gave birth to permanent life insurance policy contracts with founding of the venerable Equitable Life of London, which continues to provide lowest cost life insurance protection in the world, without having employed a single sales agent in its centuries of existence. It is also in London that Lloyd's began its honorable career; that Benjamin Franklin and Morris Robinson founder of Mutual Life of New York, learned enough to transplant some of the methods to our United States, and it is there, despite its sadly beleagured state of war, that unremitting research and study continue to improve insurance operation in all its many phases. 

It may be instinct, or proverbial English luck, or it may be simple logic, but whatever it is, choice, arrangement and continuance of policy contracts in England with unerring propriety follow a consultation with a law Solicitor representing the insured. English practice recognizes policies to be as important as deeds, wills, conveyances, or commercial instruments, each requiring assistance of an experienced legal mind if future complications or miscarriage of intent are to be forestalled. The English Bar has jealously guarded this responsibility through centuries of its unique leadership.

American Colonies inherited this wise practice but unfortunately it was interrupted by our revolutionary period, and later, lawyers' guidance was shelved in general intoxication with our dazzling natural resources and rising industrialism. "Things English were not in favor in the generation after the Revolution"— writes an eminent scholar and law historian. Overboard went not only undesirable features of English rule but also practices which have consistently proven their desirability and soundness. 

Viewed from present day vantage point, continued absence of enlightened leadership of the advocate is said by many to have been a major contributing cause of the grave abuses in our national economy during the post Civil war period. We were, in words of Carl 

Dreher, "half gutting a continent and astounding the world by an exhibition of piracy and profligacy such as it had never seen before...," or, as Gerald W. Johnson recently put it, "... conquest of a continent was, in fact, altogether too much like the looting of a continent." Reckless exploitation reached its apogee in three decades of locust like swarms of "carpet baggers"; Credit Mobilier; 

Boss Tweedism; incredible manipulations in railroad building; uncontrolled patent medicine rackets; state and national corruption of brazenly venal odor; impeachment of a President, and origin and development of high pressure insurance agent. Traditional learning came in disrepute, moral principles abandoned, hence "decadence of the Bar... passing of leadership from the advocate to the agent ...," as aptly phrased by our eminent Roscoe Pound.

Operation of insurance during this lawless period suffered perhaps more than any other social practice. Cunningly drafted policies became glittering commercial commodity, sold not to supply a definite human need of protection, carefully appraised by a competent legal mind, but sold by any means, for the profit inuring to the agent and his company. Anything and everything seems to have been condoned if it led to a successful parting of the prospect and his money. 

Thumbing the pages of company circulars of that period, scanning newspapers files, court decisions and policy contracts, one is not so much amazed at the "unlimited credulity of the public," for the public did not have access to law tomes and experience of the Justice so complaining, as at the persisting unconscionable methods used by promoters in the guise of benevolently named insurance companies, to extract millions of dollars yearly from bewildered policyholders and the indifference or helplessness of the law profession at this "moral flabbiness born of an exclusive worship of the bitch goddess 

Page Four

Success," to use the words of our William James. “Policies are but a permission for a lawsuit,” wailed a prominent forerunner of Westbrook Pegler. And when the cases on the docket were reached could the presiding judge make a new policy contract for these claimants? 

Obviously not,- so most of these clamoring hopes went to naught.

Absence of competent legal guidance in this complex field of law, finance and actuarial experience, has cost the American public billions of dollars with disastrous disappointments to those policyholders whose expected protection proved to be but wishful thinking. 

There were, fortunately for us, corrective forces rising to oppose, and with varying success to remedy, and correct this rampant chicanery, lawlessness and abuse of moral principles. The American Bar 

Association came into being to stem the downward descent of American lawyers. In insurance, Alfred M. Best saw the need of impartial statistical studies of financial operation of companies, beginning his fifty years of constructive endeavours; Frank Holmes of Chicago in critical moments of 1900 did a noble job to preserve uncontrolled professional aspects of agents and brokers in fire and casualty insurance; Dr. Carl S. Huebner brought his unquestioned sincerity in founding of Chartered Life Underwriters, albeit further deepening an already too pronounced cleavage between life insurance and other forms of protection.

The law profession need also be proud of time and energies given so freely and unselfishly toward improvement of insurance functions, by Elizur Wright, who finding competent lawyer guidance to policyholders lacking, initiated the first statutory regulation of companies in Massachusetts; by our beloved Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes, whose fearless probing during the Armstrong investigation in 1905, remains an unequalled high water mark in constructive leadership; by former Justice Louis D. Brandeis, whose wholesome interest in low income groups brought into being Savings Bank Life Insurance now available in Massachusetts and New York; by Louis H. Pink of

New York and Ernest Palmer of Illinois, whose bold attempts to improve the lot of present day policyholders, unfortunately are too little known to be fully appreciated. As emergency measures, legislative acts helped considerably, but the bludgeon of statutory enactments admittedly is not the best answer to the policyholders' quest for full utility of insurance protection. Preventive individual guidance by competent legal minds appears to be the only answer-as evidenced so well by the time hallowed English practice. 571687

7HAT OF THE present day operation? What degree of poten tial utility is the average American policyholder enjoying today? 

How does his lot as a premium contributor compare with that of policyholders in England, Sweden, or Australia? In 1935, halfway in the decade of general stock taking of our national tendencies, the 

Research Division of THE CYZIO SERVICE INC.,—an association of inde pendent insurance technicians and counsel set out to find the answers. 

The findings are offered herein in two sections, viz., 1. Conclusions as to general operation of insurance in U. S. A. and, 2. Tabulation of frequency of important deficiencies, errors and misdirection found to plague policyholders, as these were uncovered in study of 15,3801 individual and corporate insurance holdings. Let us consider the first section of these findings. 

A, Quantitatively in growth of assets and premium income American companies present an imposing picture, but when viewed qualitatively, in terms of policyholder potential utility, they leave much to be desired, in fact we are behind not only England but also recent newcomers in insurance practice, as Sweden, Japan and Australia. Without detracting in any sense from values now flowing from our institution of insurance, the derelictions and limitations are too many and too poignant to be glossed over by time worn cliché of massive company assets and gigantic premium intake. 

Page Six 

A conclusion of first importance seems unavoidable, viz., that our present day methods penalize the policyholders with excessive and unnecessary costs. While our automobile, food and electrical industries together with medical and other professional services have forged ahead in consumer education permitting substantial reduction in distributive costs, insurance companies continue their primitive pattern of mumbojumbo mysteries and unimaginative “footwork" of plodding servile agents,-only a degree removed from mendicancy. 

Resultant policy purchases appear to be more of an escape from crude and often obnoxious persistence, than policyholder wholehearted participation in this great, if not the greatest, economic invention.

Unbiased actuarial observers agree that a high degree of policyholder service can be had in life insurance with approxi-

mate $1.50 renewal" expense charged per year for each $1,000 unit of life insurance. That this amount is practical is evidenced by two of our life insurance institutions, whose steadfast adherence to this limitation in decades of operation, indeed merits admiration. In England and Australia this item is frequently under $1.00,-but for the most part our companies, take from each premium for this administrative expense amounts ranging from $3.50 to as high as $11.00, for each $1,000 unit per year. This relatively luxurious expense amount, it need be noted, comes after and does not include initial selling cost, which in itself is a princely portion. 

Premiums are further pyramided under an antiquated mortality table compiled when deaths occurred two to three times the present ratio; by excessive rigid interest charges on loans or premiums paid otherwise than annually. While death payments take less and less of the premium dollar,—mortality each year registering an all time low-life insurance premiums are raised with simultaneous increase in expense portion. 

Decrease in interest earnings sometime pointed out as a reason, fails as an excuse. To add to this strange paradox, increase in premiums is usually heralded in advance by each company for the hard to believe purposes of selling more policies at the allegedly unprofitable rates. Low income groups are penalized by excessive loading of industrial premiums and misdirection of selfishly inclined, to put it mildly, agency personnel.

A brief glance into other lands may suffice. In England life insurance is a quasi public institution, with directing heads recognizing this trust de facto if not so much in public utterances as here. Selling is not as rampant and swollen executive salaries unknown. Professional pride in qualitative operation of companies is an important factor, with eagerness rather than our noticeable aversion to give more than originally stipulated in policy contracts,-which by the way are very simple in their makeup and consequently more flexible in their functional uses. The American life company president who, emulating Louis XVI, was accustomed to say: “I am the company," implying the directors as his yes-men, would be a veritable anachronism in England or Scotland, caustically reminded that policyholders are true owners of a mutual company, and that "L'etat c'est moi" cycle closed with the beheading of that fated monarch.

Page Eight

For low income groups, England provides Post Office life insurance. This has been most serviceable not only in its low costs, but more so, by being a powerful check on assessment and industrial insurance promotions by companies. Simplicity of access to His Majesty's Industrial Insurance Commissioner by policyholders hardly able to spend much money for legal counsel, disposes of most grievances without cost or court delays, a public service very much needed here. 

Japanese predominantly follow the English pattern,--with Post Office life insurance, low administrative costs, etc. It is interesting to find there that life insurance is designed to be more than just a financial transaction. Sincere interest in member policyholder's health motivates extensions into almost unbelievable hospital services and periodic clinical examinations, free or at actual cost. No advantage is taken of a policyholder disclosing a serious impairment in the process of such friendly examination,-so as to somehow "forget" to mail premium notices thereafter. Financial assistance is also extended to policyholders suffering any grim catastrophe as earthquakes, floods, etc., with many other similar considerations which make an informed American policyholder's mouth water. While many of these extensions may not be suitable here, access to cash value reserves when in need, on better terms than the rigid American practice of 6% interest would certainly seem proper, especially when company funds are available to other borrowers at one half this interest charge. 

A Japanese annual life company report to its policyholders is a jewel of clarity and interest compelling power, -readily understandable and a pleasure to peruse. All these things,-and life insurance too,-at amazingly frugal costs of operation. 

Sweden offers very attractive costs to its citizen by extensive socialization, while Germany is reaching for the same goal by a different road, exploring new controls in a regulated dynamic national capitalism. Pre-war French policyholder was not quite so fortunate, its pattern of production and operation being closely modeled on ours even as to the pretty pictures on policy frontispiece. Russia continues life insurance to its workers in a mystical way all her own,-with no comparative comment possible. What is especially noticeable in this globe trotting is the general tendency, except in India and other less favored sections, to look upon life insurance 

Page Nine

as a public economic service to be applied by professional minds, not to be confused with commercial commodities or stimulated in sales because the company head reaches another birthday.

In fire and casualty insurance the premium dollar is still too heavily loaded with pyramidal distributive costs out of proportion to resultant policyholder return. Singularly enough, while stock companies, fire or casualty, controlled by fishy. eyed directors presumably watching every penny, should be the ones to achieve the peak in efficient economical to consumer administration,-most of these contrast strangely in this respect with an occasional "mutual," which quietly and unobtrusively minds its policyholders' interests, even if the latter remain unconscious of such noble ministrations. In other lands, similar to life insurance, costs of administration in property forms of protection are more favorable than here. 

Part of the differential undoubtedly is due to the difference in living standards,-but not all of it. Seldom does one find 

English, or for that matter Scandinavian, German or Australian fire or casualty company whose costs of management equal, not to exceed as here, the portion of the premium dollar used to pay losses to policyholders. It just isn't cricket,as Arch Oboler would say.

Add to this wastage the millions of policyholder dollars lost in defaulted companies whether life, casualty or fire, and you have a general picture not exactly beyond possible improvements. We have done well,-but not enough. 

Efforts so far to bring distributive and administrative costs down to a reasonable portion of the income dollar have proven futile. Most insurance departments have no statutory powers on this point - for costs, unless obviously fraudulent, are left to competitive control in the open market, the State insisting only that the same terms be available to all. Since competition between companies has long disappeared - the costs to policyholder are about all that the traffic will bear. 

When not long ago, Alfred M. Best's research staff (Best's 

Insurance News, October, 1937) found to its surprise that

English and Australian policyholders were enjoying lower life insurance costs, this otherwise fearless authoritative observer took refuge in the thought that maybe our mass distribution necessitates higher costs. That this distinguished journal 

Page Ten

should ignore comparative mass production benefits in other fields of endeavor is in itself curious. What other use is there for mass production but reducing costs to the consumer? 

Since all efforts so far to remedy these unnecessarily high costs have failed in their objective, it is a reasonable conclusion that they will continue to fail, unless unified policyholders, preferably guided by a vigorous and active American Bar, do something about these vestigal traces of profligate post- 

Civil War decades.

B. Next in importance is the alarming growth of litigation by disappointed policyholders or beneficiaries of insurance. Estimates for 1939 give a ratio of 1 insurance lawsuit in every 7 cases, of every type, filed in all courts of primary jurisdiction,insurance thus achieving a dubious honor of becoming largest single source of court litigation. 

According to a recent report to the Association of Life Insurance Presidents,-each day an average of 21 life insurance claims are denied payment,-affecting an estimated 18,000 widows and children each year. In automobile insurance the 

New York Herald Tribune (September 30, 1938) records 

29,000 policyholders of a collapsed "mutual" being sum 

Page Eleven

moned as defendants, with sizeable judgment for costs of final legal internment of their "company" assessed against them. Policyholders unfortunate enough to have a claim pending were hopelessly left to their own resources in continuing defense or in payment of judgment against them. Conservative estimates show that an average American policyholder has but 1 chance in 9 to receive indemnities as expected under his accident or sickness protection; 1 chance in 6 as to fire insurance; 1 in 9 as to burglary insurance. As Judge Albert 

L. Reeves points out- "To often, the adjuster who settles the benefit is far from the mental attitude of the solicitor who wrote the insurance. The latter was enthusiastic, solicitous and eager to help the assured; the former is cold, reserved, calculating, sinister and Scrooge like. It is not surprising that so much bitterness exists between policyholders on one side, and insurance companies on the other." 

In England insurance claimant litigation is a rarity. The policyholder is found to be better informed on his policy provisions, which favorable point added to his Solicitor's care in completion of contractual requirements, usually brings full freedom from disappointment. In a sense, the family Solicitor also protects the company, for he acts as a sieve, winnowing out unjust or fraudulent claims and undesirable policyholders. The English claim adjuster seldom is saddled with weight of suspicion so noticeable here. He assumes validity of a claim unless definite evidence appears to the contrary. Our claim departments appear to follow the contrary assumption, namely that every claim is questionable unless proven to the contrary. An experience of an eminent Judge is not uncommon. As related by him in a recent address to members of the local Bar association:

Page Twelve 

"Being greatly over persuaded, and not being trained in sales resistance, on one occasion I purchased a sick benefit policy from a glib tongued and loquacious agent.... In due course I suffered from ptomaine poisoning... the doctor suggested I make a claim for benefits under my policy. I did this, whereupon, after much argument, the company paid disappointing benefits and at the same time the adjuster placed in my hands a book on the subject of malingering."

C. Third major undesirable characteristic of American operation of insurance lies in our crazy quilt of taxation. The voracious appetite of our taxing bodies is too well known to need much comment. What is not well known, is that the institution of insurance is penalized unbearably each year by increasing taxes and extortions which at times cannot be distinguished from unlawful filching of money from a till. The conflict and duplication of state taxes on premiums of migratory policyholders shifting from one jurisdiction to another; the provoking retaliatory measures of states against each other; the back room politics appointment of inept insurance commissioners, these continue to amaze all probing investigators. The hydra headed monster now takes over $200,000,000.00 each year, not including financial examination expense and similar sundry charges borne by the companies. This money goes exclusively to state and local authorities, there being as yet no Federal supervision or tax. Of this amount less than 5% is actually expended for supervision beneficial to policyholders. 

Contrast this with benevolent attitudes of English or Swedish governmental bodies in taxes on insurance operation. An infinitesimal fraction of the premium Pound or Kronen is assessed solely for costs of supervision. In the preceding decade when our state tax demands skyrocketed, the Swedish, English and Australian insurance taxes were actually markedly reduced. The Englishman, the Swede, the Australian or the 

South African policyholder furthermore is given additional recognition of social value of his thrift by income tax deduction of insurance premiums as paid each year. Only in our 

United States of America is this economic foresight penalized to an unreasonable degree.

To summarize briefly: American operation of insurance suffers from unnecessarily high distribution costs; from recurrent heavy losses in defaulted companies; from loose statutory enactments permitting exploitation of so-called insurance companies for selfish ends of promotional "geniuses"; from wide-spread nepotism in company executive ranks; from general policyholder misdirection; from agent misrepresentation; from burdensome taxation; from mediocre management; and what looms most important, from serious miscarriage of protection as evidenced by our mounting litigation. To these may be added many intangibles, such as, a distorted perspective of both policyholders and agents which views insurance monies as an 

Page Thirteen

end, rather than as means to an end; maudlin sentimentality stimulating overdosage of life insurance with maleficent effects not only to policyholders and their beneficiaries, but indirectly perhaps to our national economy; and the tempting speculative aspects of insurance when coupled with present unchecked distribution methods inviting attempts at fraudulent gain. Such is the price we continue to pay for absence of competent guidance of policyholders by the only professional body of men equipped to this purpose, the American Bar. There are of course commendable exceptions to these findings but these only prove the general rule. Unfortunately where it does not pay to be good, and professional honor is lacking, exemplary conduct remains a rarity, serving only as a contrast and evidence of what is possible under more favorable conditions of operation.

ET US NOW examine the second section of our findings gained through study of 15,380 individual and corporate policy holdings. These concern themselves with the policyholder as an individual,-reporting on his current status. For this purpose all the many forms of policies issued are broadly classified here in two subsections, thus, -"Personal Insurance," - comprising life insurance, annuity bonds and sickness and accident forms, either as part of life insurance or independently thereof,—and, "Property Forms of

Protection," this designation including fire, automobile, burglary, liability, etc., types of policies. Findings have been tabulated so as to indicate frequency of undesirable deficiencies found in the holdings studied. 

These are the findings in "Personal Insurance": 

98.4%-of boldings examined showed pitiful lack of balance between accident and sickness protection, life insurance and vitally important annuity or old age protection. The result- a distorted reflection of individual needs or requirements. 

Personal insurance protection fails, or at best limps inefficiently, unless it is arranged by an experienced guiding mind so that the policy holdings are fitting proportions of the best accident forms,-this usually stands second highest in probabilities and danger to individual,-sickness policies, life insurance, and attractive types of annuity contracts. The proportions of these basic requirements naturally vary, since individual policyholder protection requirements are seldom 

Page Fourteen

exactly the same, both as to point of time in the life of a policyholder,--and between policyholders as individuals. It is in this diagnosis of policyholder needs where an experienced mind is invaluable for its objective perspective,-not dissimilar to medical diagnosis forming a base for subsequent treatment. Future outcome and individual welfare patently depend upon wisdom of this direction. It is a rare group of policies that is found to meet this basic requirement of sound, wellproportioned balance of these forms. Each portfolio appears to have grown like Topsy, but without the benefit of Nature's checks and balances. The result, at times, was found to be about as grotesque as if Topsy grew to be all tail, with legs out of proportion to each other, or with an eye hidden in some abdominal cavity instead of serving as visual guards of forward movement.

That this sound objective of well balanced holdings against all insurable personal hazards is not entirely lost sight of by average policyholder can be sensed from attempts to effect rounded out complete protection exclusively through life insurance policies under the impression that these policies do all the things necessary for this purpose. Unfortunately, however, such attempts are frustrated at the outset. Disability riders attached to life insurance policies are not proper protection against loss by sickness or accidents, except in rare cases of "PERMANENCY” or “inability to perform duties of ANY occupation for gain, remuneration or profit." Sufficient evidence to prove "permanency" is well nigh impossible,-and selling apples or shoe strings on a corner is also an occupation to which the insured may be directed by his company in lieu of indemnity he expected. Too often these dubious policy provisions become a ringside seat to a lawsuit. 

Nor does a life insurance policy in itself protect against old age dependence with anywhere near the efficiency of annuity contracts specially designed for this purpose but seldom known to the public. Its primary purpose is protection in case of premature death, to which end major portion of the premium is consumed. The remainder called "cash value" is further depleted by new costs in converting these cash values into retirement income in old age, for very few policies were found to contain automatic conversion privilege. A recent comment by Judge Albert L. Reeves in addressing a group of 

Page Fifteen

lawyers illuminates this misdirection to which even judges appear susceptible: "I recall the rosy tinted representations made by the soliciting agent. According to him, I was assured that at my present age the dividends from the . . policy would support me in my advancing years, my dividend for 1937, was $2.50.”

91.08% of holdings examined contained one or more unnecessary or misfit policies whose premium requirements could be devoted to more worthwhile end.

This by-product of haphazard method of collecting policies without competent guidance or supervision is a veritable termite feeding on premium dollars. Each insurance policy,and there are many forms in all phases of protection, has some special applicability to individual needs. Average insured's unfamiliarity with these variations, restricts his choice to contracts offered to him from time to time by soliciting agents. 

Naturally an agent's persuasion concerns itself with policies providing more lucrative return to him, and in inverse ratio, proportionate less value to policyholder. Agent's limitation in representing but one company adds to the difficulty since it restricts policyholder selection exclusively to contracts of his company. Misfit policies waste about 21% of total premium disbursements. 

89.2%-of policy owners are not aware of their policy limitations and restrictions, although as party to each contract clear understanding is essential if future disappointments are to be avoided.

This prevalent condition is very dangerous, for it leads to much too frequent miscarriage of expected protection. 

Unaware of cold realities of laws of contracts, policyholders continue to expect broad protection against all accidents under so-called "double indemnity" riders with life insurance policies, uninformed as to their limitations to "violent and external" accidents, further subject to numerous other exclusions. They continue to expect liberal protection under similar "disability" riders, only to be reminded by company adjuster when disabled, that their policy provisions do not apply in their type of disability. They continue to expect pay- 

Page Sixteen

ment under accident policies when injured, only to discover that "bodily injuries" insuring clause would pay them indemnity, but that instead they have less inclusive "accidental means" policy, which according to the adjuster is a horse from another stall. They continue to ignore proof of age requirement under life insurance,—and readily sign applications incorrectly completed by agents, unaware that full responsibility rests upon them for these statements thereafter, to confound them in case of a claim if living, and to add to bewilderment of beneficiaries in case of their death. It is not uncommon to find that reality and policyholder expectations are far removed from each other. Selling agent's interpretation of policy provisions definitely appears to be an unsafe reed for the policyholder to lean upon. 

82.1%—of boldings showed no attempt to coordinate policies into an integrated, serviceable, total. 

It seems odd at times that good money is so loosely spent on uncontrolled and uncoordinated holdings. The average policyholder seldom can even make a guess as to his sum total of protection against each hazard. Beneficiary provisions continue to conflict-errors in statements under one policy repeatedly jeopardize protection under others. This incredible situation of each policy going its own sweet way, it need be noted, is almost unknown in England, Sweden or Australia. 

Law Solicitor guidance makes certain that each policy contributes to the sum total, with respective provisions coordinated under his experienced and informed supervision. 

78.9%—of life insurance holdings examined show an improper practice of naming minor children as first or succeeding beneficiaries.

This repeated lack of thought for minor children's future welfare, necessitates intervention at insured's death, or the death of his first beneficiary, of impersonal machinery of law of guardianship, expensive surety bonds, and other legal requirements,-plus usually detrimental effects of payment of the remainder of the monies to these children at an economic immature age of 21,-for the ponderous law machinery no longer concerns itself with them beyond that point. Few children, if any, involved in this way, overcome the harmful effects of this lack of insured parent foresight.

Few, if any, personal insurance holdings follow a map of probabilities confronting each individual. Most often less probable hazards are overstressed to the exclusion of others more important to the individual and more probable to function protection. Individual probabilities, of course, vary-reflecting habits, training, physical capacity, mental equipment and actual age at point of computation. 

The reader might find it interesting to review his own policyholdings in this light. 

Page Eighteen

What should a considerate father do to protect wisely his minor children? The answer lies within easy access of every lawyer, viz., minority trust provisions, wherein adult relatives or friends, not less than two, are designated in advance as trustees or legal representatives of minor children, with written instructions to guide them, continuing their beneficial supervision for as long after the children reach legal maturity of 21 as may be desired in each instance. This thoughtful care by a father familiar with his children's needs, providing a flexible pattern for them to follow is immeasurably preferable to impersonal grinding of court procedure, the latter at best, but a poor substitute for father's failure to care for his own. 

Minority trust provisions are not to be confused with rigid specifications in beneficiary provisions, parading as adequate provision of income for children's future requirements. 

77.4%-of life insurance policies show unwise arrangement in modes of payment to widow beneficiaries, troublesome and detrimental to their future welfare. 

That insurance proceeds to widow beneficiaries should act as an economic shock absorber at untimely and unexpected departure of a family head appears beyond dispute. This objective however eludes too many policyholders resting content with obsolete and demoralizing one sum payment, involving bother of investment and care thereto, and waste inevitably following large sums of money in inexperienced hands, or,almost equally handicapping rigid monthly income provisions, short sightedly not including privilege of alteration or partial withdrawal to a widow under some future emergency requirements. Something more than these extreme alternatives is necessary if life insurance is to provide sound economic protection for insured's family. 

A competent family lawyer would instinctively see to it that largest amount of income possible would be paid during immediate years, softening the necessity of financial readjustment and making possible a gradual reorientation for the future. 

Some fund earmarked før children's education, with a modest amount guaranteed monthly to the widow in her advanced years of life,-further qualified with limited or unlimited rights of alteration and one sum withdrawal as may be necessary in some future emergency,-the pattern further supplemented with creditors' exemption and tax protection-then, Page Nineteen

and only then does life insurance truly bring realization of its full utility. 

63.4%—of holdings examined were found deficient as to sound diversity of companies. 

Insurance companies as institutions guided by men are subject to all the perils of human errors. This has been forcibly demonstrated in all branches of insurance since 1932 during which time over $200,000,000.00 of policyholders' money was lost in defaulting companies, many of these previously unquestioned in their impressive size, age and reputation. Regrettable as this loss may be it was further accentuated to those having claims pending and the problems of obtaining new protection. It is interesting to note that English, Swedish, Australian and Canadian operation of insurance, where a deeper financial depression has taken place, at no time either policyholder monies or protection was jeopardized due it seems to more careful choice and diversity of companies under guidance of competent Solicitors representing the insured. 

52.7%-of applications forming part of accident or life policies were found to contain one or more misrepresentations or "breaches of warranty.”

Policyholders appear to be unaware of the import of carelessly or hastily signed applications during the contestable period in life insurance, and, throughout continuance of sickness and accident insurance policies, thus giving rise to a serious problem indeed, affecting not only their own future welfare but their beneficiaries' interests as well. Applications, signed without benefit of informed lawyer guidance, appear to be the principal cause of this common deficiency. It is a dangerous practice indeed, productive of grave disappointments and increasing litigation for applicants to sign forms filled in by soliciting agents. Like cancer, this deficiency can only be remedied by timely correction of errors or misstatements as found by a searching legal eye engaged to study currently owned policies. 41.7%- -of holdings examined show little care to provide suitable beneficiary provisions.

97.9%—of policyholders were found almost totally unfamiliar with provisions and limitations of policy agreement to which they have become a party.

The small print of a fire or burglary policy is in itself discouraging to a policyholder. And yet it is the small type that is of importance, since policies are as Judge Lamm points out, “big lettered promises and small lettered limitations.”

None of the policyholders interviewed could even guess when automobile insurance became non-effective, or, what effect non-occupancy of residence, or placing a mortgage on a building or contents thereof, would have on his fire or burglary protection. Statutory enactments prescribing policy provisions appear to be not enough, for mass treatment by law at best is a less satisfactory alternative to individual knowledge and care. 

Centuries of English and Swedish experience evidence the wisdom of simplicity in policy makeup with less legislation and more individual familiarity with insurance contract promises as well as limitations. While a policyholder on closer acquaintance with his policies may lose some rainbow hued

delusions as to what his insurance will or will not do for him in turn, it seems preferable that he gain more desirable freedom from future disappointments. 

93.4% of individual and 42.1% of corporate holdings were found to be a distorted application of protection to property owned with guesswork dominating both the amount and type of policy. 

How much fire insurance should one have? Is burglary protection more important?-or should one stress liability protection as an owner of an automobile or real estate? The answer to these basic questions at present appears to follow pure chance, with homes carrying superfluous amounts of fire insurance yet with inadequate amounts on personal property contained therein; complete exposure to risk of having property taken away by liability judgments, while less important forms of insurance are scrupulously paid for each year. Clearly such condition is not participation in insurance protection but a game of selling pressure and sales resistance. 

Business institutions seem to fare somewhat better in that their insurance at least attempts an accurate reflection of current inventory, etc. Unfortunately, these fail in many instances for want of competent lawyer supervisory guidance. Legal aspect and interpretation of policy contracts reached by untrained or inexperienced minds of "insurance departments" in the end prove to be usually misleading. 

83.8%-of individual and 13.8% of business holdings showed lack of evidence of ownership of articles insured. 

As mentioned previously there are other requirements from a policyholder beside paying of premium. One of these is a satisfactory evidence of articles lost and their value. A prudent policyholder in addition to his policy also has an inventory of insured items. Compliance with this requirement is one of the duties of the agent or company representative at the time the policies are written or renewed but as yet is seldom recognized by them. The argument that since only a fraction of policyholders suffer losses no concern need be had in exercising this foresight patently is no excuse for their neglect. Just as an insuring company considers each policy a potential loss, with every step possible taken to protect its interests, so the policyholder also need be prepared albeit loss is not immediately expected.

64.6%—of individual fire policies, and 27.5% of business or corporate forms lack "extended coverage." 

This deficiency makes protection against fire limp in its usefulness to the policyholder. Fire destruction is only one of many hazards which fire insurance companies economically can assume. For a nominal additional premium, any fire policy can be extended to reimburse loss by water, windstorm, smoke, collapsing building, strikes, riots and in this age of air transportation, also risk of damage from the air. That such extension is of practical value has been amply demonstrated. 

One need only consider New England's repeated, yet uninsured against windstorms, and recurring damage done by aircraft, riots, water, etc. to conclude that this extension is often more valuable than the basic fire policy to which it is attached. 

36.4% of policies examined contained descriptive inaccuracies as to location or designation of property insured or permit death of named assured to complicate payment of loss. 

An error in serial numbers of an automobile, or a wrong street number of property location specified in policies may appear trivial to the policyholder, but in effect this carelessness is far from that. Disregard of absolute accuracy brings about an anomaly of insurance on some unknown stranger's automobile or property, with none on the motor car or home actually owned by the holder of the policy. To extricate the policyholder in case of a loss in such case is no light task for the best of lawyers.

While we insure our lives to protect against possible death, uninformed policyholders ignore this possibility should death and a property claim occur at about the same time. Yet full protection may be had at no additional cost. Joint tenancy, where feasible, distinguished from tenancy-in-common, in assured names is just as serviceable in policy ownership as in ownership of property.

42.3%-of boldings (states where these are available), did not have more desirable inland marine policies. 

Early insurance contracts simply promised to pay when the article insured was lost or damaged by causes beyond insured's control. Few, if any, of the present day qualifications and limitations were contained therein. Marine forms of insurance continue this old simplicity of policy contracts, extending their scope in recent years to insuring all personal property wherever situated. When properly applied, “inland marine policies" are preferable to combinations of fire or burglary policies since they provide broader protection usually at about the same cost as combined fire and burglary policies.

That these forms are not available in all states is a reflection upon general unconcern with policyholder or their welfare. Reactionary opposition of fire and casualty companies and lackadaisical insurance commissioners, seem to be the principal barrier to full usefulness of inland marine forms. American lawyers readily could remedy this deficiency by aiding growth and expansion of this worthy improvement in property protection, assuming, of course, that they resume their traditional duties to lay policyholders. Recent flourishes by self annointed “experts,”—“analysts,”—“advisers,”—and similar proves to be but a variant of the classic,-"blind leading the blind." 

Insurance being what it is, a compound of law, finance, and actuarial assumptions, with contract laws dominating all its functions, undeniably belongs within the jurisdiction of our law profession,the only group equipped to cope with its many intricacies, trained to look beyond appearances, competent to weigh future probabilities. 

As Dean Harno points out: “It is the lawyers' responsibility to furnish the talent to overview the whole structure," a very fitting motto for the advocate. Properly equipped, a lawyer is the sole person capable of guiding a policyholder to full enjoyment of this modern economic necessity.

This conclusion, however, poses another question. American lawyers unlike English Solicitors have grown away from preventive insurance guidance. In turn, American policyholders have necessarily drifted away from lawyers, weaned away by less desirable ministrations of agents. Can these two be brought together again?—or is it too late? Can lawyer supervision be synchronized with current operation of insurance? Can his control gear be meshed into the vast machine of insurance without too much grinding or interference to smooth operation?

Unbiased observers appear united in that these questions can be answered in affirmative. To avoid misunderstanding, it need be stated here that lawyer supervision and guidance, are not to be misconstrued as solicitation of insurance. Obviously this would destroy professional and ethical values. Mechanics of evoking public's interest in policies or companies properly belong to improved advertising methods and to modified agency system. The proposed lawyer guidance simply intervenes with technical supervision to avoid miscarriage of protection or misdirected effort, thus helping the client to reach full utility potential in this form of mutual economic assistance.

Would such intervention add to policyholder costs? There is sufficient evidence that it would not; in fact that this assistance would effect considerable saving to premium payors. Superfluous and misfit policies, expensive cancellations, “twisting" and improper application of Insurance Principle to individual requirements, are estimated

Much of this waste would be eliminated, with substantial saving to policyholders even after a fraction of it is paid as compensation to lawyers for worthwhile work well done. And this is not all, for in time corrective measures flowing from enlightened unified policyholders' interest, would in turn bring general reduction in premium rates through reduced administrative expenses, decreased parasitic taxation, and litigation cluttering up company files. In time, soundly coordinated cooperative efforts of companies, policyholders, and unified lawyer supervision would demonstrate to the civilized world that as a nation we merit ranking position in operation of insurance, not only quantitatively as at present, but qualitatively as well. 

Some, perhaps, will voice an objection to the resolution suggested, on the theme, that while insurance policies admittedly belong under lawyer guidance, the years elapsed in non-practice make resumption now a possible interference, a block, so to say, to rolling machinery of agency production. Disturbance of present production might have bad effects on company financial structures, these men may add in a hushed voice.

At first sight, this contention may appear plausible, but in actuality it has little substance. Agency production of new business under present methods is far from healthy. It may be compared to a cat chasing its own tail, amusing perhaps, but getting nowhere. Each year in life insurance, for instance, new business production about equals business lost by cancellation of previously issued policies. 

Each year, the mountain of agency staffs grunts and labors only to bring forth a mouse.

In casualty and fire lines, our antique agent appears to be principally concerned with piratical aspirations for the other agent's policies.
