INTRODUCTION 

The Federal Trade Commission is one of the oldest of the inde pendent administrative agencies in Washington. The Interstate Commerce 

Commission alone precedes it by any appreciable period, The Federal Trade Commission Act was approved September 26, 1914, and the agency was formally organized on March 16, 1915, Purpose of Commission 

Its purpose was to supplement and strengthen the Sherman Act of 1890. Both the Federal Trade Commission Act and the Clayton Act were passed during the reform era of President Wilson amid a burst of public protest against trusts and monopolies, It was then generally felt that the sanctions of the Sherman Act had failed of their purpose. 

The general atmosphere in which the legislation was passed was summed up in the message of President Wilson on January 20, 1914 when he asked Congress for an interstate trade commission. Enough knowledge had been accumulated, he thought, on the "actual processes and methods of monopoly and of the many hurtful restraints of trade" to list item by item the particular practices which were forbidden. In this manner business men would have something more than the "menace of legal process"; they would have definite guidance on the conduct of their business. The opinion of the country would instantly approve of such a commission. It would not wish to see it empowered to make terms with monopoly or in any sort to assume control of business, as if the Government made itself responsible. 

It demands such a commission only as an indispensable instrument of information and publicity, as a clearing house for the facts by which both the public mind and the managers of great business undertakings should be guided, and as an instrumentality for doing justice to business where the processes of the courts or the natural forces of correction outside the courts are inadequate to adjust the remedy to the wrong in a way that will meet all the equities and circumstances of the case,

Where corporations had combined to a point inconsistent with the public interest and the maintenance of competition, dissolution was necessary; but this was a highly difficult and complex process fraught with widespread consequences to the economy. For this reason Wilson urged the establishment of an administrative commission "capable of directing and shaping such corrective processes." Thus the new legislation represented an approach different from the Sherman Act. The older statute was to be invoked after the consummation of the monopolistic acts. The newer statutes were designed primarily as corrective measures--to be used to prevent the development of monopolies and monopolistic practices.

The original intent of Congress had been to specify "unfair methods of competition" which were to be prohibited. But as the work on the statute progressed, it became evident that complete specification would be an endless task; new practices were constantly in the process of creation. This attempt was abandoned. Instead the Congress now contemplated that the Commission itself would take on the task. It would operate along the periphery of the antitrust law; and as an administrative agency gradually etch out-on a case-by-case basis--an array of unfair trade practices which would be categorized as illegal. Thus the venture envisaged was essentially of a pioneer nature, with the Commission ever vigilant and abreast of the changing mores in the business economy. In this manner it was hoped that the system of free enterprise and competitive practice could be preserved and strengthened. On the other hand, the older agency already in the field--the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice--was looked upon merely as an instrument of prosecution; it was to take the existent law in the field and apply it against violators.j

In fact, however, their roles have largely been reversed. the exception of a number of cases relating to the basing point system, the Commission has, on the whole, been content to apply established law when violations were brought to its attention--and when it had the funds to proceed. The Antitrust Division, on the other hand, has particularly in the last decade--revealed imagination and boldness in the enforcement of the antitrust law. In its application of the law to patent monopolies, international cartels, and restrictive practices which rest on something less that "overt conspiracy," much fresh ground has been broken by that agency.

This reversal of roles is perhaps less surprising than the fact that the Federal Trade Commission has continued to exist at all. Virtually every government department or agency at some time in its life is threatened with loss of powers or even extinction; and it must bring to its support in Congress certain pressure groups which, for one reason or another, are interested in its continuance, The Department of Agriculture, for example, can call on organized farm groups; the National 

Labor Relations Board has behind it the large labor unions; the Interstate Commerce Commission, Federal Communications Commission, Civil Aeronautics Board, and similar agencies are supported by the particular industries involved in the scheme of regulation. Its task is to serve the public interest--an interest so general, so diffused and so unorganized that it cannot be enlisted support the work of the agency. sionally an isolated voice is raised in its defence; but, for the most part, the beneficiaries of its activities are indifferent or unaware of its existence. As a result, when the Federal Trade Commission is under attack, it stands virtually alone.

Being an independent agency, the Commission lacks even the protections accorded the Antitrust Division-situated, as it is, in the center of an established government department. Thus it is a real acccmplishment for the agency to have weathered some thirty-odd years of existence. To achieve this has taken courage and purpose, and a sense of responsibility for a fundamental public policy laid down by Congress, Activity of Commission 

Like most institutions, the Commission has had periods of great vitality and other periods when it was generally looked upon as moribund. 

Curiously enough, those periods of greatest activity occurred when the Antitrust Division was in temporary eclipse; and, correspondingly, periods of activity in Antitrust coincided with a decline in vitality of the Commission, Thus the two agencies have tended to complement each other in keeping the philosophy of competitive enterprise alive and current.

CHAPTER I STATUTORY FUNCTIONS AND RESPONSIBILITIES 

The Federal Trade Commission has four major responsibilities under its statutes. In general terms these are as follows: (1) under Section 5 

of the Federal Trade Commission Act, to establish a plane of fair competition for all industry; (2) under Section 7 of the Clayton Act, to see to it that a large number of competitive units are maintained in the trade; (3) to insure, under Section 2 of the Clayton Act (as amended by the Robinson- Patman Act) that companies refrain from price discriminations which affect competition; and (4) an investigative and reporting function, under Section 

6 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, to serve as a medium of education for Congress, the public, and industry generally on the current facts of competition and monopoly.

1. Enjoining Illegal Competitive Actions 

Unfair Competition 11 

The most used section of any of these statutes is Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act which makes unlawful "unfair methods of competition in commerce, and unfair or deceptivo acts or practices in commerce. Under this section, the Commission has developed two major lines of activity: (1) an attack upon a number of relatively minor offenses, such as false and misleading advertising, lottery, and similar types of violation; and (2) a series of cases involving basic restraints of trade or rather, in the parlance of the Federal Trade Commission, its "antimonopoly" program of which the recent Cement Institute case is an outstanding example.

A large number of the false and misleading advertising cases have involved the food, drug and cosmetic industries. Section 12 of the Act specifically makes any false advertising of "food, drugs, dovices, or cosmetics" an unfair or deceptive act or practice within the meaning of 

Section 5. 

Stock Acquisition 

Section 7 of the Clayton Act was potentially of the utmost importance. 

This section forbade corporations from acquiring the stock of other corporations where the effect was substantially to lessen competition or create a monopoly. Its purpose was to check monopolies in their incipioncy in order to safeguard the compotitive system. Almost immediately the section was reduced to a nullity in a serios of historic 5-4 decisions of the Supreme Court.

In 1926 it was held that, where a corporation had illegally acquired the stock of competing corporations and had used the control to obtain

I M Rev transfer of all the business and assets of such corporations, the Commission was limited to a divestiture of the now valueless stock of the absorbed corporations.1 In 1934 the Court held that where a corporation had unlawfully acquired stock of another corporation but took over the physical assets prior to the Commission's issuance of an order for divestment, the regulatory agency could not act. 2 The consequence of these decisions was to leave the Commission virtually helpless in the merger field. Its economic result was to foster the formation of monopoly by consolidation of companies the most complete and final kind of control. 

Price Discrimination 

Section 2 of the Clayton Act commonly known as the Robinson- Patman Act -- is designed to maintain an equality of competition by eliminating price discriminations, The act makes it unlawful for any person engaged in commerce, in the course of such commerce, either directly or indirectly, to discriminate in price between different purchasers of commodities of like grade and quality where the effect of such discrimination may be to lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly in any line of commerce, or prevent competition with any person who either grants or knowingly receives the benefit of such discrimination, or with customers of either of them...

Two provisos are added. The first permits differentials which make "only due allowance for differences in the cost of manufacture, sale or delivery." The seachd empowers the Commission, after investigation and hearing, to establis quantity limits on discounts in any case where the number of large purchasers is so few as to make such discounts unjustly discriminatory. Up to the present the Commission has not yet used its statutory powers to fix quantity limits on discounts for any industry, Other Regulatory Functions 

The Federal Trade Commission has a wide array of other duties under a miscellany of acts. Section 3 of the Clayton Act prohibits tying or exclusive dealing contracts where the effect may be to "substantially lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly." The Commission also administers the Export Trade Act. Here corporations may form associations for the purpose of engaging in export trade (without violation of the Sherman Act) provided that none of their activities restrains the trade of the United States or the trade of any domestic competitor of such association.

The Wool Products Labeling Act requires labeling of wool products to prevent the passing off of substitutes upon the trade and the public. The 

Lanham Trade-Mark Act passed in 1946 adds a new responsibility to the Commission. It places upon the agency the duty of applying for cancellation of trademarks to the Patent Office where the mark has been secured fraudulently or where misrepresentation or other special circumstances are involved.

Thus, as the years have passed, a medley of related -- and loss related functions have been placed upon the Commission. instances these duties have been added without an increase in the budget commensurate with the new responsibilities. The Commission has patiently accepted these new functions and attempted to spread its small sums over its enlarged gamut of activities. 

Procedure and Review 

Both the Federal Trade Commission Act and the Clayton Act provide for administrative procedures before the Commission. Under the former, the 

Commission can proceed only if it appears that a proceeding would be "to the interest of the public." It is empowered to issue a complaint, hold public hearings, make findings of fact, and issue an order to cease and desist.

Appeal may be taken to a circuit court of appeals, if made within 60 days, to set aside the Commission's order. In this tribunal the findings of the Commission as to the facts, "if supported by substantial evidence," are "conclusive." Final appeal may be taken upon certiorari to the United States Supreme Court. Violation of a cease and desist order, after it becomes final, involves the civil penalty "of no more than $5,000 for each violation."

The administrative procedure under the Clayton Act is identical, but the Commission's orders lack the same degree of finality. Here the agency can enforce its orders only by obtaining court affirman ce and a court decree commanding obedience to the order. Violation is punishable only with a contempt proceeding for failure to abide by the court decree. 

2. Investigations and Reports 

Authority The Commission also has wide investigatory powers over the business economy. Under Section 6 of the FTC Act it has power:

To gather and compile information concerning, and to investigate from time to time the organization, business, conduct, practices, and management of any corporation engaged in commerce and its relation to other corporations and to individuals, associations, and partnerships.

Purposes 

Thus the Commission has very major responsibilities in providing economic intelligence for Congress and the general public. The current state of competitive conditions in specific industries, changes in industrial concentration, new developments in restrictive trade practices, the character of the business organization in international trade - all of these fall within the Commission's orbit of study and recommendation. 

In addition, the "spotlight of publicity" on industrial sore spots in the nation's economy was to have its own corrective effect. 

It was thought that business men would hesitate to continue the use of trade practices which had been subject to the widespread criticism of the Commission and, subsequently, of the public. Such practices, it was thought, were employed only if they could operate in secret; if once exposed to public scrutiny, corporation officials would hurry to effect correction. 

And they would hurry all the more with the threat of triple damage suits at their heels. At the time of the passage of the Sherman Act, considerable reliance had been placed upon private suits by individuals who had been injured by oppressive business tactics. It quickly became evident, however, that these individuals lacked access to the facts to prove their case in the courts. The economic reports of the Commission were to supply this lack; it was hoped that, with this information, small business could sue and collect triple for their injuries. In this manner Section 6 of the 

Federal Trade Commission Act was intended to play a major role in the maintenance of free enterprise.

Sanctions 

To insure access to this information both for economic reports and prosecution of cases the Commission has been equipped with subpoena power. Failure of any person to testify or to produce requested. documents involve maximum financial penalties of $5,000 or one year in jail or both. 

Tampering with records or "willful" refusal to submit documentary evidence to the Commission involves maximum penalties of $5,000 and 3 years imprisonment, or both. In the American Tobacco decision, the Supreme Court categorized the Commission's investigation as a "fishing expedition" for forreting ou violations, and declared such activity outside the purpose of the Act. In consequence, for a time there was a serious crippling of the powers granted under Section 6 of the Act, though the Court has since reversed this trend. 

3. Major Folicy Areas 

The bulk of the Commission's work has centered around Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act. And in this area, by far the largest number of cases have been concerned with relatively ninor violations, particularly false and misleading advertising. Up to the present, approximately 70 percent of the Commission's cases consist of this type of violation.

The remaining 30 percent have involved violations of the Robinson- Patman Act and antimonopoly cases, the large proportion of which have also been relatively insignificant in importance. However, because of the importance of some of these cases and the greater amount of time required in their preparation, there has been a somewhat more equitable division of labor between these major lines of activity. 

Many of the bigger cases of the Commission are brought both under 

Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act and Section 2 of the Clayton 

Act. This has been particularly true of the basing point cases, where the attack has been made both in terms of the trade practice and the alleged discriminatory price results of the trade practice. 

Merger cases under Section 7 of the Clayton Act have been almost nonexistent since the mid-thirties. Up to the present the Commission has not sought to have this section re-examined by the courts, but has confined its efforts to attempts to secure remedial legislation from Congress. This procedure has largely been dictated by the fact that judicial reversal of opinion would not affect a major loophole in the law the legality of purchases of assets in the first instance.

CHAPTER II ORGANIZATION OF THE FEDERAL TRADE COMMISSION 1. The Commission 

There are five members of the Federal Trade Commission appointed by the President each for a term of seven years. Not more than three members may be of the same political party. During his term a member can be removed only for "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office." 

Even after his term expires, he continues to serve until his successor has been appointed and qualified. 

The statute provides that "the Commission shall choose a chairman from its own membership." In the first three years of the Commission's life, Joseph E. Davies was its permanent chairman. Subsequently, the Commission determined to rotate annually the office the the chairman among the members, and this practice has consistently been followed. 

Altogether since 1915 there have been 26 commissioners. During the early days of the Commission, there was frequent change in personnel of the commissioners. 2. Organization of Staff 

On February 15, 1946 the Commission submitted to the President a "plan which is designed to improve and expedite the Commission's normal operations, and to expand and enhance the cooperative phases of its work." 

In part this was occasioned by the Attorney General's Committee on Administrative Procedure which, in 1940, had critically scrutinized the agency's administrative procedures, In part, also, it proceeded from the vigorous pressure of the newest commissioner whose interest has primarily been in trade practice conference work.

As a result, the settling of cases through stipulation was taken out of the hands of the Trial Examiners and set up independently. This was intended to create conformity with the impending Administrative Procedure Act and also to permit a greater emphasis upon the cooperative phases of the

Ging CHAPTER III OPERATIONS OF THE COMMISSION 

14 In analyzing the case work activities of the Federal Trade Commission, it must be borne in mind that with the exception of the trade practice conference work these are essentially in the nature of adversary proceedings. The Commission institutes proceedings against offenders which are intended to have a two-fold result: (1) to correct specific practices which are deemed to be "unfair methods of competition"; and (2) to use the particular offender selected as an example which will act as a deterrent to others who are engaged in the same or similar kinds of practices. 

Obviously, the Commission cannot proceed against all violators of statutes which are under its responsibility. Until 1935 its budget never exceeded $2,000,000, with small additional sums for printing. For 1948-49 it has $3,400,000 the largest budget in its history. With these small sums it has had to be highly selective with respect to its cases. Even where as in the past the Commission has relied almost exclusively upon complaints from outside sources, extreme selectivity is inescapable. 

The Commission simply does not have the staff to investigate and carry to conclusion more than a few of the complaints that come to its attention. 

1. Outline of Procedure 

A brief summary of the sequence of the Commission's tasks may be helpful before examining the matter in fuller detail. 

Investigation 

A complaint from the outside -- or in the parlance of the Commission, an application for complaint is received and is referred to the Bureau -1 of Legal Investigation. This Bureau may disregard the matter on grounds of lack of legal issue, lack of jurisdiction, or any one of a series of other reasons, It may feel the complaint is valid, but lay the matter aside for lack of funds. If it decides to investigate and the issue is relatively simple, the full investigation is made at the outset. If the problem is extremely complicated, an initial investigation may be made to determine whether the matter warrants a full-dress affair, 1/ The word "complaint" in the Commission is used only to apply to the formal issuance of a legal complaint by the Commission.

I When the full investigation is completed, the Bureau submits the data to the Commissioners with the recommendation of (a) issuing a formal complaint, (b) referring the matter to stipulation, (c) holding the matter in suspense, or (d) closing the case. 

Stipulation results in a voluntary agreement to cease and desist from a particular trade practice without litigation. In some cases stipulations are held in suspense until a particular case involving the same practice has been litigated. Where the practice is widespread, an attempt may be made to end it industry-wide through a trade practice conference. 

Disposition 

If the Commissioners decide to accept the recommendation to proceed with the issuance of a formal complaint, the matter is referred to the Bureau of Litigation which prepares the case. Hearings are then held before a trial examiner of the Commission; and at their close he prepares his proposed findings of fact, conclusions of law and recommended order. Exceptions may then be taken to the Commissioners, briefs are prepared, and oral argument made. Subsequently a cease and desist order may be issued. The matter may then be appealed to a circuit court of appeals and by certiorari to the Supreme Court. 

The Commission itself acts on the case in any one of these intervening stages. In each instance, when a matter is referred to it-whether it considers closing the case without issuance of complaint, or sends it to stipulation, or decides to issue a complaint or a cease and desist order--the case is assigned to a reporting commissioner who studies the documents and reports to the full Commission. Following his recommendation, the Commission then considers the matter and determines upon its line of action.

I Initial Steps. When an investigating attorney is assigned the case, he visits the company, acquaints them with the charge, and asks for the privilege of inspecting their files. Ordinarily there is no attempt to go beyond the scope of the particular charge. However, if an attorney discovers some additional violation during the course of his work, it is his responsibility to report such additional violation. 

This policy has the effect of expediting the investigation of the case. 

It can, however, easily result in proceedings against a company for a comparatively minor violation, while more important ones--which were not complained against--are allowed to continue. 

Relations with Trial Attorneys. An enormous flood of paper work seems to accompany each investigation. Normally an investigating attorney does not take statements in affidavit form; he simply files a detailed report. Failure to secure sworn affidavits has frequently created problems for the trial attorney; witnesses have proverbially easy memories and it takes considerable courage-if the witness is a member of the industry--to incur the enmity of his larger competitors. 

One result of the present practice is that an investigator's detailed research is frequently unfocussed. He tends to prepare a long, rambling account of the practices prevailing in the particular industry--without too much thought about the use to which they are to be put. 

This detailed report is then followed up by careful and prolonged study by a review attorney whose interest is largely in the legal aspects of the case. The review attorney then prepares a written memorandum and an accompanying recommendation to the chief of the Division of Field Investigation. The latter in turn prepares his recommendation for action for the consideration of the commissioners, This is formally routed through the Director of the Bureau of Legal Investigation. He may add his own recommendation; but all previous documents, including recommendations, must be submitted to the Commission. 

The activities of the Bureau of Legal Investigation stop with the conclusion of the investigation. A case that goes on to trial is handed over to another group of attorneys--the trial staff who take up from there. This sharp break in continuity has decided administrative advantages, but creates serious substantive weakness which will be discussed later in the section on trial of cases. 

Time Requirements and Backlog. Because of the varied character of the cases, it is virtually impossible to estimate the length of time necessary to spend on an investigation. Where a case is of minor importance, a day or two may suffice. Where it involves an entire industry, a handful of attorneys may spend several months. At the present time, the drift is toward concentrated work in the field for a few weeks by a large staff--rather than running the case interminably for several months

Division of Compliance nimThe División of Compliance was established on March 15, 1947. rattorneys have been assigned to this work. Vicissitudes of the Commission In a field as controversial as the one in which the Commission operates, it is not suprising that there have been marked conflicts in policies among commissioners. After the original enactment of the law, five months went by before President Wilson completed his selection of commissioners. During the next two years the Commission was torn with dissension on the question of jurisdiction.1 

Period of Eclipse 

Then came the first World War and at its end an atmosphere generally favorable to big business. With the appointment of William E. Humphrey in 1925, the Commission fell under the domination of a strong personality basically antagonistic to the purposes of the Federal Trade Commission 

Act. 

This situation continued until 1933 when Fresident Roosevelt asked for Humphrey's resignation. Although the ultimate legal victory was 

Humphrey's, the practical effect of the President's move was to eliminate

By this time a new situation had developed. The National Industrial

CHAPTER V APPROACH TO REGULATION 

In the Federal Trade Commission, policy has not been the result of conscious appraisal of functions and an orderly program. It has been the by-product of a series of acts done largely in response to the immediate, impinging problem. 

1. Reliance upon Complaints 

The strategic point at which the legal work of the Commission is determined upon is in the selection of cases. In the past the Commission's docket of cases has developed from two major sources: (1) complaints that it has received from outside the agency, and (2) cases developed by the Division of Radio and Periodical Advertising. Only in the rare instance has the Commission itself initiated a case. When this has occurred, it has usually been an important antimonopoly case, based on the work of the economic staff within the agency. Disadvantages

5. The Planning Council 

The most significant recent development in the organization of the Federal Trade Commission has been the establishment of the Planning Council. 

Abler members of the Commission staff have long been cognizant of the need for a correlating agency within the Commission and for a well thought-out program of action. The Planning Council was set up to perform these functions.

At present it tends to be a rather unwieldy body, made up of the heads of the bureaus and important operating divisions. This has not yet tended to constitute a real disadvantage since these officials have given unstintingly of their time and resources to the Council's work. In fact, the participation of these individuals has been of considerable benefit. It has provided marked prestige for the Council's work particularly necessary for a body which is starting off fresh in fields bound to involve issues of major controversy.

The Council can only make recommendations to the Commission. To insure a full airing of views, it must submit not only recommendations of the majority of the Council but also dissenting positions. Any member disagreeing with a recommendation is privileged to submit his views in writing to the Planning Council chairman for submission to the Commission.

Failure to Use Subpoena Power 

The Bureau of Investigation's method of operations is, however, fixed by Commission policy. It does not make use of the Commission's wide subpoena powers under its statute. In large measure this stems from the adverse decision of the Supreme Court in the American Tobacco case.1

Since that time a number of decisions -- particularly the Oklahoma Press Publishing Company case? has gone far in lifting the Court's prohibition in the earlier case.

The Commission, however, has never sought to retest the American Tobacco decision in the light of later pronouncements. Instead, it has adhered rigidly to the restrictions in the earlier case. Only in the investigations made at the specific authorization of Congress has the Commission seen fit to use its statutory subpoena power. 

This fact is all the more striking in the light of the experience of the Commission's sister agency the Antitrust Division of Justice with the same problem. Antitrust has been vociferous in its complaint of lack of subpoena power. It has publicized this weakness particularly in its grand jury proceedings, declaring that the latter type of proceeding was necessary to secure access to violator's files. Equity proceedings were minimized on the ground that the agency had to institute action in court before it could secure a subpoena to examine filos. 

Current Investigational Practices 

The Federal Trade's current practice is to seek access to files on a voluntary basis from potential respondents. Obviously, no executive will go out of his way to supply evidence which is going to be used against him for violation of the antitrust laws.

Or and the more usual way the Commission investigators can attempt to put together the story from interviews with a large number of smaller competitors. Delays in the Handling of Cases 

In the absence of leadership in the Commission, the pace in the trial of cases has been of the most leisurely sort. Except for the unusual case, there has been little public curiosity about the activities of the agency; and the Commission has tended to shun publicity rather than seek it. The prolonged hearings before the trial examiner are usually attended only by the handful of attorneys directly participating in the trial. Delays which would not be tolerated in the ordinary court are the customary order of the day..

The respondents have no irresistable urge to speed the issuance of a cease and order against them. Similarly, the Commission has not delegated to anyone supervisory responsibility for moving the case through the agency with the utmost dispatch. In consequence, each case takes its time and is subject to all the vicissitudes and delays which can be found in any administrative agency..

Intermittent Hearings. For example, hearings before a trial examiner are rarely held continuously until the case is completed. In order to secure testimony from witnesses scattered over the country, hearings are held for a few days in one city. Then two or three months may elapse before further hearings are held elsewhere.. 

Often this delay is due to the tactics of respondents. Obviously, the longer the case takes, the longer the company can continue with a particular trade practice. But this is not the whole answer. Sometimes respondents are anxious to get the whole matter cleared up as quickly as possible. They find these itinerant hearings laborious and time-consuming; and would prefer to have them held continuously in one spot until their completion. In part, the particular technique used by the Commission arises out of its failure to use subpoena power; as a substitute it must rely heavily upon masses of testimony from small competitors throughout the country who have been injured by the particular trade practices. 

A major factor in delay is the poor administrative procedure within the Commission with respect to hearings. A trial attorney may work out a well organized itinerary for holding hearings continuously from one city to the next until the case is completed. Too often a hitch develops the trial examiner is unavailable, new questions may arise about the case,

Where an attorney is indifferent, of course the situation is much worse. Then a case may be carried along for months with hearings held intermittently in various cities and long delays between,

Accepted Tradition of Long Hearings. As a result, something of a tradition has developed that the trial of a case must run for a year and a half or longer. Failure to use subpoenas in investigation has a further unfortunate result. A case half-prepared at the outset of hearings presents real problems; the trial attorney then must rely upon securing subpoenas from the trial examiner to get the additional necessary evidence. This means he must feel his way through the case on a day-by-day basis. The new data produced may simply supplement what he already knows; or it may create startling complications requiring a rather different orientation and a shift in the theory of the case. 

General agreement exists that cases in the Commission tend to be over-tried. Good reason for long hearings undoubtedly exists in some cases where the venture is particularly complex or of a pioneer character. 

Antimonopoly cases, particularly, can be justified in a thorough exploration of the economie issues. But even here the work can be over-done; and too much economic data- unassimilated and unorganized tends merely to befog the issues..

Intermediate Appeal. Rule XX of the Commission's Rules of Practice is not based upon a specific provision of the Act but upon its general tenor and its clear intent to increase the stature of trial examiners in proceedings. The rule bars interlocutory appeals during the course of hearings before trial examiners wherever practicable and at the same time attempts to make certain that the recommended decision of the trial examiner will be based upon a record identical with that submitted to the Commission.

To achieve these ends, at the conclusion of the taking of testimony, parties are permitted to move for reconsideration and reversal by the trial examiner of any adverse rulings made by him during the course of trial. The trial examiner must rule on this request, and permit an appeal to the Commission on any adverse decision. After the Commission itself rules upon such appeals, the record is corrected accordingly; and it is upon this final record that the trial examiner makes his recommended deci si on.

Obviously, this procedure can take considerable time. The respondents themselves are becoming aware of the delaying possibilities that this rule offers. In one of the recent cases of the Commission, there was appeal from some 500 separate rulings of the trial examiner. as- Findings and Exemptions. The Commission has also made important changes in procedure following the taking of testimony, Prior to the sage of the Administrative Procedure Act, trial examiners filed their reports; the parties prepared exceptions; and the case and the exceptions were considered by the Commission at the same time. No separate ruling was prepared on exceptions, though, in some inst nces, references might be made to them in the findings of fact. 

Section 8(b) of the Act requires that the parties may submit proposed findings to the trial examiners, and the record must show his ruling upon each proposed finding. It also provides that the parties may file exceptions to the recommended decisions of the trial examiner; and the record must show the ruling upon each of these. As these matters are now handled by the trial examiner, considerable time is taken up in their consideration and the preparation of written drafts for their disposal. 

Section 8(b) further requires that the parties may submit proposed findings and conclusions to the Commission, and the record must show the ruling upon each of these proposals. Normally, these roups of exceptions are passed upon at the time the Commission is considering the case for disposal on its merits. In actual practice, considerable extra time is taken by the Commission in considering the se matters in some detail; and, if denied, in considering and aproving the reasons stated for the denial.

Matters of equal or even greater importance--which need attention-reach no desk and are not handled at all. 

So far as the staff is concerned, the situation is at the least confusing. Accident may make a minor matter directly up to the Commission for prolonged deliberation and formal decision, Or settlement may be reached amicably on lower levels among division chiefs.

III CHAPTER IX COORDINATION WITH OTHER GOVERNMENT AGENCIES 1. Relation of Antitrust Policy to Other Government Activities Continuing 

The bitter struggle within the government respecting antitrust has created a persistent dualism in government policy. On the one side, there are a number of government agencies whose activities — through their loan or subsidy functions, their grant of contracts, their licensing policies, or other means have tended to intensify business. concentration and the drift toward monopoly. On the other, there are a few agencies notably the Federal Trade Commission and the Antitrust Division of Justice which have attempted to stave off this economic trend. 

Pressures Toward Concentration 

In large measure the battle arises out of the differing functions of the two types of agencies. "hen a particular agency is given responsibilities for regulating an industry or a specific area of industrial activity, it necessarily uses its prestige and influence to create conditions where such regulation may be effective. Frequently this means the encouragement of mergers so that there will be fewer units to police. An agency engaged in the granting of government loans places major emphasis on the likelihood of future repayment; and thus there is a tendency to place such loans with the larger, more stable and well-established corporations in the industry. This in turn helps to entrench their position at the expense of smaller competitors.

The contrast is particularly striking in wartime. In 1941 the War and Navy departments and the temporary war agencies centered their interest on getting out the productiion of necessary war goods. Obviously, the larger unis offered the greatest possibilities. Such contracts took less time to negotiate; and the potential volume of production was substantial. In consequence, the largest corporations secured the bulk of the contracts; the allocations of scarce raw materials were swung in their direction; and the lion's share of war profits was garnered by the se corporations. It was not until the Congress exhibited real alarm over the future consequences of the se arrangements in terms of industrial concentration and monopolistic control that an effort was made to spread out these contracts to the smaller units. 

Wartime Suspension of Proceedings 

Comin The agencies engaged in antitrust work sought to stem the tide, but they were doomed to defeat from the outset. In fact, their own wings were clipped in the early months of the war. The war agencies protested that antitrust suits against major corporations interfered with the war effort, and should be postponed for the duration.

Position of Antitrust Agencies 

The situation being what it is, the whole problem is highly confusing and complex for the agencies engaged in antitrust enforcement. They have insignificant budgets and inadequate staffs to carry out a statutory mandate to maintain competition. They are faced up against other government departments and agencies with extraordinarily large budgets for programs, in the practical handling of which, the drift is wholly in the opposite direction.

It has been suggested that it is high time for the government to take stock of this curious contradiction and affirmatively take a position one way or the other. Such a proposal accords with commonsense, but it is at odds with reality. The maintenance of a competitive system is basic in current American philosophy. At the same time it sharply conflicts with other ends sought to be achieved by other branches of the government. 

The rift is further intensified by the pressures of large scale industry each year growing larger and more concentrated to lift the strictures of antitrust enforcement. Perhaps nowhere else in government is there such a sharp antithesis between popular belief and economic fact as in this domain of government activity. -~

CHAPTER XI THE NEED FOR AN INDEPENDENT COMMISSION 

It has been almost thirty-five years since the Congress set up an independent agency to supplement the enforcement of the Sherman Act. Has the agency found a place for itself in the antitrust field? Has its performance warranted the continuance of its existence? Or should there be a return to the older procedures of enforcement exclusively through the courts?

These questions are of particular interest because the two forms of antitrust administration have operated side by side. The Antitrust 

Division of Justice has continued to institute its proceedings directly in the district courts, and to secure court orders for the maintenance of the competitive system. The Federal Trade Commission has followed the administrative procedures of an independent agency to secure the same ends. 

1. Continuity of Program 

As indicated earlier, the two agencies have had their ups and downs in terms of vitality of enforcement. If the comparison were made in the late '20's, when the Federal Trade Commission was engaged in a series of major investigations, it would be this agency which stood first. On the other hand, if the Commission is compared with Antitrust in the late '30's, when Thurman Arnold was in command, it would be the Justice Department which contributed the major accomplishments. Thus it is virtually impossible to isolate administrative machinery from the personnel engaged in its use; and strike a balance-in the abstract-in favor of one type of procedure as against another.

Relative Independence 

Consider, for example, the question of the independence of action of the two agencies, The Commission is formally fitted out with all the appurtenances of the independent agency. It is bi-partisian; the terms of members are permanently fixed; commissioners cannot be removed "without cause. There is no clearance of cases at the White House; the agency is free to pursue whatever course it might desire in the administration of its statutes.

In contrast, the head of the Antitrust Division is a political appointee. He may be removed at any time for any reason. He operates under the supervision of another political appointee, the Attorney General; all cases must be cleared through the Attorney General's office; and if the matter is of major importance, it is customary for the Attorney General to secure clearance from the White House, There is hardly a step which is taken by the Assistant Attorney General in charge of Antitrust which cannot be checked by someone above him.

IV 

Influence of Personalities. 

But this kind of analysis does not take into account the personalities that may be involved: At various times in its history the Federal 

Trade Commission--as then constituted--was sensitively responsive to political pressures. And in Antitrust, there have been individuals in charge who exercised an extraordinary amount of independence. They have built up strong support for a vigorous campaign of antitrust enforcement; and the Attorney General and the White House have at times been powerless to interfere.

As a matter of fact, it is almost impossible to over-emphasize the factor of personality, The Federal Trade Commission is made up of five members. Each member has his own particular line of interest; each. his own area where a cautious streak is dominant, Add up all these areas of caution and the result is a system of checks within the Commission which makes a fairly conservative trend in policy almost inescapable. On the other hand, a strong personality in Antitrust can take the rains in his own hands, and--with luck and skill--can develop a bolder and more imaginative program than can come out of a Commission of five members. 

Conversely, a weak personality can sink Antitrust into levels which cannot be reached in the Commission. 

Potential Disadvantages 

But, putting this significant human factor aside, what can be said of the independent agency as against the older procedure of enforcement? 

Certain disadvantages are apparent. It is easy for the administrative agency to settle into a rut. It can engage in a routine administration of its problems; it can lose perspective on its functions; it can develop slow and unwieldy procedures which grind out its cases at a snail's pace. 

It can become obsessed with the legal minutae of questions in its cases; and in its absorption with specialized problems, lose the common touch which ordinary courts--faced daily with a vast miscellany of different problems--cannot easily do. To a greater or less degree, the Federal 

Trade Commission has suffered from all of these ailments. 

2. Effectiveness of Trial 

Of major importance is the tribunal in which the antitrust issue is first tried.

Weakness of Court Trial 

Often the ordinary district judge y attempts to try one of these cases in the same manner as a tort involving private parties. He, bompts to invoke the established rules of evidence; and rules out large masses of material which appear to lack relevance. Unless he is exceptional, he has difficulty in wading through a complicated industrial structure and its maze of trade practices. All too frequently he has revealed the prejudices of his class and position in society; it is hard for him to look upon a group of responsible business men as "law-breakers."

IV 

As a result, there is a notable lack of consistency in decisions in the district courts. Every important case in the Antitrust Division becomes a squabble between the defendants and the government as to the particular forum in which the issue will be tried. The selection of the judge becomes strategic in the outcome of the case. One is known to be antitrust-minded; another has an unbreakable record for always finding for the defendants. This has made appeal to the Supreme Court almost a necessity, Any holding in the district court or the circuit court of appeals can be offset by another reaching the opposite result. The conflict in lower jurisdictions can only be ironed out by appeal to the court of last appeal. 

But this presents its own problems. To a considerable extent the final court is limited in its view of the issues by the actions of the trial court. Only the record can go up on appeal; and the contents of that record are determined by the trial court. 

Advantages of Administrative Tribunal 

Trial of a case before an administrative tribunal is quite different: 

The same kinds of issues arise daily; and over a period of time the trial examiner cannot help but attain some expertness and competence in the field. 

The same is true of the Commissioners. And here there is the marked advantage that all of the cases must be funneled through a single body of Commissioners. Thus continuity in policy can at least be approximated; and even when the membership of the Commission changes, there is a tendency to look back to previous decisions as a rough guide. In this way the preliminary confusion of a multitude of diverse decisions--faced by the Antitrust Division in the miscellany of district courts--can be avoided. 

Of equal importance is the building of the record in the administrative agency.

The very purpose of this type of agency is to permit a fuller airing of issues than can be attained in the regular courts. So far as the 

Federal Trade Commission is concerned, this virtue has almost been carried to excess

But the practice is highly useful in an issue as complicated and important, for example, as the basing point system. 

It has been suggested that, had this particular type of problem been tried in a district court, the likelihood of failure would have been great. 

The judge would have been impatient with the reams of economic evidence brought in by the parties; he would have failed to see its relevance and would have ruled out much of the testimony. In consequence, the Supreme 

Court would have had a limited picture of the problem; and might have sustained the reversal of the Commission by the Circuit Court of Appeals. 

In contrast, the handling of the case by the trial examiner of the administrative agency permitted entry of this evidence for consideration by the higher courts.

3. Development of Standards 

A basic purpose of the Federal Trade Commission Act was to develop, systematically and continuously, an up-to-date corpus juris in "unfair methods of competition." The Congress quickly recognized that a legislative listing of such unfair methods could never be complete, and could never be kept current. There seemed to be almost universal agreement in Congress that "human inventiveness" was too great for that.

The need was for an administrative tribunal to build up such a list of unfair trade practices by action in specific cases. This was admittedly difficult. Some trade practices are undesirable in themselves and should be outlawed. Others, however, are undesirable only in their context-in their practical use and combination with other practices. It was hoped that the administrative agency could, by a process of trial and error, trace out with some definiteness these difficult and often subtle distinctions for the guidance of business. 

Need for Administrative Agency 

For this type of work there is no substitute for the administrative agency.

There must be thorough analysis of the problem before the cases are selected; they require full exploration in open hearings; and there must be consistency and coherence in result which can be attained only by use of a single tribunal. 

This is not to say that the Federal Trade Commission has fully lived up to its purpose. A significant accomplishment has been made in the basing point field with a series of cases over several years. 

In this instance there was conscious intent on the part of the Commission to develop a body of law along a new front, Some attempt has also been made with respect to open price reporting & trade associations, though less systematically and continuously. The whole area of so-called "formula pricing"--the fixing of prices under pre-determined formulas for an industry--has merely been touched on. In the domain of pricing by technique and formula-rather than outright conspiracy-the procedures of the administrative agency would seem to be far suited for attack than trial in the district court. "It is impossible to frame definitions which embrace all unfair practices. There is no limit to human inventiveness in this field. 

Even if all known unfair practices were specifically defined and prohibited, it would be at once necessary to begin over again. If 

Congress were to adopt the method of definition, it would undertake an endless task. It is also practically impossible to define unfair practices so that the definition will fit business of every sort in every part of this country. Whether competition is unfair or not generally depends upon the surrounding circumstances of the particular case. What is harmful under certain circumstances may be beneficial under different circumstances.". Report of House Managers of the Conference Committee on Federal Trade Commission Act, Report No. 

1142, 63d Cong., 2d Sess. 1914, p. 19.

Development by Regulation 

The administrative agency also offers a flexibility in its handling of problems which has real values. It can, for example, issue rules and regulations in aid of enforcement. This technique might be useful in the area of false and misleading advertising where the problems are comparatively simple and may lend themselves to solution through this form of administrative device. It is not clear whether the Commission has this power under

Here again the Commission has not utilized the advantages of its organization as an administrative agency to explore and perfect the machinery at hand.

Informal Settlement 

In methods of correcting practices through informal settlement, the administrative agency offers much that cannot be secured through the older procedure, In this area the Federal Trade Commission has shown real ingenuity. Particularly with reference to minor violations, it has developed a variety of procedures--informal settlement through an exchange of correspondence, the stipulation procedure and the trade practice conference. Much can still be done to streamline and speed up these techniques; but in recent years, as the Commission has placed more emphasis on these forms of settlement, marked improvements have been made. 

4. Economic Investigation 

One very important power of the administrative agency should not be overlooked. That is the power to make economic investigations as a regular and continuing aspect of its functions, Such power could have been given to the Antitrust Division; but this was not done because the agency was looked upon primarily as a litigative instrument. And in fact, in the 

Antitrust Division, the emphasis has been almost wholly upon litigation, Control of policy has always been in the hands of lawyers; the economists have had subordinate positions; the interest has been largely in the winning of cases in the courts.

This situation has been much less true in the Federal Trade Commission, The economic investigations of the past brought the professionally trained economists to positions of some importance, The success of these investigations in terms of subsequent legislation in the securities field, public utilities and elsewhero, gave prominence and respectability to this work.

CHAPTER XII RELATIONS WITH THE EXECUTIVE AGENCIES 1. Coordination of Antitrust with Other Government 

Policies A major problem of coordination lies in the relation of the antitrust agencies to other parts of the government. As indicated earlier, it is branche the common practice of other operating of the government to approach their problems in utter disregard of public policy respecting competitive enterprise. In consequence, the drive toward industrial concentration comes from two sources. One comes from large-scale industry which is interested in holding and expanding its control over the market; the second comes directly from the government itself in the form of particular decisions on particular problems. 

Lack of Effective Coordination 

In the past the antitrust agencies have almost been helpless. 

Decisions with far-reaching consequences to antitrust enforcement were made often without knowledge by the responsible government officials that such would be their result. In many cases the antitrust agencies never heard about them until they were put into effect. 

If they did get information in time, their only recourse has been informal contacts with officials close to the President. During the latter part of the Roosevelt era, there were a few officials in high administrative posts interested in a practical application of antitrust policy. 

These individuals would be informally reached to insure at least on significant issues that the antitrust aspect of the problem was placed before the President.

Antitrust Division since the Attorney General is a member of the President's cabinet. Even here, however, there has frequently been diversity of view between the Attorney General and the Assistant Attorney General in charge of Antitrust work. At least on occasion the latter has sought to bring directly the expert views of his staff to the attention of the President. 

So far as the Federal Trade Commission is concerned, this connection does not exist. As an independent agency, the Commission is subjected to an isolation from the White House which is almost complete. A personal

2. Relations with Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice

In view of the advantages of the administrative agency, the questions immediately arise: What then of the Antitrust Division? Should it be incorporated into the administrative agency? Or should it continue to remain outside? And if so, what should be the relation between two agencies which operate, in some respects, in the same field? 

Here the theoretical arguments all point to amalgamation. There seems little point in maintaining one segment of antitrust work in an executive department, and another in an independent administrative agency. 

This can be argued on the ground of sheer efficiency in administration; it can also be lifted to possible divergencies between the two agencies in the field of policy. 

The antitrust problem is sufficiently complicated to the ordinary business if handled by one agency. What could be worse--to pile up confusion--than to have authority divided between two agencies, and particu larly where one agency is located in an executive department, with a political appointee of the President's at its head, and the other is an independent agency of a bipartisan character?

In actual fact, these arguments have little practical validity. The field of activity is so broad--and each agency so limited in funds--that overlapping has been slight. Even if they were to secure adequate funds, the likelihood of getting in each other's way has been reduced almost to zero by the new procedures for preliminary clearance of investigations. 

Effect of Agencies on Each Other 

On the policy level, the two agencies have been in general accordance, Differences have occurred--particularly among members of the staff of the two agencies; but as the issue has reached top levels in policy making, agreement has been reached.

A competent executive director should also be appointed by the Commission. In the past the Commission has been notably lacking in a program and an objective. At the present moment, however, this weakness seems to be in process of correction.

A promising start in this direction has been made by the relatively new Planning Council. This group, made up of the ablest staff in the agenc; have shown vigor and intelligence, and a willingness to strike new paths.

It is apparent, however, that as their work expands, the Council will need the help of a small research staff to handle the preparation of particular programs for presentation to it and to the Commission. 

Expansion of Economic Investigation 

At one time the Federal Trade Commission used its statutory responsibilities for economic research to become the outstanding agency in government in this field. Up until one or two years ago it was living on this reputation, though the economic work done since the twenties has been rela tively insignificant. In part the responsibility for this state of affairs lies with the Congress, where a jealous fight between the House and the Senate resulted in the loss of Congressional appropriations for economic investigations. In part it lies with the Bureau of the Budget which regularly stripped the Commission of its funds for economic work. 

It has also been due to default by the Commission itself. Instead of continuing some significant economic research at the cost of other work in the agency, the Commission allowed such work to sink into oblivion. And instead of raising its voice as an agency of antitrust enforcement on th need for constant study of the facts of competition and monopoly, the agency timidly acquiesced in the loss of funds for this purpose. The immediate need is for a revestment of the agency with its old economic functions. From an administrative point of view, this should not t difficult. No agency has arisen in the government to take over this functic indeed most of them have assiduously veered away from this work as perhaps t hot to handle, Thus the wide gap which was created when the Federal Trade Commission dropped this work still remains to be filled.

In fact, the Federal Trade Commission should constitute a reservoir of economic information for use throughout the government. All of this should have its focus on the structure and organization of industry, the points at which monopolistic conditions prevail and those where competition. has some play. Particular attention should be given to the "power systems" of the country's major corporations--the industrial spheres in which they exercise dominant control, the methods of restraint employed, the techniques used to hold other corporations in something approaching feudal bondage.

It should be noted that no other agency collecting information is attuned to this particular function. The Department of Commerce collects. data to report trade conditions; the Tariff Commission is concerned with our import and export picture; the Department of Labor, with a cost of living index, the labor situation and other matters, Somewhere in the government responsibility should be placed for a continuing up-to-date service on the way industries are organized and the competitive conditions under which they function.

This kind of information would have great usefulness. The agencies concerned with national defense are constantly faced with questions in their placing of contracts, as to what corporations are strategic in the field, what industrial areas are under their control, and what freedom to produce other companies may have. In the field of foreign trade the State Department has similar questions when it is approached by another government for aid in its industrialization program or when a particular contract with an American corporation is submitted for approval, The same kinds of questions arise daily in the work of the Economic Cooperation Administration and in the activities--both domestic and foreign--of our loaning agencies. 

The Federal Trade Commission should also be the center for gathering information on monopolistic controls which exist abroad and which are of consequence to the United States. For example, in industrial diamonds, tin and natural rubber--all commodities of importance to this country--there are thoroughgoing controls in which this country has no voice though it constitutes the world's major consumer. In at least one instance--that of industrial diamonds--the Department of Justice sought to attack the monopolistic controls through the antitrust law, but failed in the courts because it could not secure jurisdiction over the foreign parties. Obviously, another approach must be found; and this can be developed only by a detailed understanding of the arrangements that exist and their impact upon the United States. The Commission has power, under Section 6 of the 

Federal Trade Commission Act, to make these investigations but has not had the funds to give them real attention.

2. Statement of Policies 

The Commission's regulative policies have, in the past, received no general statement. They have been lost in a maze of individual cease and desist orders which, characteristically, are framed in the most extreme legalese. Each seems to exist in its own vacuum; and, with some exceptions each seems to be wholly unrelated to any other work of the Commission. As a result, an examination of these orders sheds little light on the general policies of the Commission, and supplies a minimum of guidance to business. 

Use of Regulations 

It is believed that there would be better public understanding of the Commission's policies if it promulgated general rules in respect to minor infractions of the statutes under its jurisdiction. Whether the Commissior. has this power under Section 6 of the Federal Trade Commission Act is open to some doubt--both in the light of the legislative history and also the particular language of that provision.

The matter, however, deserves exploration. The issuance of general rules is usually the simplest and most direct way the Commission can make its policies known. Cases involving false and misleading advertising, misrepresentation and similar offenses offer little in the way of novelty; and for these run-of-the-mill cases, use of the general rule might offer a partial solutione It is recommended that the Commission inquire into this matter further; and if the proposal seems to have feasibility, to attempt to use Section 6 or secure additional legislation, For the more complicated antimonopoly cases, this procedure would not seem to be practicable. Ordinarily such a case involves a host of trade practices which--used in the particular way they are--constitute a violation of the Federal Trade Commission Act. A general rule could be promulgated outlawing price-fixing; but this has already been made illegal by a host of decisions in the courts. The question in these cases is not whethe price-fixing is unlawful, but whether the particular set of practices engaged in by the industry constitutes price-fixing. Thus the problems ten to revolve around the import and consequences of the specific trade arrange ments; and these vary from one industry to another. 

In a few instances, however, it is possible to isolate, For example, ar industry conspiracy to make use of a basing point system would seem to be illegal irrespective of what other kinds of trade practices may be involved. 

Here the promulgation of general rules could be employed; but it would seem wiser to have such a practice first tested in litigation, with subsequent r view by the Circuit Court of Appeals and the Supreme Court. 

In general, this type of practice is so basic to the structure of the in dustry--and so firmly entrenched--that a judicial determination of legality would carry far more weight with industry than an administrative order. 

Also, a serious question is involved as to whether an administrative agency should have this much power. A major practice like industry-wide using of

IV Rev. the basing point system is the key to the entire organization of the industry. A decision to outlaw it should be accompanied by all the legal procedure in use in our society for its fullest deliberation. 

However, after a particular practice has been outlawed for one corporation, it would seem only common sense to use the rule-making power to apply that prohibition against all other units in the industry. The particular practice operates in the same industrial milieu; if it is socially undesir able for one company to use it, it is equally undesirable for others. It would simplify enforcement if, once a practice has been subjected to litigation and found unlawful, a general order were then issued by the Commission and the agency could move against others on the ground of violation of its order.

3. General Administration of Operations The Institution of Cases 

The selection of cases is the key to the work of an agency such as the Federal Trade Commission. If the agency is content with the run-of-mine complaints that come in, it is bound to spend most of its energies on an assortment of petty violations. The more significant issues of monopoly wil rarely come its way in this manner. The practices are too complicated for complaints to be expected from the public. So far as small business is concerned, they are equally uninformed; or they are fearful of retaliation; or they may accept the practices without question. 

The need is for a small economic staff to work constantly on major cases to be instituted by the Commission. A program should be developed where a certain number of these cases are started annually and are given top priority within the agency. The remainder of the budget could then be employed on relatively minor problems. Undoubtedly there is considerable valu for enforcement purposes -- in a miscellaneous volley of shots distribute widely over the industrial landscape. 

The major cases selected should be jointly approved by the Commission': legal adviser and chief economist. They should have an important role in the general program of the Commission; and should be specifically selected to expand new frontiers in the field of antitrust law. And, before submission to the Commission, the program should be thoroughly aired in the Planning Counci for general criticism and correction. Were this done, the agency would have, within a short time, a practicable program and a goal to work toward over a three or five year period.

Investigation and Trial of Cases 

Defects of Present Situation. The present bureau structure of the 

Commission has developed largely in response to the bulk of the agency's work on fairly trivial offenses. But it has been frozen into a rigidity which now creates problems even for this type of cases, The current sharp dichotomy between investigative and trial work may serve the purposes of personal aggrandizement in office; but it has no function in the efficient operation of the Federal Trade Commission. 

The situation is particularly acute in the handling of complicated antimonopoly cases. Here the economic facts are anything but simple, and the investigative work never comes to an end. It continues right up to and during the course of trial.

There is need for a close knitting of the investigative and trial aspects of the case from the beginning. Unless this is done, the facts collected by the Bureau of Legal Investigation tend to be scattered and unrelated. The lack of focus or any "theory of the case" means lack of direction in the assembly of data; and this makes the job of the Bureau of 

Litigation exceedingly difficult.

Recommended Changes. The Federal Trade Commission should adopt the practice now used so satisfactorily in the Antitrust Division. Not all 

Antitrust investigators leave a case upon the completion of the investigation; two or three are carried right through as assistants to the trial attorney who argues the case in court. Thus the trial attorney has available on his staff a few men who are completely saturated in the factual details of the case and have been on it from its start. 

If new facts suddenly have to be collected, it is not necessary to break in a new man and have him spend a couple of weeks in reading documents to catch up on the work that has already been done. If additional files are secured through subpoena by the judge, these men can immediately get busy fitting in the new evidence with that which has already been collected, Or, assume that the case goes into consent decree negotiations. Here the specialist in the negotiating techniques takes over--but again he is in a position to call in for advice and consultation those men who have become thoroughly versed in the facts of the particular case, In view of the current emphasis on antimonopoly work, the Commission might as well recognize at the outset that its present procedure is wholly unsuited for the handling of these cases; and it should institute these changes now rather than later, when it may be subjected to severe criticism for lack of achievement.

Litigation there åre separate divisions for antimonopoly trials and false and misleading advertising cases. In the Bureau of Legal Investigation there has developed a strong tendency to specialize particular investigators

The conciliatory work is entirely confined to false and misleading advertising and similar types of cases. contrast, the work of the Bureau of Industrial Economics lies most wholly in the antimonopoly field.

The two types of work differ in economic significance, in investigator; and trial technique, and in the character of the policy issues which are raised by the Commission. It would seem likely that if this issue were faced frankly by the Commission--particularly in the light of adaptations that have already occurred in its organization--there would be real advantages in a conscious reorganization of the agency to effect a fuller separation of these two functions. It is recommended that the Commission inquire into this further for the purpose of ascertaining its desirability. 

Use of Subpoenas. It is also high time that the Commission made use of one of most important statutory powers--the right to use subpoenas for investigatory purposes. Here, in view of recent decisions of the Supreme 

Court there is no plausible explanation for continued Commission reliance upon the old Tobacco opinion of Mr. Justice Holmes, The effect inevitably is to waste the Commission's funds and resources in a prolonged campaign tc persuade business men to relinquish voluntarily the incriminatory evidence of antitrust violations.

It means that the Commission must engage in itincrant hearings all over the country to fill out--and only impartially the gaps with testimony from smaller competitors. It means too that cases go to administrative hearing only partially prepared; add subpoenas may then have to be secured from trial examiners to permit completion of the necessary evidence, The point is that while the Commission wastes its small budget in this reckless fashion, it is difficult to make a valid argument for its need for more funds. The usual argument still heard within the Commission that use of the power of subpoena would merely invite a lawsuit based on the Tobacco case has little relevance today.

Compliance Activities 

The Commission has given a belated recognition to the importance of securing compliance with its decrees in its recent establishment of a 

Compliance Division. 

Obviously, an agency cannot devote all of its funds to policing past decrees; it must constantly take on new business. The tendency on the part of both the Antitrust Division and the Federal Trade Commission has been to secure a decree, close its files, and forget the whole business. As this fact has constantly been brought home to them--in criticism by Congressiona committees--both have done the next best thing of setting up a small sectio overloading it with work, and then leaving the staff to work out its own solution, Obviously, this is no answer either,

IV T- Rev.

Correction can lie only in setting up compliance work as a major part of the Commission's functions; There must be, first of all, a staff large enough to handle the job. Because of the little work that has been done in this field, compliance cases represent novel investigative and litigative problems. For this reason, it would seem highly desirable to abandon the sharp bureau distinctions between the invesigational staff and the compliance staff. Just as in the handling of major antimonopoly cases, the two staffs working on a particular case should be emerged at the outset; and they should carry on as a unit from the investigation stages through to final settlement.

Under the statutes, compliance cases must now be handled by the United States district attorneys. This procedure was originally employed in the trial of all antitrust cases under the Sherman Act, and was so unsuccessful that it was abandoned. An informal arrangement might be worked out permitting Commission attorneys to take the major responsibility in these cases. If this is not possible, the statutes should be revised to secure this result. United States district attorneys have neither the time, the technical resources, nor the inclination necessary for the successful completion of compliance litigation in the antitrust field. 

Conciliatory Work 

Although the expansion of the stipulation and trade practice technique is fairly recent and offers promising results, both seem to be relatively cumbersome procedures as they are now used. Weeks and frequently months intervene between the beginning of negotiations on a stipulation and its final formal issuance. Even longer time intervals exist with respect to trade practice conferences, although here, of course, the problem is considerably more complex because of an entire industry--and frequently a complex array of trade practices-is involved, It is urgently recommended that the Congress develop a simpler and more expeditious method of settling relatively minor offenses. One likely device would seem to be the citation procedure now used in a large number of administrative agencies. The person charged with violation is called in for informal discussions with representatives of the agency; and the situation is corrected quietly and speedily without the issuance of formal complaint or formal instrument of settlement. If the violation is repeated after a first or a second warning, the more formal administrative procedures of the agency are then called into play.

The Commission is currently considering this kind of a proposal, but up to the present no action has been taken, The use of this procedure, it is believed, would do much to end the cluttering of dockets within the agency; it would free the personnel for more responsible work; and it would also increase the popularity of the Commission with business.

IV 4. Presentation of its Program Relations with Congress 

The relations of the Federal Trade Commission with Congress have been typically those of an agency active in a highly controversial area of government. Attack has come from two directions: (1) those who are dissatisfied with the Commission because it has not gone far enough in enforcement and been vigorous enough; and (2) those who feel that the agency has men been a heavy cross to bear by business throughout the country. Thus the 

Commission has always been in the unpleasant position of seeming never to satisfy any one on either side of the fence. 

Inadequacy of Presentation. The great weakness of the Commission in its relation to Congress has been its conventionality and its timidity. Its annual reports are, like most of such documents, singularly unreadable; and the real life of the agency is lost in a flood of statistics which even as statistics fail to illuminate its work. This situation is less true of the data submitted to the Appropriation Committees; here the difficulty has been that the committees are so anxious to get through all of the agencies as rapidly as possible that there is no time to hear the facts on any one agency.

When the Commission's representatives have appeared before investigating committees, the attitude has been consistent whether the committee was friendly or unfriendly. In general, the Commission presents an array of colorless statistics, it gives a formalized version of its operations, and then stands on its record. As a result, friendly committees tend to become almost as critical as those whose basic premise is the undesirability of the antitrust laws. With some exceptions such as its testimony on amending 

Section 7 of the Clayton Act the pattern of presentation is routine and fixed.

Needed Improvement. The basic need is for a fresh approach to the whole problem of providing intelligence on its activities to Congress. In general, the Antitrust Division of Justice has set up a pattern which has been far more effective. Here the attempt has been to present some of the monopoly practices of particular industries where Justice has felt that corrective action was needed. The Division gives a colorful word picture of the dynamics of a few giant corporations engaged in throttling its competitors. It gives the facts on concentration for a few sample industries, and outlines the power channels which have been built up to insure control by a key segment. It describes the practices which have been used to absorb or kill off recalcitrants; and analyzes the pricing techniques which have been used when monopoly was achieved. A few candid instances of this character give a far more vivid and understandable picture of industrial practices tha a cerload of unrelated statistics.

The Commission, however, has veered away from this kind of presentatio on the ground that it smacks of "propaganda." The usual explanation given i that the Commission is more than an enforcement agency; it is also a judicia

IV T- Rev. body. There is impropriety in a judicial body's making any statement on the particular facts of a case before it has reached final settlement. 

Accepting this latter statement, it would still seem possible for the Commission to discuss publicly cases which have reached final settlement. 

The Cement Institute decision, for example, has set all industry by the ears; and has resulted in the Capehart Resolution which provides for study of the problem to the end of legalizing the basing point system. 

Amid all of the conflict on the interpretation of the decision and the speculation as to the Commission's future policy in this field, the Commission--in the initial period--remained silent. It was not until the flood of publicity accumulated in the press and the trade journals that the agency reconsidered its previous policy and decided to make public its own views. Since then a policy statement on the basing point problem, and a number of speeches and articles by top officials in the agency, has done much to clarify the Commission's position and program. 

The point is that the Federal Trade Commission is a dual purpose body. 

It has judicial functions. It also has enforcement functions. Thus, the agency bows its head silently under attack--because of the proprieties involved in its being a judicial body it is in effect abdicating as an enforcement agency.

To be an effective instrument in the antitrust field--for enforcement purposes--the agency must boldly state its program and policies. It must illuminate its work with the hard facts of monopolistic practice in industry; it must outline its campaign of action with vigor and drama; it must act as if it means business. 

Failure to do this has been the outstanding weakness of the Commission and accounts for the indifference which some of its friends in Congress have felt towards it.

Public Relations 

The Federal Trade Commission has failed markedly in the field of public relations. This has been largely due to the same insistence that it is a quasi-judicial body and should not engage in "propaganda"; that it should allow the decisions and rulings to speak for themselves. 

Disadvantages of Attitude. This policy has had two serious disadvantages. One is that, since the Commission is more than a judicial body, it has been subject to attacks to which courts are generally immune. These attacks have currently culminated in the O'Hara bill and the Capehart resolution--both threats of real consequence to the work of the agency. 

By its policy of silence, the Commission has allowed itself to become peculiarly susceptible to attack, The second disadvantage is that its emphasis upon its judicial functions has thrown out of focus the responsibilities of the Commission as an enforcement agency.

There has been no vigorous effort to make known the work of the agency; to publicize the kinds of offenses against which it believes action is needed in the public interest; to make specific proceedings serve as "examples" to deter others from engaging in similar practices.

IV T- Rev.

With certain conspicuous exceptions, even its economic reports--containing startling data on the changes that have occurred in the economy--have failed to secure space in the press. And, so far as general publicity is concerned, the legislative program of the Commission is non-existent. 

Character of Releases. A major problem has been the character of the public releases issued by the Commission. Usually such releases appear only after formal action by the Commission, such as the issuance of a complaint or a cease and desist order. Often their language is almost identical with that in the formal documents, and is completely unintelligible to the average newspaper man, Such releases are prepared by the Public Relations staff, but they must be submitted to the lawyer working on the case. By long experience the Public Relations men have learned that, if they sum up the legal documents in laymen's language for the press, this wording will be deleted by the attorneys. The releases are then submitted to the Chairman of the 

Commission; here there is usually no difficulty if the material has already been approved by the attorney handling the particular case. 

Relation to Enforcement, The Commission needs to recognize itself as, in part an enforcement agency--with all that this entails in public relations. Releases should be phrased not only to make clear the economic practices that are involved; they should also contain a reasoned explanation as to the purpose of the action and the remedies that are sought. The 

Commission should work up ways and means of permitting coverage of significant issues while the case is still in process of trial--without requiring representatives of the press to read thousands of pages of testimony. 

At the present time most statements of the staff of the Commission are cloaked in anonymity. It is "the Commission" which speaks rather than the particular attorney in charge of the case. This contrasts with the practice in the Antitrust Division where public statements are regularly made in the names of particular individuals. This seems to have the effect of making for more readability and informality in the form of the statements; and it is probably also good personnel policy. The success of an agency depends, in large part, upon its top staff; and there is no reason why these individuals should not receive some personal credit for their accomplishments.

The Commission has also failed to make use of the general press conference on issues of particular importance. This is a practice quite common in other agencies. Usually representatives of the press are invited in; a general statement is read and circulated; a brief informal statement is made by a top official of the agency; and the meeting is then open to questions from the press. As a means of securing needed press space for bringing a matter to general public attention, this procedure is very useful; and it also seems to have a good deal of appeal to reporters as a method of getting acquainted with the agency. The Federal Trade Commission might well use this and similar devices for making its activities known to the general public.

A-VI(A) APPENDIX VI - A Case Study: In The Matter of Dip Net Smelt Fishermen's Association, et al. (Docket No. 5055) Alleged Price Fixing on Fish and Fish Products in Violation of Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act 

This case was instituted on the basis of a detailed complaint from a responsible official of a government agency who was thoroughly familiar with the affairs of the industry. 

A letter setting forth the price-fixing practices in the association was sent to the Seattle office of the Federal Trade Commission on January 30, 1943. The complaint was forwarded to the Chief Examiner (now the Director of the Bureau of Legal Investigation) on February 1, 1943; and an accompanying letter from the head of the Seattle office stated that, if action were contemplated, it should be instituted immediately becasue of the seasonal character of the industry. 

On February 4, 1943, the Chief Examiner ordered the matter docketed, and referred it back to the Seattle office for investigation.

APPENDIX VIII Establishment of Planning Council 

At that time

First public mention of the proposal for a Planning Council came during the Senate Appropriations Committee hearings in June 1947. 

Chairman Freer mentioned the proposed establishment of a central planning office in the Office of Legal Investigations, and with close liaison with the general counsel and the chief trial counsel, staffed by expert attorneys and economists who will be able to keep reasonably current as to important developments in American industry. 

For some time previous the chiefs of the Bureau of Industrial Economics had been interested in the establishment of a planning agency. 

Pressure also came from the Bureau of the Budget which was recommending to all agencies in the Federal Government that groups be established to plan and formulate programs for work within cach agency. The Budget Bureau proposals were conveyed to the Assistant Secretary and Budget and Planning Officer of the Commission. Since Commissioner Mason was then in supervisory charge of the Bureau of Industrial Economics and the Commission's budget officer was a personal friend of that commissioner, the preliminary staff moves were centered in his office.

On July 17, 1947 Commissioner Mason addressed a joint memorandum to the other commissioners stating that the recent reorganization adopted by the Commission was designed to effectuate programs which would adequately carry out the mandates of Congress. He also submitted three separate plans--all embodying the same general principles and procedure for planning and clearing the Commission's program of work--but containing separate proposals with respect to the channeling of recommendations to the Commission. 

All three of the plans provided for staff meetings of the heads of the various important bureaus. One plan provided that staff meetings should be presided over by the Chairman of the Commission or another commissioner; another provided that the staff meetings should be presided over by the Director of the Bureau of Legal Investigation; and a third provided that the staff meeting should be presided over by the Assistant Secretary and Chief of the Budget and Planning Division, All three of the plans provided for discussion and recommendation of matters to be taken up by the Commission, but they differed as to the official who would present the recommendations of the staff meetings, with dissenting opinions, to the Commission monthly. All three of the plans provided that the Commission would act upon the recommendations within 30 days.

A-VIII 

T- Rev.

Thus the Commission had before it two opposing plans--one providing for a planning office in a single bureau and the other for a committee made up of the heads of the bureaus. One school of thought believed that the latter proposal would have the desirable effect of bringing together all of the staff heads of the agency--for the first time--in a joint discussion of their problems; and it was thought that such an arrangement might insure fuller cooperation. Placing responsibility in one individual, on the other hand, might result in his isolation from the practical work of the agency. 

It was also feared that placing a small staff in a particular bureau might have the effect of tying the group too closely to that bureau, with consequent indifference and possible antagonism by other bureau chiefs. Proponents of the other school were of the opinion that what is everybody's business is nobody's business; and that bureau heads would lack the time and resources necessary to give vitality to any planning work. 

As the Planning Council idea was subjected to further scrutiny, a major question centered around the chairmanship. Should the chairman be a member of the Commission sitting permanently? Or should a system of rotation be used which would permit all commissioners at some time to sit? Or should the chairman be selected from the staff? It was generally felt that rotation would result in lack of continuity of work; and with a commissioner permanently sitting, there might be hesitation on the part of staff members to take unpopular positions or to oppose a stand taken by the commissioner. 

On October 7, 1947 Commissioner Mason formally submitted to the Commission for consideration of a plan involving the same general principles and procedure for planning and clearing the Commission's program of work as were embodied in the three previous plans. Staff meetings were to be presided over by the Chief of the Planning and Budget Division. The latter and the Director and Associate Director of the Bureau of Legal Investigation would confer and prepare the agenda for the meetings from reports submitted by the different bureaus and divisions of the Commission to the Budget and Planning Officer and from complaints and information received from the public and industry by the Bureau of Legal Investigation. Matters on the agenda would be discussed at the staff meetings and the Director and Associate Director of the Bureau of Legal Investigation would submit the program recommended by the staff to the Commission. 

None of these plans submitted contemplated that this group would develop an integrated program of work for the fiscal year. 

On October 7, 1947, Commissioner Davis submitted to the Commission a plan, drafted on August 12, 1947, providing for the creation of an Investigational Planning Officer, with consultative powers, responsible only to the Commission. This plan discussed at length the objectives of planning, the dangers of a large group digressing into a debating society, the advantages of centralizing planning duties in a Planning Officer, etc. In its recent reorganization the Commission had delegated investigational planning duties to the Bureau of Legal Investigations, and Commissioner Davis recommended that the Commission designate the Associate Director of the Bureau of Legal Investigation as the Investigational Planning Officer.

A-VIII 

T- Rev.

As discussions progressed, Commissioner Davis and certain other commissioners began to favor a Planning Council--as the group now came to be called. Such a group--with a Director or other presiding officer, would have the following responsibilities: to plan and recommend an integrated program of work to be undertaken by the Commission each year in advance of the ensuing fiscal year, to make quarterly reports to the Commission showing progress made on the work program adopted by the Commission, and to assist the Commission in the presentation of its budget requests to the Bureau of the Budget and the Appropriation Committees of the Congress. 

On October 14, 1947, Chairman Freer submitted to the Commission a plan creating an Advisory Planning Council and a Chief Planning Officer. His plan provided that the Associate Director of the Bureau of Legal Investigation should be the Chief Planning Officer of the Commission and ex officio Chairman of the Advisory Planning Council and the Planning and Budget Officer of the Commission should be ex officio Secretary of the Council. It made the Council and Advisory Council to the Chief Planning Officer. 

Chairman Freer's plan also provided that the Advisory Council should have discussions with the view and end of affording greatest aid to the Chief Planning Officer in his formulating and recommending for the Commission's consideration a program of work to be undertaken by the Commission. It further provided that the Chief Planning Officer and the 

Budget and Planning Officer should make quarterly progress reports to the Commission and assist the Chairman of the Commission in presentation of the Commission's budget requests to the Bureau of the Budget and the 

Appropriation Committees of the Congress. 

It is evident that the major controversy centered around the choice of particular individuals to head up this planning work. The Commission was sharply split along the lines of the traditional division among commissioners --a division which is frequently brought into the open in the voting on particular cases and opinions written by Commission members. It was clear that the selection of a Planning Officer constituted a major problem; and that there was little likelihood of unanimous agreement on any single person. 

Subsequently, after further discussions with other commissioners and the staff, Commissioner Davis submitted a second plan. This constituted the compromise plan which was adopted by the Commission with certain amendments. 

The compromise plan submitted by Commissioner Davis provided for the creation of a Planning Council in the Commission, with members of equal rank, and specifically set out in detail and defined its powers and duties. It provided that the Associate Director of the Bureau of Legal Investigation should be Chairman of the Planning Council and that the Chief of the Budget and Planning Division of the Commission should be Secretary of the Council. 

The Council was to be made up of nine members. It further provided that the 

General Counsel of the Commission should be eligible to sit in on meetings of the Council and listen to the discussions at his discretion, but excluded him from membership on the Council so he would be free at all times to advise the Commission on any Council recommendation, not having previously voted on the matter.
