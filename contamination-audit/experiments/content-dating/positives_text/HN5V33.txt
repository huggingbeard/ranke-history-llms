HOW THE GAME IS PLAYED 

HAT AMERICA NEEDS is a good two-handed card game." I have said this for several years; and now, it seems, Gin Rummy is going to fill the bill. 

There are already millions of card players who are playing Gin Rummy, or who want to learn how.

Two play, using a standard deck of fifty-two cards. 

They cut, and holder of the low card deals the first hand, ten cards to each. The next card is turned up and is called the "up-card." If the non-dealer refuses the up-card, the dealer may take it. If the dealer does not want it either, the non-dealer draws the top card of the undealt pack-called the "stock"-and the play proceeds. Each player in turn picks up the last discard, or draws from the stock, and then discards, keeping his hand to ten cards. According to the rules, the pile of discards must be kept neat, so that only the top card is in view. If the players prefer, however, and so agree beforehand, they may glance through the discard pile. 

As in any rummy game, the object is to match up the cards into sets. Three or four of a kind are a set-

CULBERTSON SYSTEM 

three nines, for example. Sequences of three or more cards in the same suit form sets-Club 4-5-6 or Spade K-Q-J, for example. The ace may be used only in the sequence A-2-3, and not to "go around the corner," as

A-K-Q. 

Gin Rummy is sometimes called “knock rummy," because the game ends when a player knocks. You may knock whenever the unmatched cards in your hand count to 10 or less. Face-cards count 10 each; other cards their spot value. The ace, being only 1, is the most valuable card to hold.

Suppose, after discarding, you have only three unmatched cards-an ace, a deuce and a five. These total 

8, and you may knock. When you knock, your opponent shows his hand and you count up the score. 

The only cards that will count against your opponent are cards which are not matched and which he cannot "lay off." He lays off any card which matches one of your sets; for example, if you have shown Diamond 

7-8-9 he may lay off the Diamond 6 or the Diamond 10.

You win the difference between your unmatched total and his. Suppose his unmatched total is 15, and you have knocked with 8. You are the winner of the hand by 7 points. You write those 7 points down on the score sheet, and proceed to deal. The winner always deals the next hand, to even things up, for in Gin Rummy the dealer is at a slight disadvantage. 

If you knock and you do not win, the penalty is severe. Your opponent will receive 10 points in addition

GIN RUMMY 

to any difference in your totals. If you knocked with 8 and he also has 8, he scores 10 points and wins the hand; if he has only 6, he scores the difference as well, and will get 12 points in all. 

The biggest thrill is to "go gin"-to knock with all ten of your cards matched. For this you get a 20-point bonus and you win the hand automatically. Your opponent may lay off all his cards but you still get your 20 points. If he has any unmatched cards, you score those as well. 

The first player to score 100 points wins the game, and gets a 100-point bonus for winning it. Thus, if you score 105 points and your opponent has only 83, you get 22 points for the difference in your score, plus 100 points for winning the game. For a shut-out game the bonus is 200 points.

There is a further bonus, figured up at the end of the game, of 20 points for each hand you have won. This is sometimes called a "line" or "box" bonus, because after winning each hand a player should draw a line under his score. After the game is over he counts the lines, 20 points for each.

If you win a game in which you have won five hands, and your opponent has won only three hands, your bonuses, at 20 points per hand, amount to 100 points, against his 60, and you add a net of 40 points to your score. Conversely, the game winner may have to subtract some points from his grand total because the loser has won more "boxes."

stages of the game. Knocking is always a gamble, but in the early part of the game it is a good gamble. Your opponent may have been dealt a bunch of face-cards which he has not been able to get rid of. By knocking you may win 40, 50 or even more points.

The early part of the game covers the first four or five draws by each player. During this period you knock with a count of 10 or with anything less. 

The "middle game" begins when there are about 20 cards left in the stock-about two-thirds of the original stock. During the middle game, you should assume that your opponent has been able to develop his hand, achieving one or two matched sets and discarding all his high unmatched cards. During the middle game you should knock only if you have a count of 5 or 6 (or, of course, anything better). 

Of course, you might win by knocking with 9 or 10 during the middle game, but you could not expect to win much. Not enough, at any rate, to balance the risk of losing the hand. Every hand won means a 20-point bonus at the end of the game, and while these points do not count toward game they are well worth playing for.

Furthermore, by lying low with a count of 7 or so during the middle game, you may trap your opponent into knocking. If he does, and especially if you are able to lay off one of your cards on his matched sets, you will beat him and get the 10-point bonus for beating the knocker. 16

IV THE FIRST RULE OF PLAY 

HERE ARE A FEW standard principles of scientific play which the Gin Rummy player must learn, from experience or otherwise, before he can be a consistent winner.

The first rule of good play in this: At least three of your unmatched cards should be low cards. In general, you should try to develop your hand so that these three cards total at most 10 points. If you have more than three low cards, you should keep them when you draw them. If necessary, you should even pick them up from your opponent's discards.

Thus, if you hold on to every possibility for a matched set, at the expense of not keeping the low cards you are lucky enough to draw, you are, in effect, playing for "gin" every time. You must fill at least three matched sets in order to win. Experience tells us that this is a losing game, since just one hand in four ends with a player's going gin.

If your opponent during this early period should discard an ace or a deuce, you should pick it up and reduce your count further. In the first place, the very fact that he throws such a low card warns that he has a good hand and is likely to knock very shortly. You do not want to be caught with too high a count.

During the first few plays it is good to hang on to pairs of face cards, provided you do not hold too many of them. Your opponent will be reducing his own hand, and the first cards he will want to get rid of are kings, queens, jacks and tens. By keeping your pairs, you will often make a matched set very quickly. The only warning here is not to carry this policy to extremes. It works when you have been dealt three or four low cards; it also works when you can pick up or keep low cards without throwing away your high pairs. It must always be subordinated to the primary policy of getting and holding the three cards which total 10 or less. 22

THE BEGINNER'S WORST MISTAKE 

N GIN RUMMY, as in all card games of skill, there is a mathematical basis for many correct plays. But mathematics plays only a very minor part in Gin Rummy strategy. You do not have to be a mathematician to play well, nor do you even have to memorize mathematical rules laid down by others. 

There are, of course, some matters of probability which are apparent even to the beginner. It is obvious that you have a better chance of making a matched set if you have, for example, a pair of queens than if you have Spade K-Q. In fact, you have exactly twice as good a chance. You will make a matched set with the pair of queens if you draw either of two possible cards-either of the two other queens. There is only one card you can draw to give you a matched set with the Spade

K-Q-the Spade J, since an ace can be used to make a sequence only in A-2-3.

Likewise, a chance for a sequence is twice as valuable if it is open at both ends. Thus, Club 9-8 offers two chances to fill-the Club 10 or the Club 7; whereas 23

CULBERTSON SYSTEM 

Club 9-7 offers only one chance, the Club 8. With a choice of breaking up one of these combinations or the other, the one which offers the greater chance to fill must naturally be kept. 

Carrying this a bit further, the best three-card holding is one which can be made into a matched set with any one of four cards. Suppose you have two sequence chances, the Heart Q-J and the Club 6-5. Any one of four cards will give you a sequence Heart K, Heart 

10, Club 7 or Club 4. However, four cards in your hand are occupied in reserving these chances to make a sequence. If you have a holding like Spade 8-7 and 

Diamond 7, you still have four chances to fill-Spade 9, Spade 6, Heart 7 or Club 7-but only three cards in your hand are occupied in waiting for one of these chances to come through.

Realizing this, the beginner often has a tendency to pick up his opponent's discard when it gives him a three-card combination. Thus, holding Spade 8 and

Heart 7, if his opponent discards the Spade 7 he will pick it up. This is perhaps the worst mistake he could make.

In the first place, your picking up the discard will warn your opponent. He will not throw a spade close to the seven which may give you a sequence, nor will he throw another seven which may give you a matched set. Being fearful of throwing these cards, he may find himself later with a pair of sevens, or an incipient sequence in spades; and if you should later want to 24

GIN RUMMY 

break up your combination of Spade 8-7, Heart 7, you may be giving him a matched set.

Furthermore, your opponent can hold spades and sevens fairly safely, knowing that he can probably lay them off if by chance you should knock.

There are occasions when you do make such a pickup, but usually you would have done so anyway: 8-8-8 ◆ K-Q 10 o9 3-2 it

If your opponent discards a three or a deuce, you pick up, but only partly because it gives you a three-card combination. Far more important is the fact that it reduces your count at the same time. A four is about the highest card you should pick up for this purpose; rarely a five, and never anything higher. 

One of the comforting things we learn from the mathematics of Gin Rummy is this: If you are dealt a poor hand, your opponent has probably been dealt a poor hand too. If you need a certain card to fill a matched set, it is probable that your opponent does not need this card, and if he draws it, he will promptly discard it (unless it is a very low card). 

Therefore, unless your opponent's discard fills a matched set for you or is a very low card, you had better take your chance at drawing from the stock. Eventually 25

This method is also known as Hollywood Gin, because it started in the movie colony in Hollywood, one of the places where the present revival of Gin Rummy began.

In this variation, Gin Rummy remains a two-handed game and the two players actually play only one hand at a time. However, they score as though they were playing three games. There are several variations in scoring, but the most popular and most equitable scoring method may be described as follows: 

Suppose "A" and "B" are playing each other. They cut as usual, deal, and play the first hand. "A" wins the first hand by 21 points. This is entered on the score sheet to his credit, and play proceeds. “A” deals, and "B" wins the second hand by 16 points. The score of this second hand is entered to "B's" credit in the first game only, because it is the first hand he has won. 27

Game 1 Game 2 Game 3 21 16 12 33 

When "B" wins his second hand, it will be credited to him in games 1 and 2; when either "A" or "B" wins his third hand, it will be credited to him in all three games, 1, 2 and 3, and from that time on all hands he wins will be credited to him in all three games. 

That is: The first hand a player wins is credited to him in game 1 only; the second hand he wins is credited to him in games 1 and 2; the third hand he wins, and all subsequent hands, are credited to him in all three games.

NY GAME, like any trade or profession, develops a language of its own. Gin Rummy is no exception.

While a game is in progress one hears of "going gin" (having all ten cards matched); "going down for six" (knocking with a net count of 6); "lines" or "boxes"; "laying off," and other terms incomprehensible to anyone who does not play the game. Some of these terms were inherited from the parent game of rummy. An example is "live cards" and "dead cards."

A live card is one which has not yet shown up; a dead card is one which has been discarded and may no longer be drawn. A combination is "dead" when no card to match it remains in play. Thus, three kings are "dead" if the fourth king has been discarded. The sequence Heart 5-4-3 is dead if both the Heart 6 and Heart 2 have previously been discarded; it is dead at one end if, for example, the Heart 6 has been discarded but the Heart 2 is still at large. 

Knowing what cards are dead, and what cards may still possibly be drawn, affects the entire strategy of 31

CULBERTSON SYSTEM 

play: when to knock and when to refrain from knocking; what cards to hold, and what cards to discard. It would be silly, for example, to keep such a combination as Club 9-7 if the Club 8 is dead. A pair of tens, mathematically, are twice as easy to match as a combination like Diamond 6-4; but if one of the tens has been discarded, it becomes mathematically just as easy to match the Diamond 6-4, which is consequently a better combination to hold because, if the opponent knocks, it counts just half as much against you, 10 points as against 20. 

The easiest way to keep track of live and dead cards is to look back through the discards. The official laws of Gin Rummy say it is illegal to look at previous discards "unless the players agree in advance to do so.' 

The players should decide before the game begins whether they will permit looking at the discards or not. 

If one of the players is more skillful than the other, it tends to even the game, if looking at the cards is permitted. The better player can probably remember the discards more readily than his opponent.

VIII READING THE OPPONENT'S HAND 

WAS TALKING about Gin Rummy to one of my friends who has recently begun to play the game, and she made this comment: "It's so hard to remember the cards," she complained. "I have much more trouble than I ever had in bridge." "Perhaps your trouble comes from trying too hard,”

I suggested. "Remembering the cards is difficult in any game, including bridge, if you depend on memory alone. But in bridge you find yourself remembering the cards because every play means something to you. If the ace of spades wins the first trick, and you hold the king, it isn't the ace of spades you actually remember. 

It's the fact that your king is now good. It would be just as easy to remember the cards in Gin, if you go about it in the same way. "For example, when your opponent discards something, don't try to memorize his discard. Instead, ask yourself what the discard means. Why doesn't he want the card? If he throws away the eight of clubs, tell yourself that he isn't saving eights, and isn't trying for 35

CULBERTSON SYSTEM 

a club sequence like 10-9-8 or 8-7-6. Gradually you will get a picture of his hand. It's easy to keep a picture in mind; much easier than to remember a series of letters, or figures, or playing-cards.”

Nothing will help you more than getting a picture of your opponent's hand. From the cards he picks up from the discard pile you can learn what he does have, and from the cards he throws away you can judge what he does not have. You can even read your opponent's hand from the cards he does not pick up. 

Suppose on a certain deal the up-card is the Heart 8. 

Neither you nor your opponent takes it. After two or three plays, in which both of you discard nothing but inconsequential face-cards, your hand looks like this: K-K-K 8-6 7-5 4 A

XI PARTNERSHIP GIN RUMMY 

GIN RUMMY is primarily a game for two players, but it is possible to have three-handed, four-handed or six-handed games. In some cases, there are even "round-table" Gin Rummy games with eight, ten or twelve players. 

Three-handed Gin Rummy is played exactly the same as the two-handed game, as far as the scoring is concerned. Only two of the players can play at a time, of course. 

First, the three players cut. The lowest stays out and the other two play the first hand. This is scored to the credit of the winner, and the loser drops out, being replaced by the player who was idle during the first hand. 

This continues, the winner always remaining in the game and the loser always dropping out. 

The game ends when one of the players reaches 100.

The scores are totalled, and each player pays each other player who has a higher score. Suppose “A," "B" and "C" are in the game. "A" scores 106, “B” 82 and “C” 

65. "A" wins 24 from "B" and 41 from "C," a total of 47

65. "B" loses 24 to "A" but wins 17 from "C," so his net loss is 7 points. "C" loses 41 to "A" and 17 to "B," so his total loss is 58.

If one of the players is shut out, the winner of the game gets an extra 100 points from him, but this does not affect the score of the player who was not shut out. 

Four-handed Gin Rummy is a partnership game. The players cut for partners. If they are playing at a regular bridge table, the partners sit across the table from each other, as they would in a bridge game. Each player plays a regular two-handed game against one of his opponents and then, after each hand is finished, he plays the next hand against the other opponent. One player on each side deals the first hand, but after that both members of the side that won the last hand deal the next.

In playing, the regular laws of Gin Rummy are followed. The "four-handed" game consists, in fact, of two separate two-handed games played simultaneously. 

However, they are scored as one hand. If "A" and "B" are partners against "C" and "D," and if "A" wins his hand against "C" by 15 points, while "D" wins his hand against "B" by 13 points, the A-B partnership wins the first hand by 2 points, and that is the only score entered. 

The game is not ended, in four-handed play, until one side reaches 125 points. However, the bonus remains 100 points for winning the game.

XIV THE GAME OF PERSIAN RUMMY A Four-Handed Partnership Rummy Game 

The game of Persian rummy is a variant of the better-known game of 500 rummy, except that the partnership factor is added and the deck is increased by the addition of jokers. These add greatly to the element of skill in the game.

In some respects Persian rummy is a more scientific game even than Contract Bridge, in the opinion of many expert Bridge players; but it has the advantage of being very simple, and easy to learn, and skillful play is not necessary to the enjoyment of the game. 

No laws for Persian rummy have been previously published. As in the case of all games, the rules which are followed by the best players are considered the "official" laws of the game. The following instructions for playing Persian rummy are based on the procedure of the best players.

THE DECK AND THE DEAL 

Persian rummy is played with a 56-card deck-the regular 52-card deck plus four jokers. No deck of play- 56

The other deck will still be a standard 52-card deck and may be used for Gin-rummy, Bridge, and other games which require no joker.

GIN RUMMY SCIENTIFIC PLAY 

The two prerequisites of scientific Persian rummy play are card-reading and a system of partnership signals.

Card-reading is the same as in any rummy game (see pages 35 to 38). You watch the cards that the other players discard and the cards they pick up, and try to get a picture of the cards they need and the cards they cannot use. In Persian rummy, a defensive game is better tactics than an effort to “go out" on every hand. You should try to build up your own and your partner's melds, and to prevent your opponents from building up their melds. You will usually score more points by melding than you can score by going out.

Often you are not hasty in melding. For example, with three of a kind it pays to hold on to them for a while. If you can draw the fourth, the set will count double for you. It also pays, very often, to leave some card in the discard pile which would make a meld for you if you picked it up. Usually you will be able to pick the card up later, and meanwhile some other card may be discarded on top of it which will be valuable to you. 

Remember, you cannot pick up from the discard pile unless you can immediately meld the bottom card you pick up.

It is very difficult to remember all the cards another player picks up from the discard pile, because he often picks up four or five cards at a time. Trying to remem- 61

CULBERTSON SYSTEM 

ber all these cards slows up the game and wastes time. 

The experts therefore speed up the game and make it a bit more pleasant with this rule: Cards picked up from the discard pile are left face up on the table, not put in the player's hand. In this way everyone at the table knows what cards were picked up. 

Since Persian rummy is a partnership game, it helps you just as much to build up melds for your partner as to make melds for yourself. Therefore a system of partnership signals has been devised, so that the partners may help each other. Most of the good players stick to a fairly simple signalling system, of which the following are the high-lights: 

Discarding an ace, king, queen or jack asks your partner to discard a joker for you, if he has one. 

Discarding a ten or a joker asks partner for an ace. 

Signals are off if you meld before discarding. If you want your discard to serve as a signal, postpone melding until your next turn. If you want to discard a high card, but do not intend it as a signal, make some meld so that your partner will not read your discard as a signal. 

Signals are also off after the first few plays-some good players use the ten as a signal only on the first or second play. They use a discard of a nine to call for an ace if the discard is made on the first play only. After that, the discard of the nine has no meaning as a signal. 62
