PRECISION MEASUREMENTS 

In any series of measurements of the same physical quantity, it is found that the individual readings, or results, differ from one another. These differences in absolute measurements are sometimes very great, more often very small, depending upon both the quantity measured and the instruments used; e.g. determination of the velocity of light; wave lengths of light. No physical measurement is of any scientific value unless the numerical estimate of its reliability is known. The best numerical measure of the reliability of a result is that obtained when all the known sources of error have been eliminated or corrected. This is known 1

Precision Measurements 

2. 3. 

as the PRECISION or the PRECISION MEASURE of a result. 

By the ACCURACY of a RESULT is intended the DEGREES OF CONFORMITY be tween the RESULT OBTAINED and the TRUE VALUE of the quantity measured. 

The errors that occur in the measuring of any physical quantity are mainly due to: a) Imperfections of the instruments; b) Errors in making readings; c) Faulty computations or incorrect methods. of observation. We shall be concerned here chiefly with the first two. a) Instrumental errors will arise from unscientific apparatus or from faulty adjustment of some part or parts. These errors may be constant or variable. It frequently happens that such errors cannot be entirely removed, but they may be greatly reduced by comparison with results obtained from other instruments or taken by other observers. b) The second class of errors is not to be understood as mistakes in calculation or the errors that may occur in recording observations. The errors referred to in the second class are accidental errors of observations over which the observer has no control; e.g. law of dispersion in artillery. If a number of readings be taken of the length of a metal bar, the observations will be found to be minus as often as they are plus. The effect of such errors is diminished by taking a great number of readings. For it can be shown that if (n) readings be taken, the mean or average of these (n) readings is the square root of (n) times as accurate as any single reading. Thus, if we take 9 readings of the length of a metal bar, the mean of the 9 readings is three times as reliable as any single reading.

Use of the micrometer caliper in measuring linear distances.

THEORY 

Several kinds of measuring instruments operate on the principle of an accurately measured screw pitch. The pitch of the screw is the distance between threads; and in one revolution the screw moves through a distance equal to this pitch. In the micrometer caliper and in the spherometer the screw itself moves carrying with it a torsion head or disk which is graduated into precise fractions of whole turns of the screw. In the comparator, the screw is fixed but the nut in which it turns is movable and one revolution of the screw moves the nut through a distance equal to the pitch of the screw.

In the micrometer, the screw moves to and from a stop which is one piece with the frame that holds the screw. The end of the screw is perfectly even so that when contact is made between it and the stop the two are flush. The arm of the frame has a longitudinal scale which is graduated into whole turns of the screw. The screw carries with it a graduated torsion head which may be calibrated in any desired fraction of a turn.

Experiment Two

In measuring an object suppose the torsion head has moved over the longitudinal scale a distance of 6 units. Experiment Four THEORY 

The comparator is an instrument for measuring horizontal lengths and operates on the same general principles as the micrometer screw: and the spherometer. It differs from these in that it consists of a fixed screw carrying a movable nut. The screw is supported at both ends and to one end is attached a disc divided into one hundred equal parts, therefore giving a reading to hundredths of a turn. The movable nut controls a carriage which carries a microscope which is focused on the object to be measured. 

On the frame which holds the screw and nut is a scale which is graduated into whole turns of the screw. If the pitch of the screw is .5mm, then the scale will be grad-. uated to half millimeter divisions. And the disc being divided into one hundred equal parts will read to 1/100 of .5mm or .005mm. 

The carriage which moves with the nut has an index mark which lies in the same vertical plane as the crosswires and the image when the microscope is focused on an object. In reading the instrument it is only necessary to read the number of whole divisions which this index has passed on the main scale and add to it the reading of the disc. Supposing that the index mark on the carriage has passed 25.5mms on the main scale. On the end of the screw which carries the disc is another index mark by which we read the disc. Supposing that the 

74th division mark on the disc is in line with this index. Therefore we must add to the 25.5 mms on the main scale the quantity on the disc or 

74 x .005mm. Our reading then, for the above case, is 25.5 + .37 = 25.87mms. If it should happen that the screw index were between two divisions on the disc, say between 74 and 75, it would then be necessary to estimate this fraction to tenths. 

NOTE: It is sometimes quite difficult to determine whether the main scale index has passed a division mark on the main scale or not, due to the fact that the index mark and the scale mark seem to lie in the same straight line. 

The way 14

PROCEDURE 

1. Determine the value of the smallest division of the scale. 

2. Determine the pitch of the screw; i.e., the distance that the nut moves in one complete revolution. 

3. Determine the value of the divisions on the disc. 

4. Focus the cross-wires in the eye-piece of the microscope. Do this by moving the eye-piece in or out until the wires stand out most sharply. 

5. Focus the microscope on a certain inch mark on the rule. Do this by moving the microscope up or down until the image of the inch mark is most distinct.

NOTE: 

Since the main scale length is limited to 50mm, always have the main scale index near the zero of the main scale before moving over a given length. This will avoid having the terminus of the given distance outside the graduations. 

6. Have the fixed cross-wire coincide with one edge of the inch mark. 

7. The setting being perfect, read the main scale and disc. 

8. Make five readings on this same inch mark. To do this, merely turn the disc so that the cross-wires move from the mark. Then bring them back slowly until they are in the same position as before. Sometimes the screw will turn without moving the nut.

THEORY 

When a body is forced to change its shape or size it is said to be strained and the deformation it undergoes is called a strain, (change). 

The force within the body resisting this strain and tending to restore the body to its original condition is called a stress, (force). 

That property of a body by which it tends to recover from a strain is called elasticity. The elastic limit is reached when the strain becomes so great that the stress is not sufficient to restore the body to its original condition.

Hooke's Law states that within certain limits the stress (internal) is proportional to the strain, or:

STRESS/STRAIN = E the constant of elasticity.

To determine Young's modulus by bending for a given specimen of wood.

THEORY 

This experiment is designed to give some idea of the factors on 26

Experiment Nine which the strength and stiffness of a transverse beam depends. In the case of a beam of rectangular cross-section and supported at both ends, the formula is 3 M = Pg L 4 F a b Where P is the mass on the bar (gm) g the acceleration due to gravity (cm/sec/sec) L the length of the bar between supports (cm) F the average deflection (cm) a the depth of the bar (cm) b the width of the bar (cm) This formula is derived by Calculus. 

It is assumed that the bending is resisted entirely by the tension and compression of the fibres of the material. EXPERIMENT XXXII SENSITIVITY OF A GALVANOMETER OBJECT 

To determine the sensitivity of a moving coil galvanometer and to verify the law of deflection I = Kd. 

THEORY 

In this type of galvanometer a light coil of fine wire is suspended in a strong magnetic field. The magnetic field is supplied by a permanent magnet. The coil suspension consists of fine metallic ribbon and serves to conduct the current to the coil. The deflection of the coil due to a current is observed by means of a small mirror fastened to the coil and reflecting the image of a scale placed in front of the galvanometer. 

A current flowing through the coil sets up lines of force perpendicular to the lines of force of the permanent magnet and the interaction of these two fields causes a torque which causes the coil to rotate. If for a given current the rotation of the coil is great, the instrument is said to be sensitive. The sensitivity of the galvanometer may be expressed in various ways: 

1. Current sensitivity The value of the ratio of the galvanometer deflection to the current necessary to produce the deflection, or the deflection per unit current. 

2. Volt sensitivity The value of the ratio of the galvanometer deflection to the voltage across its terminals or the deflection per unit voltage. 

It is therefore the current sensitivity divided by the resistance of the instrument. 

3. Ohm sensitivity - The number of ohms which must be inserted in the circuit to obtain a unit deflection when the impressed voltage is unity or one volt. 

4. Figure of Merit - The ratio of the current in amperes to the deflection of the galvanometer or the current in amperes flowing through the galvanometer necessary to produce unit deflection.

To test a watt-hour meter.

THEORY 

The watt hour meter is a split phase induction motor. The aluminum disc is between the poles of one shunt and two series coils. The shunt coil is the potential coil. The series coils carry the current of the load to be measured. The interaction of these three coils produces the rotating fields and hence develops the torque that drives the rotor disc. Since the weight of the disc and shaft is small and the torque rather large in proportion, the ratio of the torque to the load is large, and the motor starts on very small loads. The aluminum disc also passes between the poles of two permanent magnets. The eddy currents thus produced in the disc act as a drag on the disc and the effects of this drag can be controlled by varying the position of the magnets with regard to the center of the disc. 

Since the value of the torque that drives the discs depends on the amount of power passing through the meter, the speed of rotation of the disc is in direct ratio to the power passing in the circuit. Hence one rotation of the disc corresponds to a definite quantity of electrical energy. value, called the constant of the meter, the disc.

EXPERIMENT XXXVIII VACUUM TUBE CHARACTERISTICS OBJECT 

To determine the characteristics of a thermionic vacuum tube.

THEORY 

If a conductor is heated to incandescence it will emit electrons. 

These electrons being negative will be propelled toward any positive body within their sphere. If the positive body consists of a conductor joined to the terminals of a battery and the negative end of the battery is joined to the heated conductor, then the emitted electrons will close the battery circuit and cause a current to flow. A radio vacuum tube consists fundamentally of a heated conductor the filament and a positively charged body - the plate. A grid is interposed in the path of the electrons and serves to regulate the flow to the plate. If a negative voltage is impressed on the grid it will repel the electrons and no current will flow in the plate circuit. If the grid is positive then a larger current will flow in the plate circuit since the flow of electrons will be aided. 

In this experiment you will be required to test the mutual conductance of the tube and obtain its amplification factor. These are functions which determine the value of the tube as an amplifier or detector. A high mutual conductance and amplification factor are desirable.

The mutual conductance of the tube is high when a small change in grid voltage produces a large change in the plate current. This general statement holds whatever the use of the tube. In practice the filament voltage and plate voltage are kept constant and the grid voltage varies through successive stages. The plate current is then read for each value of grid voltage. The grid volts are plotted as abscissae on a curve sheet and the plate current as ordinates. The slope of the curve taken at the working voltage 87

Experiment Thirty-Eight gives the mutual conductance of the tube. For example, if the tube were to be used with a grid biasing battery of 4 volts negative, the slope of the curve would be taken at that point. 

The amplification factor of the tube is also an important consideration. It is defined as the ratio of the change in plate voltage necessary to change the plate current a given amount, to the change in grid voltage that will produce the same change in plate current. This constant can be taken from the curve of the tube by taking a reading at some slightly different value of plate potential. This point should be plotted on the cross section paper in its proper position with reference to the grid voltage and the plate current flowing at the new value of plate potential.

EXPERIMENT XLV SPECTRA OF ELEMENTS AND COMPOUNDS OBJECT 

To obtain the dispersion curve of spectroscope by using lines of known wavelength and from this curve to analyze mixtures by determining the wavelengths of their spectral lines. 

THEORY 

Although the speed of light in a vacuum is the same for all wavelengths yet in a glass prism the speeds of the different wavelengths are different. The speed of red is greater so that the red is deviated more than the violet. In the direct vision spectroscope the dispersion between c and F lines of the hydrogen spectrum is 5°30' and so it is useful for measuring line spectra. Gases and vapors give forth only certain definite wave lengths. Each substance has its characteristic spectrum and since the spectrum of an elementary substance, for example sodium or hydrogen, will appear in any spectrum of a compound containing hydrogen or sodium, it is easy to see how the spectrum of an unknown compound, furnishes a method of finding the presence of the substance in the compound.

The direct vision spectroscope consists of a train of prisms made of various crown and flint glasses and replaces the single prism in the ordinary spectroscope. Because of the differences of dispersion produced by various kinds of glasses it is possible to obtain dispersion with very little deviation of the beam. The slit lenses and prisms are in nearly the same straight line and hence the instrument is pointed at the source. The collimator is a tube with an adjustable slit at one end and a converging lens at the other. When the slit is illuminated by the source the 100

Experiment Forty-Five 

collimator lens renders the light parallel. The light is then refracted through prisms and then dispersed and falls on the objective lens of the telescope which forms a real image in the focal plane of the telescope. By means of a micrometer screw operating a drum graduated O 100 the telescope may be moved across the whole spectrum. At right angles with the telescope is the scale tube with a scale at the outer end and a lens at the inner. A lamp is placed at the scale end and this light passing through the scale is converged by the lens and reflected by the prism into the telescope so that a real image of the scale is superimposed on the spectrum. In this way distances between the spectral lines may be read off directly from the scale. In the prism spectroscope a 60 degree prism causes dispersion and deviation. The telescope is moved around as in Exp. 44. There is a scale tube as in the direct vision spectrometer. 

A set of readings from the scale from substances that give spectral lines of known wavelengths can be obtained. With this data a dispersion curve can be made, plotting the scale readings as ordinates and the wavelengths as abscissae. Gas or vapor of an unknown substance illuminates the slit, the wavelengths of the spectral lines can be found from this curve and the nature of the substances determined.
