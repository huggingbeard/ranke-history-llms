§ 1-A GENERAL VIEW 

THERE is a story told of an English business man whose engagements were very many each working day, who chose for his telegraphic address the significant words, "Undisturbed, London." This man knew how to control his thoughts, and out of that control emerged his attitude towards life-undisturbed.

Think for a moment what it means to exercise control over one's thoughts. It means being able to harbour only the kind of thoughts that will be beneficial in one's life. It means order instead of chaos, harmony instead of tumult. It means definiteness of aim instead

of a policy of drifting. It means throwing overboard all the useless lumber in the mind and living a happy contented life. 

The question naturally arises-is this possible? Is it possible to acquire a carefree state of mind as far as that means the stamping out of undesirable thoughts? The answer is-it is possible, with training. 

It is quite easy to begin the practice of thought control, and easy to acquire a fair degree of proficiency if the student will be reasonable and not expect to move mountains right away. 

You would not think it reasonable if after only a month's tuition and practice at the piano a friend asked you to play the "Moonlight Sonata" or a Waltz by Chopin. You would reply: "I'm only a beginner. I'll need a good deal more practice before I can play music of that degree of difficulty."

The mind is a much more complex instrument to master than the piano, and yet I have known students of thought-control expect to 10

have full control over their thoughts after a few weeks' practice. They complained: "It doesn't seem to work in my case," forgetting that they have not given it a fair chance. Let them give to it the same patient persevering practice they give in learning to play a musical instrument, and success in an ever increasing degree can be guaranteed. 

§ 2-WHAT THOUGHT-CONTROL IS 

In essence, thought-control is simply the substituting of one thought for another; a good thought for a harmful thought, let us say. It means thrusting aside thoughts that tend to disturb your peace of mind, and replacing them by good thoughts, inspiring thoughts, brave thoughts, hopeful thoughts, cheerful thoughts. It is comparable to a switch whereby at a touch you change a dark or gloomy room into one bright and cheerful. 

If it is as simple as that, you ask, why speak of difficulty? The reason lies in the nature of the mind itself. Thousands of thoughts enter 11

the mind every hour.

They enter by three gateways: (1) The gateway of the Senses sensations of sight, hearing, contact, pressure, smell, taste, temperament and organic sensations; (2) the gateway of the Feelings, i. e., the Emotional gateway; (3) the conative gateway; through it enter all our impulses, cravings, desires, and wants, urged forward by the conative spirit or life force within -the mainspring of all human action. § 3-HINDRANCES TO THOUGHT-CONTROL 

The emotions are the principal hindrances. 

They come from the things we see and our way. of reacting to them; from ideas that enter the mind; and from disturbances in any bodily organ.

There are seven primary emotions—Fear, Anger, Disgust, Wonder, Exhilaration, Depression or Despondency, and the Tender Emotions. Each of these emotions has its 12

Fear arouses the in stinct of Flight or Concealment, Anger arouses the instinct of Pugnacity, Disgust arouses Repulsion, Wonder arouses Curiosity, Exhilaration arouses Assertiveness, Depression arouses Self-Repression, and the Tender Emotions arouse the Parental Instincts. 

The complexity of our emotional life can be traced back to these seven primary emotions. 

Derived from them, really compounds of them, are a number of secondary emotions.

Besides the instincts we have mentioned are four others which have a great effect upon our lives-the Reproductive, the Gregarious, the Acquisitive, and the Constructive. Of these, the Reproductive and the Gregarious are the most important in connection with thought-control. 13

§ 4-HEREDITARY MODES OF ACTING AND HOW THEY AFFECT THOUGHT-CONTROL 

We are born with certain tendencies or modes of acting, which, though they partake of the nature of instinct, differ from instinct in that they have no characteristic emotions. The most important of these are Suggestibility (under which we include Auto-Suggestion),

Imitation, Sympathy, Play, Habit. Under their influence we tend to act in ways that greatly affect our lives. Let us look at two of these tendencies. (a) Suggestibility: No student of thoughtcontrol can understand what he has to battle with in the constant conflict of thoughts without some knowledge of Suggestibility.

There are certain thoughts that enter the mind in a peculiar manner. They make a forcible entrance and any other thought struggling for entry is pushed to one side. These thoughts are suggestions, and the state of our readiness to receive suggestions is called sug- 14

HEREDITARY MODES OF ACTING 

gestibility. Our suggestibility varies from hour to hour and with every change of mood. It is increased by emotion, fatigue, nervous exhaustion, and in moments of panic. 

Suggestion thoughts, besides opposing all other thoughts, weaken the reasoning faculties; they are at their strongest when we are under the influence of an emotion. As an illustration, note the readiness of a crowd to proceed to acts of violence when incited to do so by a leader capable of exciting their emotions; or the readiness of an audience to accept a panic suggestion when some foolish person shouts "Fire!" 

Suggestions come to us in many ways; from books, newspapers, advertisements, and from the people with whom we come in contact. 

Liability to suggestion depends to a large extent upon our receptiveness at the time when the suggestion makes its attack, and on the degree of our knowledge. If you know a great deal about any subject you will at once be on your guard when a suggestion is made 15

to act contrary to your knowledge. On the other hand, if a suggestion seems to be in agreement with your knowledge, it has you in its grip. (b) Auto-Suggestion: Suggestions not only come to us from other people and the outside world; they also come from ourselves-from our inner world. Whenever you say to yourself "I'm very tired," "I cannot do that," "I think I'll take it easy today," you are making suggestions to yourself; for a great part of 

Auto-Suggestion is simply talking to oneself. And

These talks with ourselves have a great effect upon our lives; they either make or mar. 

They affect for good or ill our health, our ambitions, our relations to other people-in short, our whole attitude towards life. as they are made unthinkingly, these daily talks do their work unnoticed. Successful people owe their inspiration to them, for their little talks are of the right kind: "I'm going to make my mark," "I can do this or 16

HEREDITARY MODES OF ACTING 

that." Failures, as surely, owe their lack of initiative to this talking-this "It's no use my trying," "I can't make any headway." 

Auto-Suggestion, like habit, owes its power to repetition. What we repeatedly say about ourselves we tend to become. Test this. Make a note of the habitual sayings of the people you know well, and you will realise the truth of what I have said.

If you encourage the right kinds of talks (Auto-Suggestions) not only will your health and prospects benefit thereby; you will also make it easier to control your thoughts. (c) Habit: It is a peculiarity of our mental makeup that we tend to follow on the old lines. We dislike new ways of doing things; we are creatures of habit.

A habit has its origin in one of two sources; it comes either from instinctive tendencies, or from a chance happening which pleased us at the time and invited us to repeat what gave us pleasure. The drug-taker, for instance, liked the effect of the drug; it gave him a pleasur- 17

able feeling the first time he took it, and to regain that feeling he takes a second dose on another occasion. Other doses follow on other days, until finally a habit of taking the drug is established.

It is very important that you understand that habits are formed by repetition. Acts, thoughts, sayings, mannerisms, etc., repeated time after time, become habitual. When a habit is set, or fixed, it has the following recognisable features: It always acts in the same way, it dislikes interference, it needs little or no attention.

All habits, good or bad, begin in the same way-through repetition. It will pay you to reflect upon this, especially in its relation to thought-control. 

§ 5-OUR TEMPERAMENTS 

All our tastes, all our likings, all those traits which shape our attitude towards life, are determined by our temperaments; they colour our thoughts to a great extent. 18

OUR TEMPERAMENTS 

Owing to their complex nature, very little is known about the temperaments. They may be described, popularly, as the glasses through which an individual views life, or as an inherited tendency to view the world from a certain standpoint. Thus, the Sanguine temperament is hopeful, confident, impulsive, changeable; the Choleric temperament is passionate, determined, and not easily influenced; the 

Phlegmatic temperament is sluggish and often weak in will-power; the Melancholic temperament is gloomy, uncertain in conduct, keenly susceptible to sense impressions, hence often highly imaginative; the Nervous temperament is marked by great sensitiveness, excitability, and activity of the nervous system, with a strong tendency to emotional impulses and excesses.

The temperaments are never found pure in any individual; they tend to shade into each other. Hence a man who may be melancholic in temperament is not always melancholyhe has his moments of jollity and fun. Be- 19

cause of this tendency of temperaments to shade into each other, we should be careful how we label ourselves as belonging to or under the influence of any one particular temperament. We should all seek to cultivate an approach to a temperament that will be sunny, cheerful, not easily discouraged, determined to make the best of life. The ability to control our thoughts will help us to attain this temperament. 

§ 6-THE ROOT OF THE DIFFICULTY 

We have seen some of the hindrances to thought-control, and some of the inherited tendencies which affect our thought-world. We are now in a position to get to the root of the difficulty-a knowledge of the Self. 

If you look at your reflection in a mirror, you are looking at your Self. That is how you look to other people-a self different from any other self. But there is an inner self which the world cannot see; the fundamental self which is always struggling to assert its 20

THE ROOT OF THE DIFFICULTY 

supremacy over the other selves in your being. 

The other selves? Yes; a little consideration will convince anyone that he is a bundle of selves each struggling to get its own end.

That is why we find ourselves doing things we do not want to do, saying things we do not want to say. What is the cause of this warfare between the different selves? It is due to our conative nature-the life force that is constantly spurring us on to action. When this life force comes into conflict with our desires (which themselves may be in conflict) and with our motives, trouble ensues.

The Real Self-the you that is fundamentally you-is the Will which has constantly to assert itself if it is to control the different selves in our make-up. Sometimes these selves capture the Will and bend it to their own purpose; they work principally through our desires and by twisting and turning our motives until we find it difficult to know the 21

real motive for our doing something from the false motive.

To sum up. The total Self is your real self plus the other selves which you feel, from their warring against you, are not you. The total self is a compound of powerful impulses and instincts, likes and dislikes, preferences and aversions, desires and motives. And behind, and working through all, is the conative spirit or life force constantly spurring the self to some mode of action. Thought-control resolves itself therefore into an attempt to establish the supremacy of the Real Self. 22

§ 7-A SIMPLE APPROACH TO THOUGHT- CONTROL 

FIRST of all, get it quite clear how thoughts enter the mind-through the Senses and the Feelings, and through Conation, the life force within that is constantly urging us on to do things.

When one reviews the field and grasps in full measure the magnitude of the task, one is apt to be appalled; to control such powerful influences seems a frightening task. 

The right thing to do is to forget the magnitude of what lies ahead. Approach it by easy stages. From the outset begin to cultivate the cool, calm, collected attitude of mind. "That is not easy," you will probably remark.

No, it is not easy if looked at from the point 25

of view of the finished product, so to speak; but to try is easy. Make it a rule to try each day, amid all the hurry and bustle of life, to be cool, calm, and collected in all you do. I'm not fretting about your failures-you'll probably have a great number. All I am concerned with is that you try each day to acquire this attitude of mind. Repetition will bring its own reward. 

Here are some other things you must do.

Cultivate a right mental attitude. By mental attitude I mean the way you approach anything you have to do. If you think a thing difficult you limit your powers and the thing becomes difficult. It is right (and wise) to recognise that a thing is difficult, but it is wrong (and foolish) to dwell on the difficulty. 

Cultivate the attitude that there is a way (and that it is up to you to find it) to overcome the difficulty. This is a right mental attitude. 

Adopt it and you'll find your tasks easier to master. 

Cultivate interest in what you are doing; 26

A SIMPLE APPROACH 

things become easier to do when you are interested.

Guard against fatigue, for it is one of the great enemies of thought-control. Rest when tired in mind or body, and you will find it much easier to control your thoughts.

Learn to control your imagination by adopting a critical attitude. Imagination tricks us all at times. When it tends to make you afraid or nervous, the critical attitude is the best mode of attack: "Why am I afraid? What is there to be afraid of?" To treat imagination in this way robs it of a great part of its power. 

Develop your Will-Power by easy stages. 

You will know when you are using your willpower by a feeling of resistance. Begin on little things-any undesirable habit (not the bad habits of long standing) will provide you with practice. Practise till you experience the feeling of resistance, then drop the practice for the time being and take it up again later. 

When you feel your powers growing and the 27

feeling of resistance weakening, go on to bigger things.

Cultivate concentration-the power to keep the mind on one thing at a time. An easy way to begin is to repeat multiplication tables. 

They should be sufficiently difficult to provide a test. First you must learn the tables and then repeat them to yourself from memory. A good series of tables is 12 times 12 up to 12 times 20, 13 times 12 up to 13 times 20, and so on with 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, and 20 times 

12 up to 20 times 20. Avoid strain in practising. At the first hint drop the exercise for the time being. 

§ 8-How To SWITCH THOUGHTS ASIDE 

There are two methods: Direct switching and Indirect switching. In Direct switching you must work by substitution; you substitute a thought by its opposite. Thus if a hate thought troubles you, you switch the mind over to a love thought. Let me make this clear. If the thought of Mr. A causes hatred, 28

SWITCH THOUGHTS ASIDE 

switch over to Mr. B-someone for whom you have a great regard. But remember, the switching must be done instantly. I shall deal with the reason of this shortly. If the hate thought is strong it keeps coming back. What is to be done then? You must keep switching it aside. With each switching it loses power.

Indirect switching is simply diverted attention. The old method when a man felt angry was to count up to 10 or 20. A better method is to act contrary to the thought; timid men simulate bravery, angry men simulate a pleased expression. Disraeli, the great English statesman, when a man injured him, instead of seeking to be avenged used to write the man's name on a slip of paper and lock it away in a drawer. "It is marvellous," he said, "how men I have labelled have a knack of disappearing." The only objection to Disraeli's method is that if employed universally, its effect upon the death rate would have to be considered.

Owing to individual differences each stu- 29

dent of thought-control must experiment until he finds which of the two methods of switching is best suited to him. In either method the switching must be done instantly. Here is the reason. Educationalists found that when a pupil goes immediately from one subject to another subject-say M to R, where either represents a language, a science, or some other subject of study-what he has learnt about M will be lost. M has had no time to establish itself in the mind, and consequently is forgotten-lost. 

§ 9-THINGS THAT HELP IN THE PRACTISE OF THOUGHT-CONTROL 

We have seen (§4,a) that suggestibilityour tendency to accept suggestions-is one of the things we have to guard against. A suggestion is always an invitation to act—to do something, to give way to something. If you value your peace of mind, make it a rule to scrutinise every suggestion that seems to appeal to you. The stronger the reasoning power 30

PRACTISE OF THOUGHT-CONTROL 

you bring to bear on suggestions, the less will be their power to harm you. A harmful suggestion is weakened by this critical attitude; a good suggestion is strengthened. 

Auto-Suggestion, as we have seen (§4,b), is sometimes helpful, sometimes hurtful, to thought-control. The harmful type is mainly used unconsciously. Check the tendency to say "It's just my luck," or "It's too late to mend." By constant repetition these sayings acquire the power of habits; they check initiative and encourage gloomy, despairing thoughts. Oppose these auto-suggestions with conscious suggestions. Say "Better luck next time." "It's never too late to mend." 

These thoughts encourage you to persevere.

I have not space here to deal with auto-suggestion further. I have dealt with it fully (as far as it affects thought-control) in my book, "Thought-Control in Everyday Life." 

Bad Habits have a powerful effect upon thought-control; they cause worry and annoyance and must be opposed by good habits of 31

an opposite character. Both bad and good habits have their origin in thoughts, ideas, acts, repeated many times. 

To cure a bad habit you must give it no exercise; it was formed by repetition; it must be opposed by repetition, that is, by good habits of an opposite character: vice-virtue, alcohol -water, bad temper-good temper. The habit should be one that cuts off the stimulus which causes the bad habit to function. It is not easy to break with the old habit, but every repetition of the new or opposing habit not only renders it easier, but brings about in the mind a proneness to perform the new habit and to forget the old one. 

There is the matter of lapses. With the vast majority of people lapses are sure to occur. The safest rule is to refuse to dwell upon them. Switch the mind away from them and redouble your efforts. Repetition, if persevered with, will help you to establish the new habit. 32

III § THE CONTROL OF EMOTION 

IN DISCUSSING suggestion, I touched on the advantage of the critical attitude. This critical or analytical attitude is the key to emotional control. To understand what this means, every time your emotions are stirred, more especially when stirred unpleasantly, study how you can profit by the experience. 

Imagine yourself a spectator looking calmly at your angry self, your fearing self, your nervous self. If you can do this, you will find that almost magically the emotion will tend to subside. The calm, critical attitude robs the emotion of its power, for an emotion can be powerful only when it can run on undisturbed or unchecked in any way.

It is easy to understand this. Imagine, for 35

a moment, a young man making a declaration of love to his beloved, and, while declaring his love, trying at the same time to analyse the state of his feelings. The loved one would know at once that something was wrong. 

If you find it difficult, at first, to analyse the course of an emotion, begin the study by watching a play on the stage. When you feel it influencing you, ask yourself questions. "Why am I disturbed?" "Is my emotion due to the story or to the skill of the actors?" 

Watch, too, the people round about you. 

When you see them in tears, or excited, ask yourself the reason. A little practice of this kind will soon convince you of the value of a critical attitude in mastering or controlling, to some degree, an emotional state. It is the road to inner harmony and peace. 

John Wesley, the great itinerant preacher, knew this, for he was a shrewd man and a close observer of his fellows, as his Journal testifies. He was once attacked by a hostile mob. His brother Charles, describing his 36

appearance afterwards, said: "He looked like a soldier of Christ, for his clothes were torn to tatters." Then he adds: "Throughout the fight he carried a peace in the knapsack of his heart." There is a deep psychological significance in "the peace that passeth understanding." 

§ 11-THE FEELINGS 

Guard against strong feeling; it always tends to affect one's judgment. Many people who feel rather restless and unsettled, ready to fight with their own shadow, and who cannot understand what has caused the feeling, would find great relief if they were to get busy in some form of work or in some useful hobby.

A business man once told me that whenever he felt angry he used to apply to some task the energy aroused by his anger. "At such times," he said, "I get through a prodigious amount of work and feel quite happy when through with it." 37

Let us look at feeling from another aspect. 

Something has annoyed you and you feel deeply hurt. How do you feel about it a month afterwards? Most people will answer that its importance has dwindled away. There is not even a little kick in it; it is like stale news.

Think of this the next time you feel hurt. 

Say to yourself: "I'm not going to let this affect me; a little later it will not seem nearly so bad, therefore I'm not going to worry about it. I'm going to ignore it." 

§ 12-WORRY 

It has been well said that nine-tenths of the things one worries about never come to pass. 

The obvious thing to do, therefore, is to take these big odds-this sporting chance-and stop worrying.

Worry relies on repetition for its power. It is built up on repetitions; saying or thinking the same thing day in and day out. The cure for worry lies in refusing to encourage the 38

THE BLUES 

repetitions; the worrying thing must be switched aside every time it makes its appearance. If to the switching aside is added the critical attitude, worry will lose a great part of its sting.

The people who worry about the futureits uncertainty as regards their health or prospects-will find relief in the remedy, "Live from day to day." It is a healthy rule, a sane rule; for if you are conscious that you are living wisely each day there is no need to worry about the future. 

§ 13-THE BLUES 

This is a peculiar form of depression that is liable to attack anyone at times. Strange to say (and yet not strange when one knows the cause), busy people, clever people, the leaders in any movement are more liable to be attacked by the blues than people of a less strenuous life. They are so keenly interested in their work that they overstep the safety line in their expenditure of energy, and their work 39

becomes distasteful; or they lose heart, and everything is surrounded by gloom. The rea son is simple; they have exhausted themselves.

Nervous energy has a rhythm-a high period marked by buoyancy and great activity, and a low period marked by listlessness and inactivity. To avoid the reaction of the blues and its consequent influence on thought-control, look upon the depression as nature's hint to rest. Cease work for a time, or slow down as much as possible. If gloomy thoughts persist, seek relief in auto-suggestion. Say to yourself, "I know the cause of this depression and I'm not going to allow it to affect me. My work will be all right again when I am rested. 

I am in the low period of nervous energy. 

Soon nature, by my resting, will restore the balance, and once more my mood will be joyful and buoyant.” 

§ 14 FEAR 

A vigorous old hunter, in a narrow trail in 40

the Rocky Mountains, came face to face with a bear. Paralysed for the moment, he sought refuge in the brief prayer, "God help me."

Immediately the thought struck him that he had little claim on God, and he made haste to add: "If you can't help me, don't help the bear, and you'll see me put up a big fight.' 39 This old hunter realised, as all of us must realise, that fear as a state of mind must be fought against; we must either fight or go under. 

There are thousands of fears, and the greatest weapon to fight them with is belief in self. To acquire belief in self—your Selfask questions about yourself: "What things can I do? What things can I not do?" Put it down on paper, the good and the bad about yourself; your strong points and your weak points. The good and the strong points in your favour must be the foundation on which to build your belief in self. That foundation represents what you stand for and the things you can do. 41

Whenever you feel tempted to say, "I'm no good, everything goes wrong with me," look at your list-your foundation-and say: "It's not true I am no good; at least I can do the things I have written down here." Have faith in your foundation, and you will find you will be able to build on it. You will soon know you are really building, for before it is apparent to you it will be apparent to others. 

Probably they will comment on it, and this will encourage you to go on with the building process. Finally, whenever you are inclined to doubt your own powers (and by doubt I mean the feeling rather than any vocal expression), switch aside instantly the thought generated by the feeling. 

§ 15-ANGER 

George Bancroft, the great American historian, once remarked that any man could live to old age if he never lost his temper, no matter what the circumstances tempting him to lose control. He was over ninety at the time 42

ANGER 

he said this, and though, no doubt, other things contributed to his longevity, he evidently knew the danger of giving way to anger. 

Old people realise the danger more than young people, for its effect upon the organism is more severe in old age than in youth. 

It is fairly easy to control the outward expression of anger, but very difficult to control the inner tumult. The agitation affects the whole system, and time is needed for the tumult to die down.

To control anger, we must switch it aside instantly, for it spreads with amazing rapidity to adjacent brain areas. It summons to its aid all the emotions and instincts akin to it. 

If you have ever stood on the seashore after a storm you will have noticed how the tossing of the waves continues long after the wind has gone down. It is much the same with a fit of anger. The neural (nerve) disturbance continues to affect one for a long time after the fit has passed. Seek refuge from the disturbance in simulating the cool, calm, col- 43

lected attitude of mind, and turn your thoughts into peaceful channels by means of an entertaining book or some form of work. 

§ 16-PETTY ANNOYANCES 

Many people who can put up a good fight against a big trouble are easily disturbedbowled over-by the little annoyances, or, as someone has termed them, the pin-pricks of daily life. An expected letter has not come to hand, someone has been rude to us, we have missed an appointment, we have been blamed for something unjustly, bad weather has spoilt an outing, things have gone badly at home or at business. What a long list we could all make of these pin-pricks!

How is it that these things annoy us out of all proportion to their seeming importance?

The answer is simple. Because we dwell upon them. We keep talking about them to ourselves (and sometimes to others) until we work ourselves up into a feverish state. It is all very foolish, as we can easily see when the 44

PETTY ANNOYANCES 

irritation has passed and we can look at the matter calmly. We have allowed ourselves to get in the grip of an emotion, and as one emotion always tends to arouse others, we have to contend with a host of enemies. 

There is only one way to deal with these little annoyances and that is to turn the mind aside from them instantly. Get busy with something, or switch into some other line of thought at once. Do not say you can't. The sting of any annoyance can be removed very quickly by a change of thought. It is a matter of training-using our power of invention to alter the course of the thought stream-and sometimes luck helps us. A chance happening may turn our thoughts into another channel without any aid from us. 

If you will look at these annoyances as training-ground in control, you will rob them of a great part of their power to annoy you. 

You will grow accustomed to deal with them in a critical mood, and there is nothing an emotion dislikes more than that. 45

§ 17-OPTIMISM AS AN AID TO THOUGHT- CONTROL 

There is healthy optimism and unhealthy optimism; the former is grounded on knowledge of the real Self, the latter on the feelings. Healthy optimism takes a square look at the worst thing that can happen to a man and then turns its back on the dark picture. 

The early Christians, perhaps, were the most healthy optimists the world has ever seen; they knew full well that terror abounded on every side, yet they marched on, obedient to the call, "Let not your heart be troubled."

Bearing on this, it is significant that the words "joy" and "rejoice" occur over one hundred times in the New Testament. 

The greatest enemy healthy optimism has to fight is what we term "flat days." Your work lacks interest, your spirits flag, you feel tired and out of sorts, and nothing seems to go right. What attitude should we take to these flat days? I like that taken by a certain

§ 18-HOW THOUGHT-CONTROL HELPS TOWARDS SUCESS IN LIFE 

No one can hope to win success unless he can exercise some measure of control over his thoughts. He must be able to turn aside all the doubting thoughts, the despairing thoughts, as soon as they make their presence felt. Disappointments, too, have to be dealt with; if 47

we dwell on them, initiative takes a back seat. 

Get it firmly in your mind that you are writing your life story; it is not being written for you. The people who think in that way are the people who turn to the back page to see how the story ends. You must know how the story will end. Accident apart, it must end according to plan.

In story-writing, a publisher values construction above every other virtue. A soundly constructed story possesses interest; every part fits into its place, and the end is consistent with all that has gone before. To write our life story well we must build it scientifically. Nothing must be allowed to enter unless relative to the end we have plotted. 

To ensure sound construction we must look at life as a process of becoming; it is an intelligent process where nothing is left to chance. It is bounded throughout by law and order. Gloomy, despairing thoughts are outside the process; they interfere with the tale, they must be cast to one side. 48

§ 19 THE RELIGIOUS ASPECT OF THOUGHT- CONTROL 

To a great number of people the religious aspect of thought-control is very important. I propose to say a few words about it. 49

Consider the early Christians, with their backs to the wall; "troubled on every side, yet not distressed; perplexed, but not in despair; persecuted, but not forsaken, cast down, but not destroyed." How did they acquire such power? Not through faith alone, mighty though it is; be assured of that.

St. Paul gives the secret. They were required to submit themselves to a rigid discipline of thought-control. In 2 Cor. x 5, he states that the follower of Christ must bring into captivity every thought to the obedience of Christ. In the original Greek St. Paul uses military expressions; the Christian is engaged in warfare; he must make a prisoner of every thought that raises itself against God, and lead it into subjection to Christ. To the modern Christian this message, in simpler language, runs thus: "You must put aside all evil and harmful thoughts if you are to fight the good fight successfully." 50

IV § 20-SUMMARY 

THOUGHT-CONTROL means a mind where order and harmony rule. A happy, contented and successful life is its aim.

In essence, thought-control is simply the substituting of one thought for another; a good thought for an evil thought, a beneficial thought for a harmful one. The difficulty in controlling one's thoughts lies in the nature of the mind itself.

The principal hindrances to thought-control are: (1) the Emotions and their correlate

Instincts; (2) certain Innate Tendencies, or 

Modes of Acting, of the mind, such as Suggestibility, Imitation, Sympathy, Play, Habit; (3) our Temperaments. 53

The root of the difficulty, in which all these hindrances play a part, is the warfare between the different selves in our being and our Real or Fundamental Self, which constitutes the Will.

The student of thought-control must begin by cultivating (1) the cool, calm, collected attitude of mind; (2) a right mental attitude; (3) interest in what he does; (4) the control of the imagination; (5) the development of will-power; (6) concentration of mind. He must also guard against fatigue in mind and in body.

There are two methods of switching thoughts aside-Direct and Indirect Switching. The direct method works by substitution, and the indirect by diverted attention. In both cases the switching must be done instantly, so that the harmful thought may have no time to establish itself. 54

Suggestion must be fought by a critical attitude towards it, so as to call into play the reasoning powers-suggestion's most powerful enemy. 

Both bad and good habits are formed by repetition. Bad habits must be opposed by habits opposite in character.

The things you worry about rarely come to pass. Encourage this thought and worry will not appear so deadly. To fight worry, keep switching the worrying thought aside. Remember that worry grows by repetition; check the repetition and you check the worry. 55

The Blues is simply nature's call to halt and rest. As a state of mind it will soon pass with the restoration of nervous energy. 

Fear makes its attack in thousands of ways, but the most troublesome forms are Fear as to the Future, Fear of the Unexpected-(something that may happen but which we cannot foresee)—Fear of Responsibility. To control these fears we must refuse to dwell on them; we must fight them by a belief in Self. Students should recognise that fear is useless, a drag upon energy and initiative, for it tends to suppress all mental activity other than that which any particular fear calls forth. 

Anger must be checked instantly, or it becomes difficult to control. The best safeguard against it is the cool, calm, collected attitude of mind.
